Microsoft fixes Windows activation as admins pick through Exchange outage wreckage

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Another unscheduled patch for IT teams already having quite enough fun with Redmond

Microsoft has released an out-of-band update to fix licensing and activation errors introduced by November's Windows security update.

The fix is available from the Microsoft Update Catalog for Windows Server 2025, 2022, and 2019, Windows 10 22H2 ESU, and Windows 10 Enterprise LTSC 2021 and 2019. Windows 11 23H2 users can obtain it from the Catalog or through Windows Update's Optional updates menu.

The "non-security OOB update" targets affected enterprise devices. Microsoft stated: "Certain Windows licensing and activation operations using the Software Licensing Management Tool (SLMGR) might fail with error 0x80041008 (Invalid parameter).

"This primarily affects organizations using slmgr.vbs commands or automated processes to configure Key Management Service (KMS) activation, install product keys, or manage Windows licensing."

Devices that are already activated will remain activated, Microsoft said.

Breaking the machinery customers use to activate paid-for software is a bad look for a Patch Tuesday release. Administrators may wonder how Microsoft's updates are tested when installing one means waiting for another to repair the damage.

Those same administrators may also have been fielding calls about last week's widespread Exchange Online outage, which Microsoft has partly attributed to a defect it introduced in July.

On top of that, Microsoft told administrators just days ago to consider unsupported Exchange Server 2016 and 2019 "permanently vulnerable." Exchange Online avoids that particular support deadline, but the recent outage was a reminder that handing over the servers doesn't make software defects disappear.

Forget "security by obscurity." Last week's approach was more like "security by unavailability."

Between getting Windows activated and keeping Exchange Online online, Microsoft gave administrators plenty to do. None of it was likely to feature in their original plans for the week.

Former Valve developer Chet Faliszek – writer behind the likes of Half-Life 2, Portal and its sequel, Team Fortress 2, Left 4 Dead, and even Counter-Strike: Global Offensive – has one big regret about his time at the Steam company: a joke he didn't make.

Yes, you read that right. He reveals as much in a new short over on YouTube, asking, "What's the thing I regret the most at Valve? I was there for 12 years. So, what's the thing that I regret, that I did or didn't do?" Apparently, after sitting on this question for some time, he's become brave enough to publicly share his answer – an answer he thinks of "almost every year," as it weighs that heavy in his mind. Brace yourselves for this recollection, y'all.

"If you remember, Valve had Steam Dev Days. We actually had a really early Steam Dev Days where I spoke at, and I spoke horribly, and I made a note that I've got to learn how to speak in public. I'm still not good at it, but it was just so bad. I was just shaking. It was just horrible. So, at this point, when Steam Dev Days two comes around, I'm like, 'Hey, I'll help out.'" Valve then decided to make him the host.

The joke Faliszek mustered up was "going to put Gabe on the spot" – a joke about his polo shirt, to be precise. "My joke was, 'And here's Gabe Newell modeling the latest in polo,' because he always wore a polo shirt. 'Today's selection is polo after dark.'" The dev knew Newell would "lean into it" had he said it, but he kept it to himself and laughed "so hard" without ever, uh, actually making the joke out loud.

Apparently that's nothing new for him, as he's often "laughing at jokes I don't say out loud because I'm thinking, 'That's going to push it.'" He does, again, regret not making this one in particular, though. "I regret that. I should have said that joke. That was a good joke. Robert Pattinson cast as next James Bond

Robert Pattinson has been cast as the next James Bond in the upcoming film from director Denis Villeneuve. It will be the 26th James Bond film and the first since Amazon MGM took over the iconic franchise.

Pattinson will be the seventh actor to play the role. Monday's announcement — which came in part via an Instagram post — ends a long period of speculation about who would take over as "007" after Daniel Craig.

"James Bond is one of the most legendary characters in cinema, and to be entrusted with 007 is the honour of a lifetime," Pattinson said in a statement. "I couldn't be more excited about this partnership and the work ahead with this fantastic team."

Pattinson has appeared in several big-ticket films this year, including Christopher Nolan's "The Odyssey" and psychological thriller "Primetime," and he'll soon appear in "Dune: Part Three," also directed by Villeneuve. Pattinson, who rose to fame with the Twilight movie series, is also in production for Matt Reeves' "The Batman Part II," a follow-up to the 2022 film based on the DC comic.

The upcoming Bond film will have a script by Steven Knight and will be produced by Amy Pascal, known for her work on the Spider-Man franchise, and David Heyman, who produced all eight Harry Potter films.

"We were surrounded by extraordinary talent, but there can only be one 007. I am overjoyed to welcome Robert Pattinson as our new James Bond. He has the timeless soul of 007 and brings a spirit that is completely new. I cannot wait to begin," Villeneuve said.

In 2022, Amazon bought MGM Studios, which had long produced the Bond movies, for roughly $8.45 billion. Amazon MGM then acquired creative control of the James Bond intellectual property rights in 2025, when it closed a deal with longtime producers Barbara Broccoli and Michael G. Wilson.

The film is still untitled, and Amazon MGM hasn't announced a projected release date.

My cat, Mitzi, is 16 years and 8 months old. She has early-stage kidney disease and hyperthyroidism, which she is on medication for. About 2 months ago, she became deaf, and since then has refused to leave the master bedroom. I have moved her water bowl and litter box there and feed her there.

Is there anything I can do to get her to come out?â

At her age, sweet Mitzi has certainly earned the right to enjoy her golden years at her own pace, but I can imagine it is difficult to see her withdraw from the rest of the house, especially when you remember how she used to move around. However, I would not necessarily interpret her reluctance to leave the bedroom as something we need to correct. Losing her hearing can be quite disorienting. Imagine walking into a room where you can no longer hear footsteps, familiar voices, or the little sounds that help you understand what is happening around you. For Mitzi, the bedroom may now feel like her safest little world, where everything is familiar and predictable.

I actually think you have done a wonderful job adapting her environment by bringing her food, water, and litter box closer. Rather than encouraging her to leave right away, I would focus on helping her feel confident enough to explore if she wishes. You could start by sitting with her near the bedroom doorway, offering a favorite treat or a little affection. Gradually place treats a short distance beyond the door, allowing her to decide whether she wants to investigate.

Take care to only approach her within her visual field, avoid startling her while she sleeps, and keep pathways clear and nonslip. Think of it as extending her new world one comfortable step at a time, rather than asking her to return to her old routine.

There is one thing I would not overlook, though. Mitzi has both chronic kidney disease and hyperthyroidism, and at her age, reduced mobility or withdrawal can also be associated with arthritis, high blood pressure, visual changes, or inadequate control of her thyroid disease. I would recommend a senior cat veterinary checkup that includes blood pressure measurement, assessment for arthritis, and follow-up kidney and thyroid testing. Sometimes what looks like a behavioral change is actually a senior cat quietly telling us that they are in pain and something does not feel quite right.

Most importantly, please do not feel that you are encouraging a bad habit by keeping her essentials in the bedroom. You are adapting her home to meet her changing needs, and that is a beautiful expression of your love and care for her. If the bedroom is where Mitzi feels safe, eats well, drinks, uses her litter box, and enjoys your company, that can be a perfectly good quality of life.

At this stage, happiness is not measured by how much of the house she explores, but by how comfortable and secure she feels in the little world she chooses.

Children’s brain responses to food may help predict later fat gain

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Children who ate more ultra-processed foods showed stronger brain responses to food pictures, which were linked to greater body fat gain later.

Breakfast cereal, packaged bread and instant soup get blamed for childhood weight gain because of what’s in them.

A new study links children’s ultra-processed food intake to how their brains respond to pictures of food.

A year later, the children with stronger reactions had gained more body fat. Every child was a healthy weight when the study began, and this kind of study can show a link but not prove a cause.

Dr. Kathleen Page, a physician and professor of medicine and pediatrics at the Keck School of Medicine of USC, is the study’s senior author, and Jasmin Alves is the first author.

“Children’s brains are still developing, habits are still forming and food preferences are still being made. It’s an important period where the brain is very sensitive to food and the food environment,” Dr. Page said.

The 46 children were 7 to 10 years old and part of the BrainChild study.

That long-running project follows children whose mothers did or did not have diabetes during pregnancy, and it recruits them through Kaiser Permanente Southern California.

Each child had a BMI below the 85th percentile for their age and sex.

On two separate days, each child and a parent walked trained staff through everything the child had eaten in the previous 24 hours.

The researchers then sorted every food by how processed it was. On average, ultra-processed foods supplied about 68% of the children’s daily calories, close to the 67% a 2021 analysis found for American kids as a whole.

About a month later, each child lay in an MRI scanner after a 12-hour fast and then a glucose drink.

The scanner recorded how active two deep brain areas were during the food photos.

Both areas are part of a structure called the striatum, which handles motivation, learning and reward.

The ventral striatum is involved in anticipating and experiencing rewards. The dorsal striatum helps link cues with learned actions and habits.

Children who got more of their calories from ultra-processed foods had stronger dorsal striatum responses to the food photos.

The ventral striatum showed no clear pattern. The association with dorsal striatum responses remained after accounting for each child’s age, sex, body size and whether their mother had diabetes during pregnancy.

In an interview with Earth.com, Dr. Page emphasized that the association held even after the researchers accounted for total calorie intake, suggesting that how much children ate did not fully explain the pattern.

The children also consumed a standardized drink before their scans, “so the stronger response to food cues wasn’t simply due to hunger,” she said.

Rodent studies had already shown that a steady diet of sugar-rich food changes how the dorsal striatum works.

No earlier study had looked directly at how much ultra-processed food people habitually eat and how their striatum responds, the authors wrote.

The children came back for measurements roughly a year later, with an average gap of about 15 months.

Their BMI had risen by one point on average, their body fat by close to one percentage point, and their waists by about 1.5 inches (3.9 centimeters).

Children whose dorsal striatum had reacted more strongly to the food photos gained more in BMI and body fat. Eating more ultra-processed food was linked to a bigger waist a year later.

Ultra-processed food intake’s links to BMI and body fat were weak enough that chance could explain them.

The team then asked how much of the link between diet and fat gain involved the brain.

Once the dorsal striatum response was in the model, the association between ultra-processed food and body fat gain shrank by 44%, and the one with BMI by 31%.

Dr. Page said the result does more than repeat that these foods are tied to weight gain, because it points to a pathway in the brain.

“It actually gives us insight into the neurobiological underpinnings by which these foods may be linked to body composition over time.”

What stood out most to Dr. Page was that every child was a healthy weight when the study began.

“This suggests that the link between ultra-processed foods and the brain’s response to food cues may be detectable before obesity develops, rather than simply resulting from excess weight,” she told Earth.com.

Added sugar is in most ultra-processed foods, so the team checked whether sugar was driving the result.

Children who ate more added sugar tended to have stronger striatum responses too, but the pattern wasn’t clear enough to rule out chance.

When the team accounted for added sugar, the link between ultra-processed food and the dorsal striatum weakened and was no longer statistically clear.

Still, the authors wrote that sugar is unlikely to be the whole story.

Forty-six children is a small group, and the authors wrote that this limited their power to detect modest effects. Brain scans of this kind are also hard to reproduce in small groups.

Each child was scanned only once, so no one knows yet whether the stronger responses persist or fade as children get older.

An observational study like this one can’t say which came first. Children whose reward circuits already react more strongly to food may be the ones who reach for ultra-processed foods, rather than the other way around.

And because every child started at a healthy weight, the pattern may look different in children who already carry extra weight.

The research team said the burden shouldn’t fall only on families and children, because these foods are often the most accessible, affordable and convenient ones available.

“Changing the food environment to help people access healthier, more minimally processed foods is an important part of the solution,” Dr. Page noted.

For families in the meantime, Dr. Page said the practical goal is to shift the overall diet toward more minimally processed foods. “Parents should not feel that they have to eliminate every packaged food.”

Her examples were water instead of soda and fruit instead of fruit juice or fruit snacks. The aim is more vegetables, beans, nuts, eggs and other foods closer to their original form.

One way to test whether the diet is driving the brain response is to change the diet.

“A future intervention study could test whether replacing ultra-processed foods with mostly minimally processed foods changes how children’s brains respond to food cues and helps prevent excess body-fat gain,” Dr. Page said.

Meanwhile, the BrainChild team will keep following these children through puberty and into young adulthood.

Dr. Page said the next step is to see how their diets relate to changes in the developing brain. The team will also look at their risk for obesity over time.

The full study was published in the journal JAMA Network Open.

NASA's Voyager 1 reaches a light-day away from Earth

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

When just saying 'hello' takes a two-day round trip, you're a light-day, or 26 billion kilometers, from Earth. Only one human-made object has ever traveled that far — and its journey into interstellar space continues.

Hate long-haul flights? Well, imagine having to travel 3,300 years to get to your destination.

Imagine departing in 1977 and arriving in 2026, on November 18 at 11:16 a.m. Central European Time to be precise, and then, not stopping but continuing the journey.

NASA's Voyager 1, a spacecraft launched almost half-a-century ago, has traveled a light-day from Earth.

A light-day is the distance light covers in 24 hours — about 26 billion kilometers (or around 16 billion miles). Put another way: Light does in one day what it took the spacecraft 49 years, two months and 11 days to do.

But Voyager 1 is not only far away, it is also traveling at an incredible speed of 61,000 km/h (38,000 mph).

When Voyager 1 launched, it was in sync with a planetary alignment that was as rare as the spacecraft itself — one that happens only once every 176 years. It was the alignment Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune, which scientists wanted to investigate.

Once the original mission was accomplished, Voyager 1 used the planets Jupiter and Saturn to perform a gravitational slingshot.

It flew close to each planet, got pulled and accelerated by their massive gravity, and swung around faster than before. And that is how Voyager 1 has achieved its speed.

In the vacuum of space, nothing slows you down.

On Valentine's Day 1990, from beyond Neptune, Voyager 1 took the only portrait of the solar system from the outside ever made by a spacecraft.

In 2012, it became the first human-made object to reach interstellar space.

Its journey is ongoing. In about 40,000 years — long after its plutonium power supply dies out — it will still be flying and pass by the star Gliese 445, which is more than 17 light-years away from Earth.

That's light-years, not light-days. A light-year is the distance light travels in a year. It will take Voyager 1 about 18,000 years to reach the one light-year milestone.

Voyager 1 is destined to wander the Milky Way "perhaps eternally," as NASA puts it.

The spacecraft is the first human-made object ever to be one light-day away from home.

"If you want to say 'hello' to Voyager, you send your message and then you go home and you wait two days before you finally get a response," says Suzanne Dodd, the mission's project manager at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory.

But you do get a response. Even today it sends back data that helps scientists on Earth.

"[It's] still working great, sending back data from a region of space no other human-made craft has ever explored," says Kareem Badaruddin, the mission manager at JPL.

No story about Voyager 1 seems complete without mentioning The Golden Record, a phonographic record, that is part of the spacecraft's cargo.

The spacecraft is carrying other Earthly objects as messages and artefacts from Earth for extraterrestrials. But the Golden Record is what we tend to focus on because it contains soundbites representing languages and cultures from around our planet.

We're told it's very unlikely that any intelligent lifeform will ever receive the cargo or even know how to play The Golden Record.

But if they do get the record and work out how to play it, the first thing they will hear is a controversial voice — not one of Earthly unity, as was the original idea. Those are the words of Kurt Waldheim, a former UN Secretary-General (1972-81) and Austrian President (1986-92), whose concealed wartime past as a German army officer in the Balkans surfaced a decade after Voyager 1's launch. It was alleged Waldheim had participated in Nazi crimes.

Some years, Lamar Jackson's thumb hits another player and is fine. Some years, his ankle just gets hit rather than rolled up on. And those years, he has been -- and will be -- a player who can win an MVP and perhaps a Super Bowl.

Jackson's latest injury was almost too cruel to believe. After missing the previous three and a half games with a high ankle sprain, the two-time MVP was finally back. So, too, was the Ravens' hope. Jackson had looked phenomenal -- MVP-level phenomenal -- in new offensive coordinator Declan Doyle's system. Not only was he back, but he was back in a big spot: against fellow MVP Josh Allen in a crucial matchup vs. Buffalo on the first day of November, with Baltimore trying to end a two-game losing streak.

And then, on the Ravens' first play, Jackson hit his thumb on Greg Rousseau's arm. Jackson looked down at his injured right thumb, trotted toward the sideline and then took a knee.

"Oh boy," NFL on CBS analyst Ross Tucker said.

Medical personnel surrounded Jackson, draped a towel over his hand and brought him down the tunnel, off the field.

What more was there to say? Lamar Jackson, one of the defining players not just of the era but of NFL history, was hurt. Again. And it was serious. Again.

Jackson officially landed on IR with a thumb injury that will require surgery. It is "potentially" season-ending, and with the Ravens 4-5 and losers of four straight, it's looking like a lost season in Baltimore. This is the fourth time in the last six years the 29-year-old has missed at least four games. It has robbed the Ravens of their Super Bowl aspirations. It has robbed the NFL of a superstar. And it has robbed Jackson, a Heisman Trophy winner and twice an NFL MVP, of even higher levels of all-time greatness.

On the positive side, the thumb injury is the first ailment to land Jackson on IR. He has never had an ACL tear, an Achilles tear, etc. On the negative side, Jackson has racked up so many injuries of so many different kinds that it seems like something is constantly getting in the way, whether it's nagging at him or keeping him out of games. He even said the ankle pain entering the Bills game last week was merely "bearable," not gone.

Since the start of last season alone, Jackson has had hamstring, ankle, toe, knee, back, and thumb ailments. Not all of those kept him out, but they add up. He admitted he came back from last year's hamstring injury too early, impacting his play all season.

And yes, for what it's worth, it's bad luck. If Jackson's hand hits Rousseau at any other angle, he might not hurt his thumb enough to need surgery. If a diving Jermaine Johnson II doesn't awkwardly trap Jackson's ankle to the turf in Week 3, Jackson might not miss the next three games. Josh Allen and Patrick Mahomes have both had hundreds of similar plays and, with the exception of Mahomes' torn ACL last year, avoided injury for the most part. Sometimes, it's simply human anatomy, something Jackson cannot change.

For as prolific a runner as Jackson is, he does an excellent job avoiding hits on those plays. None of his injuries have been on runs. It has just been a constant parade of misfortune. Unfortunately for Jackson and the Ravens, the NFL rolls on, whether you're lucky or not.

Perhaps time and injuries have taken their toll and blocked our collective memory from how incredible Jackson has been. That would be truly unfortunate.

But he only threw 14 touchdowns. Jackson did things modern football has never known.

Jackson became the youngest quarterback to win MVP and joined Tom Brady as the only unanimous MVPs in NFL history. There are many ways to measure individual season greatness, but Pro Football Reference's "Approximate Value" is as good as any. Jackson had an approximate value of 25 in 2019, tied for the second-best season on record; LaDanian Tomlinson's 2006 season, in which he scored an NFL-record 31 touchdowns, is the only one better.

Jackson was healthy in 2020 and had another strong season, including a second straight 1,000-yard rushing effort. He has as many 1,000-yard rushing seasons as all other quarterbacks in NFL history combined (Michael Vick and Justin Fields both have one).

Then the injuries started. On their own, though, they can get a little monotonous, especially if you're making them every day, so it does help to add an ingredient here or there. And if you happen to enjoy omega-3-rich cold-smoked salmon, well, it pairs seamlessly with a classic scramble, boosting both the flavor and the protein content.

Once considered an expensive delicacy, smoked salmon is quite popular now in the U.S., and as a result, it's much easier to find at your local grocery store (even Aldi carries its own version, and it can be found at Walmart, too). Cold-smoked salmon, despite the fact that it tends to be packed in super-thin slices, is buttery and velvety. While it does taste fishy, there are also light, smoky notes that give it a deeply savory flavor, which works beautifully when combined with the mild egg scramble. Additionally, a serving (which will vary in size) might contain anywhere from 12 to 18 grams of protein, which is great if you have daily goals you want to meet or you're just looking to get more of the macronutrient in general.

Truly, the most difficult part about incorporating smoked salmon into your scramble is cooking the eggs to the right consistency without burning or browning them. All you have to do with the salmon is slice the ultra-thin pieces into slivers or small squares. Once your eggs have reached the texture you desire, you'll take the pan off the heat and fold in the salmon. That's literally it; you don't have to actually cook the salmon over direct heat because it's so delicate that the residual warmth of the scrambled eggs will gently bring it up to temperature.

Now, because smoked salmon is so light and thin, you might want your eggs to match that energy, so create a fluffy, soft texture with a bit of cream whisked in. Alternatively, you could take a page out of Bobby Flay's book and fold in a dollop of crème fraîche, or really up the decadence and melt in some cubes of cream cheese (and take care not to overcook anything, because smoked salmon with burnt, rubbery eggs? How Are Hotels Paid When You Redeem Points, And For Elite Perks?

There’s a real thrill to redeeming points for stays at luxury properties, and getting outsized value. I’m sure many people have wondered about the economics of that. How much is the hotel getting reimbursed for your stay? Who is paying for any elite perks that you receive? Does the hotel care if you redeemed points or not? Let’s talk about those various points in this post…

The major global hotel groups (Hilton, Hyatt, IHG, Marriott, etc.) don’t own a vast majority of their properties. Instead, the companies have an asset light strategy, and have management or franchise contracts for the individual properties.

In some cases, large investment firms own hotels, and they may have properties belonging to all kinds of brands. In other cases, a hotel may even be family owned.

The way the major hotel groups make money is primarily through fees (they’re also increasingly directly monetizing their loyalty programs, but that’s a whole different topic). There’s typically an initial fee to open the hotel, and then on top of that, there are fees on ongoing revenue. Then any upside (or downside) typically falls with the hotel owner. So the major hotel groups are making money, regardless of whether a hotel is raking in the cash or hemorrhaging money.

Now, one of the reasons that consumers flock to certain hotel groups is because of their loyalty programs, which are massive marketing machines. A big part of the value proposition of these loyalty programs is the ability to redeem points.

As you’d expect, those points are redeemed centrally through the program, and then the loyalty program has to “buy” that room from the hotel. After all, the hotel owner wants to be compensated for each head in a bed, especially since they’re already paying a system fund fee, to fund these perks. That brings us to the question of how hotels are reimbursed…

As you’d expect, hotel groups don’t publicly disclose how they reimburse their properties when members redeem points. However, over the years we’ve been able to get some insights into this.

Sometimes guests are given folios that are actually intended for the loyalty program, while in other cases the reimbursement rates showed up online when they weren’t supposed to.

So I’m totally making this up here, but if a hotel is charging $400 on a particular night, maybe the loyalty program would pay $80 for that room if occupancy is under 90%, while it might be paying $350 if occupancy is over 90%.

Note that the number of points you redeem for a stay doesn’t directly determine reimbursement for a hotel. In other words, a hotel doesn’t get paid X amount per redeemed point. Similarly, if a program offers a fifth night free on award redemptions, the hotel is getting reimbursed for all five nights, as it’s the loyalty program incurring the cost there.

Instead, points redemption rates are set by the loyalty program, and are intended to reflect the cost incurred by the program. That’s why in many cases you’ll find that redemption rates don’t correlate directly to how much a property would cost in cash, but rather also factor in average occupancy at a property.

A hotel loyalty program would probably rather you redeem your points at a $1,000 per night hotel that’s 50% full, rather than a $500 per night hotel that’s 95% full, because the latter redemption will cost the program more. Hotel loyalty programs use historical data to determine points pricing, and the costs they incur.

Let me actually give a real example from a recent stay. I recently redeemed points for a two night stay at a Wyndham Rewards property, at the cost of 30,000 points per night. The folio I was sent accidentally had the amount Wyndham had to pay, rather than the amount I had to pay.

Even though I was paying the same number of points both nights, the hotel was being reimbursed around $87 for one night, and $184 for the next night. Funny enough, while sitting in the lobby working the first morning, I overheard one front desk agent tell the other that they were fully sold out the following night, so that tracks. The first night was being reimbursed at a reduced rate, while the second night was being reimbursed based on something closer to the average daily rate.

If you have elite status with a hotel loyalty program, how does taking advantage of elite perks factor into this when redeeming points? Who pays for breakfast? Who pays for suite upgrades? Who pays for waived destination fees and parking?

For those curious, generally individual hotels don’t know how you earned your elite status. So it doesn’t matter to them if you earned it through actual hotel stays, or just by holding onto a credit card. Presumably a front desk agent could look at your stay history, but they typically wouldn’t have any reason to do so, in this context.

This is a loaded question, and there’s obviously no single right or wrong answers. A few thoughts, though.

Hotel staff are trained by corporate to treat guests redeeming points the same way as those paying cash. Many hotel groups even train front desk staff to thank people for redeeming their points, since it reflects engagement in the program. Officially, you should expect exactly the same treatment as if you paid cash, and you absolutely shouldn’t feel sheepish about the fact that you didn’t pay cash for your stay.

Now, in practice that might not always be how things play out. There’s sometimes a back and forth between hotel owners and hotel groups. Hotel owners often want it both ways — they want the power of the major hotel group distribution channels and loyalty programs, without actually incurring the costs associated with it, whether it’s complimentary upgrades, points redemptions, or a decent breakfast.

Will you sometimes find a hotel that’s perhaps a bit condescending if you redeem points and have an issue? Absolutely, but it’s not how it should be, and it’s the exception rather than the norm. Some may remember my stay at the Aegon Mykonos, at the time a Marriott Autograph Collection property (the hotel has since… mysteriously disappeared?).

Long story short, I was downgraded despite using a confirmed upgrade, and the manager was completely unapologetic about it, and was unwilling to do anything meaningful. One of his defenses was that I was staying “complimentary,” and therefore shouldn’t be complaining.

The hotel industry is complex, given the various parties involved, from the hotel group to the individual hotel owners. Hopefully the above is a useful rundown of how reimbursement works when redeeming points. The important thing to understand is that hotels get paid a lot more when you’re redeeming points if the property is full.

When redeeming points, you definitely shouldn’t feel sheepish about it. You earned your stay, and the reason hotels belonging to major loyalty programs are able to command a revenue premium is because of the points and loyalty perks they offer.

Grammy Awards: All the Artists Who’ve Been Nominated in the Big 4 Categories in the Same Year

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Remarkably, 13 of these 17 artists are women.

When the nominations for the 69th annual Grammy Awards were announced on Monday (Nov. 16), Ella Langley became the 17th artist to receive nominations in each of the so-called “Big Four” Grammy categories – album, record and song of the year plus best new artist – in the same year. Remarkably, 13 of those 17 artists are women. How to account for that? Grammy voters seem to like to give extra encouragement to talented young women artists.

Langley is the first country artist to be nominated in each of the Big Four categories since Bobbie Gentry in 1968, who was the first artist from any genre to achieve the feat.

Three times in recent years, two artists achieved the feat in the same year. Billie Eilish and Lizzo both achieved the feat in 2020. Olivia Rodrigo and FINNEAS both achieved it in 2022. Chappell Roan and Sabrina Carpenter both achieved it in 2025.

This has happened more often in recent years because the number of nominees in each of the Big Four categories has expanded. Until the Grammy ceremony held in 2019, there were generally five nominees in each of those categories. The upcoming 69th annual Grammy Awards, set for Sunday (Feb. 7), will mark the ninth year in a row there have been eight or more nominees in each of those categories. There were 10 at the ceremonies in 2022 and 2023, but the Recording Academy (mercifully) brought it back down to eight in 2024.

All but one of the 16 previous artists to receive nods in each of the Big Four categories had a spotlight performance slot on the Grammy telecast. The exception was FINNEAS, and even he was featured in sister Eilish’s performance of “Happier Than Ever” on the 2022 telecast.

Here’s a complete list of artists who have been nominated in each of the top four Grammy categories in the same year. The years shown are the years of the Grammy ceremonies.

Gentry, who was 25 at the time, was nominated for her debut album Ode to Billie Joe and the intriguing story song that was its title track. She won best new artist and in two other categories – best vocal performance, female and best contemporary female solo vocal performance. Gentry hasn’t won another Grammy since that night, though she was nominated for her 1970 album Fancy.

Cross, then 29, was nominated for his eponymous debut album and his hypnotic ballad “Sailing.” He won in all four categories and for best arrangement accompanying vocalist(s). Cross received three more nominations the following year for “Arthur’s Theme (Best That You Can Do),” though he hasn’t added to his trophy collection since his big night.

Lauper, then 31, was nominated for her first solo album, She’s So Unusual — and, in a unique occurrence, two different works for record and song of the year, respectively. The zesty “Girls Just Want to Have Fun” was nominated for record; the poignant “Time After Time,” which Lauper co-wrote with Rob Hyman, was nominated for song. She won best new artist. She won a second Grammy 29 years later – best musical theater album for Kinky Boots.

Chapman, then 24, was nominated for her eponymous debut album and her surprise hit, “Fast Car.” Chapman, the first Black artist to be nominated in each of the Big Four categories in one year, won best new artist and in two other categories – best pop vocal performance, female and best contemporary folk recording. She won a fourth Grammy eight years later for “Give Me One Reason.”

Carey, then 20, was nominated for her eponymous debut album and her sleek ballad “Vision of Love,” which she co-wrote with Ben Margulies. She won best new artist and best pop vocal performance, female. She has since won three additional Grammys.

Cole, then 29, was nominated for her debut album, This Fire, and her hit “Where Have All the Cowboys Gone?” She won best new artist. She had seven nominations that night, including producer of the year, non-classical, but hasn’t even been nominated since that night.

The singer, then 26, was nominated for her debut album, Acoustic Soul, and her single, “Video.” She co-wrote the song with Carlos “Six July” Broady, Reginald Harris and Shannon Sanders. India.Arie was the first artist to receive nods in each of the Big Four categories but not win in any of them. She went 0-7 that year (though she came back to win two Grammys the following year and two more in the years since then).

Winehouse, then 24, was nominated for her second (and final) studio album Back to Black and her retro-sounding hit “Rehab.” Winehouse, the first English artist to be nominated in each of the Big Four categories in the same year, won for record and song of the year, best new artist and in two other categories – best pop vocal album and best female pop vocal performance. Winehouse won a sixth Grammy four years later for “Body and Soul,” a duet with Tony Bennett. Winehouse died in 2011 at age 27.

Smith won record and song of the year, best new artist and best pop vocal album. Eight years later, they won a fifth Grammy for “Unholy,” a record-setting collab with Kim Petras.

The simple and convenient Apple Maps feature every CarPlay user should know about

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Let your friends know what time you'll be arriving.

For many iPhone users, Apple CarPlay has become synonymous with a quality driving experience, from playing their favorite tunes to navigating to their next destination. And although Apple continues to upgrade its popular infotainment system, one of its most useful features has been hidden in Apple Maps for years and is critical to letting your family, friends, and, if you are like me, enemies know when you will arrive at your destination.

I am, of course, referring to sharing your estimated time of arrival (ETA) through Apple Maps. Most users are probably familiar with Apple's Find My and location-sharing functions, but CarPlay's Share ETA feature is woefully underappreciated. Both simple to use and highly customizable, the feature is a godsend for worried parents and surprise parties everywhere.

To enable Share ETA, go to Settings > Apps > Maps. From here, scroll down to Share ETA and enable the feature. You can also enable location sharing in Messages, Contacts and Find My. You may also want to turn on Siri to make sharing an ETA more convenient. To do so, go to your iPhone's Settings, tap Siri and enable Turn on Siri.

Sharing your ETA is a simple way to improve your CarPlay experience. If you're using Siri, you can initiate a voice command when you're following Maps directions, either by holding the voice command button on your steering wheel or saying "Hey Siri" if your car supports voice activation. Next, say "share my ETA with (contact name)."

If you would rather not use Apple's voice assistant, you can share your ETA directly in Maps. After you've set off, tap the route card at the bottom of the screen, then Share ETA and choose a recipient. If the contact isn't on the list, you can find them under Open Contacts. To share with more than one person, tap Sharing with at the bottom of the screen, Open Contacts and select your intended recipients. You can also stop sharing your ETA by opening the route card again, tapping Sharing with, selecting Stop Sharing ETA and choosing the contact you want to stop sharing with.

You can also automatically share your ETA with someone by adding their contact to a pinned location. First, go to Maps > Places > Pinned. Next, tap the blue information button > Add Person. Next, tap the desired contact and confirm the selection. Now, whenever you select the pinned location in Apple Maps and tap Go, it will automatically share your ETA.

Backlash Over ‘Robots’ Taking Photos at Christmas Lights Event

A video of Christmas lights being switched on in Central London has provoked a debate about people’s picture-taking habits and the importance of living in the moment.

The video was shared by BBC London on November 4. It shows a sea of people, seemingly every single one of them with their phones aloft, filming in anticipation of the elaborate lights on Carnaby Street turning on and the fireworks that followed.

The comments were flooded with people disgusted that no one appeared to be taking in the moment, instead watching a real event that was unfolding right in front of them through the small screen of their phone.

“I’ve stopped filming most things like fireworks and concerts when I realized I haven’t watched a single one of them back,” says Sufian Ahmed. “It’s so much about being in the moment. Experiencing it with the person you’re next to. You lose all that when watching it through a narrow screen.”

Many compared it to an episode of the Black Mirror TV show, while others called out the people for being “robots.” Others pointed out the hypocrisy of social media users complaining about the video while watching it on their phone.

Previous studies have found that taking a photo of an event affects human memory. A 2018 psychological study suggested that shooting photos or videos gives people a false sense that they preserved a moment, when in actual fact it’s the camera that did it for them.

In 2013, researchers asked one group of people to tour an art museum without taking photos, while another group was assigned to take photos. Those who took photos were significantly worse at identifying items in the museum and recalled fewer details about the items they did remember than those who didn’t take photos.

Dr. Kaitlyn Regehr of University College London and the University of Toronto says public attitudes toward smartphones and technology are shifting.

“This is not the first year that people have attended events and watched those events through screens,” the smartphone academic tells the BBC.

“But what’s significant is that this year there’s a lot of critical public discourse happening. And that, I think, is really, really positive.

Dr. Regehr says the backlash could be down to “our discomfort with the amount of influence that big tech is playing on our lives.”

Custodia's legal journey has spanned over the past six years to try to obtain a master account, which allows institutions direct access to the Fed's payment systems and provides the most direct access to the U.S. money supply available to financial institutions. Those without master accounts are often forced to rely on partner banks with master accounts to provide services.

Custodia Bank, a Wyoming-chartered crypto-focused firm founded by Wall Street veteran Caitlin Long, began its master account quest in 2020 when it applied for that account in Kansas City. The Kansas City Fed then denied its application in January 2023, citing concerns about the bank's crypto-related business model. Custodia sued the Kansas City Fed in June 2022, initially over a 19-month processing delay and later arguing that federal law requires access for eligible institutions.

In March, an appeals court denied Custodia's petition for rehearing en banc, leaving intact a previous ruling that the Fed has discretion to approve or deny a master account for eligible institutions.

Custodia then petitioned for the high court to take its case up in July. The Supreme Court's decision to not take up the bank's challenged now leaves intact the previous decision. Custodia did not immediately respond to a request for comment from The Block.

The Fed asked for comments on the proposed slimmed-down master account last year and said it would not allow for interest on balances and could not access the discount window to borrow funds, among other limits. Comment letters from crypto advocates called the proposal "overdue" and also asked for some revisions. Community banks, however, warned that novel financial institutions were not subject to the same "robust regulatory compliance regime."

HIV treatment, antiretroviral therapy (ART), has seen dramatic advancement in the past four decades. HIV is now a chronic condition. When people take their ART as prescribed and maintain an undetectable viral load and a high T-cell count, life expectancy for those living with HIV approximates that of those who are uninfected (Trickey et al., 2023).

In addition to the direct health benefits of maintaining an undetectable viral load, there is an additional prevention benefit. When someone living with HIV has an undetectable viral load, they are unable to transmit HIV through sex (Eisinger et al., 2019). Public health campaigns promoting ART adherence have called attention to this phenomenon, terming it Undetectable = Untransmissible or U=U.

We might assume that prevention messaging that draws on U=U would be most effective among people living with HIV who are partnered with someone who is uninfected. Having an undetectable viral load would decrease or eliminate the potential for sexual transmission of HIV to their partner. Alternatively, people living with HIV may be generally motivated to take their ART and maintain an undetectable viral load when they are having sex with partners who may be uninfected regardless of relationship status.

In two recent studies (Hillesheim & Starks, 2026; Starks et al., 2024), our team examined relationship status group differences in ART adherence and viral detectability in large samples of sexual minority male adults.

Ordinarily, we expect people in relationships to have better health outcomes compared to single people (Kiecolt-Glaser & Wilson, 2017). In both of these papers, findings suggested this was true for sexual minority men living with HIV in non-monogamous relationships and those whose partners were uninfected. These groups were more likely to be adherent to ART and report an undetectable viral load compared to single sexual minority men. Presumably, these are groups where men may be most likely to consider preventing transmission of HIV to sexual partners.

There was also a warning sign in both papers. Sexual minority men in relationships where both partners were living with HIV were significantly less likely to be adherent to their ART and achieve an undetectable viral load. For these men, prevention motivation may be of limited relevance. It may be important to emphasize the personal health benefits of HIV treatment to maximize HIV outcomes in these subgroups.

Effective HIV treatment not only prolongs the lives of people living with HIV, but it also eliminates the potential for sexual transmission. This prevention motivation may explain why rates of ART adherence and achieving an undetectable viral load are higher among sexual minority men in non-monogamous relationships and those with uninfected partners. We may need different messages, emphasizing the personal health benefits of HIV care, to reach subgroups who have low levels of sexual HIV prevention motivation.

The years 2000 to 2009 are some of the most significant years and helped birth some of the most popular franchises in cinematic history as a whole.

Sam Raimi's Spider-Man series, the X-Men franchise, the Marvel Cinematic Universe, Christopher Nolan's The Dark Knight series and so much more all got their big breaks in this era. Without these abundantly important 10 years, superhero movies would not even be close to where they are today. Knit Health’s co-founders, from left: Jonas Knecht, co-CTO; Jonathan Kolstad, CEO; Maya Petersen, chief scientist; and Ted Robertson, COO.

Knit Health, a startup building an AI model that predicts what care patients will need next, has raised a $35m Series A led by NEA. Town Hall Ventures, SignalFire and existing investors joined the round, Knit Health said in a statement on Monday.

Chief executive Jonathan Kolstad first disclosed the round to Will Maddox at Axios Pro. The money will fund the rollout of Med-BEAM across health systems and other healthcare groups. Knit calls the model a world model for healthcare.

Scientists from the University of California, Berkeley founded Knit in 2025. It has raised $46.6m so far. Earlier seed and pre-seed backers include Frist Cressey Ventures, Uncork Capital, Moxxie Ventures, Coalition Operators and The Aligned Fund.

According to Knit, much of healthcare’s key information never makes it into notes. It sits in the orders, referrals and diagnoses that clinicians make, and in what happens next. That makes it hard for large language models to tell where a patient is heading, the company said.

Med-BEAM learns from billions of real healthcare decisions and hundreds of millions of patient records that follow people over time. It predicts the care a patient is likely to need and shows how different treatments could change their course.

Knit said it trains only on data it has clear, documented rights to use. All of that data is fully de-identified before it reaches the company. Med-BEAM checks its predictions against real clinical outcomes. That lets it flag when there is too little evidence to be confident.

“Healthcare still operates too often on what amounts to chance: which doctor you see, which day you arrive, which hospital you enter,” Kolstad said in the statement.

Knit compared Med-BEAM with a group of models from frontier AI labs. Each had to predict the clinical actions for hospitalised patients over the next 24 hours. Knit picked no outcome in advance.

Med-BEAM was up to four times more accurate, 100 times faster and 250 times cheaper than the models tested, Knit said. The company added that its model’s confidence matched real outcomes more closely.

Blake Wu, a partner at NEA, said one model could power “dozens of critical workflows”. He listed care management, patient navigation, clinical trial recruitment, specialist scheduling, hospital-at-home care and treatment-response prediction.

Jordan Belfort’s new AI voice startup, Tone, wants to sell like the ‘Wolf of Wall Street’

Years ago, as questions mounted about WeWork’s soaring valuation, I sat down with co-founder Adam Neumann. We were negotiating a stage interview, and he asked to meet me twice in the weeks leading up to it, once at a WeWork in San Francisco and again at his office in New York.

I knew full well that the numbers that SoftBank in particular had assigned to WeWork made little sense, but I came away understanding why investors wanted to throw in their lot with him.

It’s similarly easy to understand why people are enchanted by Jordan Belfort, the self-proclaimed “Wolf of Wall Street.” Belfort famously pleaded guilty in 1999 to securities fraud and money laundering tied to Stratton Oakmont, the Long Island brokerage where salespeople pushed penny stocks in pump-and-dump schemes while Belfort acquired a Southampton mansion, a 167-foot yacht, and at least one helicopter. (He destroyed the latter two while high on Quaaludes and cocaine, as depicted in Martin Scorsese’s famous adaptation of Belfort’s autobiography, “The Wolf of Wall Street.”)

You might think someone who spent 22 months in prison and was ordered to pay $110.4 million in restitution to 1,513 victims wouldn’t get another shake. The victims ultimately received “far less” than they were owed, TMZ reported in July, noting that Belfort completed his court-ordered restitution obligations in late April of this year.

But Belfort, whose energy has a galvanizing quality, keeps resurfacing, including in recent years as a Miami-based sales consultant and one-time host of crypto workshops at his Miami Beach home. (A New York Times reporter attended a weekend workshop several years ago where the price of admission was one bitcoin or its equivalent value at the time of $40,000.)

Now, he is moving into the intersection of what he knows best — sales — and the hottest trend on the planet, AI, building out a voice-based AI platform. The secret sauce is that it imbues its voices with the kind of tonality that wins deals, he says, for example, by phrasing declarative statements as questions (which “builds engagement”) and even whispering at times (which “implies scarcity of information”).

Belfort says the platform, called Tone, is already being used by companies that have more sales leads than their human teams can handle. Now, in a move that could also help expand its training data, Tone is launching a self-service platform for content creators and authors who want to turn their books into “expressive AI-generated narration.” Users can select from Tone’s AI voices or, as a voice-cloning feature rolls out, create a clone of their own voice.

It’s easy to argue the market is already saturated with AI voice startups and technologies, and Tone comes with added risk: Belfort’s co-founder in the business is longtime investor Shervin Pishevar, who has a controversial past of his own. We also can’t independently verify that the technology works as described (neither co-founder is an engineer).

But if my time with Neumann taught me anything, it’s that doubts about the numbers rarely get in the way of a good pitch. When Belfort looks for investors to back him in this new venture, finding believers won’t be too hard.

Here is part of our conversation, edited for length and clarity.What have you built?

It’s a very unique way of going about building a voice, and it stems from a system of influence and persuasion I’ve been teaching since the late 1980s. It’s not just what you say, but how you say it. When you’re not in person, body language collapses into tonality. There are about 15 very powerful tonalities that people use all the time. The phrase I invented for this was “goal-oriented communication.” If I’m trying to be perceived as an expert, I apply tonality strategically.

It started that way. As a non-engineer, I went to first principles: How would I teach a human to do this? One tonality at a time. I have 30 years of super high-quality data , and I started teaching the model. It was a breakthrough moment, because it not only sounded exactly like me, it had nailed the tonality. Then I taught it a second one, and then how to link them. The key to all of this is data. Even the littlest bit of corrupt data dramatically negates the progress you’ve made.

How did you and Shervin Pishevar come together?

Shervin and I have been personal friends for a long time, and we were out for dinner one night. I had been pitched by an AI company that said it had an AI that could do selling. I listened to it. It was awful. But it put the idea in my head. I thought I was just going to teach models how to sell, but I couldn’t find a voice that was good enough. Necessity is the mother of invention, so we built our own.

Who are your customers, and how do they use it?

We have law firms and health insurance companies. We do a lot in polling and automobiles. It will take you through an entire health insurance sale. It asks qualifying questions, like what type of insurance you want, your budget, your ages. Then it chooses a plan and explains the features and benefits. Our latency is like 600 milliseconds. If the payment is too high, it’ll say it can bring the payment down and explain how to raise your deductible. If you don’t like the plan, it swaps you to a different one. It does literally the whole entire sale.

How does the AI handle a sales call that goes off script?

It never says “That’s a great question,” unless it really is. I hate that. It’s so ingratiating. In any sale there’s a happy path, how we’d want the perfect conversation to go, and every last word can be mapped out. But people have questions and concerns, and they take you off the straight line. So the trick is a model that can carry that perfect conversation, handle anything thrown at it, answer elegantly and return to the straight line, without breaking rapport and without hallucinating — or, as a salesperson, lying. We’ve trained the model to do that.

Have you generated any revenue from Tone yet? Have you raised funding for it?

Revenue, probably about a million bucks — $700,000, maybe. We’ve raised friends and family and seed, about $10 million.

I want to address the elephant in the room. You have a fraud conviction. Pishevar has faced misconduct allegations that he denies. Why should a bank or a hospital or a consumer brand trust this product coming from the two of you?

All I can say for myself is that I did make mistakes. It was a very, very long time ago, and I’ve spent the last 20-plus years teaching people all over the world. I’ve been hired by companies all over the world to teach sales and persuasion. If some companies don’t want to work with us, that’s their decision. But I think there would be more than enough that would see the value of what we created.

I still don’t think it’s quite as good as a top producer. A top producer in the field would outdo it call for call. I think that will change in the next six months to a year. To make a model respond this quickly, you’re not using frontier models. We’re using our own very small, very light models, so they don’t have quite as much intelligence. I think it will significantly outperform a mediocre closer.

Where is it used most effectively? Low-dollar sales opportunities? I’d think the more money someone is spending, the more human contact they’d demand.

I don’t think it’s about replacing humans. What I see most with customers is that they simply don’t have the human capital available. They could get a lot more leads than they have people to call, or they need coverage at different times of day or in different languages. I think there’s always going to be a need for great human salespeople. I really believe that.

You’re competing with ElevenLabs and Cartesia and a lot of other voice AI companies. Tone is your edge?

I don’t think sounding human is really what this is about. They’ll all sound human in six months or a year. ElevenLabs is a great company, and Cartesia as well, but those voices didn’t work for us.

The FCC ruled in 2024 that AI voices fall under robocall rules under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act. How does outbound sales calling work?

We only call opt-in. You’re not going to use AI to cold call people. We would not allow that. You’d get sued by every TCPA lawyer in the world. It’s either opted-in leads or existing customers. The exception is polling, where there’s an exemption that lets you call private homes without opt-in.

We could launch the cloning tomorrow. We didn’t. If you create a clone in our system, you speak into the computer, so we know who is creating it, and that person signs a consent. If someone wants to upload audio, which we don’t recommend, you’d have to match a live voiceprint in real time.

Who created the technology? You’re not an engineer, and Pishevar isn’t, either. I saw a name on the patent, Jordan Anthony Metzner.

No. Our VP of engineering is Ben Sussman. He’s a brilliant engineer. I’m the judge and the jury and the executioner of what sounds perfect. I’ll prototype something in the most obscene way, and my engineers rebuild it from scratch.

Do you charge per minute, or based on the outcomes customers see?

A combination. For streaming, it’s per minute. The content platform will be monthly subscriptions. And we have some clients where the bulk of our money is made through success.

Is this an independent company or something that belongs inside a larger AI company?

If the right offer came up, and it was substantial enough, and I thought it made sense, who wouldn’t entertain a conversation? But right now my plan is to raise a significant amount. I don’t think we need to own data centers, but we need enough reserve compute. I wouldn’t think I’d be selling it anytime soon, but who knows what the future brings.

Paramount+ released a documentary about you this summer. Did you watch it?

I didn’t. Believe it or not, I don’t watch or read anything about me, good or bad. I never read a comment. When you’re well-known, you have to live by who you are inside. I go by my own internal compass.

Unlike many tokenized stocks, which give holders a claim on a share, Bullish's issuer-sponsored token "is itself the share," Jupiter COO Kash Dhanda said in a presentation at Solana's Breakpoint conference in London.

Dhanda framed the announcements around what he called the "post-tokenization era." Most tokenized assets today are "parked, not productive," he said.

Jupiter Liquidity Services, meanwhile, will help asset issuers design their markets, seed initial capital, find market makers and integrate with Jupiter's routing so that their tokens are available across the platforms that use it. "You integrate once, you will be available everywhere," Dhanda said.

"Shallow credit markets don't drive liquidity on chain," he added. "If you cannot do more with your onchain asset than you can do with the off-chain version of it, you're probably not going to switch."

Numa, a San Francisco startup that sells AI software to car dealerships, has raised a $45M Series C, the company announced on Monday. Blue Cloud Ventures led the round, with Threshold Ventures, Costanoa and Google’s Gradient Ventures taking part. Total funding now stands at $96M, after a $32M Series B in 2024.

Numa’s software answers dealership phone calls, texts and chats, books service visits and sends callers to the right staff. The company says more than 1,500 dealerships in the US and Canada use it. It claims to have handled over 1 billion conversations and booked more than 4 million appointments.

This year Numa pushed into sales. A new agent knows every car on the lot down to trim and mileage, and can suggest another if the one a buyer wanted has sold. In August, Numa bought SocketTime, which makes scheduling and repair tools for service shops.

“In two years, every general manager will have an AI COO,” said founder and CEO Tasso Roumeliotis.

He said the new money will pay for more engineers. Numa says it tripled its annual recurring revenue this year, but did not share figures or a valuation.

Carmakers are opening doors. GM has approved Numa as a vendor for its dealers, and more than 240 GM stores use it. In those stores, Numa says reply times fell from 23 hours to 24 minutes.

FordDirect, Ford’s dealer marketing venture, picked Numa as its AI vendor in August, giving it access to more than 3,000 Ford and Lincoln dealers. Numa is also approved for Stellantis dealers, about 3,000 more stores. Those are open doors, not signed customers.

Numa is not alone. Startups such as BadCo.AI are building AI for car buying, and voice AI firm Vapi raised a $50M Series B in May. Detroit’s three carmakers have also cut 20,000 office jobs since 2022, and AI is expected to speed that up.

Ahead of its IPO filing, Anthropic pledges to prioritize humanity’s benefit. Here’s how

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When Anthropic goes public on Wall Street, the new pressure to deliver profits will test a commitment it has made since its founding: to prioritize humanity’s benefit as it develops artificial intelligence.

The San Francisco startup, which plans an initial public offering this month, has sketched out a governance structure that it says will protect the company’s broader mission against the pull of financial interests. But the mechanisms it describes give the company wide leeway to decide how to measure whether it’s benefiting society, experts say.

Like its rival OpenAI, Anthropic is a public benefit corporation, a legal designation used by companies that pledge to balance shareholder interests with the public good. How that will play out for companies worth potentially trillions of dollars is uncharted territory.

“I think then the real test emerges if and when the question of public benefit becomes costly” — or when the public benefit comes into conflict with making a profit, said Sarah Kreps, a professor and director of Cornell University’s Tech Policy Institute.

In its regulatory filings, Anthropic pledges to pursue long-term benefits of humanity through the responsible development of advanced AI. “We strive to help bring humanity safely into an era where transformative AI exerts tremendous and potentially decisive influence over a broad range of human undertakings,” the company said. Anthropic also has pledged at least 5% of its stock to a “public benefit” fund and its founders have committed to donate 80% of their wealth.

A pledge to do good is baked into corporate structure

Other tech companies have pledged to do good including Google, which prominently featured its then-motto, “Don’t Be Evil,” in its investor prospectus when it went public in 2004. But the words were not legally binding, and critics have accused the tech giant of leaving them behind.

Elon Musk’s takeover of Twitter in 2021 shows how the conflict between profit and public good might play out in real life, said Stavros Gadinis, a law professor at UC Berkeley School of Law.

“Let’s say that Twitter’s directors felt that Elon was the wrong guy to police the public square because they didn’t trust him, they didn’t like him, they didn’t think he’d be fair, whatever it was,” Gadinis said. “But he made them an offer that was so high compared to what their advisors told them the company was really worth that it would be against their fiduciary duties to not sell to Elon. This is the situation that the public benefit charter is trying to avoid.”

Had Twitter been a public benefit corporation, it could have been required to consider factors beyond maximizing shareholder value when selling the company.

There aren’t many public benefit corporations whose shares are publicly traded and they are all much smaller than Anthropic and OpenAI. They include the eyewear company Warby Parker, the insurance company Lemonade and software developer Veeva Systems, which had a conventional corporate structure when it went public in 2013 and converted to a public benefit corporation in 2021.

Anthropic’s idealistic ambitions for how it develops AI could run up against competitive pressure, such as from China, Kreps said.

Anthropic and OpenAI’s models have gone from answering almost any question, even if it was dangerous, to becoming much more circumspect. But as they face more competition, including from open-source Chinese models that likely will tell you whatever you want, she said, “will they start to rethink some of those guardrails? And what happens then?”

The setup generally is not preferred by shareholders because it gives more power to the company’s board than it does to shareholders.

Whatever a company decides as its public benefit mission, it will likely be much more difficult to measure than profits. That’s particularly true for AI companies, whose leaders have exalted AI as the solution to many of humanity’s problems — from curing cancer to creating economic prosperity.

“There are these echo chambers, the Silicon Valley bubble where they really think that what they’re doing is for the good of humanity,” Kreps said. And someone immersed in that environment, she added, might not even ask the right questions or understand how “public benefit” should be measured.

A trust aims to hold Anthropic accountable

A Long-Term Benefit Trust created by Anthropic is intended to operate as an independent body of trustees with expertise in AI safety, national security, public policy, and social enterprise.

Through a special Class T common stock, the trust “generally has the authority to elect and remove” Class T directors, who together will constitute a majority of Anthropic’s board of directors, according to the IPO filing. The Class T common stock is a single share that allows the five trustees to vote as a unit. It has no economic rights — meaning they get to vote but they don’t see any financial benefit or losses from the share and can’t sell it.

“The LTBT’s arrangements are designed to minimize the LTBT’s and the Trustees’ exposure to our financial performance and grant the Trustees sufficient independence to ensure we are striking this balance appropriately,” the filing said.

Three of the company’s board directors are elected by the trust directly, and another three are elected by other shareholders, which include the company founders with a 50.1% voting majority. A seventh director, with a potentially tie-breaking vote, is nominated by the trust and the holders of the Class T shares, but cannot be appointed or removed without consent from the board’s nominating committee, which is expected to be controlled by the founders.

Effectively, the trust can only hold Anthropic accountable on issues such as AI safety and the company’s broader mission up to a point, Gadinis said.

The trust “cannot determine the everyday decisions of these directors or the management of the company,” he said. “If they disagree, they will have to go through the effort of removing directors, and they may not be able to remove a majority.”

As a public company, Anthropic also will be required to report finances and generally be more transparent than a privately held one. Many public companies dealing with AI, including Google and Microsoft, regularly update the public on efforts to develop AI responsibly.

The incentive for transparency could be as powerful as other mechanisms set up in Anthropic’s corporate structure, Gadinis said.

“I do think that by being public, we are going to get a lot more information, because a lot of people are going to be asking questions, and companies will be responding to these questions. not only because they’re required by security laws, but also because they want to build this kind of trust with the investing community,” he said.

—Barbara Ortutay, AP Technology Writer

Novo Nordisk's weight loss injection cut obesity in young children, trial shows

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

In the STEP Young trial, BMI fell an estimated 17.4% with semaglutide and rose 2.2% with placebo at week 68 among children 6 to under 12

Novo Nordisk shared results on Monday from a Phase 3 trial showing its once-weekly semaglutide injection, sold as Wegovy, lowered body mass index in children ages 6 to under 12 with obesity. The company presented the STEP Young data at ObesityWeek 2026 and published them in The New England Journal of Medicine.

At week 68, estimated BMI fell 17.4% from baseline among children taking semaglutide and rose 2.2% among those on placebo, assuming all children stayed on treatment, the company said. The estimated difference was 19.7 percentage points. Counting all children regardless of whether they stayed on treatment, BMI fell 13.5% with semaglutide and rose 2.7% with placebo, a difference of 16.2 percentage points.

If all children had stayed on treatment, 40.4% of those on semaglutide would no longer have been classified as having obesity, versus 0% on placebo. Regardless of adherence, the figures were 38.4% and 2.1%. Both groups were put on an eating plan with fewer calories and encouraged to move more for the length of the study.

The trial enrolled 167 children, and more than 85% had class II or III severe obesity at baseline, Novo said. Doses reached a maximum of 1.7 milligrams or 2.4 milligrams, depending on baseline weight. At week 104, BMI had fallen an estimated 9.4% with semaglutide and risen 8.4% with placebo when all children were counted. Novo said the week 104 results were not adjusted for multiplicity and cannot be used for inferences.

Safety and tolerability matched earlier Novo pediatric and adult semaglutide trials, and no unexpected safety concerns or concerns about growth or pubertal development emerged, the company said. The 2.4-milligram injection carries a boxed warning for possible thyroid tumors, including cancer.

"Effective treatments for severe childhood obesity may shift a child's trajectory back towards one of improving weight related health," Ania M. Jastreboff, a professor at Yale School of Medicine and director of the Yale Obesity Research Center, said in a statement.

Semaglutide injection is not approved in the U.S. for children under 12, and Novo said there is no guarantee it will become commercially available for that use. Wegovy is approved for obesity in children 12 and older, and no medications are approved for younger children, according to CNBC.

Close to half of the children in the trial had at least one obesity-related complication, among them hypertension, elevated cholesterol and sleep apnea, according to CNBC.

Dr. Andrea Traina, Novo's medical director lead of obesity, said the two-year study cannot show if starting therapy sooner alters how the disease unfolds over a lifetime, or what ongoing care would require once these children become adults, calling it "an evidence gap," according to CNBC.

Childhood obesity touched about 177 million young people between the ages of 5 and 19 around the globe in 2025, according to the World Obesity Federation, as cited in Novo's release.
