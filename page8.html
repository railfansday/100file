Stuck in years-long lines for grid connections and new turbines, some AI data centers are making their own electricity with converted jet engines, the New York Times reported Tuesday.

The engines, pulled from retired aircraft and rebuilt to burn natural gas, aren’t anyone’s idea of a permanent power plant. But they’re another sign of how far power supply has fallen behind the AI buildout.

For companies stuck in those lines, the main draw of converted jet engines is how quickly the engines can be up and running. “It’s all about getting power as fast as possible,” Rystad Energy analyst Audun Martinsen told the Times. With jet-powered units, “they can have a data center running in 24 months,” he said.

Hooking up 100 megawatts to the grid in Northern Virginia, by comparison, takes about seven years, according to the International Energy Agency. Most power capacity is expected to come on by 2031, an executive at Mitsubishi Power, one of the world’s top makers of power generation equipment told Bloomberg last week.

In some cases, these delays are already costing some data center operators business. Last month, Microsoft pulled out as the anchor customer for Nscale’s data center in Loughton, England, after the local utility said it couldn’t deliver enough power until the early-to-mid 2030s.

Elon Musk’s xAI is among the companies already using the engines, having installed eight converted jet-engine turbines in Mississippi to help power its Colossus 2 data center in Memphis, the Times reported, citing state permits and satellite imagery. The units come from ProEnergy, which rebuilds a GE engine commonly found on Boeing jets.

In 2025, OpenAI followed Musk’s lead with gas turbines at its first Stargate site in Abilene, Texas. Crusoe, a Denver-based startup that builds AI data centers, is constructing that Abilene campus and ordered 13 of ProEnergy’s converted units in April.

In September, Crusoe walked away from a $1.25 billion deal to buy 29 natural gas turbines from Boom Supersonic, a startup developing a supersonic passenger jet that has also begun selling a version of that jet’s engine as a power plant for data centers. A Crusoe spokesperson told TechCrunch at the time that the company still plans to use turbines.

Currently the Trump administration is adding to the pressure by encouraging data centers to generate their own electricity.

Converted jet engines come with trade-offs, though: They’re less efficient and pollute more than turbines designed for conventional gas plants and it takes more of them to make the same amount of power, according to the Times. That may ultimately make converted jet engines a short-term play with some experts saying that efficiency and environmental concerns may grow in importance in a year or two.

The Origins of ‘Elf’: How Will Ferrell Came to Replace Chris Farley in the Christmas Classic

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In an exclusive excerpt from his new oral history, comedian Andy Richter — who has a small part in the movie — retraces the unlikely birth of the 2003 holiday hit.

The following is excerpted from Elf Unwrapped: Behind the Scenes of a Modern Christmas Classic – Andy Richter‘s Oral History of the Making of Elf the Film That Became a Holiday Classic, by Andy Richter (who had a small a role in the hit 2003 comedy) with Aaron Kino.

The story of Elf begins like most Christmas tales do — with a Jewish kid from Philadelphia. In 1993, an aspiring screenwriter named David Berenbaum moved from Philly to L.A. with dreams of making it big. He had no connections, no experience, and for the most part, no clue how to make it in “the biz.” But what he did have was an overactive imagination and a brain brimming with fanciful ideas. David was a cockeyed dreamer, as many of us are when we land in Hollywood, but David just felt completely out of his element. Compared to the East Coast, L.A. is just . . . weird. No one seems to have a real job here, but they all talk like they’re showbiz big shots. People act like they’re your friend but then look past you mid conversation, just in case someone better walks by. David was lonely, especially when the holidays rolled around — which are even weirder in L.A. Suddenly, it was an unholy eight- five degrees around Christmastime! To cope, he rented a bunch of Christmas movies on VHS so he could watch them over and over. He binged classics like It’s a Wonderful Life, Miracle on 34th Street, Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer — anything to feel a bit more Christmassy. Feeling inspired, he sat down to write his first Christmas film: a screwball romantic comedy called Christmas in New Jersey, which centered on two people having the worst days of their lives, who decide to completely start over as if they had never been born (very It’s a Wonderful Life coded).

Fortunately for him, that script actually got optioned — which is when a production company or studio pays you a little bit of money for the “option” of one day making the movie and then paying you a lot of money. Unfortunately, 99 percent of optioned projects never get made, and that was the case with Christmas in New Jersey. The good news, though, is that it opened up a ton of doors for David. Impressed by the quality of his writing, the film executives he met with kept asking him, “Okay, what’s next?” “Next??” David thought. “Damn, I better come up with something else!” Luckily, he was still in that Christmas frame of mind when he started working on his next big idea: the script that would eventually become Elf.

When David first started writing Elf in 1995, he was writing on spec. In Hollywood terms, “spec” means “speculative” — in other words, no one was paying him to write this script, so he was doing the work for free, hoping that he’d be able to sell it down the line. And though he loved a lot of the holiday movies he’d been bingeing, it was ultimately a 1964 Rankin Bass TV special that really inspired this particular project.

For some background: Rankin Bass was this quirky little production company that basically dominated Christmas TV programming for a couple of decades. If you’re a fan of Elf (and holiday movies in general), you’ll probably recognize their stuff right away. They specialized in stop-motion specials, the kind where the puppets move one frame at a time. Their most famous production, Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer, has been on TV every December since LBJ was in the White House. But they also made Frosty the Snowman, Santa Claus Is Comin’ to Town, and The Year Without a Santa Claus — basically all those herky-jerky yet very magical holiday staples that feel like fever dreams nowadays but absolutely shaped everybody’s idea of what Christmas looks and sounds like.

Rankin Bass’s rendition of Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer (adapted from a 1939 booklet published by the department store Montgomery Ward) follows the time-honored tale of Rudolph, a poor reindeer who gets mocked by all the other reindeer because of his glowing and very shiny red nose. After those mean bullies cast him out, he stumbles across a fellow misfit named Hermey, an elf who longs to be a dentist. Rejected by their peers at Santa’s workshop, they set out on an adventure, stumbling upon the Island of Misfit Toys and running into now-classic characters like the Abominable Snow Monster along the way. When they finally return home, Rudolph ends up saving Christmas by using his nose to guide Santa’s sleigh through a terrible snowstorm, proving that what made Rudolph different is exactly what made him special.

David knew what it felt like to be a misfit; after all, it wasn’t that long ago that he’d been working in a lingerie store, sweating in eighty-five-degree heat at Christmas. Essentially, he saw himself in Rudolph and wanted to create a character of his own that followed a similar sort of hero’s journey. Rudolph’s innocence also reminded David of Josh Baskin, Tom Hanks’s character in Big: a somewhat whimsical weirdo who doesn’t really fit in. Thanks to his wish being granted by a magical fortune-telling machine, Josh is quite literally bigger than all his peers, and this is also what sparked the idea of a giant (well, human-sized) elf named Buddy.

So, at this point, David has an idea with a great hook: a human who is raised as an elf in the North Pole. It’s a funny concept, it’s packed with potential for physical humor, and it also has this “fish out of water with a Christmas twist” angle that hasn’t really been done before. But as excited as he was in the moment, David knew he needed to flesh it out wayyyy more. He didn’t want it to just be a one-note gag. Plus, after watching all those Christmas movies, he realized the ones that really stuck around — the ones that reached the pantheon — had a little something extra.

In the original drafts of the script, when Buddy left the North Pole in search of his human family, both his mother and father were still alive. This presented a few problems — namely that it required a whole lot more explaining. It seems believable that a man might not know he had a kid, but what had Mom been doing for the past thirty years? Searching for her missing baby? Or just going on with her life, sipping margs by the pool and pretending nothing happened? Either way, it doesn’t exactly scream “jolly good fun.”

So David decided to follow in the footsteps of Cinderella and Snow White and pretty much every Disney movie and Grimm fairy tale ever — and killed off Buddy’s mom. Focusing the script around Buddy’s relationship with his dad made a lot of sense story-wise, but even more than that, it was a deeply personal choice for David. His dad had passed away when he was young, and this idea of Buddy searching for his father really resonated with him.

Ultimately, it was this emotional yet endearing through line that elevated Elf from a funny premise or silly sketch into a full-blown movie with the potential for real staying power. Before, the drafts felt a lot more jokey, but with that last piece in place, the script finally transformed into what he’d wanted it to be all along: a holiday comedy with a whole lot of heart.

DAVID BERENBAUM, SCREENWRITER: I just went back to what would be the most personal connection for me — my dad’s passing — so that’s why I made that choice. Once I found that, the writing became a whole lot easier and I had something that I thought was good. Plus, I thought, comedically, it’d be funny for Buddy to go, “I’m here, Dad!”

In 1996, after writing many (many, many, many) drafts of the script, David sold his screenplay for millions of dollars, and it was smooth sailing right into production.

Of course that’s not what happened. Instead, the film got stuck in “development hell,” the term for projects in Hollywood that languish with production companies and studios for years and never actually get the green light.

First, Elf was optioned by the Motion Picture Company of America (aka MPCA), a production company known for making critically acclaimed classics like the Academy Award–nominated Ulee’s Gold (starring Peter Fonda) and Beverly Hills Chihuahua 2 (starring George Lopez). It was while MPCA held the reins that the very first actor was attached (or in Hollywood speak, “verbally agreed”) to play Buddy: the one and only Chris Farley, fresh off Saturday Night Live. Once he was on board, David began refining the script with Chris in mind, and, at several points, was even scheduled to sit down with Chris himself so they could go over the new material. Alas, that meeting never materialized, and after a year, MPCA’s exclusive option period expired, relegating the what-if of Chris Farley starring in Elf to Hollywood legend.

Then, another production company optioned Elf, but after that endeavor didn’t work out either, the revised script earned David acceptance into the prestigious Disney Writing Program. This program brings in a couple of writers each year and has them rewrite whatever is in production at the time. It’s a trial by fire—Disney-style—but it’s also an incredible opportunity. David spent two years there, working on anything and everything they threw his way; in fact, one film he wrote there — The Haunted Mansion, starring Eddie Murphy—actually came out a few weeks after Elf. During this time, Disney also had the opportunity to make Elf, but alas, once again, for the innumerable and inscrutable reasons that most Hollywood projects die, it never came to fruition.

Luckily, the wait to bring Elf to life would soon be over, once a fairy godmother in the form of another SNL legend came along.

The people who eventually brought Elf to the big screen came from wildly different corners of Hollywood, from nobodies to legends, executives to improvisers, Comedy Central to Saturday Night Live. It was a jolly band of misfits, and the strange alchemy that united them — part design, part luck — would end up paying incredible dividends.

In 1999, a subversive sitcom called Strangers with Candy premiered on Comedy Central. Cocreated by a group of Chicago improv comedians (including Stephen Colbert and future Elf scene-stealer Amy Sedaris), the show was an offbeat, crass send-up of after-school specials — those preachy ’90s TV movies that aired right when kids got home from school and taught very obvious lessons about very serious issues. Strangers with Candy set out to offend audiences everywhere — and it did so brilliantly. There’s this truly unhinged episode in the third and final season in which Amy’s character discovers that she has Native American heritage and tries to learn more about her “roots.” I make a cameo in this episode, as does a tall, goofy up-and-coming SNL cast member named Will Ferrell. This was actually the first time Will and I worked on the same project. But more importantly (at least for the tale at hand), Strangers with Candy was produced by Comedy Central executive Kent Alterman, who would also go on to play a pivotal role in the Elf saga. So maybe the true reward of making weird television that doesn’t quite get its due is the friends you make along the way.

In 2000, David Berenbaum sat down with Jon Berg, a fresh-faced manager/producer who loved David’s Elf script — even if nobody wanted to make it, yet. Both Jon and David were nobodies at the time, but Jon was really impressed by David’s script and offered to become his manager. Before long, Jon and his producing partner Todd Komarnicki came on board as producers — essentially agreeing to hustle, unpaid, to try to get the movie made (and then get paid). Obviously, finding just the right Buddy would be crucial to getting this thing off the ground, and the two of them had a very specific person in mind for the role.

WILL FERRELL (BUDDY): The first time I heard about Elf was when I got a call from Julie Wixson Darmody in Jimmy Miller’s office. She was like, “Hey, there’s this script. The basic premise is a human raised at the North Pole who has to go to New York. It probably needs some work, but you should take a look.” I read it and I was like, “Oh yeah. I see what you mean. This could be something . . .”

John McGinn heard the question. His eyes darted around Barcelona's packed media auditorium for a second or two on Monday night. What was it like to be about to play in the Nou Camp, the Aston Villa skipper had been asked, as if this were to be some sort of spin off from Bill and Ted's Excellent Adventure.

McGinn was careful with his answer. He did not seek to downplay the nature of the occasion or the thrill that comes with lining up in such a majestic arena. But he was determined not to play the wide-eyed ingenu. 'What our club is trying to do now,' McGinn said, 'is make this normal, make this a yearly experience.'

So when he and his team-mates walked up the flight of steps from the dressing rooms into the vast bowl of the stadium on Tuesday evening, they were here to show that they were not arrivistes. Villa is a historic club. They won this competition in 1982, ten years before Barcelona ever did. They were not here for a treat or to get patted on the back just for turning up.

It is nearly 20 years since Liverpool became the last English club to win here and nobody gave Villa too much chance of ending that drought. They are being told constantly that the odds are stacked against them and that their much-admired manager, Unai Emery, may be tempted by the entreaties of struggling 'giants' elsewhere.

Fresh European Fridge Attacks Put Cold-Chain Security in Focus

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Beyond food storage, the same combination of Internet connectivity, remote access, and inadequate security practices can put temperature-controlled infrastructure across other critical sectors at risk.

A second wave of attacks on supermarket refrigeration systems across Europe this week has heightened concerns about the broader "cold chain's" vulnerability to similar attacks, given that supermarkets aren't the only businesses that rely on cold storage.

As in mid-October, an unknown threat actor appears to have gained access to refrigeration controllers at multiple European supermarkets and switched the systems to defrost mode, raising temperatures to 50 degrees Celsius (122 degrees Fahrenheit) and causing food spoilage.

Reporting by the BBC, Reuters, CNBC, and other outlets suggest the latest attacks were smaller in scope compared to the first wave in October that impacted more than 700 retail and grocery stores primarily in France. A smaller number of stores in Belgium, Germany, the Netherlands, Portugal, Poland and Spain were also caught up in the October attacks.

BBC reported the new attacks as impacting "dozens of supermarkets" in seven European countries including Belgium, Luxembourg, Estonia, Ireland, Sweden and Finland. Spokespersons from several supermarket chains like France's Carrefour, Belgium's Ahold Delhaize and Colruyt Group, and Sweden's Coop chains confirmed varying but relatively limited impacts from the new attacks.

In a Nov. 2 statement, Carrefour said a cyberattack targeting its refrigeration systems had impacted 15 out of 700 stores in Belgium. In an undated statement, US-based Copeland, which manufactures technologies for regulating commercial refrigeration systems, alluded to a cybersecurity incident affecting refrigeration systems in France, which it said related to a discontinued generation of supervisory controllers.

No individual or group has claimed responsibility for either the first or the second wave of attacks, and the motivation behind it remains a mystery. European authorities are currently investigating the attacks.

Researchers, including those at France's CERT, have linked the hacks so far to Internet-connected refrigeration controllers made by Denmark-based Danfoss, Italy-based Dixell, and Copeland. Organizations using these controllers often connect them to the Internet to enable remote maintenance and support, but in doing so also potentially expose them to unauthorized access and remote attack by threat actors.

Risks include unpatched vulnerabilities in these technologies that attackers can exploit, like three in Danfoss' AK-SM 800A controllers that researchers at Claroty disclosed this August and another two they reported the same month in Copeland controllers. Weak configurations and poor security hygiene are also other major problems.

"Misconfigurations and systemic hygiene failures represent the vast majority of the threat surface," says John Gallagher, vice president at operational technology (OT) and Internet of Things (IoT) security vendor Viakoo. "While unpatched vulnerabilities exist in older firmware, they are rendered dangerous only because basic architectural hygiene was skipped, especially [when] making them Internet-facing."

Common mistakes include widespread use of default passwords, vendor-shared service codes, or static credentials across hundreds of locations makes brute-forcing trivial, Gallagher adds.

The weaknesses are not confined to supermarket refrigeration systems. The same combination of Internet connectivity, remote access, and inadequate security practices can put temperature-controlled infrastructure across other critical sectors at risk, according to security experts.

Sean Tufts, field chief technology officer (CTO) at Claroty, points out that "pharmaceutical storage and distribution, food distribution centers, and industrial facilities all rely on connected operational technology to maintain precise environmental conditions."

The level of exposure will vary depending on how these systems are deployed, connected, and secured. But many organizations that rely on remotely accessible refrigeration or temperature-control equipment face similar challenges around asset visibility, third-party access, and securing legacy systems, Tufts says. "The same types of weaknesses can exist across different industries, even if the equipment and operational requirements differ."

The potential consequences of a compromise, says Tufts, can vary by sector.

"A disruption to pharmaceutical storage could compromise temperature-sensitive medicines, while an incident affecting food distribution could lead to spoilage and supply chain disruptions," he notes. "Industrial environments may face additional operational consequences depending on the materials and processes involved."

The supermarket attacks are a reminder that refrigeration and temperature-control systems are part of essential operations and not just back-of-house equipment, Tufts adds. Organizations should identify these assets and know how they connect to the Internet and third parties.

"Where remote access is necessary, use a VPN, strong authentication, or a centralized secure-access tool with session tracking." Changing default passwords, keeping firmware current, and segmenting are also recommended security practices, he says.

Gallagher says that while it's unclear how the attacks on the commercial refrigeration systems played out, the most straightforward theory is the threat actor likely compiled a target list by scanning the public Internet for specific controller fingerprints, such as those tied to Danfoss, Dixell, and Copeland. They then likely deployed an automated script across that target list to exploit known web vulnerabilities or push default credentials, triggering the defrost mode simultaneously.

Gallagher says the same attacks that worked on commercial refrigeration systems can potentially work across almost any form of OT and IoT systems. That's because these systems often are managed outside of IT, lack automated controls to keep firmware and passwords updated, and have porous network borders. "The growth over the last three years in operational ransomware and in cyberattacks causing physical damage shows that these attacks are on the rise and not specific to any one kind of system."

Finding the perfect holiday gift isn’t easy. Between the pressure to get it right, the endless wish lists, and the people who insist they don’t want anything, shopping for loved ones can feel like an impossible mission. This festive season, the Weekend Update co-host is putting his comedic talents to work with Kay by starring in the jeweler’s latest holiday campaign, “Love All In.” The cheeky ad follows Jost on an increasingly ridiculous quest to find the perfect present, poking fun at the lengths we’ll go to for the people closest to us. It’s a fitting partnership for someone who knows a thing or two about making people laugh but also takes gifting pretty seriously.

“I think I’m a good gift giver,” Jost tells ELLE. “Giving gifts is stressful because I do want it to be a meaningful gift. I hate the idea of just buying something because you have to give someone something.”

For Jost, the secret to getting it right has less to do with how much you spend and more to do with how much thought goes into it. “At every kind of price point for any gift, there [are] thoughtful and unthoughtful versions,” he says. “Something can be really expensive but not thoughtful.”

It’s a sentiment that aligns with Kay’s latest campaign, which encourages shoppers to go “all in” when expressing their love. And while Jost might not be the first person you’d think to turn to for jewelry advice, he’s developed an appreciation for it over the years, thanks to his very chic wife, Scarlett Johansson.

“Until five years ago, I didn’t own a watch,” he admits. That changed when the actress gifted him his first one, introducing him to an entirely new world of accessories. As for whether she’s influenced his personal style? “In that it’s exclusively managed by her, yes, I would say she’s influenced it.”

Despite his Staten Island roots, Jost’s jewelry preferences are surprisingly understated. “I don’t really wear a ton of jewelry, but I also am from Staten Island, so I do have to have certain pieces, or else they won’t let me back over the Verrazano Bridge,” he jokes.

While browsing Kay’s assortment, he found himself drawn to simple gold pieces, including a ring, bracelet, and necklace. “They were really nice, but also they weren’t gaudy or anything,” he says. His preference for gold also comes with some rather unconventional reasoning: “If we get into a post-apocalyptic situation, it feels like it’s better to melt down for trading.”

When it comes to shopping for the people closest to him, Jost admits that both Johansson and his Weekend Update collaborator Michael Che are difficult to buy things for, though he found a creative solution for the latter. “I got Che a really large and heavy, realistic statue of a velociraptor,” he recalls. “It was so heavy that he literally can’t get rid of it.” The real expense wasn’t the dinosaur itself. “The main cost was shipping,” he says. “It was a huge eyesore, and it’s prohibitively expensive for him to have removed from his property.”

The hardest person to gift on Jost’s list? His father, who insists he doesn’t need anything. “I think he actually means it, but it’s so unhelpful,” Jost says. After years of nostalgic gifts inspired by his dad’s past, he’s running out of ideas. “I’ve got to figure out something that breaks through his defenses.”

In terms of gifting etiquette, Jost has a few rules of his own. For starters, he’s big on surprises. While he understands the appeal of a wish list for children, he thinks adults should leave a little room for the unexpected. “For adults, it’s got to be ‘surprise me,’” he says. “The surprise is half the fun, three-quarters of the fun.” Regifting, on the other hand, comes with a few conditions. He’s open to the idea, but only if the gift is a perfect fit for someone and there’s full disclosure.

But perhaps the most surprising revelation comes when I ask what’s on his own holiday wish list. After spending much of our conversation discussing what to buy everyone else, Jost seems relieved to finally be on the receiving end. “Oh, thank you! Finally, someone asked me. What do I want?”

His answer? Nothing material. “At this point in life, I mostly want experiences,” he says. Whether it’s a trip or simply someone taking the initiative to organize an outing, he’s more interested in making memories than collecting more things. “Even if it’s just the plan of, ‘Let’s go do this at this time,’ I think that’s a great gift.” Or, when in doubt, a velociraptor.

The largest crypto theft to date was pulled off by combining some well-known hacking techniques over the course of a few months, according to Binance. A postmortem from the company says the process that started with one developer downloading a tampered installer in June apparently spread by a malicious Google ad, exposing details about the engineers and how they work. Then, in September, another engineer was targeted by someone pretending to be interested in hiring them, who set up a series of calls and multiple rounds of interviews, sharing social media pages to build trust, and eventually prompting them to download a fake interview assignment that was actually malware.

Binance claims that its security teams did detect the first installation of malware in June, and replaced the worker’s device, but that its security tools didn’t detect the second attack that deleted the pyenv executable and replaced it with a malicious version that gave them backdoor access:

The malware employed sophisticated obfuscation and evasion techniques, including replacing a legitimate development tool component with a malicious version to execute commands and establish communication with an attacker-controlled external server. The 0-day malware was not detected by the endpoint security protection tools at the time. The attacker also took steps to conceal the malicious activity and remove traces of the compromise, making detection and subsequent investigation more challenging.

By October 20th, the story goes, the attackers, whom the FBI has identified as a North Korean unit dubbed “TraderTraitor,” used Binance’s own internal AI tool to gain control over wallets for the first engineer compromised in June, as well as a third employee, before logging into the third employee’s computer on October 29th and launching their attack.

The postmortem also goes into why Binance wasn’t able to stop the transactions that occurred nearly two hours after the first unauthorized transfers, saying that “Because the hot wallet used Multi-Party Computation (MPC), there was no single private key that could be used to immediately revoke the malicious EIP-7702 authorizations.” Binance says that it’s prevented 57 suspected North Korea-linked cyber threat cases, and CEO Richard Teng writes that no user is bearing the loss, with about $2.85 billion in stolen assets frozen or intercepted: “At this moment, 98% of impacted tokens had been replenished; we’re completing the remaining 2%. Withdrawals have broadly resumed.”

What’s less clear is why this particular attack worked — the danger of job interviews that ask people to download and execute code on machines with access to employer material has been warned about for years, and there’s little detail on the “internal AI tool” that was used by the attackers and why it was able to help them in the first place.

NASA is reviving discussions to rescue the Hubble Space Telescope, which is operating well but is on track to reenter Earth's atmosphere about six years from now.

The agency issued a request for information (RFI) on Oct. 29 for "in-space servicing" capabilities, which could include boosting Hubble's orbit (which is naturally degrading due to atmospheric drag), or even a mission to "enhance its capabilities" in some way.

Hubble, which launched in April 1990 in partnership with the European Space Agency, was serviced five times by astronauts on space shuttle missions, the most recent being in 2009. The observatory's groundbreaking achievements include helping to show that the expansion of the universe is accelerating — a discovery that won the Nobel Prize — as well as a swath of finds about planets, galaxies, stars and other parts of the universe.

Hubble also regularly teams up with other telescopes for science work, and its observations have led to the publication of at least 23,000 scientific papers, NASA figures suggest. And, despite its age, Hubble remains in high demand. "Astronomers request about seven times as much observing time as is available each year," the agency stated in September.

Hubble is also widely appreciated by the public. People have long enjoyed and celebrated its iconic images, such as the Eagle Nebula's "Pillars of Creation," a gorgeous shot that has been revisited several times over the decades. As such, this is not the first time in recent years that discussions have opened about keeping the famed observatory running for a while longer.

In 2022, for example, billionaire tech entrepreneur Jared Isaacman proposed using SpaceX hardware to mount a Hubble-servicing mission. (Isaacman also purchased and commanded two crewed SpaceX missions to Earth orbit, which flew in September 2021 and September 2024.) NASA declined, however, citing possible risks to the healthy observatory.

But things have changed, apparently. Isaacman, who in December 2025 became NASA administrator, said in an Oct. 30 agency statement that Hubble's science return means the agency should "be looking at every reasonable opportunity to keep it delivering for as long as possible" — especially if doing so would be a cost savings compared with launching another mission.

In an Oct. 30 post on X, Isaacman added that it's "still worth exploring the options" and noted that NASA had previously considered "a far lower-cost solution" to keep the observatory flying. "That was then. Still worth the effort — Hubble deserves it," he added.

The new RFI also allows for alternative scenarios, however, which could include a "safe, controlled reentry into Earth's atmosphere or orbital disposal at the end of Hubble's mission."

Hubble will naturally enter the atmosphere in 2033 or 2034 due to orbital drag, so any attempt to service it would have to be in 2032, the agency stated.

Earlier this year, NASA worked with Katalyst Space Technologies on a $30 million mission that aimed to boost the orbit of the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory, which is also falling back to Earth due to orbital drag.

That long-shot rescue mission — which was developed quickly to race against accelerated solar activity, which ballooned Earth's atmosphere and increased drag — launched successfully this summer. But control issues prevented Katalyst's LINK spacecraft from meeting up with Swift. Swift is expected to reenter Earth's atmosphere tomorrow (Nov. 4) after 22 years of operations — far past its two-year design lifetime.

It takes a village to publish a magazine—and website and app and Youtube channel and more. Phew! Today we’re introducing you to Bon Appétit’s research director Ryan Harrington.

For the non-journalists out there: What does research and fact-checking actually mean?

At Bon Appétit, every story or recipe goes through multiple edits. After that, it’s eligible for research review. At that stage, there are a few major things me and my team looks out for:

Information accuracy: This includes checking the spelling of names and brands, making sure descriptions of restaurant menus and dishes are up to date, reviewing the credibility of the sources we point to, verifying portrayals of quoted individuals, and anything else to make sure that when you read a Bon Appétit story, you know it’s true.

Cultural sensitivity: For a long time, food media has been dominated by a certain privileged perspective. This perpetuated a system that sort of relived the Age of Explorers, with people “discovering” new foods and pigeonholing or exotifying other cultures. A big part of research is honoring different experiences—giving people a platform without making it a spectacle.

Legal concerns: The research department is a bridge between our actual legal team and the editorial team. We keep an eye out for things like libel issues, copyright infringement, food safety risks, protecting sources, or anything else that might need to be discussed with lawyers.

And for the aspiring journalists: How do you become a fact checker? What did you do before joining the Bon Appétit team?

I finished undergrad with a bachelor’s degree in chemistry and then spent six years managing operations of a thoracic surgery laboratory, studying forms of lung cancer. I reached a point in that career where I was asked to go back to school for a master’s or PhD. I realized if I were to invest all of my time in further education, I wanted to study something I felt really strongly about. And that was food media.

You also teach food studies at NYU. How does that inform your work at Bon Appetit?

Whether I am building a lesson plan or reviewing a story for BA, I always try to understand things from the perspective of the recipient: the students or readers. Are we conveying the story clearly? What are they taking away from it? Guiding people to those lightbulb moments, from the classroom to the newsroom is why I love doing what I do.

Can Botox Stop Working?

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Botox loyalists claim something is amiss with their favorite injectable. The neurotoxin, which has been the go-to treatment to reduce the appearance of fine lines, is supposed to last three to four months. But some allege online that their Botox isn’t working as well—at least as well as it used to work.

From accusing providers of diluting solutions to get you to spend more on treatments to people believing they are now immune to Botox altogether, the theories are conspiracy-level complicated. But before anyone freaks out and cancels their next appointment, read what the experts have to say on what’s really going on with Botox.

The good news is that Botox itself hasn’t suddenly stopped working. As New York–based board-certified dermatologist Hadley King, MD, explains, because Botox arrives at a practice as a freeze-dried powder and is turned into a liquid with sterile saline before injection, there isn’t one universal correct way to dilute it. Different injectors use different dilution volumes based on their technique and treatment goals.

King says it’s more important to pay attention to the amount of units you’re receiving and not the concentration of the saline; the units you’re receiving determine how long it will last. “Twenty units is still 20 units whether it’s delivered in a smaller or larger volume,” she says.

In reality, there are many reasons why your Botox isn’t lasting as long as you’d like it to. Some common ones include:

King says that under-dosing is simply the biggest reason. “Many people are requesting a softer, more natural look (sometimes called baby Botox), which intentionally uses fewer units, [and] less product generally means a shorter duration of effect,” she explains.

Brian J. Abittan, MD, director of skin and hair rejuvenation and hair transplantation at the Kimberly and Eric J. Waldman Department of Dermatology at Mount Sinai, adds that patients want more movement in their faces and less of a “frozen” look, which can sometimes result in lighter doses that wear off faster.

King explains that people with very strong facial muscles (men, athletes, those who have very expressive faces, etc.) often metabolize Botox sooner because their muscles are more powerful.

How someone is going to respond to Botox varies from person to person. King says some patients may experience their Botox lasting three to four months, while others may only get to two to three months despite getting the same dose and treatment. It really just depends.

“Precise placement matters,” says King. “Even with the correct dose, injections that don’t optimally target the muscle can reduce the duration of effect.”

The type of practice where you get your Botox done also matters. If you’re going to a licensed professional, such as a board-certified dermatologist or plastic surgeon’s office, the risk of getting counterfeit Botox or improper injection techniques are slim to none. “Those situations are uncommon in reputable medical practices,” she says. “But [they] highlight why it’s important to seek treatment from qualified providers.”

King lists frequent intense exercise, high muscle activity, and a rapid metabolism as things that may make it seem like your Botox isn’t lasting as long as it used to. But she says evidence is mixed and these effects are generally modest.

For most patients, King says they don’t become tolerant to Botox. In fact, she says many long-term patients may find that they can sometimes maintain results with similar (or even go with slightly fewer) units because repeatedly relaxing the muscle may reduce its strength over time. She also points out that first-time patients might notice movement returning earlier than patients who have been getting treatment consistently over time.

There is a small subset of patients who do develop these neutralizing antibodies against Botox, she says, which will make treatments less effective. But these are usually seen in those who receive high doses for neurologic conditions, get frequent touchups, and get injections at intervals shorter than 12 weeks. Though possible, true resistance from Botox is uncommon. “An experienced injector will first rule out these more common explanations,” she says.

Many have already been feeling the Botox and filler fatigue and are looking to other procedures (XERF, facelifts, etc.) to fill other antiaging needs. Abittan says it’s important to pay attention to what each treatment actually does, as they each address different issues. Lasers, for example, can help with skin tightening, improve pigmentation or texture, and address wrinkles or lines at rest. Whereas neuromodulators like Botox work on movement-related wrinkles, he says.

“These treatments are complementary rather than competitive,” adds King. “The modern approach to facial rejuvenation is increasingly individualized and multimodal. Rather than replacing Botox, newer technologies give physicians more tools to address the specific cause of each patient’s aging concerns.”

And if you’re still unhappy with how long your Botox is lasting, consider a different product. She lists Dysport, Xeomin, Jeuveau, Daxxify, and Letybo as some options that are known to offer similar but longer-lasting results. Just be sure to go to a licensed professional before injecting anything new into your face.

Germany Busts 'Crime-as-a-Service' Investment Fraud Outfit

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

German police led a multinational probe that exposed an alleged crime-as-a-service provider supplying fraudulent trading platforms to investment scammers.

You see an online advertisement promising quick returns from trading cryptocurrency, foreign currencies, and stocks. You give the "investment firm" your information, and an operator calls you and convinces you to transfer a small amount of money into a platform. You make early profits quick, so you deposit increasingly large sums of money. But when you attempt to withdraw your earnings, the operator stalls for a while until finally cutting off contact.

This pattern is familiar to victims of investment fraud, a multinational criminal industry that relies on social engineering to convince would-be-investors that they're about to make substantial gains before pulling the rug out from under them.

Europol today shared news of the disruption of one such operation, an investment fraud scheme with potential victims across more than 50 countries and a company allegedly supplying fraudulent trading platforms and leads to criminal groups. According to a press release, Europol and Eurojust supported an international operation involving authorities in Albania, Cyprus, Finland, Germany, Israel, Kosovo, and North Macedonia.

The German Itzehoe Public Prosecutor's Office led the operation, targeting a company suspected of supplying phony trading websites and multiple call centers to criminal groups; investigators estimate losses to exceed 4 million euros.

International authorities searched 17 locations, including a call center in Kosovo, leading to the seizure of 100 servers and the arrests of seven suspects.

The law enforcement action reflects a multiyear investigation that began in 2022, according to Europol, when a German couple reported they could not withdraw money invested through an online trading platform. This led to two previous "action days" by authorities, one in 2022 (involving two arrests and the seizure of evidence) and another in 2025 (involving more arrests and seizures of money and evidence).

"Analysis of the material recovered exposed the suspected infrastructure provider and its links to call centre operators, paving the way for the latest operation," the press release read.

A Eurojust press release provided further detail regarding the extent of the operation. The analysis of evidence seized during the second action day exposed a company based in Cyprus and Israel allegedly offering "crime-as-a-service," supplying criminal groups with fraudulent websites that funnel targets into the central investment scam.

The data of potential victims visiting these websites and expressing interest would be sold as leads to call centers, which contacted the victims to persuade them to invest their money, leading to repeated demands for additional investments. Authorities identified suspected call center-operating clients of this company in Albania, Kosovo, and North Macedonia.

The fraud operation guaranteed singificant returns for trades in cryptocurrencies, foreign currencies, or stocks, according to Europol. But authorities found that the seemingly legitimate platforms were run by "brokers" that used phony profits and social engineering tactics to convince victims to keep investing. Additionally, investigators also found the operators used affiliate marketing services and deceptive advertisements to lure in potential victims.

Austin Anderson, director of threat intel at cybercrime disruption firm Netcraft, tells Dark Reading that operations like these that target upstream providers can help disrupt investment fraud operations at scale.

"Fraudsters have increasingly specialized in one piece of an attack's supply chain, as seen in this scheme where websites collected personal data that was sold to call centers to ultimately coerce funds from victims," he says. "While this can breed sophistication in each actor's niche, it also introduces fragility when a large dependency like the call center is disrupted. Ultimately, without arrests that remove fraudsters from the market, these operations only temporarily disrupt operations while actors rebuild their infrastructure."

On Location: How Coastal Newfoundland Set the Scene for Netflix's No. 1 Series ‘Below’

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A mysterious sea creature is captivating audiences on Netflix as the Josh Hartnett-led series Below remains the No. 1 show in Netflix’s global rankings. The six-episode thriller paints an eerie image of small-town life in Newfoundland, and viewers are intrigued by the mystique of the striking Canadian backdrop.

Despite the fictional giant squid terrorizing Snook’s Arm, the show pulls on elements of reality, from climate change affecting the Atlantic Canadian sea life to the government-run resettling of remote fishing towns, which started in 1954 and continues to this day. It’s that history that drew production designer Emmanuel Fréchette into the project.

“When I found out that more than 300 towns in Newfoundland had been resettled, I was amazed,” he tells Traveler. “The script is very interesting, mixing that reality with the mundane life in Newfoundland—and this creature!” After all, creator Jesse McKeown (The Umbrella Academy) had frequented the region for years, inspired to bring an authentic depiction of its culture to the screen.

That started behind the scenes as the cast and crew engaged in Newfoundland and Labrador’s most famous tradition. Visitors, who they called CFAs (or Come from Aways—this is also where Broadway hit musical draws its name), get “screeched in” by kissing a cod to become honorary citizens. So they all went to St. John’s Christian’s Pub to pucker up. “The guy there is such a great character, and he has a routine and speech that’s very entertaining," Fréchette says.

When it came to filming locations, the remote regions posed practical challenges, so the majority of the sets were built by production, a joy for Fréchette, who was trained as an architect. “I was stunned by the humble architecture, built by boat owners and builders,” he says of the homes’ low ceilings. “The structures are very endearing and make you think of a boat, so I wanted to follow that thread.”

That’s also what helped root the fantastical storyline into reality. “This was vernacular architecture—architecture without architects—just following the needs of the people out of survival, and I found that so beautiful,” he says. “So I really tried to mimic what I was seeing all around me that survived the decades.”

And it’s clearly had an effect on audiences. According to data provided by Booking.com, not only have searches for the province’s capital of St. John’s risen 16.9% by Americans on the platform compared to last year, but the coastal town of Twillingate, home to the floating icebergs, has skyrocketed 123.7%.

Here, Fréchette shares the filming locations from the popular Netflix series Below.

The show is set in the actual resettled town of Snook’s Arm, which had a population of 10 when it was approved for resettlement in 2017, according to the CBC. But its remote location would have been difficult to film in with such a big crew, so Brigus South—a town with a population of about 700 at last count in 2021—stood in.

“We picked that place because it had a connection with the water,” Fréchette says of the inlet going deep into the land. “Lots of the characters are dragged into the water, so it was great to have this presence for the eeriness.”

But to get the cozy town ready for its close-up took work, as the crew designed and built about 80% of what appears in the series, including the fish plant, Fishermen’s Hall, and wharf. The Penneys home was an existing home they covered, and the church was also a house that they retrofitted, adding the bell tower.

When Fréchette was scouting locations, he stayed in a hostel by the sea in Twillingate, about 275 miles northwest of the capital. “We’d be having a coffee with the window open, and suddenly we were freezing,” he recalls of a sudden drop in temperature from 68 degrees Fahrenheit to 50 degrees Fahrenheit. That’s when they’d look up and see an iceberg floating their way. “It lowers the temperature all around the bay—it’s such an amazing phenomenon.”

As it turns out, the Atlantic town sits along Iceberg Alley, where from late April through July, the remnants of broken glaciers glide down the coast. Many of the shots of Calvin (Hartnett) harvesting icebergs were actual footage from these massive blocks of ice naturally floating along.

The capital served as the home base for the cast and crew, who were spread out across hotels and home rentals during filming. But its Ocean Sciences Centre, about 30 minutes north of downtown, found the spotlight as one of the most striking structures in the series, the marine research center where Dr. Fonda Howander (Mackenzie Davis) worked.

While Fréchette admits it took some work to convince the operating facility to shoot there, he’s excited it worked out because “it really adds production value and is a great location.” After all, the circular wooden structure reflects that government building architect of the 1960s and early 1970s, “at a time we were dreaming and imagining things beyond what was possible to build today.” Reminiscent of designs by Canadian architects like Arthur Erikson, he adds that “it was ambitious, but it had a vision—and is fantastic!”

About 20 miles north of St. John’s at the top of the Avalon Peninsula sits Pouch Cove, a town of about 2,000 residents. The art director told Fréchette about the Pouch Cove Heritage Society, a historical building that was closed since they were fundraising to preserve it, so one weekend, they went up to visit. “The floor was sunken in and it was structurally unsafe,” he says.

The show’s head carpenter and engineer got to work, fixing it up, temporarily transforming it into the interior of Below’s Fisherman’s Hall, while permanently restoring it for the community. “It was a win-win because they were happy to get the money from the location, but also the work we did on it was helpful for them.”

When Calvin returns to his abandoned childhood town of Little Pot Rock, that dilapidated setting was an actual resettled town, about a 30-minute drive down from Pouch Cove on a dirt road that the production had to grade in order to shoot there.

“It was quite tricky, but just stunning—really wild and rugged,” Fréchette says, calling it “the most amazing location.” All that was there was one tiny house, about 12 by 20 feet, but it never showed up on camera. Instead, the production custom built all of the Budgell family’s dwellings. But the area can be visited as part of a 4.3-mile hike along the East Coast Trail.

The fishing village of Bay Bulls is home to North America’s largest Atlantic puffin colony, as well as a prime whale watching destination. But for Below, it was all about the functional purpose of its Pennecon Marine Base. Though it never shows up on screen, its industrial harbor was essential in order to dock the boat used as the research vessel since it had a draft (depth) of nearly 10 feet, and they needed an appropriate spot that the cast and crew could board. Also shot here were the exterior scenes of the submersible, which was hung from the boat by a crane and lifted onto the dock.

Pittman’s fish farm played an integral part of the plot, as Calvin confirmed that the sea creature was real—but it turns out the location was landlocked. “It was very complicated to do this on the sea because we’d have to deal with tides and storms, so we decided to cheat it on a lake in Brigus,” he says of the town that’s actually about 60 miles northwest from Brigus South. There was no actual fish farm there, so they custom-built the floating dock and cabin.

While the outside of Linda’s Bar was filmed using a retrofitted Brigus South home, the interior was actually the Rising Tide Convenience shop in the town of Harbour Main, about 33 miles southwest of St. John’s. The local business, best known for its homemade ice cream, “looked great inside, so we dressed it up,” Fréchette says, taking extra care to ensure the exterior entrance they built flowed seamlessly between cuts. As for Noah (Kaleb Horn)’s on-screen screech-in? That was a prop, but an actual frozen fish to ensure the fish kiss was genuine.

He Left Google to Fix a ‘Really Stupid’ Household Product. The ‘Delightful’ Solution Has Generated $20 Million in Revenue.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

You're viewing the US website. Looking for the Site edition?

Matic co-founder Mehul Nariyawala says the goal is to sell 30,000 robots by the end of the year.

For Mehul Nariyawala, a household product’s costly mistake was the jumping-off point for a $20 million company. Nariyawala had a $1,000 robot vacuum, which he watched destroy a rug in his home while he was away. The machine had powerful suction but wasn’t intelligent enough to free itself from the rug. It got stuck, kept running for 45 minutes and tore away a patch of the rug.

“That was a realization that, Wow, this thing is really stupid,” Nariyawala tells Entrepreneur in a new interview. Worse, he found that many robot vacuums could not reliably return to their own charging docks. “To me, it’s an oxymoron to call these things robots because if it’s a robot and it can’t even find its own dock, what good is it?” he says.

That irritation planted the seed for Matic, the home-robotics company Nariyawala co-founded with CEO Navneet Dalal after the pair left Google Nest in 2017. Nariyawala is Matic’s president. Their goal was not simply to make another robot vacuum. It was to build a machine that could safely and quietly navigate the disorder of an actual home, avoiding pets, toys, cords, spills, furniture and the people moving through it.

Nariyawala says Matic has sold more than 13,000 robots since its November 2024 launch, generating an estimated $15 million to $20 million in revenue. Matic’s robot vacuum costs $1,495. Nariyawala said the company employs about 110 people and is aiming to sell 30,000 robots by year-end.

Before they started building Matic, the co-founders did something less glamorous: They wrote a 21-page “problem memo.”

Instead of jumping to a solution, Nariyawala says, they asked what customers disliked about current products, why robot vacuums failed them and what a genuinely useful home robot would need to do.

“We just kept going again and again and again at every level until we deeply understood the problem,” he says.

That process eventually produced Matic’s first product, a privacy-focused robot vacuum designed around computer vision, on-device processing and a more human-like understanding of a home.

Nariyawala’s path to Matic began in India, where he grew up. He moved to the United States in high school and attended the University of Maryland for undergraduate studies. After graduating in 1999, he joined a startup based in Northern Virginia that burned through $30 million in 11 months.

“It turned out to be a spectacular failure,” he says. “That was my how-not-to-ruin-a-startup 101.”

After a stint in consulting at Accenture and an MBA from Chicago Booth, Nariyawala joined Like.com in 2005, an early Silicon Valley computer-vision startup. There, he met Dalal, a researcher and engineer whose doctoral work became influential in the field of classical computer vision.

Google acquired Like.com in 2010. Nariyawala and Dalal then formed Flutter, a gesture-recognition startup that let people control iTunes and Spotify using a webcam. Users could wave to change a song or make a shushing motion to mute audio. The app reached No. 1 in Apple’s Mac App Store in 73 countries in 2012, according to Nariyawala.

But the founders learned a crucial product lesson: A flashy interface does not necessarily create a business.

“No one wakes up in the morning and says, ‘Today I’m gonna buy gestures,’” Nariyawala says. “It’s a really cool app but not necessarily a product that can potentially generate revenue.”

Google acquired Flutter in late 2013. The founders later moved to Nest, where Nariyawala led products in the Nest camera portfolio, including the Nest Cam, Nest Hello doorbell and related devices. Dalal led perception and computer-vision work on the same set of products.

At Nest, Nariyawala learned that a product is more than its hardware or software. It encompasses every point a customer encounters the company, from learning about the product to purchasing, onboarding, receiving support and deciding whether to stay loyal to the brand or not.

“One of the biggest learnings there was that it’s really just about the entire experience of the product,” he says.

Customer support, he argues, is especially important for an expensive consumer device. “When they reach out to you, that’s the first time they’re meeting humans behind Matic,” he says. “How we respond to that is actually really, really critical.”

Matic’s founding idea grew from both professional experience and family life. While working on Nest cameras, Nariyawala noticed that many indoor cameras were pointed outside. He could personally relate. His wife had done the same thing, and after leaving Google, he says she did not want any indoor cameras monitoring their home.

“That’s when I realized that for her, privacy mattered,” he says.

The founders concluded they did not want to trade away privacy merely to automate a household chore. They wanted a robot that would process its understanding of the home locally rather than operate like an always-recording cloud-connected camera.

“As fathers and homeowners, we don’t want to jeopardize privacy just to keep our floors clean,” Nariyawala says. “Why would I invite something that is recording them and putting it out onto the cloud?”

The pair saw a broader opportunity in the gap between the robotics ambitions emerging in self-driving cars and the mediocre performance of household cleaning robots. If companies believed they could develop vehicles capable of safely navigating roads, Nariyawala wondered, why was no one making a household robot that could move through a living room without crashing into objects?

Nariyawala says that homes are among the most difficult environments for robots because they are cluttered, dynamic, and highly personal. Furniture shifts. Lighting changes. Children leave toys out. Pets cross a room. A cord may be on the floor one day and gone the next.

Matic’s answer was a vision-based system built around two RGB cameras and local computing. Rather than use the flat, circular shape common in robot vacuums, the company opted for a taller machine that gives its cameras a more useful perspective of the room.

“We wanted to build a vantage point that was much more like a human crawling child,” Nariyawala says.

That concept reflects Dalal’s broader philosophy: Do not bet against nature. Human beings learn to perceive their surroundings before they learn more complex skills, Nariyawala says. Young children learn where they can walk, what they should avoid, how to get from one room to another, and how not to fall down stairs. Matic’s early work focused on giving a robot some of those same foundational abilities.

“The indoor world is entirely built by humans for humans,” he says.

Nariyawala and Dalal did not immediately seek venture funding. Having founded companies before and seen what happens after an acquisition, they wanted to build Matic for the long haul. Their ambition extended beyond cleaning floors.

“We’re really building the Apple of home robotics,” Nariyawala says.

That long-term vision informed an unusual early decision. The cofounders self-funded Matic for roughly its first year and a half. They agreed not to raise outside capital until they had a working prototype and had addressed major technical risks.

“Raising funding is never easy, but when you have gone through an exit and you try to do a second startup, it is slightly easier,” Nariyawala says. “But because it’s slightly easier, you don’t actually spend more time thinking through and developing your idea, and that sometimes plays a catastrophic [role].”

The team focused on proving key capabilities: depth perception using two cameras, voice and gesture recognition, quieter cleaning and a wheel-base design. Its first prototype arrived in 2018. By spring 2019, it had developed an early demonstration in which a person could speak to the robot, point to a mess and tell it to clean a specific spot.

Only then did the founders begin raising money. “Venture capital funding, or institutional funding, is like rocket fuel,” Nariyawala says. “The best time to go get rocket fuel is when your rocket is ready and docked.”

In 2019, Matic attracted early investment from entrepreneurs including Jack Dorsey, Stripe cofounders Patrick and John Collison and Nest cofounder Matt Rogers.

Matic has raised $115 million in outside investment to date, with additional personal investment from the founders.

The journey took longer than planned. The founders originally thought they might ship around 2020 or 2021. Matic instead began shipping in November 2024.

In August, the U.S. Federal Communications Commission (FCC) banned foreign-made advanced robots, including robot vacuums. The ruling prohibited new foreign-made models from getting future import and sales approval.

Nariyawala says that the ban does not affect Matic and instead makes it “easier” for the company to build robots in the U.S. From day one, the company has been focused on security and privacy, he says. Their existing robot is already grandfathered in for sale in the U.S., and their next set of robots is heading in the right direction to move through the FCC ban. The company is determined to source its components in the U.S.

“We cannot guarantee the security and privacy of the robot unless we assemble it in the United States,” Nariyawala says. “So for us, designing and assembling it here and putting all the software here was not an optional thing. That was something we always wanted to do, and we did that from 2017 onwards.”

Nariyawala says he reached out to the FCC after the ban was announced and showed them a path to how Matic will source as many components in the U.S. as possible moving forward.

“The FCC and government have been very much accommodating and understanding what we’re doing,” Nariyawala says. “Their entire point is that yes, you guys are heading in the right direction. So as long as it’s heading in the right direction, we’re supportive of it. We just have to show that we’re pushing in that direction.”

Matic now positions its vacuum as an intelligent, autonomous machine rather than a device that follows a fixed or random cleaning routine. Nariyawala contrasts that vision with early robot vacuums, which he compares to a blindfolded person bouncing from wall to wall.

“Hardware is hard,” Nariyawala says. “If hardware is 10 times harder than software, then building a robot is 100 times harder than software.”

Many current robot vacuums use a form of LiDAR sensing, he says, which can help a machine detect walls and certain obstacles but does not provide enough context to distinguish among a person, a cabinet, a pet, or a pile of clothes.

“It has no contextual awareness,” he says.

Matic’s system is meant to create a three-dimensional, evolving understanding of a home. Nariyawala describes this as an “absolute map,” rather than a map that only orients the robot relative to its charging dock. The distinction matters, he says, because a robot should be able to understand its environment even if the dock is moved.

Matic recently introduced “Cues,” an update enabling users to communicate with the robot through voice commands and gestures. Through the update, the robot can understand more than 70 spoken languages, and users can point to an area they want cleaned to have the robot take care of a mess.

The robot has an accompanying app that remains necessary for onboarding and detailed settings, such as setting no-go zones, but the company wants routine interaction to feel more natural.

“If this is truly an intelligent robot, then we should be able to communicate with it in the same exact way that we communicate with each other,” Nariyawala says.

The approach is designed for households that include children, older adults, people who do not want to use another app and family members who may not speak English — personalized experiences for different users.

Matic has focused less on traditional marketing than on getting customers to talk about the product. Nariyawala says roughly two-thirds of orders have come through word-of-mouth referrals, and the company did little marketing before this year.

“The only evergreen marketing channel is a great product,” he says. “When you build something really unexpected, delightful that customers love, they can’t help talking about it.”

For Nariyawala, Matic’s floor-cleaning robot is only the first stage. The company’s roadmap imagines a “trusted organizer” that can handle simpler tasks, followed by a more capable household robot that can take on complex planning and execution.

But he returns repeatedly to the same principle that shaped the company’s original 21-page memo: The technology is not the product. The customer’s problem is.

“Customers don’t want hardware or robots,” he says. “They want a solution to their problems.”
