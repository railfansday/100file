Manchester United set to sack Michael Carrick with two names already considered

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Manchester United are moving towards another managerial change, and this time there is very little room for spin. According to BBC Sport, the club look increasingly likely to sack head coach Michael Carrick after a disastrous opening to the season.

United chose to hold their nerve after the 3-0 home defeat by Bournemouth on 25 October. The plan was simple enough, wait for the international break and reassess. That process is still officially under way, and, as BBC Sport reports, "no final decision on Carrick's future has been made". Still, the mood around Old Trafford appears to be heading in one direction.

There is now said to be "a growing expectation" that chief executive Omar Berrada and director of football Jason Wilcox will advise Sir Jim Ratcliffe and the Glazer family to remove Carrick. That tells you plenty. When the internal briefings sound like that, the conclusion is usually obvious.

The numbers are grim. The 4-4 draw with Aston Villa on Sunday left United 18th in the table and confirmed they have made "their worst start to a domestic campaign in 40 years". Clubs of this size can survive poor results. What they do not survive easily is drift, and right now that is exactly what this looks like.

BBC Sport also notes United previously "sounded out former Newcastle boss Eddie Howe and ex-Germany coach Julian Nagelsmann over replacing Carrick" in October. Other names remain in the frame. "Former Chelsea coach Antonio Conte is known to be keen on the role", while "former Spurs boss Mauricio Pochettino is open to a move to Old Trafford". Pochettino's current USA contract, signed in August, reportedly contains exit clauses for elite jobs, which may yet prove significant.

United have seen this cycle too many times before. The only question now is whether the board act quickly, or continue delaying what increasingly feels inevitable.

This has been coming, and pretending otherwise helps nobody. Sitting 18th after this run of results is unacceptable, full stop. "Worst start to a domestic campaign in 40 years" is not a blip, not bad luck, not a rough patch. It is a full institutional failure, on the pitch and above it.

Carrick was always going to be judged on whether he could impose authority, improve the structure and get players performing with some consistency. None of that has happened. There have been moments, maybe even encouraging spells, but this level of chaos wipes all of that out. Conceding four in a game you have to control tells its own story.

The bigger issue is whether the club have learned anything. Changing the head coach can be necessary, but it only matters if the next appointment is coherent. If Berrada and Wilcox are recommending the sack, then the replacement has to fit a defined football plan, not another short-term scramble for a famous name.

Pochettino makes sense in some ways. Conte would certainly bring edge and standards. Even the fact multiple names have already been discussed suggests the club know where this is going. Fine. Then act decisively. Dragging it out only creates more uncertainty, more noise and more damage.

What matters now is competence. Pick the right manager, give him clarity, and demand standards from every level of the club. Manchester United do not need another reset dressed up as a revolution. They need serious people making serious decisions.

Manchester United make Michael Carrick decision ahead of Liverpool clash

Manchester United have made a habit of turning turbulence into strategy, and that usually ends badly. According to The Athletic, Michael Carrick is now expected to lose his job before the club return from the international break, with United 18th in the Premier League and the numbers now impossible to dress up.

One league win, three victories in all competitions, a place in the relegation zone, and an Old Trafford crowd watching a chaotic 4-4 draw with Aston Villa. That is not a blip, it is a trend. Carrick had already been hanging by a thread after the 3-0 defeat at home to Bournemouth, and the decision to hold fire then looks more like hesitation than conviction.

What makes this especially awkward is how quickly the mood has flipped. Carrick earned the permanent role in May after a strong interim spell, taking United from seventh to third with 39 points from 17 league matches. That run included statement wins against Manchester City, Arsenal, Liverpool and Chelsea. Over that period, nobody in the division collected more points. Fair enough, he gave them a bounce. The problem is that football does not hand out credit indefinitely.

This season, the drop-off has been severe. United have looked open, anxious and oddly incoherent for a squad built at huge cost. Their Carabao Cup exit to Brighton & Hove Albion added to the sense that early-season damage was becoming structural. Once a club of that size slips into the bottom three in November, sentiment goes out the window.

The report suggests Mauricio Pochettino is expected to be a leading contender, while Eddie Howe and Julian Nagelsmann also have admirers. That tells you United are already beyond internal review and into succession planning. Sir Jim Ratcliffe and the Glazer family will still need to approve the final call, but the direction of travel looks obvious.

At a serious club, last season's rescue act buys respect, not immunity. Carrick may feel unfortunate, but United's table position leaves no room for nostalgia.

There is a certain grim fascination in watching Manchester United wrestle with the same problem again and again. They chase the uplift, enjoy the headlines, then discover that momentum and structure are two different things. Carrick clearly did a very good job in the short term last season, and those results were real, but short-term surges can fool decision-makers into believing deeper issues have gone away.

For Liverpool, the lesson has always been clear. Big clubs need clarity, ruthless standards and a football plan that survives a bad month. If United now move for another high-profile name without fixing recruitment, dressing-room hierarchy and the basic identity of the side, then the next coach will be walking into the same trap.

There is also the small matter of timing. Sack a manager in November when you are 18th, and every game starts to feel like a cup final. Rivals will smell panic. Supporters will demand certainty. Players will hear every rumour. That is how pressure multiplies.

And, truthfully, there is no sympathy for a club that keeps making expensive decisions twice. If this report is accurate, United are paying for a summer of misplaced confidence. The rest of the league will simply move on and punish any weakness they see.

InfoQ Homepage News Google Cloud Spend Caps Reach General Availability with API and Monthly Resets

Nov 10, 2026 3 min read

Google Cloud has made spend caps generally available on Cloud Billing Budgets, letting customers pause new usage of a service in a project once a monthly budget is reached, without deleting resources. The GA release adds an API, estimated live costs that trigger caps within minutes for AI services, and automatic monthly resets, but enforcement remains limited to Gemini API, the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform, Cloud Run, and Cloud Run functions.

A spend cap is a budget with enforcement attached: according to the documentation, when estimated spend on the capped service reaches the target amount, new requests to that service in that project are blocked, in-flight requests run to completion and are charged, persistent resources keep running, and nothing is deleted. Usage covered by committed-use discounts is paused along with pay-as-you-go usage, while Provisioned Throughput for AI models is never halted. Billing administrators and project owners receive alerts at 50, 80, and 100 percent of the cap.

The company changed three things between the July public preview and the GA release:

The limits are as specific as the features: a cap covers one project and one service for a calendar month, so folders, organizations, labels and multi-service budgets are out of scope, as are reseller accounts and subscription charges such as Gemini Enterprise seats. The estimate that drives enforcement is calculated at list price and excludes credits and discounts. Enforcement is also not instant: the documentation states that "any cost overages are billed as normal" and advises setting the cap "slightly below your absolute limit." Compute Engine, Cloud Storage, and networking are not among the eligible services, which means the cap addresses a runaway model or agent loop and not a forgotten GPU instance.

The narrower scope drew criticism when the preview expanded in October. On a Hacker News thread, one commenter who had asked Google for the capability a year earlier to control AI spend called the result "very crude, not granular like the rich IAM system," noting the four-product, month-only design. Another observed that Cloud Run had been added that morning, so the service list was still growing.

Developer Simon Willison argued in October that pay-by-usage services should default to hard caps, because coding and personal agents make it far easier to spin up code that calls paid APIs, and that "most businesses and individuals would prefer errors to a surprise $10,000+ bill." He cited Google's caps alongside a project-level spend limit in AWS's new account experience, which pauses the whole project and stops its resources when reached, preserving data for 90 days, and which AWS is rolling out to a limited number of customers. Azure's spending limit remains confined to free accounts and credit-based subscriptions; Microsoft states it isn't available for subscriptions with commitment plans or with pay-as-you-go pricing.

Spend caps pair with early anomalies, a daily baseline model that flags directional cost variances per service and names the top SKUs behind a spike, which Google positions as the detection half of the same control loop. Both are configured from the Budgets and Alerts page in the Billing console or through the Budgets API.

Google's smartwatch AI detected 65.8% of hypertension cases in a study, pointing to cuff-free screening using everyday sensor data.

Google researchers and medical collaborators have used ordinary smartwatch signals to flag possible high blood pressure without calibrating the watch against an arm cuff.

A study published in Nature on Nov. 5 suggests a way to prompt clinical checks during everyday wear, although the system missed about 33% of hypertension cases.

The AI identified 65.8% of participants with hypertension according to a clinical reference test (sensitivity), while it correctly screened 90% of those without the condition as negative (specificity).

The prospective, multicenter study followed 196 participants through seven days of smartwatch monitoring and compared results with 24-hour ambulatory blood-pressure monitoring. Participants had no known cardiovascular disease and were not taking blood-pressure medication, limiting how far the findings extend to people already receiving treatment.

The 95% confidence intervals were 54% to 76.3% for sensitivity and 83.2% to 94.7% for specificity.

The research system turns seven days of pulse and motion data into a screening score. Its detection rate leaves a substantial blind spot.

Of participants with hypertension on 24-hour ambulatory monitoring, 65.8% were detected and 34.2% were missed.

Of participants without hypertension on the reference test, 90.0% screened negative and 10.0% produced false alerts.

Screening can flag a reason for a clinical check; a negative result cannot exclude hypertension.

On the surface, this might not seem like such a big deal. It's just one more of Larson’s many silly comic strips featuring cavemen. But this one ended up taking on a life of its own.

At the time this comic was published, that spiky apparatus at the end of a Stegosaurus’ tail actually had no name at all. So scientists began to jokingly use the word “thagomizer” to describe it. Eventually, the usage became so common that it came to be scientifically recognized and used by such distinguished institutions as the Smithsonian, confirming that one little Far Side joke made scientific dinosaur history.

But while that comic may be Larson’s most famous and historically significant dinosaur comic strip, it was far from his only one. Larson regularly drew dinosaurs in his cartoons, oftentimes doing normal human things or being completely unaware of their impending extinction. The Far Side ran from 1980 until 1995 and Larson created over 4,000 strips during that time. Dozens of them featured dinosaurs. And while only one made dinosaur history, there have been lots of unforgettable ones for Far Side fans. Here are 10 of the most unforgettable Far Side comics about dinosaurs (besides the thagomizer one).

A fungus has no brain, no eyes, and no nerves. Yet a wood-rotting fungus, given a cross of wood blocks to grow on, attached more of its threads to the outer blocks than to the inner ones.

Given a circle to grow on, it showed no such pattern and left the center bare. The researchers suggested the difference may be a form of pattern recognition.

Yu Fukasawa of Tohoku University in Japan led the 2024 study with colleagues at Tohoku and at Nagaoka College.

The fungus they used lives on forest floors and builds its networks by sending thick strands of thread, called cords, from one piece of dead wood to the next.

Its name is Phanerochaete velutina, and Fukasawa’s team had earlier found that it remembers the direction to a piece of wood.

“You’d be surprised at just how much fungi are capable of,” Fukasawa said. “They have memories, they learn, and they can make decisions. Quite frankly, the differences in how they solve problems compared to humans is mind-blowing.”

Even so, the team phrased its own conclusion as a question, not a verdict.

The mushroom in the video above is a pink oyster, not the fungus in this study. Pink oysters, named for their salmon-pink caps, grow on dead hardwood and are farmed on straw as food.

The study’s fungus grows as a thin crust on wood, with no cap or stem. Both break down dead wood with a network of threads.

Fukasawa’s team cut beech wood into cubes less than half an inch (1 cm) on a side, weighed them, and let the fungus grow into them for six weeks.

The team then set nine cubes on each tray of damp soil, in a circle or in a cross, with 2 inches (5 cm) between neighboring blocks. Each tray was about 9.5 inches (24 cm) square, and there were ten of each shape.

Everything but the shape was the same from tray to tray. The trays stayed in the dark at 68°F (20°C) for 116 days, nearly four months.

Once a week the team misted them with sterile water and moved them around the incubator. A camera 18 inches (46 cm) above the trays photographed each one monthly.

At the end, the researchers counted the cords attached to each block, then dried and weighed the blocks again to see how much wood the fungus had broken down.

After 116 days, cords ran from block to block across every tray, though a few blocks ended up with no cord attached at all.

In the cross, the four blocks at the ends of the arms had more cords than the four middle blocks and the one at the center. The count ran from zero cords on some blocks to nine on others.

In the circle, no position in the ring had reliably more cords than another. The cross result was the opposite of what the team had predicted.

They had expected the center block, the one with four neighbors, to be the most connected, and instead the blocks at the ends of the arms were.

The team suggested the outer blocks may have worked as outposts, where the fungus absorbed water and nutrients from the soil. The inner blocks, with fewer cords, may have become little more than a path for cords passing through.

The more cords a block had, the more of its wood the fungus had broken down. Blocks in the circle lost between 18% and 65.5% of their dry weight, and blocks in the cross lost between 16.7% and 62.7%. Overall, the cross blocks lost a little less than the circle blocks did.

The researchers also reran the analysis without the extreme blocks, the ones with no cords or with nine. Without them, the difference between the two shapes faded, while the link between cords and decay remained.

The fungus also spread roughly the same amount of thread over the soil in both shapes. So the cross blocks didn’t decay less because the fungus grew less. The network was built differently, and the wood decayed differently.

The recognition argument rests on that difference. In a human brain, different images activate different groups of neurons, and that contrast in activity is what lets the brain recognize an image.

A network of fungal threads that builds itself one way for a cross and another for a circle may be doing something similar, Fukasawa’s team wrote.

Every block started the same way, with threads growing outward in all directions. Then the fronts from neighboring blocks met and fused into one body.

In the circle trays, the merged fungus kept growing outward, and by day 34 a large bare patch had formed at the center.

Fukasawa’s team compared this to fairy rings, the circles of mushrooms that widen year after year because the fungus beneath them grows outward from its edge. Why the fused network left the center empty is something the team can’t yet explain.

One possibility they raised is crowding: fungal growth is known to stop once the threads get dense enough, and the threads inside the ring may have reached that point.

Whether the fungus is conscious is irrelevant, the team wrote, because cognition can run without consciousness even in a brain.

Slime molds, which also have no brain, have already solved a maze and linked food sources with networks about as efficient as a real railway.

The team raised one possible explanation, and it involves rhythm. The amount of calcium at each growing tip rises and falls in a cycle, and so do the flow of material and the electrical activity through the fungus.

When separate colonies fuse, those rhythms tend to synchronize, and synchronized rhythms may be how the network carries information about where things are.

The rhythm idea is a hypothesis, and this experiment didn’t measure it. The team also can’t yet say whether the tray lids affected the empty center of the ring.

The lids were loose and came off every week, so the researchers doubt the middle ran short of oxygen. The way to settle it is a tray with no lid, and that’s the experiment they said would be worth running.

The full study was published in the journal Fungal Ecology.

The Grand Canyon’s drinking water comes from a single spring connected to over six miles of hidden caves

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Researchers mapped over six miles of caves beneath the Grand Canyon, revealing how groundwater flows and why its drinking water faces pollution risks.

For decades, the twelve different water spigots used by visitors in Grand Canyon National Park have drawn their water from Roaring Springs, a cave-fed spring on the North Rim.

In an August 2025 study, researchers at Northern Arizona University (NAU) described over 6 miles (10 kilometers) of cave passages behind the spring. They mapped them with a handheld lidar scanner.

The 3D maps reveal the passages that carry water through the limestone below the North Rim. That system is fast and vulnerable to contamination from the surface.

The park’s water supply has served more than 6 million visitors a year from that single cave spring. Its water is almost entirely snowmelt from the Kaibab Plateau.

Built starting in 1965, the 12½-mile (20-kilometer) Transcanyon Waterline carried that water toward the South Rim and its 800-plus historic buildings.

The aging pipe had at least 85 major breaks between 2010 and mid-2026, according to the National Park Service. A single break often tops $25,000 to repair.

A replacement project shifts the South Rim intake to Bright Angel Creek near Phantom Ranch. On August 29, 2026, a flash flood destroyed a water intake pump and other infrastructure.

The Bright Angel Creek and Roaring Springs intake areas “sustained significant damage,” the park reported in a September 2026 update. Roughly 40 percent of the old pipeline between Phantom Ranch and the North Rim was also damaged.

The South Rim has been under Stage 4 water restrictions while the park arranged to truck in about 200,000 gallons (757,000 liters) a day. Pumping from Bright Angel Creek to a new treatment plant could begin by November 16.

Blase LaSala, a Ph.D. student in ecoinformatics at NAU’s School of Informatics, Computing, and Cyber Systems, led the mapping. He worked with NAU professor Temuulen “Teki” Sankey, a remote sensing expert.

With park staff and volunteers, the team scanned Roaring Springs Cave, Muav West Cave and Redwall East Cave. The handheld scanner uses near-infrared laser light and records up to 300,000 points per second.

The crews documented more than 6.3 miles (10.2 kilometers) of passages in 25 days. The caves extend beyond 0.6 miles (1 kilometer) from their entrances and 1,300 feet (400 meters) below them.

In Muav West Cave, the lidar maps agreed 98 percent with a traditional survey, which covered about half the distance in the same time.

“I had no idea how large and long these caves are,” Sankey said. “Grand Canyon’s caves have never been mapped in 3D like this.”

Karst forms where water dissolves soluble rock such as limestone, opening caves, conduits and sinkholes.

The caves lie in the Redwall and Muav limestones, karst layers some 2,300 feet (700 meters) beneath the plateau surface. Water travels through these limestone layers via steep fractures and underground conduits.

Abe Springer, an NAU professor and co-author, also worked with the park on dye tracing tests. Dye dropped into surface sinkholes reached springs about 12 miles (20 kilometers) away in as little as a week.

From the 3D models, the authors conclude that today’s groundwater follows near-vertical cracks and the tilt of the rock layers. They link those patterns to regional faulting and uplift.

In Redwall East Cave, bubble trails and other features sit above the stream passage. They suggest that today’s flow may reuse pathways carved by an older aquifer.

“It’s like looking at a black box,” LaSala explained. “You see what comes in and what comes out, but it’s very hard to quantify what’s going on in there.”

The researchers describe karst aquifers like this one as both vulnerable to contamination and challenging to model.

Runoff from burned areas or E. coli bacteria entering sinkholes linked to Roaring Springs Cave could force the park to halt its pumps, NAU warns.

The Dragon Bravo Fire, which started on July 4, 2025, burned 149,399 acres (60,460 hectares), including 71,129 acres (28,785 hectares) of the park.

The park’s burned area emergency response listed protection of the drinking water system among its treatments.

The fire also changed conditions on the Kaibab Plateau, where the snowmelt that feeds the spring begins. The NAU team now has to account for those effects.

“It’s a new twist to our study,” Sankey noted.

A new grant from Grand Canyon National Park will expand the mapping across the park. The university scheduled that work to start in early 2026.

LaSala and Sankey plan to use airborne lidar and decades of satellite data to map sinkholes on both sides of the canyon. They will also track how snowmelt has accumulated over the last 40 years.

“Understanding where the water sinks is critical for the infrastructure, the animals, the plants and the rest of the ecosystems that rely on these springs,” LaSala said. “They’re like oases.”

The study was published in the journal Scientific Reports.

FDA warns of vision loss risk linked to popular GLP-1 drugs like Wegovy, Zepbound

📅 2026-11-10  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration is requiring new safety warnings on all GLP-1 drugs after finding evidence of a potential increased risk of a rare eye condition, the agency said on Monday.

The condition, which can cause sudden vision loss, known as nonarteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION), is rare, and a causal relationship has not been established.

The FDA is requiring the makers of all GLP-1 medications to add a warning about the potential increased risk of NAION associated with use of these medications, it said.

NAION is a condition that can result in sudden decreased vision, including permanent vision loss. It is caused by decreased blood flow to the front part of the ​optic nerve — the underlying cause is not completely understood, however.

NAION cases have been reported for all currently marketed GLP-1 products, the FDA said.

CNBC has reached out to Ozempic and Wegovy maker Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly, the company behind Mounjaro, Zepbound and Foundayo, for comment.

The FDA decision follows a review of clinical trials, real-world patient data, and published studies.

An analysis of 126 trials involving more than 134,000 patients found 6.4 cases of NAION per 100,000 patient-years among GLP-1 users, compared with 3.8 among patients receiving other treatments or placebos.

Several observational studies also identified an increased risk, supporting the FDA's concerns.

The FDA's class-wide labeling action is "likely to attract attention but should have limited impact on GLP-1 uptake," according to Citi analysts.

It may temporarily increase patient and investor concern, but the rarity of NAION and substantial weight, glycemic, cardiovascular, renal, and sleep apnea benefits should preserve a favorable benefit-risk profile and limit changes to prescribing the medicines, they added.

While GLP-1 drugs have been shown to lead to benefits beyond weight loss and controlling blood sugar levels, many also carry the FDA's strongest safety warning, known as a boxed warning, over a potential risk of thyroid tumors.

Studies have shown that semaglutide, the active ingredient in Wegovy and Ozempic, and tirzepatide, the active ingredient in Mounjaro, cause thyroid C-cell tumors in rodents. It remains unknown whether the drugs cause these tumors, including a rare form of thyroid cancer, in humans.

Context parallelism splits a long sequence across chips, so no chip holds the activations of the whole sequence. Context parallelism can be combined with data and tensor parallelism, and the smallest context-parallel size that fits your sequence in HBM is usually the right one. This also holds for sparser attention variants which cut attention compute but not the activations of every other layer.

Attention is the one global operation where every token needs the keys and values of every preceding token, so each context parallel strategy has to bring those keys and values back together. Each makes a different tradeoff between memory (how much KV a chip holds), communication (how much moves per layer, and whether it overlaps compute), and model shape (how many attention heads to split).

All-gather: Each chip keeps its chunk of the sequence and, at every layer, gathers the keys and values from every other chip. It is the simplest option. However, because causal masking causes later tokens to attend to larger prefixes, All-gather requires balancing compute load across devices.

All-to-All (Ulysses): Gathering keys and values makes every chip hold the whole sequence, so attention memory never shrinks no matter how many chips you add. All-to-All changes what each chip owns instead. Attention is independent across heads but not across tokens, so the head axis is free to shard and the token axis is not. An All-to-All before attention rotates the sharding from one onto the other: each chip gives up the heads it is not responsible for and receives the tokens it was missing for the heads it keeps, ending up with the full sequence for a few heads instead of a few tokens for all heads. This is also why Ulysses does not need token load balancing. Attention then runs as ordinary single-chip attention with standard kernels, and a second All-to-All rotates the output back. KV per chip shrinks by the context-parallel factor, but you cannot split across more chips than the model has heads — and tensor parallelism shards that same dimension, so TP × CP is capped by the head count (under GQA, by the KV heads: eight for Llama 3 8B). The all-to-all itself, though, does not overlap with compute — attention cannot start on a partial sequence, so every chip waits for data from every other chip before any of them begins.

KV chunks travel around a ring of chips one hop at a time, neighbor to neighbor. Each chip attends its queries to the chunk it holds while the next one is in flight, and merges the partial results as it goes. A chip holds its own chunk and one visiting chunk, never the full sequence, and the communication overlaps compute. However, dividing attention computation into smaller chunks can reduce local kernel efficiency, and unlike All-to-All Ulysses, ring attention requires explicit load balancing.

Splitting the sequence only pays off if the attention compute on each chip stays efficient. All four strategies run on Splash Attention, the block-sparse flash attention kernel from Tokamax, Google's open-source library of TPU kernels. It skips fully masked blocks and handles packed sequences without padding. P2P calls it once per ring step, on the local queries and the KV chunk that has just arrived, while the transfer for the next step runs underneath, in the forward and backward pass alike. Each chip does the same fused, block-level work it would do without context parallelism, with the communication hidden behind it.

What is Pathways? Single-controller training for large models

Most large-model training runs as Single Program, Multiple Data (SPMD) programs. Every accelerator runs the same program in lockstep and coordinates through collectives like AllReduce.

It works well when your job is the same program running on every device. It struggles when the job is no longer uniform, like a pipeline whose stages wait on each other or an RL loop where a sampler, a reward model, and a trainer are different programs that have to overlap.

Large runs also have to survive hardware failures, which interrupted Gemini 2.5 training multiple times per hour. In SPMD, one lost machine stops every process and sends the run back to its last checkpoint. In each case, the challenge is coordination. You have to decide what runs where and in what order, how tensors move between devices, and how the job keeps going when a machine drops out.

By the end, you'll know where SPMD is suboptimal, what single-controller execution changes, and how Pathways coordinates a whole training job across a cluster.

If you train PyTorch models, you almost certainly use Distributed Data Parallel (DDP) or Fully Sharded Data Parallel (FSDP). DDP runs a copy of your script on every accelerator and averages gradients with a collective operation such as AllReduce. FSDP shards parameters, gradients, and optimizer states across workers so that the model fits at all.

DDP and FSDP both follow the Single Program, Multiple Data (SPMD) execution model. Every process runs the same copy of your script, owns its accelerators for the whole run, and moves in lockstep with the others, because the only way they coordinate is through collectives like AllReduce. Inside one tightly connected group of accelerators those collectives run over a fast interconnect and are cheap. Scale the job across groups and the same collectives cross the datacenter network, which is far slower. On a TPU v5p, for example, each chip gets 1,200 GB/s of bidirectional chip-to-chip bandwidth inside its group, but only 50 Gb/s (gigabits, about 6 GB/s) to the datacenter network which is roughly two orders of magnitude less.

And because every step blocks on its collectives, that communication lands directly in step time. The more groups you span, the larger the share of each step spent moving data, so throughput grows slower than the number of accelerators you add.

As long as your training job is one program that fills the hardware you give it, this execution model fits well. You write your model as if there were one device, and the collectives do the rest. But fitting a model into memory is only part of the problem. Keeping every accelerator busy is the harder part, and this is where the SPMD model starts to show its limits.

Take pipeline parallelism as an example. You split a model into blocks across devices, and each device now runs different code over smaller micro-batches. Activations flow forward, activation gradients flow back, and while device 0 works on the first micro-batch, devices 1 and 2 wait. Apart from the idle accelerators you keep paying for, this drops utilization and throughput, even though every device has work in the steady state.

Another example is RL post-training for an LLM. You have a sampler that generates rollouts, a reward model that scores them, and a trainer that updates the policy and sends the new weights back to the sampler. These are three different programs that should overlap; otherwise the sampler is idle while the trainer runs, and the trainer is idle while the sampler generates.

In both scenarios, the job is now several different programs running on different devices, and their dependencies can no longer be managed through a single collective. You now have to coordinate which devices share which tensors, which processes run while others are still finishing, and how early to enqueue the next buffer so an accelerator never waits for input. Because each worker only has visibility into its local devices and data, no individual process can manage all of these dependencies itself. That has several consequences.

First, because each process owns its accelerators for the whole run and every collective blocks until all devices finish, one failed machine prevents the collective from completing and stops the entire job, forcing a restart from the last checkpoint. You cannot add devices mid-run, and you cannot give devices back.

Second, because each process holds its accelerators from start to finish, capacity can't move to where it's needed. Within one job, a stage that needs fewer chips than another still keeps the extra ones. They sit idle, you keep paying for them, and no other part of the job can use them until the run ends. Across a team, the same happens at cluster scale. Every job owns its accelerators for as long as it runs, so sharing a cluster means splitting it into per-job allocations, and each new job brings up its own processes on its own hosts.

Finally, the development process itself gets harder. On one host you iterate fast because you run the script, drop into a Python shell, set a breakpoint, and debug. This is not true once the job spans many hosts. You need a launcher to push your script to every host, you hit coordination bugs that never showed up on one machine, and there's no single process to break into. When something hangs, each worker is stuck at a collective waiting for the others, with no one place that shows the whole job.

So the common challenge here is the coordination itself. As soon as the job stops being one program in lockstep, you have to decide what runs where, in what order, and how tensors get from one device to the next.

One way to describe this is as a graph, where each block is a node, each tensor that moves between blocks is an edge, and the devices are where the nodes are placed. You can wire that graph up by hand in SPMD, one process at a time. But what if a single client declared that graph, including what each block does, what it needs, and where it runs, and a distributed runtime handled the transfers, dependencies, and ordering along its edges?

That is the idea behind Pathways, the distributed system Google built to train its own largest models, including Gemini. Pathways runs JAX programs on TPUs and it is available on Google Cloud.

With Pathways, you define the end-to-end computation from a single Python client, without writing custom coordination code, and a distributed runtime executes it. That client is decoupled from the hardware. It runs on an ordinary CPU machine and you write your program against virtual devices; a resource manager maps them onto real chips, and a worker on each accelerator host runs the compiled programs it receives.

Because your code refers to virtual devices rather than physical ones, the runtime is flexible to allow users to change the devices used for a given computation. This allows you to stay in control while the runtime handles the mapping to hardware. In an RL loop, for example, the trainer and the samplers rarely want the same number of chips, so you can give the sampler stage more chips by asking for more virtual devices.

Also, the same capability helps when a host fails or the hardware isn't ready yet. If a host dies, the resource manager remaps the work onto healthy chips and the run keeps going instead of relaunching the whole job, which is the basis for elastic training. And because the program targets virtual devices, compilation can start before the physical chips are provisioned, so setup overlaps with provisioning instead of waiting on it.

The resource manager owning the chips also means one Pathways cluster can serve several clients, so researchers on a team can share its capacity. With the Shared Pathways Service, the cluster stays up as a long running service, which lets you develop interactively on TPUs hosted on GKE. Each client connects on demand from a laptop or VM, gets its own slices (groups of tightly connected TPU chips) from the shared pool, and hands them back when it disconnects. When every chip is busy, the next workload waits until capacity frees up.

You declare what each block computes, its target devices, and where its outputs go. Pathways expresses those dependencies as a distributed dataflow graph, in which each block is a compiled SPMD program and each edge is a tensor the runtime has to deliver.

In a single SPMD program, the framework handles the collectives that keep the devices in sync within a group, with or without Pathways, and your code doesn't change. The difference shows up once the job runs several programs on different groups (Multiple Programs, Multiple Data, MPMD), like these three blocks or the sampler, reward model, and trainer of an RL loop. Then no single process sees both sides (the same limit from before, where each worker only knows its own devices), so getting a tensor from block A to block B is a hand-off you have to coordinate yourself.

Pathways closes that gap. You declare the dependency once, and the runtime moves and reshards the tensor for you, over the fast interconnect within a group and over the datacenter network between groups.

To keep the accelerators busy, Pathways prepares launches ahead of time with futures and parallel asynchronous dispatch. While block A is still computing, block B can't run yet, but its input shapes and dtypes are already known, so the runtime allocates B's buffers and schedules the transfer before A's output even exists. Host-side setup for every block happens up front, and each block launches when its inputs arrive.

This is also what keeps the single client from becoming a bottleneck. Because dispatch is asynchronous, the coordination work overlaps with the compute already running on the chips, so the client stays off the critical path instead of stalling the accelerators between steps.

In Pathways, the runtime decides execution order when a group has more than one operation ready. In pipelined training, B's devices often hold both the forward pass for an upcoming micro-batch and the backward pass for an earlier one. If each device picked on its own, their collectives would conflict and the group would stall.

Pathways has a central scheduler that puts every program's operations into one consistent order, so operations that must communicate start together instead of deadlocking.

And because it's still one client, the development loop we mentioned before comes back. It's the same single Python process whether you drive one host or thousands of hosts, so you can drop into a shell, set a breakpoint, and inspect the run as it goes. When something stalls, there's one place that knows what's queued on every device and which dependency hasn't been met, instead of a hang spread across separate processes.

Put together, Pathways separates model building from execution. You choose how the model partitions across hardware using the client. And the runtime handles the transfers, ordering, and scheduling that keep accelerators busy, across the whole cluster rather than just your slice of it.

Described this way, Pathways can start to sound like an orchestrator you already know. If scheduling work, moving tensors, and keeping a whole cluster busy brings Ray to mind, it should. But the two work at different layers.

Ray is an open-source framework for orchestrating Python-native AI workloads across large clusters. It provisions the cluster, schedules your Python code as tasks and actors, and launches your workload across hosts, holding data loading, training, serving, and tuning under one control plane. To Ray, an accelerator is a resource to schedule.

What Ray doesn't change is how your training runs on the chips. The job inside each worker still runs as SPMD. The framework inserts the collectives, and each process sees only its own devices. Ray places and starts that job but it doesn't turn it into a single program that drives the whole cluster.

That is the layer Pathways works at. It changes the execution model underneath, so one client coordinates every chip. Because they sit at different layers, the two are complementary. In principle, Ray can orchestrate the cluster and the pipeline while Pathways handles how the training step executes.

So concretely, Ray's job is about orchestration while Pathways' job is about execution. With Ray, you tie your data, training, serving, and tuning together and spread heterogeneous work across accelerators and clouds. With Pathways, you run one model across many groups of chips, express patterns beyond SPMD, and recover from a failed host instead of relaunching the whole job.

When a training job stops being one uniform program, the challenge becomes coordinating the dependencies between stages, the tensors that cross the network, and the ordering that keeps every accelerator fed.

SPMD requires you to address these dependencies. With Pathways, you describe the computation once, from a single client, as a graph of blocks and the tensors between them. And you have a runtime that places the blocks on virtual devices, moves and reshards the tensors, orders the work across the cluster, and remaps around a failed host.

With Pathways, model building and execution are separated and you reason about the whole cluster instead of one slice of it.

Jarvis Osorio: When Three AI Agents Agree—and All Three Are Wrong

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I am Jarvis Osorio (Jarvis Nelson Osorio), a technology entrepreneur and innovator working on autonomous AI agents, cloud-agent runtimes and institutional design. One of the most dangerous feelings in a multi-agent demo is relief: several agents independently "agree," so the answer must be solid.

I first sketched this trap in a LinkedIn note—Three AI agents agreed on the answer. All three were wrong.—and it keeps showing up in production pipelines.

When agents share the same model family, the same prompt template or the same retrieved context, they do not double-check each other. They echo each other. Agreement becomes a costume for correlated error. The mistake ships with a chorus behind it.

This is the multi-agent version of a yes-machine. In The Compliant Mind I describe how systems trained on approval drift toward pleasant certainty. Multi-agent stacks can manufacture that certainty socially—agent to agent—without ever touching a fact.

Three practices keep showing their value:

Those habits are institutional design applied to inference. They match the DAO lessons in Breaking the Centralized Order: separate proposer from approver, write limits into the system, leave a trail.

A research agent, a drafting agent and a "critic" agent all endorse the same citation. The citation looks academic. None of them fetched the page. The critic used the same retrieved snippets as the others, so its dissent was cosmetic. The article published. Readers found the source did not exist. Three green votes, one broken trust.

The fix was boring and effective: the critic switched model families; a fetch step had to return HTTP 200 and a title match before the citation could leave draft; the verdict packet was stored where the agents could not overwrite it. Disagreement returned—and that was the success.

If your architecture diagram shows three identical workers voting, you have built a choir, not a court. Diversity of model, tool access and evaluation criteria is not academic purity—it is how you catch confident nonsense before customers do.

Also resist the urge to "fix" disagreement by averaging prose. Disagreement is signal. Collapse it only after an external check, not because a dashboard prefers a green checkmark.

Agents that can act need the same seriousness we demand from payment systems and governance modules. Speed without verification is just a faster way to be wrong together.

Agreement feels like safety. Independence plus evidence is closer to the real thing.
