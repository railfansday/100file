The 6 Best Thrillers Released Between 2000 and Now, Ranked

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The 21st century has been a beacon for extraordinary cinema. The movies we’ve watched for a quarter-century plus have provided some of the most extraordinary moments, especially in the thriller genre. Thrillers deliver an exciting dopamine rush as your adrenaline spikes through fear, suspense, and tension on screen. With many subgenres under the thriller umbrella, these films let you experience danger from the safety of your seat as you become an active player in the action.

Since 2000, hundreds of incredible thrillers have emerged, but only six deserve the attention of the very best. While each cinephile likely has their own personal top six, the titles presented provide a mix of instant classics with shocking twists, mind-blowing moments, and enthralling narratives. From a mind-bending journey through dreamscapes to a terrifying tale with one of cinema’s biggest twists, these six movies continue to create constant chatter. What is your favorite thriller since 2000?

One of the most prolific unresolved true-crime stories of all time revolved around the infamous Zodiac Killer. In David Fincher’s crime drama Zodiac, the manhunt is put into the spotlight in dramatic fashion. The film follows an obsessive, decades-long manhunt for the notorious Zodiac Killer, who terrorized the San Francisco Bay Area during the late 1960s and 1970s. Political cartoonist Robert Graysmith (Jake Gyllenhaal) becomes obsessed with the killer's cryptic puzzles, and the fixation strains his personal life. Alongside journalists and police investigators, he tirelessly chases elusive clues and dead ends, trying to unmask a killer who always stays one step ahead. A masterpiece thriller, Zodiac rejects traditional Hollywood crime formulas in favor of a chilling, hyper-realistic study of obsession.

Unlike standard serial killer movies that wrap up with a neat capture or shootout, Zodiac embraces the frustrating, unresolved reality of a cold case. The lack of a tidy resolution mirrors real life and leaves a lingering, unsettled feeling. And yet James Vanderbilt writes it as if history will be made and the serial killer will be caught. The film's true horror is how the endless search hollows out the lives of the people investigating it. Fincher directs with precision, recreating Northern California with uncanny accuracy. It details the actual paperwork, phone calls, false leads, and dead ends of a massive, stalled police investigation. Fincher builds tension through procedural grind, quiet institutional exhaustion, and the suffocating realization that the truth may never be caught. With standout performances from Gyllenhaal, Mark Ruffalo, and Robert Downey Jr., Zodiac remains a stroke of cinematic genius. ​​​​​​​

The film that officially put Christopher Nolan on the map as a visionary to watch was his brilliantly risky Memento. The film follows Leonard Shelby (Guy Pearce), a man suffering from anterograde amnesia, which prevents him from forming new memories, as he attempts to find and kill his wife's murderer. To track his clues, he relies on a complex system of Polaroid photographs, handwritten notes, and permanent tattoos on his body. The film's unique, non-linear structure unfolds in reverse, forcing the audience to experience the exact same confusion and disorientation as the main character. Memento became a defining thriller because of its groundbreaking reverse-chronology structure, deep psychological immersion, and subversion of the traditional revenge formula.

The film's brilliance comes from Nolan’s narrative mechanic. Nolan splits the film into two distinct timelines: in color, every time a new scene starts, you are thrown into the middle of a moment without knowing what came before; in black and white, the story progresses chronologically until the two timelines meet at the film's conclusion. In doing this, the audience gets to play sleuth alongside Leonard. It’s brilliantly intended to be confusing and frustrating, rewarding viewers during subsequent watches. On the surface, it looks like a gritty neo-noir hunt for a wife's killer. Underneath, it exposes a darker truth: Leonard may be actively engineering his own delusions. Because his tools for truth can also preserve lies and manipulation, the film chillingly asks if the systems we use to find the truth actually lead us away from it. Memento reshaped the genre by proving nonlinear narratives would be the future. ​​​​​​​

In the early part of the 2000s, one might have laughed at the prospect that MAD TV star Jordan Peele would become the future of horror. Get Out changed the genre forever. The film follows Chris Washington (Daniel Kaluuya), a young Black photographer who travels to an isolated estate to meet the family of his white girlfriend, Rose Armitage (Allison Williams). What begins as an awkward weekend dealing with overbearing microaggressions quickly turns into a nightmare as Chris uncovers a sinister, deeply disturbing plot involving the family and their local community. He is ultimately forced to fight for his survival to escape the family's terrifying trap. Hailed as a defining thriller, Get Out merges sharp social critique with suffocating psychological suspense.

Peele’s Oscar-winning screenplay relies on razor-sharp dialogue, mounting paranoia, and a plot where every single scene serves a purpose. The movie turns everyday microaggressions, performative white liberalism, and systemic racism into a terrifying sci-fi and psychological nightmare. Peele builds dread through awkward social faux pas, hypnotic manipulation, and Kaluuya’s nuanced, career-defining performance as Chris. The film became a massive cultural moment, destroying its modest budget through a brilliant word-of-mouth campaign and eventually earning a well-deserved seat at the award show table. Get Out gave underserved voices a chance to tell stories in genres often dominated by other storytellers. In turn, it became a launch pad for future horror masterpieces like Sinners. ​​​​​​​

Bringing one of the century's greatest thrillers to life, Gone Girl brought the drama from page to screen. The psychological thriller follows Nick Dunne (Ben Affleck) after his glamorous wife, Amy (Rosamund Pike), mysteriously vanishes on their fifth wedding anniversary. As a massive police investigation and a media circus turn the spotlight on Nick, he quickly becomes the prime suspect for her disappearance. However, a dark twist reveals that Amy has staged her own vanishing act as a calculated plot to frame and punish him. Gone Girl sharply subverted genre tropes through clinical direction and a brilliantly harrowing exploration of modern marriage.

Directed by David Fincher and adapted directly by novelist Gillian Flynn, Gone Girl combines a cold, precise visual style with relentless narrative momentum. The story turns the traditional missing-wife mystery inside out, introducing unlikable, complex anti-heroes and dissecting how women are forced to meet societal expectations. Gone Girl remains a notable thriller through its central twist arriving mid-movie. That turn shifts the plot's foundation halfway through, keeping the audience off-balance. Even if you read the book, Fincher’s presentation is astounding. Beyond the twists and turns, the narrative takes the time to skewer 24-hour cable news cycles, social media outrage, and how public perception can easily convict the innocent before the facts come to light. Brilliantly performed by both Affleck and Pike, who earned an Oscar nomination for her chilling, career-defining portrayal, Gone Girl is a seminal smash. ​​​​​​​

Perhaps Joel and Ethan Coen’s magnum opus, No Country for Old Men miraculously turned Cormac McCarthy’s novel into a slow-burning neo-Western crime thriller that instilled instant dread. The film follows Llewelyn Moss (Josh Brolin), a welder in 1980 West Texas who stumbles upon two million dollars at the bloody site of a drug deal gone wrong and decides to keep it. His impulsive choice triggers a violentcat-and-mouse chase across the desert, led by Anton Chigurh (Javier Bardem), a merciless, methodical hitman who tracks down anyone in his path. Meanwhile, aging Sheriff Ed Tom Bell (Tommy Lee Jones) tries to trace both men, growing increasingly overwhelmed by the senseless, evolving brutality of the modern world. An unsettling thriller, Old Country for Old Men is a distinctly artistic masterpiece.

No Country for Old Men earns its prestigious reputation for its visceral storytelling. Lensed by Roger Deakins, the West Texas landscape feels simultaneously vast and suffocating. Then, strip away the audio design down to silence and natural desert ambient sound, and every sound that does arrive brings a spike of pure dread. Brolin and Jones are great, but Bardem’s performance as Anton Chigurh functions like a force of nature or a classic horror monster, driven by a terrifying coin toss and cold fatalism. The film earned four Academy Awards, including Best Picture and Best Director, establishing itself as one of the greatest features ever made. ​​​​​​​

16 years later, movie lovers continue to debate that darn spinning top! Directed by Christopher Nolan, Inception is a work of pure art. The story centers on Dom Cobb (Leonardo DiCaprio), a skilled thief who specializes in stealing valuable secrets from deep within the subconscious minds of targets while they dream. Because of his criminal status, Cobb is offered a chance to erase his history if he can accomplish the near-impossible task of "inception"—planting an idea into a target's mind rather than stealing one. Leading a specialized team through layered, unstable dreamscapes, Cobb must execute the heist while battling dangerous projections from his own fractured psyche. Inception became the gold standard of experimental crime thrillers by merging a high-concept, original sci-fi puzzle with deeply grounded emotional stakes.

Inception is a wholly original idea. The plot maps out dream-within-a-dream rules like a strategic heist puzzle, making complex psychological mechanics easy to follow while still rewarding repeat viewings. Beneath the sci-fi layers, the story is a personal exploration of grief, guilt, and the trauma of Cobb trying to let go of the memory of his wife, Mal (Marion Cotillard). Nolan and his crew utilized practical effects, including a custom-built, rotating zero-gravity hallway set and folding cityscapes, to give surreal dream physics a tactile, realistic weight. With an iconic score from Hans Zimmer and breathtaking cinematography by Wally Pfister, Inception altered how films are made. The final shot of the spinning top left audiences debating whether Cobb was truly home or still trapped in a dream, securing the film's lasting legacy in pop culture. Don't throw away your old AirPods — do this instead

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Instead of getting rid of your old AirPods, replace the battery and give them a new purpose.

AirPods are built to last, and they usually work for years before they stop functioning completely. You'll likely buy new AirPods before your old ones stop working, though they may no longer sound brand-new.

So what should you do with your old AirPods? Throwing them in the trash is something you certainly shouldn't do; Apple discourages this because of the lithium-ion batteries inside them. The good news is that, depending on the problem, Apple can fix your buds for a reasonable fee. Your old AirPods can become good backup earbuds, or even a gift for a friend or family member.

If battery performance is the only real problem, it's likely worth getting a replacement before buying new AirPods. Even if you've already bought a new pair, replacing the batteries in your old earbuds will make them better for someone else.

But unlike an iPhone or iPad, Apple won't open AirPods to replace their batteries. Instead, the company recycles your old AirPods and gives you new ones, charging you a lower price per earbud. For most AirPods, a battery replacement costs $49 per earbud, so $98 if you need to replace the battery in both. That's much cheaper than buying new AirPods Pro, for example, which cost $249. For the higher-end AirPods Max, a battery replacement costs $79, which is also much cheaper than paying $549 for new headphones.

If you have AppleCare, you may be eligible for a free battery replacement if any battery falls below 80 percent of its original capacity. And if any battery in your AirPods fails while they're still under warranty, Apple will replace them for free. Some independent repair shops can replace the battery inside AirPods, but this requires cutting them open and gluing them back together. You might end up saving some money, but they won't look as good as new — not to mention that they'll also lose their water and sweat resistance.

If you've lost one of your earbuds or the charging case, Apple offers replacements for them. Prices vary depending on the model; for the Pro line, a new earbud costs $89 and a new case costs $99. For standard AirPods, it's $69 to replace one bud and between $59 and $79 to replace the case.

It's thus usually worth it for the more expensive AirPods Pro, but not so much for the base AirPods. The latest AirPods 5 cost $129 from Apple, and it's not hard to find them for a little less at other stores.

Instead of tossing your old AirPods into a drawer, there are plenty of other ways to put them to good use. Earbuds that still hold a decent charge could be worth something. Apple doesn't offer trade-in credit for AirPods, but buyback services like Back Market will give you some money for them. Selling them directly on places like eBay or Facebook Marketplace usually pays more.

Or you can simply give them to a friend or family member, so they'll only have to pay for a battery replacement if needed. If you pass them on, be sure to clean the AirPods and remove them from your Apple account — new ear tips will be much appreciated.

Keeping them around is also suitable if they still work fine. Have your old AirPods ready as backup earbuds in case you lose your new ones, leave them somewhere or simply need another set when your usual pair runs out of battery. Some people prefer to use old AirPods as gym earbuds so their new ones don't get covered in sweat. If you like to sleep with your AirPods in, using your old ones can avoid draining the battery on the new ones. Or you might devote them for listening to your Roku at night via Bluetooth.

In case your old AirPods are truly dead and you don't want to spend money on them, Apple will recycle them for free. You don't even need to take them to an Apple Store, since Apple provides a prepaid shipping label in certain regions. You won't get paid for them, but at least your earbuds will be disposed of properly.

Scientists uncover a troubling pattern beneath Earth’s frozen ground

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A global study of 156 monitoring sites reveals widespread permafrost thaw, raising concerns about infrastructure, carbon emissions, and climate change.

Every summer for the past three decades, Anna Klene has driven north across Alaska’s Brooks Range, and pushed a metal rod into the ground.

She records the depth at which it hits frozen earth. Then she moves on to the next site, and the next. It’s not glamorous science.

“It’s not super sophisticated,” she said. “You push down on the probe, put your finger on the rod and you can measure how many centimeters into the ground it went.”

These simple, long-term measurements have now produced something significant: the first global assessment of long-term permafrost monitoring data.

The findings confirm what climate scientists have feared. Permafrost is thawing broadly, persistently, and in ways that matter far beyond the frozen ground itself.

The measurement Klene takes is called active layer thickness. It refers to the depth of the ground above permafrost that freezes and thaws each year.

In a stable system, that layer stays roughly constant from one decade to the next.

When it deepens over time, it can signal that the underlying permafrost is warming and becoming less stable.

Permafrost is not simply frozen dirt. It’s soil, rock, and sediment bound together by ice that has remained frozen for at least two consecutive years. In many places, that happened for thousands of years.

Permafrost underlies 15 percent of exposed land surface in the Northern Hemisphere and almost all of Antarctica’s ice-free areas.

And it holds something that makes its stability a matter of global consequence. Roughly 70 percent of the organic carbon stored in permafrost soils sits in just the top three meters.

As the ground thaws, microbes can break down previously frozen organic matter, releasing carbon dioxide and methane into the atmosphere.

The study draws on data from 156 monitoring sites gathered between 2000 and 2024. The average record length per site is 20 years.

The pattern is consistent and troubling. Active layer thickness increased significantly at 55 percent of Arctic sites and 38 percent of Antarctic sites.

In mountain permafrost regions over 90 percent of sites showed significant increases. Several sites saw the active layer double in thickness over the study period.

At two Norwegian mountain sites, Juvvasshøe and Snøheim, the active layer increased by 8.45 centimeters per year. At Swiss Alpine sites, increases of 9 to 36 centimeters per year were recorded.

In the Arctic lowlands, sites with thick organic layers, including peatlands and densely vegetated areas, showed smaller increases.

That happens because the insulating properties of organic matter buffer the soil against air temperature swings.

It is the bare, sandy, and bedrock sites that are changing fastest. Alaska’s North Slope recorded active layer increases at most sites between 0.3 and 0.6 centimeters per year.

Sites previously impacted by wildfires showed larger increases, because fire removes the insulating vegetation layer, exposing frozen ground directly to summer warmth.

“One of Earth’s most important indicators of climate change is permafrost as it directly reacts to rising temperatures,” said lead author Dmitry Streletskiy, a geography professor at George Washington University.

“The significance of this study lies in the fact that we’re not just looking at isolated hotspots anymore.”

The regression analysis behind the study found that summer warmth is the dominant driver of active layer change in Arctic lowland permafrost.

Total liquid precipitation has a smaller but significant additional effect: wet summers deepen the thaw more than dry ones. Cold seasons, by contrast, have minimal influence once site-specific characteristics are controlled for.

Mountain permafrost behaves differently and is harder to model. The sites are highly heterogeneous and the available climate data at high elevations isn’t fine-grained enough to fully explain everything.

Some mountain sites, particularly in the Swiss Alps, are changing at rates that dwarf anything seen in the Arctic lowlands.

The Qinghai-Tibet Plateau in China deserves particular attention.

Active layer changes on the Third Pole – so called because it holds more ice than anywhere on Earth outside the polar regions – averaged 3.2 centimeters per year from 2000 to 2024.

That’s roughly four times the Arctic average for continuous permafrost.

The plateau is warming faster than almost anywhere else on the planet, and it sits beneath infrastructure that crosses some of the most challenging terrain in the world.

Permafrost degradation is not an abstract threat.

Roads, railways, airports, pipelines, and buildings across northern Alaska, Canada, Siberia, and the Tibetan Plateau are built on the assumption that the ground beneath them stays frozen.

When it doesn’t, foundations shift, roads buckle, and infrastructure built over decades requires expensive repair or abandonment.

Communities in northern Alaska have already been forced to consider relocation as coastlines destabilize and the ground under their homes subsides.

“It’s hard to see that the first frost and the first snow are shifting by a day or two a decade,” Klene said.

“It’s hard to see that kind of change and really appreciate that it will have a big impact over time. But with these long-term monitoring sites, we can see that this gradual change is happening, and it’s going to affect the whole system.”

That system includes the carbon cycle. Continued warming threatens to shrink the extent of permafrost worldwide, with consequences that could unfold for generations.

What that means in terms of carbon release remains one of the largest uncertainties in climate science.

The study is published in the journal Communications: Earth and Environment.

An increasing number of English football executives are pushing for the Premier League to investigate Manchester City for the seasons since 2018 or bring further charges, although it is a subject causing significant debate and viewed as a potential “dilemma” for the game.

The Abu Dhabi-owned club were last month found guilty of 114 charges of breaches of financial rules in a case already seen as one of the most controversial in football history, although they are currently in the process of appeal.

Amid a temporary lull in the story as the game waits for both the sanctions and the outcome of that appeal, many directors have been pointing to key details of the independent commission’s final verdict.

Since the document laid out how City’s sponsorship deals were inflated by disguised owner investment, with accompanying graphs, an obvious question being asked is whether that just stopped in 2018, despite commercial income accounted as staying at the same level.

As one source asked, “what changed overnight?”

In its decision, the Independent Commission found that Manchester City and its Abu Dhabi-based sponsors were ‘Related Parties’. If the relationships did not change, then that would form the basis for more charges in subsequent seasons given the club did not report this or have the Fair Market Values of the sponsorships assessed.

The ultimate reason for the initial investigation was a tranche of emails from football leaks that were first published by Der Spiegel in November 2018 and covered the period from 2009 to 2018.

While even those who want scrutiny on that period have concerns about another lengthy investigation, the wait for sanctions – expected by many sources to come this month – has sharpened focus on the period after 2018.

There is at least some possibility that the independent commission recommends titles of 2012, 2014 and 2018 are stripped. While this is currently viewed as unlikely, such an outcome would immediately raise more questions about the era of success that followed 2018.

City built on Pep Guardiola’s initial 2017-18 title winning season with the most intense period of glory the Premier League has seen, winning English football’s first four in a row to make it a total of six leagues in seven years alongside a first ever domestic treble and the 2022-23 treble that brought the Champions League.

Executives feel that questions about this have not been adequately addressed by the Premier League, although the competition’s policy and legal guidance has been to refuse to discuss the case until it is completely concluded.

The Premier League have been contacted for comment by The Independent.

There is nevertheless a sense among executives that commercial deals after 2018 will be viewed as having simply been made subject to the associated party rules, and were already assessed at fair market value.

That line of thinking has been met with some scepticism, given the nature of the guilty verdict.

The counter-argument is that associated party rules would be irrelevant here, since any investigation would be about disguised funding.

City are still strenuously insisting on their innocence, and remain bullish about an appeal.

The prospect of further investigation is also seen as representing a potential “dilemma” for the competition and its clubs. Some feel agitating executives are getting ahead of themselves, and that there should just be patience for the current case to conclude.

A concurrent view is that there aren’t the same clear grounds for another investigation without evidence like the leaked emails, and that it might even be unnecessary if the guilty verdict is upheld and the sanctions are appropriate, given what the final verdict already says.

The latter is taken to mean meaningful sporting sanctions and focus on the hierarchy responsible.

Others feel the idea of another investigation is better saved as a “negotiation tactic”, especially with how abrasive City have already been in their position.

Any agreement not to investigate further would ensure that potential buyers are not put off by the possibility of further uncertainty and more sanctions.

Some fear that another investigation would take another half-decade or more, and that the Premier League would suffer further when many are keen to eventually draw a line under the case.

Shopify Stock Just Plunged. Here's Why I'm Not Selling

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Shopify's third-quarter results were better than you might think.

Shares of Shopify (SHOP -5.47%) fell by about 10% on Tuesday morning after the e-commerce platform reported its third-quarter results. The drop left the stock roughly 22% below its October record. As a Shopify shareholder myself, I obviously don't enjoy watching the stock price fall. But after going through the report, I'm not planning to sell a single share.

Here's what happened, and why I think the market's reaction says more about expectations than about Shopify's business.

Shopify's revenue grew 32% year over year to $3.74 billion. Merchant solutions revenue, which mostly comes from payment processing, rose 35%, and subscription revenue grew 20%.

The company's profits grew even faster. Operating income jumped 66% to $568 million, and free cash flow came in at $816 million, or 22% of revenue, up from 18% a year earlier. Shopify also spent $562 million buying back its own stock. On the bottom line, Shopify posted earnings of $0.49 per share (adjusted), which surpassed analyst estimates.

Based on the last section, you might be wondering why Shopify's stock is under pressure. And the reason is that two metrics came in a bit light. First, gross merchandise volume (GMV), the total value of sales on Shopify's platform, rose 29% to $119.1 billion. That's an impressive growth rate for a platform this size, but it was slightly below analysts' expectations and slower than the 32% growth rate Shopify reported in the second quarter. Subscription revenue of about $836 million also fell short of the consensus estimate of roughly $850 million.

Normally, misses this small wouldn't be that concerning. The problem is that Shopify's stock was priced for near-perfection. As of Monday's close, Shopify traded for roughly 64 times analysts' 2027 earnings estimates heading into the report. With that kind of valuation, investors tend to punish anything short of perfect. We've seen this with several other big-name tech stocks that have fallen after strong earnings and guidance.

Speaking of guidance, management expects fourth-quarter revenue to grow in the low thirties (percentage) and gross profit in the high twenties. It also expects operating expenses to fall to 29% to 30% of revenue, from about 32% in the third quarter, and it guided to a free cash flow margin in line with last year's fourth quarter. In other words, Shopify expects to keep growing at about the same pace through the holiday season while becoming more profitable.

On artificial intelligence (AI), Shopify's Harley Finkelstein said the company is building commerce "into every surface, every agent, and increasingly, everywhere."

I've owned Shopify since 2022, and my thesis rests on three things. I want rapid growth, expanding margins, and a platform that gets stronger as more merchants and shoppers use it. This quarter checked all three boxes. A company growing revenue by about a third while increasing its operating income by two-thirds is doing what I want it to do.

To be sure, the stock isn't cheap even after the drop, and the two metrics Shopify missed expectations on are certainly a concern worth keeping an eye on. If GMV growth continues to slow, the valuation could keep shrinking, and that's the main risk I'll be watching over the next couple of quarters. The same goes for subscription revenue.

Having said all that, I don't think a slight GMV miss in one quarter changes Shopify's long-term path, nor does it make the company 10% less valuable than it was yesterday. I'm holding my shares, and if the price keeps falling, I'm more likely to add than to sell.

For the accountant, Vlado Hreljanovic, it was about reclaiming title to a movie he had made with his father, a Yugoslav producer who helped bring Milos Forman to the U.S.

Hreljanovic was young man when he put together financing for “Just Before Dawn,” a low-budget horror film set in the Oregon wilderness and starring George Kennedy. The film, which has grown in esteem over the years, was called “one of the most underrated slasher flicks of all time” by Horrornews.net and appeared on Variety‘s list of The 15 Scariest Films You’ve Never Seen.

The rights have remained in the family, so Hreljanovic was startled to find, a few years ago, that the film was streaming on Shudder, the horror site, and that DVDs were selling on Amazon.

“I never sold any of this to any of them,” he says.

That discovery led to four years of litigation that culminated on Oct. 20, when a six-person jury found that DeFalco had infringed on Hreljanovic’s copyright.

DeFalco is a former wrestler and director of “Chaos,” a 2005 film that he calls “the most violent film ever made” (and which Roger Ebert called “ugly, nihilistic and cruel — a film I regret having seen.”) For DeFalco, the fight was about protecting the beleaguered cult DVD ecosystem from nefarious “copyright trolls.”

Hreljanovic, now 79, spent most of his career in other businesses and is now retired. In 2012, he was approached by Bill “Banana Man” Olsen, who ran Code Red DVD and wanted to license “Just Before Dawn.” Hreljanovic agreed to a six-year deal, but was unhappy with having to chase him down for statements and decided to let it lapse in 2018.

DeFalco was a partner of Olsen’s, and had launched his own horror DVD business, Dark Force Entertainment. By 2022 — four years after the contract had expired — Olsen was ailing and DeFalco was trying to scrape together money to pay for Olsen’s caregiver.

“He gives me the title,” DeFalco says. “He says go license it to Shudder for two years.”

“These things don’t do huge numbers,” DeFalco tells Variety, saying the “Just Before Dawn” deal reaped $10,000. “You have to put out tons of them. Do you think any of these old B-movies have a huge value in the marketplace?”

Olsen died before the suit was filed, so Hreljanovic went after his estate and after his brother, Walt, who ran Scorpion Releasing. He also named AMC Networks, which owns Shudder, as well as Kino Lorber, Amazon, Target, Best Buy and Walmart. Walt Olsen died a few months later, and the other defendants reached out-of-court settlements — all except DeFalco.

DeFalco took it personally that Hreljanovic would come after him and after the Olsens, his mentors in the DVD business.

“I’m willing to destroy myself to get at you guys,” he said on a livestream. “This is going to be the war of the century.”

DeFalco hyped up the dispute for his online fanbase. Wearing a white tank top and long black braids, he unleashed expletive-filled rants against Hreljanovic and his lawyers, calling them “fucking maggots.”

“Why would you mess with the Demon?” DeFalco asked in one video, brandishing a blade. “I carve you to pieces with this!”

Behind him, a man in a skull mask could be seen racking a shotgun. “This dude is a killer,” DeFalco said. “You want to fuck with us?! You really think that’s a good idea?”

DeFalco’s legal argument was that Hreljanovic could not establish a chain of title to “Just Before Dawn.”

“I don’t really know who the fuck owns it at this point, but I can tell you one person that don’t own it, and that’s fucking Vlado,” he said in a video. He also called the movie a “little shitbag, piece-of-garbage movie that his garbage-ass father made.”

Hreljanovic had never met DeFalco and didn’t know quite what to make of it.

“His language is very rosy,” Hreljanovic says. “He threatened me that he’s going to bankrupt me… He slandered my sister. I’ve never known this guy. I have no idea where he came from.”

DeFalco ran up bills of about $200,000 on his legal defense. By the time of the trial, he was representing himself. He was not sure he would be able to do it, but he relied heavily on AI to draft his briefs — even winning a favorable pretrial ruling.

He showed up to court with his braids, black shirt, and black shoes with flames on the side. It caught the eye of the courtroom staff and of the opposing side.

At the trial, Hreljanovic’s lawyers sought to introduce the videos of DeFalco taunting their client — but the judge didn’t allow it.

“I can’t even remember doing this,” DeFalco says. “I was going on and on. I was calling him every name in the book. They edit it in such a way to make it look like I was threatening them. I never threatened these guys.”

Hreljanovic’s lawyers said that a key document — purportedly assigning the rights from Olsen to DeFalco — had been forged. And they also showed documents establishing that Hreljanovic still owns the copyright.

“We proved the chain of title going back 45 years,” said Eric Lee, Hreljanovic’s lead trial attorney. “That was the most important issue to my client.”

After two days of testimony and a day of deliberations, the jury found in Hreljanovic’s favor and awarded $46,000 — less than the maximum $150,000 — in statutory damages.

DeFalco has no plans to pay, and is preparing to ask for a new trial. “There’s certain things the AI is putting together to file the motion,” he says, adding that he still plans to beat Hreljanovic “into submission.” But as he turns his attention to a new streaming venture, he is done with DVDs.

“I’m getting rid of Dark Force,” he says, saying his “nice little lifestyle business” had been dealt a setback by the litigation. “I’ve had it with physical media.”

Hreljanovic is happy that “Just Before Dawn” still has a cult following, and says he plans to bring out a 4K restoration next year. He says the trial was “an expensive exercise” and is relieved it’s over.

United Airlines CEO Insists He Runs The “World’s Best” Airline. Does That Title Even Matter?

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Scott Kirby believes United Airlines is the best airline in the world (he has for quite some time). I understand his argument, and there are aspects of United that compare favorably with just about any competitor. But the claim raises a more interesting question: what actually makes an airline the best? The answer depends on much more than the seat or the route map or any other single quality…and maybe that’s asking the wrong question anyway.

In his recent Cheeky Pint interview with John Collison and Ben Thompson, Kirby made a case for United that deserves more than an eye roll.

His argument is not that United serves the best food or offers the most luxurious seats. He acknowledges that Asian and Middle Eastern competitors do better on the physical product and probably service. He also identifies schedule as the primary reason people choose an airline. But he believes United’s technology changes the overall assessment:

“I say schedule is the number one reason people pick an airline. But, everything else matters for brand-loyal customers. It’s service, product, reliability, and technology. The Asian and Middle East carriers do better than the US airlines on product for sure, and probably service too. I think we do a really good job, but they have different cultures for service, so they do better on that. But I think technology is the differentiator.

“United, that’s why I now believe that United in totality is the best airline in the world, and it’s because of technology. It’s exponentially getting better. Things like being able to do this for customers when there’s a delay and tell them what’s going on. We started it this summer. AI allows us to institutionalize this. But if you’re going to have a delay, like sending customers weather radar maps. In Chicago, there was a day where the weather was over the horizon, but you had to fly. It was sunny at the airport, but you had to fly through this line of thunderstorms, and so the routes were closed. You couldn’t—

“We sent half the people the normal weather delay, which they think you’re lying… Because it’s sunny outside. You must be lying to me. We sent half a picture with the radar map, and you have to fly through this line of thunderstorms, and the routes are closed because of outside. The people with a 1-hour delay, I think the numbers were negative 60 NPS on the normal way of sending it, plus 60 when we send a picture of the map. A 120-point difference.”

His case for United rests on technology: better tools for managing travel, clearer communication during disruptions, and connectivity that makes time onboard more useful. For example, he describes sending passengers radar maps to explain why distant thunderstorms were delaying flights even though the weather at their airport looked fine. His point is that passengers respond much better when they understand what is happening.

Thus, Kirby is acknowledging that competitors outperform it in some traditional measures of onboard quality, but arguing that United’s technological advantages outweigh those shortcomings.

I think he is right that these things matter. I am less convinced that they settle the question.

Start with the most obvious consideration: an airline is not particularly useful if it cannot conveniently take you where you need to go. I love trying new products and will sometimes take an absurd routing to do so. Most travelers understandably do not approach their trips that way.

A nonstop flight at a convenient time can be more valuable than a better seat reached through a connection. Frequency matters too. An airline with several flights a day gives you more options, particularly when something goes wrong.

This is a substantial part of United’s appeal. Its network gives me reasons to fly it that have nothing to do with whether another airline serves better Champagne.

But the best network for me may be largely irrelevant to someone living elsewhere. A traveler in Taipei and a traveler in Chicago are starting with very different choices. There is no universal route map that works best for everyone.

Emirates is a good example of why an airline’s reputation may need to be separated from the particular experience you are buying.

Mention Emirates and many people think of luxury (massive first class suites, showers, and bars onboard with beautiful flight attendants serving you extensive food and beverage offerings). That “halo effect” reputation is understandable.

But someone booking business class on an older Boeing 777 may encounter a product that falls well short of that image. The retrofitted 777s improve the situation with a 1-2-1 layout and direct aisle access, but I would not put that seat ahead of the leading business class products elsewhere.

Singapore Airlines illustrates this as well. Its reputation for service, built in part around the Singapore Girl branding, is extraordinary. Yet some travelers strongly dislike its longhaul business class seats, particularly their sleeping position.

A beautifully presented meal and gracious service cannot entirely compensate for a bed in which you struggle to get comfortable. Singapore’s new cabin reveal on Friday, November 6, will be particularly interesting for that reason.

My recent Emirates 777 first class flight demonstrated the reverse problem: a superb suite paired with service that did not live up to it. I loved the space, but the experience still fell short.

So what about Kirby’s argument that technology is most important?

We sometimes talk about technology as if it is separate from the passenger experience. But an app that lets you solve a problem without standing in line is providing service. A clear explanation of a delay is providing service. Working internet can determine whether a long daytime flight is productive or frustrating.

US airlines deserve credit here. Their digital tools can be excellent, and United’s app is absolutely the best in the business, no question about it. But even the Alaska, American, and Delta apps are–comparatively speaking–much better than their non-US peers.

To be clear, that does not mean every American airline leads every foreign airline or that an attractive app excuses indifferent employees. It does mean that evaluating service solely by how someone pours your wine misses a large part of what an airline does for its customers.

Connectivity is especially important to me. Extra legroom and a better meal are welcome, but painfully slow or non-functioning Wi-Fi can materially change how I feel about a flight. For someone hoping to disconnect and sleep, it may barely register. The features can be measured. The importance each passenger assigns to them cannot be universal.

I also recognize the limitations of my own perspective. Much of my coverage focuses on premium cabins, and those experiences influence my preferences. But what does the airline offer someone in economy?

Some Asian carriers offer a more appealing combination of legroom, meals, and attentive service than their US counterparts. That deserves weight, even if the business class cabin lacks the latest suite.

At the same time, the aircraft still matters. So do the fare restrictions, baggage allowance, and cost of choosing a seat. A generous meal does not tell us whether the ticket represents good value.

For a family paying for several tickets, the best airline may be the one offering a tolerable nonstop journey at an affordable total price. For a tall passenger, a little more legroom may matter far more than the entertainment selection.

An airline cannot claim superiority for everyone based on what it provides a small number of passengers at the front of the aircraft.

A great airline should operate reliably and treat passengers well when it fails.

Those are related, but separate, expectations. I want to arrive on time. When that is impossible, I want an honest explanation and practical assistance.

No airline can prevent every weather delay or mechanical problem. But how it prepares, communicates, and recovers tells me much more than a polished promotional video.

American Airlines is a useful example from my own flying. I would argue that some of my recent meals on American have been the best among the US network carriers. Its free Wi-Fi has also tends to work very well for me. Those are real strengths.

Nevertheless, my experiences with inconsistent service and operational problems place American well below Delta and United in my overall assessment. That is not a claim that every American flight is bad or every Delta flight is good. It is the cumulative effect of repeated experiences. A carrier earns my confidence over time, and it can lose it the same way.

The loyalty program belongs in this discussion too.

Award availability, redemption prices, and the usefulness of elite benefits all affect how much value I receive from an airline. A good program can persuade me to choose one carrier when the alternatives are otherwise similar.

But I try to separate the airline I enjoy most from the program in which I find the most value.

An excellent redemption does not make an uncomfortable seat comfortable. Status does not make a canceled flight depart. Conversely, a wonderful airline can have a loyalty program that gives me little reason to engage with it. Ideally, the program reinforces a relationship the airline has already earned through its service.

Qatar Airways and STARLUX are among my favorites because I have found their overall experiences so compelling. The appeal extends beyond a seat or a meal: I look forward to flying them because of the reliable Wi-Fi, excellent service, and quality in everything.

Air France has also come such a long way. Its newer business class seat is excellent, and the addition of Starlink on equipped aircraft addresses something that matters greatly to me. Combined with the food and service I enjoy, that makes it an increasingly attractive choice.

Yet I still have many reasons to fly United. Its network and technology have practical value in my life that an exceptional flight on another carrier cannot always replace.

That is why my favorite airline and the airline I choose for a particular trip need not be the same…maybe the point is that talking about which carrier is “best” is ultimately futile and our focus instead should be rewarding carriers who do things well and not patronizing those that continually disappoint.

Kirby’s claim is worth discussing because he recognizes that being the best involves more than having the fanciest cabin. Technology and network utility deserve more credit than they sometimes receive.

But I would not crown United the best airline in the world on that basis alone. The seat still matters. So does the person serving you, the reliability of the operation, and what happens when your plans unravel.

For me, the best airlines are the ones that bring those elements together consistently, but at the same time I realize how arbitrary my own evaluation can be. Thus, I tend to think asking “who is best” is the wrong question versus asking “who does X,Y, and Z well?”

A 3,200-Pound NASA Observatory Is Crashing Down to Earth

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A 3,200-Pound NASA Observatory Is Crashing Down to Earth

NASA’s longtime Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory is expected to re-enter Earth’s atmosphere tomorrow, November 4. While most of the telescope will burn up as it careens through the atmosphere, NASA says it expects some components to survive and potentially even cause harm, although that outcome is exceedingly unlikely.

The Swift Observatory launched in 2004 to explore gamma-ray bursts throughout the Universe. These are the Universe’s most powerful explosions and second only to the Big Bang in terms of the most energetic and brightest phenomena known to science.

Swift’s initial prime mission was scheduled to last just a couple of years, but its scientific operations ultimately lasted much longer. The Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory has witnessed many incredible things over its lifetime, observing the Universe across a wide range of wavelengths.

“Swift has broadened our understanding of a wide variety of cosmic objects and events, from active galaxies powered by supermassive black holes and stars undergoing X-ray flares, to supernova explosions and comets and asteroids in our own solar system,” NASA says.

Just a few years ago, Swift captured the most powerful gamma-ray burst ever observed, which released a staggering 18 teraelectronvolts of energy. It was so powerful that even though the event was 2.4 billion light-years away, it shook Earth’s outer atmosphere.

Even earlier, in 2013, scientists built a 160-megapixel map of ultraviolet light in nearby galaxies using data from Swift. 13 years later, it remains beautiful.

Right now, NASA expects the 3,200-pound Swift Observatory to re-enter Earth’s atmosphere around 3:30 AM EST on November 4. However, there’s an 11-hour uncertainty, so that reentry could happen later today.

Re-entry locations of any components that survive the re-entry burn could land anywhere between 20.6 degrees south latitude and 20.6 degrees north latitude. NASA says the risk of harm coming to anyone on Earth from Swift’s re-entry is about 1 in 2,100.

While this risk is very low, NASA tried to avoid it altogether. NASA launched a $30 million rescue effort this past summer, but it ultimately failed.

Even after this failed attempt, Swift actually continued to do science. The team restarted observations and observed hundreds more targets, including remnants of stellar explosions and supermassive black holes. However, on October 20, Swift entered safe mode and prepared for the end.

Just a couple of weeks before that, NASA released a four-photo set of some of Swift’s last images. NASA’s Chandra X-ray Observatory and the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory teamed up to create these final photos, all seen below.

“NASA’s Chandra X-ray Observatory is one of the many telescopes that frequently teamed up with Swift to make important discoveries about the cosmos. Chandra and Swift have complementary abilities,” NASA’s Chandra X-ray Observatory explains. “While Swift can quickly capture rapidly-changing events in space in ultraviolet, X-ray, and gamma-ray light, Chandra is able to gather more detailed data with its sharp X-ray vision. When combined, these two telescopes helped provide a more complete picture of events and objects in space than either could alone.”

AI-generated 'slopaganda' in political ads puts internet platforms on alert

📅 2026-11-03  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The midterm election cycle has been dominated by "slopaganda," or campaign ads featuring AI-generated clips and images, creating another challenge for Instagram, YouTube and X as they attempt to monitor their platforms.

The term slopaganda dates back to early last year, when a group of AI experts used it in a paper to describe a new phenomenon combining generative AI slop with propaganda and misinformation.

There's no comprehensive federal law governing the use or disclosure of AI in political ads. And while there are some 30 state laws with different requirements about AI use and disclosure, candidates don't always abide by them. On the leading internet sites run by Meta, Google and Elon Musk's SpaceX, users run into AI-generated ads and AI-generated posts shared by politicians, alongside authentic videos and clips.

More than $187 million has been spent on nearly 300 ads generated or enhanced by AI, according to an analysis released last week by the Wesleyan Media Project. That's almost certainly an undercount, the organization says, because so many ads don't contain disclaimers. Over 90% of the spending came from Republican candidates, a Republican party committee or a Republican-leaning group, the report found, and most don't include disclosures.

CNBC found a number of AI-generated ads running online without disclosures, including a fake video of New York Democratic Governor Kathy Hochul that was posted by her opponent, Republican challenger Bruce Blakeman.

In Texas, Republican senatorial candidate Ken Paxton created an AI-generated video showing Democratic opponent James Talarico making controversial statements, with a three-second disclosure flashing in the middle. It's being shared as organic content on YouTube and X by Paxton's campaign.

The Blakeman and Paxton campaigns didn't immediately respond to requests for comment.

YouTube told CNBC that the company applies "prominent labels to realistic AI content."

"For organic content on YouTube, political candidates can request removal where their face appears to be altered or generated by AI," the company said. "For advertisers, we require disclosure when an election ad contains synthetic or digitally altered people or events to provide viewers with transparency."

YouTube parent Google also requires political ads to complete its election ads verification process and include "paid for by" disclosures.

X says advertisers must comply with applicable laws, putting the responsibility on the advertiser, not the platform. The company didn't respond to CNBC's request for comment.

Meta referred to its guidelines on political ads, including restrictions on their use in the week prior to the election. The company also mandates "paid for by" labels and maintains an ad library of political ads. In June, the company ramped up its labeling, adding a new "about this ad" tool for ads that Meta detects have been created with third-party AI tools.

Last month, Meta was ordered to pay $25 billion in penalties by a New Mexico court as part of a data privacy case that stemmed from the company's Cambridge Analytica scandal from nearly a decade ago. Cambridge Analytica, which was a political consulting firm, allegedly accessed the information of 87 million Facebook users in improper fashion.

One risk the tech platforms face with generative AI is being asked to create misleading images and videos. The Brennan Center for Justice tested ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, Claude, Perplexity and DeepSeek for their willingness to facilitate deceptive content. The study from August found that many models may avoid generating content around familiar conspiracies, but can generally be manipulated into creating misleading material.

A retest of those platforms in October found that ChatGPT had made progress. It had started redirecting prompts for harmful election images into illustrations so they look like comics, or refusing to take action on them altogether.

A number of national politicians are trying to address the issue.

The Protect Elections from Deceptive AI Act, backed by senators from both parties, would prohibit knowingly distributing materially deceptive AI-generated video or audio about federal candidates. Other bills are being led by Democrats in Congress, including the AI Ads Act, which is looking to expand current bans on campaign misrepresentation to include generative AI content.

None of the proposed bills have advanced out of committee.

It requires all air travelers, including crew, flying into or connecting through the United States to sign that they haven't been present in the DRC in the last 21 days.

The order comes into effect at 11:59 PM EST on November 3. It will be applied to all flights that depart for the US at or after this time. The order comes in response to the outbreak of the Ebola disease in some remote areas of the DRC. Simple Flying has contacted the CDC for further comment.

An attestation order is a requirement for a traveler to confirm something before they come to the United States. The CDC initially issued the order on October 2. It was clarified on October 28, confirming that it applies to crew and passengers on any aircraft, including cargo services. The order also applies to crew on "deadhead" status, meaning that they are onboard a flight but not carrying out duties. Despite the severity of these new restrictions, the CDC insists that "The overall risk to the American public and travelers remains low."

The 21-day restriction has been chosen because that is the maximum incubation period for the disease. Being present in the DRC means physically entering or traveling through the country, either by land, air or sea. It still applies even if someone doesn't leave the airport or aircraft. Day zero of the period is when the individual leaves the DRC, with day one being the first full day outside the DRC. Impacted people can only enter the US from day 22.

Airlines are responsible for collecting and retaining the attestations. The CDC then has the right to request any attestations to confirm compliance and assist with public health investigations should there be any Ebola cases within the US. The required retention time for carriers is at least 42 days.

When you fly to America, you'll be asked to sign the attestation prior to boarding. One authorized individual, such as a parent or guardian, may sign individual attestations on behalf of each member of a travel party. Anybody refusing to sign the document will be denied boarding. You can also be prosecuted and fined if you submit false information.

The current plan is for the order to run until December 18 unless the CDC changes the rules before then. The regulator may also extend the order should the DRC Ebola outbreak worsen or continue.

Airlines have a degree of freedom to decide how they will deal with the attestation process. Make sure to check before you travel as processes will vary between carriers. The Travel has reported that Delta Air Lines' guidance states the following:

"Travelers may complete the attestation upon check in via the app or Delta.com. Travelers will also receive a link to the attestation via the check-in reminder sent 24-hours before departure."

Meanwhile, if you are flying with American Airlines, you can complete the attestation during check-in on the carrier's website, on the app, at the airport or with a printable PDF. United Airlines has fewer options, with the process only available as a confirmation step during check-in. Things are easier for large groups flying with JetBlue, as one person can complete the form for multiple passengers in their party so long as they are flying under the same booking.

LangChain Brings LangSmith BYOC Beta to Azure and Google Cloud

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

LangChain announced on November 3, 2026 that LangSmith Bring Your Own Cloud (BYOC) is now available in beta on Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud, expanding the managed deployment option beyond AWS, where it is generally available.

BYOC keeps sensitive LangSmith data and agent deployments in the customer’s cloud account. The company behind the platform, LangChain, manages the data plane, including orchestration, scaling, and platform updates. It is intended for organizations that want the data residency and network isolation of self-hosted LangSmith without operating the full platform themselves.

AWS was the earlier launch. LangChain’s August 12, 2026 announcement introduced BYOC for enterprises seeking a managed deployment inside their own AWS account and VPC, particularly teams with strict data-governance requirements.

BYOC separates the platform into a LangChain-operated control plane and a customer-owned data plane. The control plane contains authentication, organization and workspace configuration, billing metadata, hashed API keys, and static interface assets. The data plane contains traces, prompts, datasets, experiments, agent deployments, and workspace secrets.

An organization can maintain several data planes in different accounts and regions, with workspaces inside each one. That structure allows separation by region, environment, or business unit. A platform team could run production and staging in separate AWS accounts while a business unit standardized on Azure operates its own deployment.

The model also changes the upgrade burden associated with self-hosted LangSmith. Teams with strict compliance boundaries previously needed platform engineers to patch stateful services, configure databases, run schema migrations, and coordinate custom Helm rollouts. With BYOC, data remains in the customer’s virtual network while LangChain manages scaling, schema updates, and runtime dependencies.

LangChain said AWS customers cited continuous upgrades, faster access to new LangSmith features than the self-hosted release cycle, and automatic scaling as the main benefits.

All three clouds support Managed Deep Agents endpoints in BYOC data planes, but the implementations use each provider’s own networking and security building blocks.

On Azure, the data plane runs in a dedicated virtual network with a private AKS API endpoint. Istio and KEDA are deployed through Helm for ingress routing and event-driven scaling, and system telemetry goes to Azure Monitor. LangChain lists LangSmith Sandboxes support on Azure as coming soon. Google Cloud data planes can run in any region at launch, although they are managed from LangSmith’s US control plane; namespace-scoped resource names are intended to avoid collisions with existing infrastructure.

AWS supports an existing VPC, with the database placed in subnets the customer chooses. Security teams can attach their own security groups to application roles, allowing the data plane to fit existing ingress and egress inspection policies.

On AWS, two PrivateLink connections carry communication between the planes, and LangChain does not reach the customer environment over the public internet. Sensitive-data requests go directly from the browser or client to the customer data plane. The cross-account IAM role can provision and manage infrastructure, but it holds no s3:GetObject permission on the trace bucket, no rds-db:connect permission to PostgreSQL, and no elasticache:Connect permission to Redis.

On AWS, the shared responsibility model leaves cloud-account governance, internal connectivity, access policies, customer-side security monitoring, and compliance controls with the customer. LangChain operates and updates managed components. Incident troubleshooting may require customer-approved break-glass access, with a post-access summary covering timestamps, actions taken, and any data accessed.

For organizations moving from self-hosted LangSmith, the open-source migration tool copies users and roles, datasets, experiments, prompts, annotation-queue configuration, automation rules, dashboards, and Fleet resources. It copies rather than moves data, leaving the source instance unchanged and live. The migration documentation also leaves manual work: deployed agents must be redeployed, Insights reports do not transfer between instances, and Fleet migrations require re-entering secret values and reconnecting per-user OAuth connections.

Traces, runs, and feedback follow a separate process operated by LangChain with internal tooling. Trace migration is supported on AWS, while the open-source tool does not migrate traces.

The Terraform modules behind BYOC and the migration tool are public. Five Ways North Korea Breaks Into Crypto Firms—and How Binance Was Hacked

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Five Ways North Korea Breaks Into Crypto Firms—and How Binance Was Hacked

Hackers from North Korea are notoriously effective at stealing crypto, with one estimate attributing 60% of all losses last year to DPRK-linked groups. These hackers enlist several different vectors of attack, making it all the more difficult to spot them in the act.

Last week, it took just 20 minutes for alleged North Korean hackers to drain $6.3 billion from Binance, in what is the biggest crypto hack on record. The attackers compromised a Binance engineer's work computer through a fake job interview, used an internal AI tool to gain control of other engineers' computers, and then used stolen credentials to hijack an internal wallet signing service, the exchange said.

In the past, North Korean hackers have been known to get hired at crypto companies under fake identities, attack through third-party infrastructure, and poison widely used code libraries. More recently, they've started using AI to make all of it more convincing.

Here are some examples of the methods that North Korean hackers are known to use in crypto attacks.

Binance chief security officer Jimmy Su told Decrypt in August 2025 that the exchange throws out suspected North Korean resumes every day, and that "almost all the large DPRK hacks," at the time, had involved a fake employee helping facilitate the attack.

Su said there were tell-tale signs in resumes that indicate the applicant was from North Korea, but the exchange was not willing to reveal the specifics on the red flags. Sometimes, though, malicious actors get through to the interview stage, where the attackers use deepfakes to change their face and voice.

"The only real good detection is that they almost always have a slow internet connection," he told Decrypt. "What's happening is that the translation and the voice changer are working during the call … that's why they are always delayed."

Those who do get hired tend to be suspiciously good at the job. Su said North Korean workers are often among a company's top performers, likely because several people may be sharing one role across time zones.

The threat has not let up. Binance said its security team detected and prevented 57 suspected North Korea-linked cyber threat cases in 2026, using techniques ranging from fraudulent job interviews to fake video conferencing apps.

Attackers also flip the scam around and pose as recruiters to target people who already work at high-profile crypto companies. Often, the attacker will claim there's a video or audio issue during the interview and send the victim a link to update Zoom, which infects their device with malware

"The DPRK group will [also] try to schedule calls with the external-facing employees," Su told Decrypt. "Either as a DeFi project or investment firm. Worst yet, they'll be recruiting them for a high-level job, paying twice, three times as much, just to get them onto an interview."

This is how the Binance attack began, according to the exchange. In September, a Binance software engineer was approached through a legitimate job-hunting platform by someone posing as a project director, who set up two interviews. In the first, a purported HR representative built trust over a live video call by sharing a social media account full of personal photos and videos, an approach Binance called "more advanced and deceptive than previously seen."

In the second, held on a chat app, the engineer was told to download an "assignment" onto his work computer. The code repository contained previously unknown malware that ran during setup, swapped a legitimate developer tool for a malicious version and connected to an attacker-controlled server, evading Binance's security tools.

One North Korea-linked group infected at least 30,000 devices in more than 100 countries between roughly December 2025 and July 2026 using similar methods. It took funds or credentials from more than 7,000 crypto wallets and moved around $10.71 million to North Korea.

The group, known as WaterPlum or Contagious Interview, posed as AI, crypto, or NFT companies and set candidates a technical interview or coding task that required downloading malware-filled files. Some used AI face-swapping software during interviews before cutting their video and asking the candidate to do the same, blaming a bad connection.

Binance's Su also told Decrypt that North Korean hackers poison public code libraries, ranking it as the second most common method of attack—behind fake employees.

Node Package Manager (NPM) packages are collections of reusable, open-source code that developers frequently use. Attackers can clone these packages and slip in a small line of malicious code while keeping its original function. That means that a victim could use this poisoned code library and not notice, with the malicious code embedding itself deeper over time as more is built on top of it.

In September 2025, software security firm ReversingLabs found two NPM packages that used Ethereum smart contracts to download malware, which the firm said it hadn't seen before. The packages were tied to a wider network of GitHub repositories, mostly branded as crypto trading bots, with thousands of commits and stars that ReversingLabs thinks were mostly faked.

The tactic formed part of a “sophisticated campaign” of malicious actors attempting to hack users via poisoned blockchain-related public code libraries, a vector of attack that Binance has previously attributed to North Korean hackers.

To counter the threat, Su said, Binance makes employees go through NPM libraries with a fine-tooth comb.

Tampered developer tools also played a part in the Binance hack. In June, a member of Binance's wallet DevOps team downloaded a tampered installation of Homebrew, a popular software installer, while trying to install an AI coding tool he had found through a search engine, the exchange said. Its security team caught the problem, replaced the device and rotated his credentials, but the incident exposed the engineer and his colleagues on the wallet infrastructure team to the attackers.

Before Binance, the unwanted record for the largest crypto hack belonged to Bybit, which lost $1.4 billion in February 2025. Cybersecurity firms revealed that North Korean hackers pulled off the attack by planting malicious code into the infrastructure of Safe—a crypto wallet provider used by the exchange.

The security firms said malicious JavaScript code was directly injected into Safe's online infrastructure. Though, it was unclear how the attackers managed to do this.

Bybit's infrastructure "was not compromised," the exchange told Decrypt, and the hack came purely because a third party was exploited.

The Binance attack targeted the exchange's own systems. On October 29, the attacker used stolen credentials to deploy a container and gain unauthorized access to an internal wallet signing service, which let it initiate transactions outside Binance's normal withdrawal process. On Ethereum, it used the signing service to exploit EIP-7702, a mechanism that lets an account delegate execution rights to a smart contract, giving an attacker-controlled contract partial control over a Binance hot wallet.

Those on-chain authorizations kept working even after Binance suspended withdrawals. Because the hot wallet used multi-party computation, there was no single private key Binance could use to revoke them quickly, the exchange said.

Every single one of the above mentioned attack vectors can be turbo-charged with artificial intelligence tools.

Early this year, Google warned of an AI-powered malware campaign targeting crypto companies coming from North Korea. Similarly, Binance's Su also mentioned that AI tools can be used to increase the effectiveness of attacks.

Concerns around AI-powered hacks have risen in recent months, with Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin saying in October that it's "plausible" AI could break encryption within the next two years. It came amid an explosion in AI-driven maths, with OpenAI claiming to have cracked a 90-year-old maths problem in a matter of days. Experts urged the industry to prepare for AI-powered attacks on encryption.

There are no confirmed reports of North Korean hackers using AI to break encryption. But given how quickly they've adopted AI elsewhere, the industry may have to prepare for that next.

5 Best Movies Like The Love Hypothesis

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

There was never any doubt that Lili Reinhart's rom-com "The Love Hypothesis" would become a big hit for Prime Video. Streaming platforms have struck gold by taking romance books and turning them into comforting crowd-pleasers for the small screen. Sometimes this results in films as vapid as "My Fault: London," but often it results in charming movies that take allow us to indulge romantic fantasies via two hot people falling in love.

That's exactly what we got when "The Love Hypothesis" transformed from "Star Wars" fan fiction, of all things, into a novel and then a streaming movie. Ali Hazelwood's book about a biology PhD student who pretends to date her professor, only for the two to fall deeply in love became a hit, and was promptly given a film adaptation in which Reinhart plays the student, Olive Smith, while British actor Tom Bateman plays the ornery professor, Dr. Adam Carlsen.

There were two main tropes to both the book and the film: mismatched lovers who cross a boundary to be together, and the good old fake relationship that becomes very real. Of course, this is far from the first time these tropes have been used in romance movies, which is why it's actually quite easy to recommend films just like "The Love Hypothesis." While there are plenty of golden-age romance movies that used these plot devices (1941's "Come Live with Me" saw James Stewart and Hedy Lamarr fake a marriage only to fall head over heels), we're betting that fans of Reinhart's movie will want more modern examples. With that in mind, here's a list of the best movies like "The Love Hypothesis."

"My Oxford Year" may have a controversial ending but it's got everything you could want from a modern-day streaming flick based on a book. Melodrama and fairytale romance combine in this Netflix movie, which stars Sofia Carson (who's fast becoming the streamer's go-to romantic drama leading lady) as American exchange student Anna De La Vega. Before descending into a life of corporate drudgery, Anna decides to study Victorian poetry at the University of Oxford for a year. Wouldn't you know it, once there she meets an unreasonably charming and handsome English DPhil student and tutor named Jamie Davenport (Corey Mylchreest).

"My Oxford Year" shares much in common with "The Love Hypothesis." Not only are both based on books (though the former actually began as a screenplay before it was adapted into a novel), they both feature a student/teacher relationship that gets off to a rough start only to flourish into a modern-day fairytale. Both also feature strapping English leading men (though Bateman plays an American in "The Love Hypothesis") who start out as jerks only to reveal a tender side as things progress.

It is worth noting that there's a tragic element to "My Oxford Year," so if you're looking for something that wraps up as neatly as "The Love Hypothesis," this might not be the ideal watch. But otherwise fans of Lily Reinhart's movie should fall madly in love with Carson's alternative.

These streaming movies are all well and good but there has to be at least one undisputed classic on this list. There's no better example of the fake relationship trope than this 1990 romantic comedy in which we got to see prime-era Richard Gere and Julia Roberts fall for one another. The movie did what "The Love Hypothesis" and its fellow streaming-age rom-coms did decades before Netflix was even a thing, bringing together impossibly beautiful stars via a fake relationship that blossoms into the real thing.

If you don't know the plot of what might just be Julia Roberts' best movie, I'm not sure what to tell ya. He's a corporate raider, she's a sex worker he asks for directions. So begins a love affair that sees Gere's Edward Lewis paying Roberts' Vivian Ward to spend a week with him and act as his girlfriend. Needless to say, this confected relationship becomes a real one. Ward melts Lewis' cold corporate heart and Lewis draws out the real Ward from behind a wall of dissociation built up over her time spent as a sex worker.

If you're somehow yet to see the film, and are worried the tale of a millionaire corporate raider falling for a sex worker might not have the wholesome charm of "The Love Hypothesis," don't worry. There's a reason Roger Ebert called the film "the sweetest and most openhearted love fable since 'The Princess Bride.'" "Pretty Woman" is as romantic a comedy as they come. But beyond that, it somehow manages to feel grounded thanks to its multi dimensional characters, without compromising the fairytale romance aspect.

Sofia Carson will soon have a monopoly on streaming romance movies. Her 2025 romantic drama "The Life List" dominated Netflix just prior to the similarly successful release of "My Oxford Year." But before either of these movies, Carson made her Netflix romance debut with the 2022 film "Purple Hearts." Guess what — the whole thing was based on a book, Tess Wakefield's 2017 novel of the same name.

In "Purple Hearts" we get a modern day version of James Stewart's "Come Live with Me." This Carson-led love story sees the former Disney Channel star play diabetic waitress and musician Cassie Salazar, who's working herself ragged just to afford insulin. Her salvation arrives in the form of hunky Marine Luke Morrow (Nicholas Galitzine), who agrees to marry Cassie so she can gain health coverage and he can benefit from a pay bump. You can see where this is going.

Much like with "The Love Hypothesis," this is a story about a couple who agree to act as though they're together only to develop real feelings along the way. There's plenty of BookTok-style melodrama on display here, too, which should please anyone who enjoyed the tension of the third act fall-out in "The Love Hypothesis."

It's the film that made Glen Powell a star and proved Sydney Sweeney could make popular stuff outside of "Euphoria." If your favorite thing about "The Love Hypothesis" was watching two attractive people make out, then "Anyone But You" should be right up your alley. In fact, "Anyone But You" is arguably the apotheosis of the modern day two-hot-actors-fall-in-love rom-com. But there's a stronger connection between this film and "The Love Hypothesis."

Lily Reinhart's Olive Smith and Tom Bateman's Dr. Adam Carlsen agreed to fake a relationship for mutually beneficial reasons, and the same happens with Sweeney's Beatrice Messina and Powell's Ben. The duo pretend to be a couple so that Bea's parents will stop trying to repair her relationship with her ex, and Ben can make his own ex jealous. We all knew where it was going from the very beginning but that didn't make it any less enjoyable to watch.

Unlike with "The Love Hypothesis," the guy in this romantic comedy isn't a complete jerk. Full of himself, maybe, but not an anti-social hermit like Bateman's "Dr. Evil." Still, he and Sweeney's law student don't exactly get along, especially after their first meeting ended on awkward terms. That, alongside the fake relationship trope, gives Powell and Sweeney's couple a similar dynamic to that of Olive and Adam in "The Love Hypothesis." So if you somehow missed the unlikely box office smash that was "Anyone But You" back in 2024, now's the time to remedy that.

In 2018, "To All the Boys I've Loved Before" became the new gold standard for teen rom-coms. It also happens to be a nice little companion to "The Love Hypothesis." Based on a book? Check. Fake relationship trope? Check. Academic setting? Sort of check. Rather than college, this tale of teen love, adapted from Jenny Han's YA novel, plays out in high school, where Lara Jean Covey (Lana Condor) writes secret letters to her crushes. Of course, she never intends for any of them to read her missives, but guess what — that's exactly what happens.

Lara Jean's biggest crush is Josh Sanderson (Israel Broussard), and when she sees him approaching with his letter in hand, she kisses another one of the letter recipients, Peter Kavinsky (Noah Centineo) in an attempt to throw Josh off. Soon, Peter and Lara Jean are fake dating. This allows the former to distract from the embarrassment of Josh receiving a letter, and the latter to make his ex-girlfriend Gen (Emilija Baranac) jealous. But you know how this goes. Real romance develops, melodrama ensues, feelings get hurt.

"To All the Boys I've Loved Before" is, however, more than your standard BookTook phenomenon turned Netflix romance movie. The film is surprisingly well shot, retains a heartfelt sensibility throughout, and isn't afraid to embrace its classic fake relationship rom-com influences. It's a touching little high school melodrama that's well worth a watch for anyone who even remotely enjoyed "The Love Hypothesis."

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Oxford spin-out says its optical chips can cut AI power use sharply. The lab will check that.

ARIA’s Scaling Inference Lab will deploy Lumai’s Iris servers, which use beams of light to run AI calculations, as its first optical platform. Lumai claims big energy savings, but these have not been independently tested. The lab will test the servers in a commercial data centre.

The UK’s Advanced Research and Invention Agency (ARIA) will test servers that use light to run AI models, in a lab it funds, Oxford startup Lumai said on Tuesday.

The Scaling Inference Lab, backed by £50M from ARIA and run by the nonprofit CommonAI, will install Lumai’s Iris servers as its first optical computing platform. It will run them across several machines in a commercial data centre, to see how they cope with real AI models, power and cooling.

Lumai, spun out of Oxford University in 2021, encodes data into beams of light. Its chips do the heavy maths behind AI using light rather than switching transistors, while normal electronics handle the rest. Salience Labs, which also builds on Oxford research, raised $125M last month for optical switches.

The company aims its hardware at “prefill”, the stage where a model first reads a user’s prompt. Ordinary chips would still produce the answer.

“Lumai’s technology is the most mature optical compute platform we evaluated,” said David Lewis, ARIA’s chief science and technology officer.

Lumai claims its approach could cut data centre power use by up to 90%. It also says the system is at least 16 times more energy efficient per request than silicon alone, when paired with standard chips. None of these figures has been independently checked, and testing them is the lab’s job.

The lab already hosts UK startup Oriole, which uses light to link AI chips. Nvidia, meanwhile, has spent billions on light-based connections inside data centres. Another UK inference-chip startup, Fractile, is raising at $6.5B.
