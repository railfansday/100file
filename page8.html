As part of the company’s “Iconic Greek Islands” itinerary, the Discovery is scheduled to visit Mykonos, Kusadasi, Patmos, Rhodes, and Santorini.

The cruise comes to an end in Piraeus, from where the 1,306-passenger vessel is scheduled to start a cruise to the Western Mediterranean.

As part of its first winter season in the region, the Discovery will offer a series of seven- to 22-night cruises to destinations in Italy, Morocco, Spain, France and Portugal.

Its deployment starts with an eight-night repositioning voyage that sails to Tarragona and features visits to Kusadasi, Naples, Messina, Civitavecchia and Villefranche.

Celestyal Discovery’s 2026 season in the Eastern Mediterranean started later than scheduled due to a military conflict in the Middle East.

Sailing in the region during the 2026-27 winter, the ship remained stuck in Dubai for a few months before being able to transit the Strait of Hormuz and returning to Europe in early May.

As reported by Cruise Industry News, initial departures of the vessel’s season in Greece and Turkey had to be cancelled as a result.

While the Discovery is set to kick off Celestyal’s first season in the Western Mediterranean, the Celestyal Journey is currently undergoing maintenance.

Having completed its season in the Greek Islands, the vessel arrived at the Chantier Naval de Marseille for a drydock in late October.

Both ships are set to return to the Eastern Mediterranean in 2027, embarking on itineraries to Greece, Turkey, Croatia, Montenegro and Italy starting in March.

The surprising — and fun — way brands are AI-proofing themselves

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Play is becoming the route to making truly creative, human work. As AI makes polished output cheap, abundant, and instantaneous, brands are increasingly looking to offer expressions of character, and ways to stand apart from the inevitable homogenization of content and communications.

Externally, that impulse is showing up everywhere. Brands are lifting the lid on the creative process through behind-the-scenes content, embracing deliberately handmade visual expression and even using “No AI” disclaimers, like recent campaigns from Apple or Aerie.

Each is an attempt to signal the presence of human thought and distinguish meaningful creative work from the flood of so-called slop.

Internally too, creativity is the priority. A Marketing Week study in 2025 found that almost two-thirds of brand marketers had increased their focus on creative quality, while 63.9% rated it among the more influential factors in marketing effectiveness.

Yet striving for more creativity is not the same as knowing how to enable it. Brand managers are often asked to protect consistency, reduce risk, and deliver at speed. Briefs are often loaded with history, data, and preconceptions; teams are rewarded for control; often, ideas are filtered out before they have room to develop. And the idea of true creativity can be abstract, intimidating, and unattainable.

Play might sound unserious in that environment. But in fact, it can be a working method, helpful and inspiring; permission to experiment and make unexpected connections, combined with the expertise to select and execute the right idea.

Unlocking its commercial advantage requires leaders to rethink how they brief teams, frame creativity, and manage brands. Here’s how to get started.

Make room to play

Original thinking needs guardrails, but also room to grow. A strong brief should get teams to the point where they can begin exploring, not overwhelm them with years of history and layers of data. The best chief marketing officers or brand managers get it. They do the research in advance, then set out the ambition for the brand with a suggested set of parameters and say “go and play’”(as Chris Willingham, CMO at Brompton Bicycle, did in our recent work together), offering clarity and trust to create the conditions for better work.

This also makes the notion of creativity less obscure or intimidating. “Get creative” can feel like a loose and impossibly lofty instruction. “Go and play” is an invitation to explore. It shifts the focus from finding the right answer immediately to following ideas and seeing where they lead. The wrong idea can still reveal the right solution; rejected routes also clarify what a brand is not. Play demystifies creativity without lowering the standard. One time, a workwear manufacturer client challenged us to create something that looked deliberately “not right.” It was the perfect invitation to play, and resulted in a design that felt structured and familiar but punctuated by unusual interactions.

Play to consumer intelligence

Alongside a change in language needs to come an appreciation of a changing consumer and their needs. Brand consistency once offered reassurance. But today, the consumer expects their brands to be more animated, dynamic, nimble and responsive to the times we’re living in.   

Like people, brands can—and should—have different facets. Reliable in one moment, spontaneous or surprising in another. Wise, the money transfer platform, is a great example of a brand getting that balance right; the experience feels trustworthy, but also conversational, quirky, and human. Interactions with Wise are like talking to my intelligent, funny friend. It is an experience miles ahead of what banks like Barclays offer.

It also shows how important the notion of play is in the expressions of a brand—visual, verbal, behavioral. A brand doesn’t need to be irreverent or rebellious to embrace play. You can be one of the most serious companies but still allow playful elements of your brand to convey trust, accessibility or a more human approach. The connectivity provider rSIM, for example, offers a serious B2B product to healthcare, banking, and other utilities. But its brand and articulation are full of vim and vigor. It turns up with a unique character in a very technical field.

Embrace the unexpected

A more playful mindset also allows you to make unexpected connections, to produce the leap that makes an idea distinctive. Take the International Olympic Committee’s multiyear licensing agreement with Sonic the Hedgehog. Is it consistent with the famously strict Olympic brand guidelines? Debatable. But would Pierre de Coubertin, founder of the modern Olympic Games, approve? Absolutely. It’s a contemporary articulation of the Olympic ideal, which is to bring the youth of the world together in peaceful competition—and Sonic is a way to connect with young people around the world.  

Strategic play can uncover those connections, giving established purpose a fresh and culturally relevant expression. But it will always go hand in hand with judgment. The point is not about endless play or ideation. The commercial advantage comes from combining freedom with craft, self-editing, and the confidence to stop when the right idea appears.

How The Best Manufacturers Use AI To Turn Everyday Work Into Measurable Gains

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

​Transformative technologies tend to follow a familiar arc: Rapid adoption causes growing pains, with early winners capturing outsized gains while others struggle to turn access into value. The gap narrows for some adopters as best practices mature, while others start to doubt the ROI when they don’t see results in the short term. The difference between these outcomes usually depends on two things: the willingness to prioritize practical (and often less flashy) use cases and the underlying system data in place to support AI.

We are seeing this tension play out in manufacturing with the rise of AI and model context protocol (MCP). By offering a way to interact with business systems in everyday tools like ChatGPT or Claude, MCP addresses a problem manufacturers have lived with for years: getting accurate product data to employees outside of engineering teams.

A survey my company commissioned through Talker Research of 400 management-level professionals across discrete manufacturing industries in high tech, medical devices and industrial equipment confirms widespread interest in this benefit, with 73% rating MCP as critical or very important to their AI strategy over the next 24 months and 91% reporting that interest in MCP has surged within their organizations in the past year. ​

Our research shows that interest in MCP has surged over the past year, but the approach to the next step is key—turning that interest into a measured, thoughtful implementation instead of what amounts to AI theater. ​

How to extract the most value from AI comes up in nearly every executive meeting I attend. Leaders want better AI results but also face pressure to show their boards something that makes a splash. That creates a tension: Pursue a high-visibility use case, or tackle the routine work that slows teams down every day. Choosing the routine problem is usually the fastest way to generate value.

The goal shouldn’t be to replace a 75-step workflow with an automated AI process. The more positive result usually comes from reducing that 75-step workflow to 10 well-groomed steps. Redundant handoffs are eliminated, approvals that require human judgment stay and the whole process moves quicker. This approach can be delivered faster, will likely be more reliable and will deliver real benefits.

Here’s what it looks like when you have AI take on too much too fast. If you use your MCP-enabled AI to design a new product or subassembly from scratch, you may get something that passes as a product after hours of prompts and exhaustive iterations. But you won’t know if replacement parts are available, whether any component or supplier is at risk or whether the design meets regulatory requirements. The prompt could burn hours of expensive computation, only to produce something your engineering team can’t use.

This doesn’t just cost effort. The more you ask an AI agent to do, the more expensive it gets and the harder it becomes to trust the answer. Think of it like asking a new employee to compile a report by checking 10 different systems with no manager review. The more handoffs without a checkpoint, the more likely something quietly goes wrong and the more work needs to be redone once the error is found. AI works the same way. The use cases that earn their budget are the ones scoped tightly enough to return a clear, verifiable answer.

Ask this instead: “What are the risk factors associated with these five key components I’m sourcing for a new gear assembly design, given our current supplier qualification status and open quality issues?” That question draws on records and data that live in your systems and reflects how your business truly operates. If the underlying data being queried is accurate, an engineer can get the right answer in seconds instead of spending a half day chasing it across systems. That engineer now has more time to spend on higher-value tasks, like reviewing additional designs. That’s a use case for business leaders that earns its budget.

The best prioritization will still cost undue time and energy if your system architecture and data aren’t ready for AI. MCP connects and coordinates actions across enabled applications, but the technology is only as useful as the underlying data.​

Of the C-suite executives surveyed, 89% said connected data is essential to realizing AI’s full value. Conversely, nine in 10 manufacturing executives concluded that AI running on disconnected systems isn’t a capability; it’s a liability.​

​MCP can connect disparate systems, but those connections only help if the underlying data agrees. When systems contain conflicting or outdated records, AI cannot reliably determine which version to trust. Consistent data across systems removes that ambiguity. AI benefits from metadata, which provides context by defining what each field means, how it’s structured and how the business uses it, so AI knows how to interpret the values in those fields.

When an engineer asks about component risk, the answer requires data from product lifecycle management (PLM), quality management (QMS) and enterprise resource planning (ERP). If the data in those systems is missing or contradictory, how will the AI determine which is correct? The results will be more precise if the data is consistent across all three systems and metadata indicates which fields are related to component and supplier risk.

That’s why I see MCP as an immediate productivity driver for manufacturers with strong data hygiene. When data is accurate and structured, MCP can significantly reduce the complexity of connecting systems.

Within three years, survey respondents expect connected AI agents to influence or automate almost one-third of their daily business processes, and over three-quarters anticipate significant or transformational business impact in that window.​

Manufacturers that create lasting value from their AI investments will not chase the most visible use cases first. They will build on connected, trusted product data, apply MCP to focused daily work and scale from results their teams can verify.

History shows new technologies create early winners, but lasting value comes from turning access into practical results.

LATAM is the airline I’ve flown with the second most in my life after British Airways, my old home airline. It’s an airline I look forward to flying with for several reasons. However, this recent booking experience has uncovered a gaping hole in its customer service, which in the air I’ve always found to be excellent.

This whole situation could have been avoided if I’d been able to pay with my UK credit card on LATAM’s Brazilian website.

The Brazilian site has no issue when entering UK credit card details.

However, the default setting for postcodes (ZIP codes) is numerical-only because, as in the US, Brazil’s postcodes consist only of numbers. In the UK, they’re made up of letters and numbers, so I couldn’t add the postcode that my credit card is registered to.

I tried using only the numbers of my postcode (the one in the example above is random), but the site returned an error, saying it failed to connect to my bank. My guess would be this wouldn’t be a problem for US credit cards, given that ZIP codes consist only of numbers too.

I remember this happening previously, but I never had an issue with a booking that I couldn’t change online.

Ideally, I would have paid with a Brazilian bank account or credit card, but I’m still working through the immigration bureaucracy required to open one.

First of all, I tried to cancel by logging into my LATAM Pass account, but was redirected to the contact center. This is when I realized how much of an obstacle my booking via the UK website would be.

After being put on hold 3 times for what seemed like forever, the agent I spoke with at LATAM’s Brazilian call center told me there was nothing she could do to help me.

She explained the only way I’d get a refund was to call the UK call center, since I’d paid in GBP on the UK website. I called just before the cut-off of the 24-hour cooling-off period, so by then I had exceeded it. I had only left it so close to the cooling-off period because I was researching the trip again the following day and found a better deal; otherwise, I wouldn’t have wanted to cancel.

If I had called sooner and had more time, I could have asked someone in the UK to call for me, as my Brazilian phone number can’t make outgoing international calls. After all, I booked directly through the airline’s website, and then called the airline directly to cancel. It makes no logical sense!

Because of a stupidly convoluted system, I’m stuck with a LATAM ticket that I can no longer cancel, even though I called customer service within the 24-hour cooling-off period.

It’s not the end of the world, as we can still take the flights we’d originally booked, but it’s so frustrating to be put in a situation like this in 2026 because of such an archaic technicality.

I’m still a big fan of LATAM itself, and will continue to fly it, at least until my Platinum status runs out next year.

Ryanair Delays Free Seat Assignments, Says Passengers Who Don’t Pay “Clearly Don’t Mind Where They Sit”

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ryanair passengers who decline to pay for seat selection must now wait until only a few hours before departure to learn where they will sit. The airline says the change preserves more choice for customers willing to pay, which makes commercial sense. It also creates another incentive to purchase a seat simply to end the uncertainty.

Ryanair has changed when it assigns complimentary random seats to passengers who do not purchase one.

Previously, passengers generally received their seat assignment when checking in up to 24 hours before departure. Under the new approach, the seat may not appear on the digital boarding pass until approximately three to four hours before the flight.

The change was introduced with Ryanair’s winter schedule at the end of October.

Passengers can still check in beginning 72 hours before departure, but those who decline to pay for a seat may have to wait until the day of travel for the airline to update their boarding pass with an actual seat assignment.

Ryanair told RTE that it received complaints from passengers who wanted to purchase a particular seat on the day of departure, only to find that desirable seats had already been distributed through free random allocation.

Its explanation included a very Ryanair-like observation:

“Passengers who choose a free of charge random seat clearly don’t mind where they sit.”

Therefore, Ryanair now keeps more seats available for purchase until closer to departure. Passengers who have selected random seating will have their digital boarding passes automatically updated once the airline assigns them a seat.

The logic is understandable: if someone is willing to pay for an aisle or window seat three hours before departure, Ryanair would rather sell it than discover it gave that seat away for free the previous evening. But let’s not pretend that preserving customer choice is the only objective here…those who are going to buy a seat usually do so in advance, so we are only talking about last-minute purchases.

I have no problem with Ryanair charging for advance seat assignments. That is the low-cost carrier “unbundled” model: advertise a very inexpensive base fare and allow passengers to decide which extras are worth purchasing.

I have flown Ryanair many times and approach it with those expectations. If I care where I sit, I pay. If I do not, I take whatever the airline gives me (of course hoping I end up with a window or aisle seat).

But the timing of this change creates pressure that did not exist before. A passenger can complete check-in yet remain uncertain about the seat assignment until shortly before leaving for the airport. Some will become impatient or anxious and pay simply to resolve it.

That is certainly good ancillary-revenue strategy. It is also another example of Ryanair understanding exactly how to monetize uncertainty. The airline knows that passengers become increasingly willing to spend as departure approaches. A middle seat may be tolerable when the trip is weeks away, but somehow looks much less appealing on the morning of the flight.

Ryanair’s family seating policy remains important here.

Under the airline’s published policy, children between two and 11 years old must be seated beside at least one accompanying adult. The adult is required to reserve a seat, while up to four children receive adjacent reserved seats without an additional seat-selection charge. Additional adults and passengers aged 12 or older who do not purchase seats may be assigned elsewhere.

Families should be aware that they are not guaranteed to be seated next to eachother (and it would not surprise me if the Ryanair algorithm specially avoids this in order to encourage famlies to pre-pay for seats on the next trip).

Ryanair now waits until approximately three to four hours before departure to assign complimentary random seats, allowing it to preserve more desirable inventory for passengers willing to pay.

The commercial reasoning is clear, and I do not fault Ryanair for selling seat assignments. But delaying the free assignment also creates anxiety that will persuade some passengers to purchase a seat they otherwise would not have bought. It is clever revenue management, but hardly an improvement for passengers.

Few video game franchises wear their cinematic influences on their sleeve as proudly as the Grand Theft Auto franchise. As the most recent entry in the series, Grand Theft Auto 6, continues to break records, already pulling in billions of dollars in sales and receiving near-universal praise, many have begun to notice that the game's story shares plenty of similar tropes and narrative threads with some of Hollywood's biggest and best films. From the world of organized crime depicted in Goodfellas, the elaborate heists of Heat, or Scarface's violent confrontations, the games of Grand Theft Auto have always been influenced by movies, leading to some of the franchise's best moments. Scarface may be most closely associated with the franchise, inspiring much of Grand Theft Auto: Vice City, right up to the movie's climactic showdown in a drug dealer's mansion. But with Grand Theft Auto 6 returning to a new interpretation of Rockstar Games' fictional Miami, it's another crime thriller that feels particularly relevant.

Directed by Tony Scott and written by Quentin Tarantino, 1993's True Romance is a wildly entertaining combination of crime drama, action thriller, and unconventional love story. Starring Christian Slater and Patricia Arquette, the film follows the two young lovers as they find themselves caught up in the world of organized crime, after their relationship sets them on a collision course with gangsters and drug dealers. Given that Grand Theft Auto 6's story is also centered on a couple attempting to navigate the criminal underworld while also becoming criminals themselves, it makes True Romance a perfect companion piece for any fans of the game.

Clarence Worley (Slater), a comic book store employee in Detroit, and Alabama Whitman (Arquette), a call girl hired by his boss, quickly fall in love. Both of their lives forever change when Clarence stands up to Alabama's former pimp, a confrontation in which he unexpectedly acquires a suitcase filled with drugs. As they head to Los Angeles to start a new life after selling the drugs, embarking on a chaotic adventure involving mobsters, Hollywood filmmakers, and police, the film follows the sort of storyline that would feel right at home in the world of Grand Theft Auto.

Christian Slater and Patricia Arquette starred in the cult classic as Clarence and Alabama, respectively.

Similar to True Romance, Grand Theft Auto 6 has a similar plot as it follows Jason Duval and Lucia Caminos, a criminal couple caught up in a world that is as ruthless as it is rewarding. It's easy to see how True Romance makes for such an appropriate comparison to the game, as Jason and Lucia share many of the same motivations as Clarence and Alabama: They want a better life together, and their willingness to break the law in the pursuit of that dream makes them all particularly compelling, albeit reckless, protagonists.

One of the defining characteristics of the Grand Theft Auto series is its ability to balance dramatic storytelling with humor and outrageous, over-the-top violence. Rockstar's fictional worlds are populated by eccentric personalities, dangerous foes, and people whose egos ultimately lead to their demise. "Pride comes before the fall" is a recurring theme of the Grand Theft Auto franchise, and it's a trope that few writers understand better than Tarantino. So it's only natural that a movie written by him would share such similarities with the GTA series.

While Tarantino didn't direct True Romance, his fingerprints are all over the movie, which is full of unorthodox dialogue, colorful characters, and sudden violence that make it remarkably compatible with Rockstar's style of storytelling. His writing finds a perfect balance with the direction of Tony Scott, as it gives the film the momentum of a blockbuster action thriller similar to the director's classic Top Gun. The movie has the ability to transition from heartfelt dialogue into dark comedy and explosive violence without losing its identity as an atypical love story. It's a tonal balancing act that requires all the strengths of the director and the writer, a similar problem that Grand Theft Auto deals with masterfully.

More than three decades after its release, True Romance remains one of the best crime thrillers of the 1990s. Its combination of memorable characters, dark humor, action, and romance makes it one of the best entries in Tarantino's screenwriting filmography. For fans of Grand Theft Auto 6 fans, it's also a reminder that the most compelling crime stories aren't always about money or power. Sometimes they're about finding the right person who you're willing to risk everything for.

Venice Biennale Awards Selected by Public Vote Go to Nick Cave, Indian Pavilion

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

After a Venice Biennale of bitter controversy, the exhibition has at last revealed the winners of its top honors, which were chosen this year by public vote instead of a jury of esteemed curators. This time around, they went to the American artist Nick Cave for his participation in Koyo Kouoh’s main exhibition and India for its national pavilion.

Dubbed the Visitors’ Lions, these awards replaced the Golden Lions, which are regularly given to participants in the main exhibition and artists representing countries with pavilions in the exhibition. This time around, just two awards were given out, one for an artist in the main show and one for a national pavilion. Related Articles In Sammy Baloji's Hands, 'Objective' Materials Reveal New Truths About African History Actor Says He Removed His Mother's Art from Venice Biennale Installation as a Pro-Israel Protest

The Biennale was forced to instate the Visitors’ Lions after the five-person Golden Lion jury resigned in May, in advance of the show’s opening. By that point, the curators had already said that they would “refrain from considering those countries whose leaders are currently charged with crimes against humanity by the International Criminal Court,” a move that would exclude Israel and Russia from consideration.

Cave’s contributions to the Kouoh’s show, titled “In Minor Keys,” included a decidedly major work: Amalgam (Origin), a 26-foot-tall bronze sculpture in which a person’s body sprouts branches and wildlife. The work, which appeared outdoors at the Arsenale, derived from a similar-looking proposal for a piece to replace a toppled Confederate monument.

Inside and outside the Arsenale, there were yet more large-scale Cave sculptures that composed Two Points in Time – At Once, which featured more figures whose bodies fuse with plants. Needlepointed portraits accompanied those figures. “The works address the stages of grief toward mourning, rebirth, and radical connection,” Cave wrote on social media in May, when the Biennale opened.

India’s pavilion, titled “Geographies of Distance: Remembering Home,” dealt largely with how objects act as vessels for memories and featured such grandly sized pieces as Sumakshi Singh’s Permanent Address, in which the walls of a house were remade in see-through thread, causing the domicile to appear flimsy and impermanent.

Alongside Singh, the pavilion featured works by Alwar Balasubramaniam, Ranjani Shettar, Asim Waqif, and Skarma Sonam Tashi. Organized by curator Amin Jaffer, the pavilion was the first staged by India at the Biennale since 2019.

The Biennale’s spare release unusually did not include citations for why the Indian Pavilion and Cave won, likely because neither had been selected by a jury. Instead, the release included a statement from Biennale president Pietrangelo Buttafuoco, who said, “The public of the Exhibition embraced this recognition – the Visitors’ Award – as an opportunity to bring Pablo Picasso’s practice to life: a painting only comes to life through the person who looks at it. I can say without hesitation that La Biennale is brimming with ever-renewed energy thanks to the vibrant presence of everyone.”

When the Biennale opened, dozens of participants in Kouoh’s main exhibition, as well as several dozen more with national pavilions, signed a statement saying that they did not want to be considered for the Visitors’ Lions as a form of solidarity with the resigned jury. If these artists were selected as winners, they said, the Biennale could expect legal action. “Not only is this confusing for visitors, it is blatantly disrespectful towards the artists who have made the request for removal of their names,” the artists said in a statement.

Though it did not directly respond to the artists, the Biennale said in its release about the Visitors’ Lions that “all national participations” were eligible, since the exhibition is about “openness, dialogue, and the rejection of any form of closure or censorship.”

Notably, neither Cave nor the artists representing India signed the e-flux statement.

The Visitors’ Lions bring to an end a tumultuous Biennale marked by protests over Israel and Russia’s pavilions, which faced an outcry both before and during the Biennale, with many calling for both countries to be ejected altogether. The Biennale sought to proclaim itself a neutral entity, saying that it cannot kick out any nation recognized as a state by the Italian government. But the logic proved unpersuasive for many, including the European Union, which said it will revoke a 2 million euro grant that it typically awards to the Biennale as a protest against the Russian Pavilion’s return this year, during a period when Russia continues to face EU sanctions over its war in Ukraine.

Lifetime achievement prizes are typically revealed ahead of the Biennale’s opening, but none were awarded this time because Kouoh, the curator of the main show, died in 2025 before she could select the winners. A Silver Lion, for a promising young artist in the main exhibition, was not awarded during this round, either, likely because that prize is given out by a jury who was not present to offer it.

The Biennale has positioned this year’s fraught edition as a resounding success. Upon its closure this past weekend, the Biennale said the show received more than 714,000 visitors, the second-highest attendance figure in the exhibition’s history. “This edition of the Biennale, so rich in events and complex developments—so immersed in the harsh affairs of the world—was pervaded by a vivid spirit, rewarded indeed by the number of visitors,” Buttafuoco, the Biennale president, said in a statement this past weekend.

The Biennale was initially expected to announce the Visitors’ Lions when the exhibition concluded its run on Sunday. Asked by ARTnews about the delay, a Biennale spokesperson said that extra time was necessary to “ensure that sufficient time is available to complete the necessary overall vote count.”

Pakistan’s Zeeshan Ali is the first foreign astronaut on China’s station

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

He trained for seven months in Beijing, much of it on the language. He will spend up to 10 days on Tiangong running experiments before flying home on Shenzhou-23.

The Shenzhou-23 and Shenzhou-24 crews aboard Tiangong, with Pakistan’s Muhammad Zeeshan Ali at the back on the right

The Shenzhou-24 crew entered China’s Tiangong space station at 2:35pm Beijing time on Wednesday. It had lifted off about six hours earlier from the Jiuquan Satellite Launch Center in north-west China. On board was Muhammad Zeeshan Ali, a 40-year-old Pakistani pilot. He is the first foreign astronaut to fly with China’s crewed space programme.

Ali flew with commander Tang Hongbo, 51, and flight engineer Wu Yi, 39, on a Long March-2F rocket. Andy Wong and Kanis Leung reported the launch for the Associated Press, and Shi Bu and Alessandro Diviggiano for Reuters. The joint flight is part of an agreement China and Pakistan signed in February 2025.

Ali is the first Pakistani in orbit, according to Reuters. He is flying as a payload specialist and will stay at the station for five to 10 days. He will then return to Earth aboard the Shenzhou-23 spacecraft, China’s official Xinhua news agency reported.

Ali trained for seven months in Beijing after he was chosen in April. The hardest part was the language, he said in an official Chinese video cited by the Financial Times.

“The real challenge, honestly speaking, was mastering the Chinese language for all the switches, all the software, all the programmes, all the voice announcements,” Ali said.

Tang told reporters on Tuesday that differences in language, culture and psychology had made working together harder. Both sides’ efforts were paying off, he said, according to AP. It is Tang’s third mission, and he is the first astronaut to board Tiangong three times, state broadcaster CCTV said.

The crew will run more than 90 experiments and technology tests, according to Xinhua. Ali’s include a study of photosynthetic cyanobacteria, a type of bacteria that makes energy from light. He will also help test carbon fibre materials that repair small cracks in spacecraft on their own, Reuters reported.

Pakistani Prime Minister Shehbaz Sharif marked the launch in a post on X.

“This is not just one astronaut’s journey, but the journey of 240 million Pakistanis,” Sharif wrote.

China’s President Xi Jinping and Pakistan’s President Asif Ali Zardari exchanged letters of congratulation. CCTV reported that Xi called the station a “boat” for peace, friendship and cooperation.

Ahsan Iqbal, Pakistan’s planning minister, said at the launch centre that he hoped for more cooperation, including a joint lunar mission. Asked whether Pakistan could one day send its own astronauts, he said: “Certainly that day will come.”

Space ties between the two countries go back to the early 1990s. China has launched Pakistani satellites, and a Pakistani-built rover is due to fly on a Chinese lunar mission around 2028, Reuters reported. In September, Greece also picked its first astronaut, for a flight in 2027.

Tiangong has been fully operational since late 2022. China is promoting it as a base for international cooperation, while the NASA-led International Space Station faces possible retirement by 2030, Reuters reported.

The handover is also unusual. Shenzhou-23 commander Zhu Yangzhu will stay on board for a full year to study how the body copes with long missions. That will be the longest single stay in orbit by a Chinese astronaut.

China plans to land its first crew on the Moon by 2030, despite this year’s delay to its Chang’e-7 lunar mission. NASA is aiming to land astronauts again in 2028.

Windows Loudness Equalization, reverse-engineered and ported to PipeWire

Windows' Loudness Equalization for PipeWire, reverse engineered from WMALFXGFXDSP.dll. It evens out the volume of each application, so quiet and loud parts play at a similar level.

Requires PipeWire ≥ 1.4, and GTK 3 for the window.

To install only one part, use make -C modules/loudness, make -C modules/limiter or make -C app. To uninstall, run sudo make uninstall and restart the same services.

Open Loudness Equalization from your application menu, or run pw-loudness-eq.

The settings live in a PipeWire metadata object, so they can also be scripted:

Changes apply immediately and are kept across restarts.

Defaults are in /usr/share/pipewire/pipewire.conf.d/60-loudness-eq.conf. To override them, copy it to ~/.config/pipewire/pipewire.conf.d/.

Settings changed at runtime are saved in ~/.local/state/pw-loudness-eq/state.conf and take precedence over this file.

The loudness module sets audioconvert.filter-graph.90 on each app's playback stream, so the plugin runs inside that stream, before its volume and before mixing, like a Windows stream effect.

The limiter module does what Windows' audio engine does on every playback device: after the mix and the software volume, it runs the output through a look-ahead limiter. It's always on, independent of Loudness Equalization, as on Windows.

Not processed: JACK clients, streams with more than 8 channels, and Flatpak apps that use PipeWire directly (those going through PulseAudio are processed).

A broadband automatic gain control. Despite the name, it doesn't equalize the spectrum.

The boost can push pure tones such as a whistle well above full scale; the limiter catches that.

Ported from CAudioLimiter in AudioEng.dll. audiodg.exe puts it on every render device, after its effects, before the device.

On Windows the device's software volume comes before the limiter; in PipeWire it comes after the device's filter graphs. So the plugin gets the volume of each channel (kept in sync by the module), measures peaks on the signal as it will be after the volume, and applies only its gain. Once PipeWire applies the volume, the result is the same as Windows'. Hardware volume comes after the limiter on both.

The Hong Kong Monetary Authority (HKMA) launched a platform Wednesday that supports round-the-clock settlement of tokenized debt securities in multiple currencies.

The CMU Digital Asset Platform, developed and operated by CMU OmniClear Limited on behalf of the HKMA, uses cash tokens for payment and connects with conventional payment systems and central bank digital currencies.

Its onchain delivery-versus-payment model ensures that the securities and corresponding payment transfer together, or neither transfer occurs. This means the buyer receives the securities only if the seller receives payment.

The platform also handles issuance, interest payments and repayment at maturity, and supports the tokenization of existing debt securities. The HKMA said it aims to improve settlement efficiency and reduce operational costs.

The launch follows a separate expansion of Hong Kong’s digital payment infrastructure. On Nov. 2, the HKMA upgraded its Project Ensemble pilot to support 24/7 interbank settlement of tokenized deposit transactions using tokenized central bank money.

The pilot phase launched in November 2025 and initially used Hong Kong’s conventional real-time gross settlement system. Its applications have included corporate treasury management and transactions involving tokenized money market funds.

Airplane Turbulence Is Extremely Hard to Predict, but Maybe Not for Long

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In the early 1500s, an Italian polymath named Leonardo da Vinci took a good crack at exactly the phenomenon that makes nervous fliers clutch their airplane seats in fear and flight attendants push their carts back to the galleys with haste: turbulence.

Da Vinci was fascinated by swirling, chaotic eddies of water, movements so complex that he made hundreds of drawings of his observations. Why, exactly, did water—any fluid, really, be it atmospheric air or blood in the heart—move in that way?

Now the world has ChatGPT and self-driving cars and solar farms, and it still doesn’t exactly know how turbulence works. “It’s the hardest remaining problem in classical physics, and it’s one we need to fly through,” says Michael Murillo, a professor of theoretical and computational physics at Michigan State University.

Now a bumper crop of data-driven efforts to predict and avoid turbulence could get fliers closer to a smooth flight. Twenty terabytes of data are created by an airplane’s engine in one hour of commercial flying, according to the International Air Transport Association, with more broadcast by airplanes midflight: GPS position, altitude, speed. An “electronic flight bag,” basically a specialized iPad, is equipped with navigation charts and manuals and can transmit its own information, as long as the airplane’s internet connection cooperates. Satellites watch and collect it all.

“Air traffic has gone up, and communication costs and the costs of putting computers on aircraft have come down,” says Larry Cornman, a physicist with the National Science Foundation’s National Center for Atmospheric Research who has been studying turbulence for decades. “The time is now to work on these things because we can do them.”

This is good news for aviation safety. More than 200 people were seriously injured on US airplanes in turbulence-related incidents between 2009 and 2023, according to FAA data. Turbulence is also getting worse. Climate change alters the atmospheric conditions around the world’s jet streams, creating destabilizing wind shear. From the cockpit of an airplane, this translates into sudden, clear-air turbulence, difficult to see or predict. Historically, pilots’ only method of avoiding turbulence is heeding the warning of those who accidentally went through it before them, and hoping that nothing had changed.

But just in the past two years, Cornman’s decades of work on turbulence has been put into the field in ways it couldn’t be before, part of several projects coordinated by the US federal government and the airline industry. One breakthrough came when Cornman and an FAA air traffic controller determined that aircrafts’ federally required broadcasts of their positions, altitude, and speed could be used to spot unexpected turbulence, without requiring new hardware or software onboard. Researchers can use this crowdsourced, public data to make predictions about when and where turbulence will pop up.

In September, the National Science Foundation announced a $30 million award to nine universities to stand up a new center dedicated to predicting turbulence—in the air, but also in the charged particles inside a fusion reactor, and in the gas clouds that stretch across space.

“Turbulence is the reason we don’t have fusion energy yet,” says Murillo, who heads up the new center, called Transformative Explorations in Multi-Physics and Engineering of Scientific Turbulence, or Tempest. The chaotic swirling of superhot plasma inside a reactor allows heat to leak out, preventing the material from reaching the temperatures needed to allow nuclei to fuse. The US federal government wants to get closer to figuring that out.

“Closer,” because turbulence is impossible to perfectly predict, scientists say. For one thing, turbulence occurs at many different, small scales at the same time. Air swirling around an airplane breaks into eddies, then smaller eddies, and then even smaller eddies, with each interacting with each other—too many variables, happening too quickly, to mathematically account for. For another, tiny changes—wind speed, temperature—can eventually dramatically alter air movement, suddenly altering the turbulence once predicted. To make perfect forecasts, one would need a perfect picture of exactly what’s going on in the atmosphere at all times.

But increasing sophisticated applications of machine learning could allow scientists to get closer to knowing what’s going to happen when, says Murillo. And while brute, computational force can’t solve the turbulence problem, AI agents might give the effort a boost. OpenAI’s blockbuster announcement this fall that it had resolved the Navier-Stokes problem—equations that are the beginning of all turbulence models—suggests LLMs could help.

“Turbulence has been a problem only human minds could work on; as of this year, that's no longer true,” says Murillo.

To make flying better, the aviation industry doesn’t necessarily need to solve turbulence. It needs to get better at knowing what sort of conditions create it and not send airplanes there.

One company in Tel Aviv, SkyPath, uses those in-plane iPads and their accelerometers to collect and share crowdsourced data between different airplanes, even if they don’t fly for the same airline. (This sort of dating sharing can be a sensitive topic for competing companies; SkyPath says it assuages these concerns by ensuring that data is anonymized before it’s shared.) More than 40,000 of these devices have SkyPath installed on them, says CEO Maya Shpak; the company says that several major airlines, including American, use the technology. SkyPath combines that collected data with additional information, including weather forecasts, and uses machine intelligence to make predictions about the future state of the air. The app helps pilots avoid turbulent air and warn passengers and crew members that choppiness is coming, 10 to 15 minutes before it happens.

One pilot named Eric who uses SkyPath, who asked WIRED not to use his full name because he was not authorized to speak to the media, said the app is a helpful tool that allows him to warn passengers what to expect. His issue is the same as those studying turbulence: One can’t take measurements everywhere all at once.

“It’s like any crowdsourced software,” says the pilot. “It only works if everyone uses it, and no one wants to use it until everyone else is.” Today, several large operators are using the app, which makes it especially useful in areas with lots of flights, he says.

Cornman, the physicist, wants to keep all these advances in perspective. “No matter what people say, we’re not getting close to 100 percent, 90 percent, 80 percent [predictive accuracy] anytime soon,” he says. “It’s too complicated a problem.” Smoother skies, if not perfect ones, are ahead.

Healthcare affordability won the midterms. Now employers need to speak up.

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Employers should care about the debate over Affordable Care Act (ACA) subsidies for a simple reason: They pay for the uninsured whether or not those people ever join their health plans.

When people lose coverage, they don’t stop getting sick. They may delay treatment, skip medications or eventually show up in emergency rooms with conditions that could have been managed earlier. The costs don’t disappear, and employers are already struggling with what they pay for healthcare.

The midterm elections made clear that Americans are fed up with rising costs. According to NBC News’ exit polling, 85% of voters expressed concern about healthcare costs, and Congress is now under pressure to respond. Much of the conversation has focused on the expiration of enhanced ACA subsidies but employers have been largely absent from the debate, despite providing coverage to more Americans under 65 than any other source.

At Collective Health, we administer health benefits for self-insured employers covering roughly 700,000 people. Our claims data shows employer healthcare costs are on track to increase at least 8% if not more in 2026. While that’s an improvement from the trend we saw across the nation in 2025, it’s still an 8% increase on an already unsustainably expensive baseline.

As a physician, I’ve ordered thousands of tests and treatments. For most routine blood tests, the needles, tubes, machines and results are essentially the same regardless of where the blood is drawn. Yet in our data, employers pay roughly six times more when those tests are performed in hospital outpatient departments rather than independent labs or physician offices.

The same pattern holds elsewhere. Surgery costs approximately three times as much in hospital outpatient departments as it does at more affordable sites of care, while infusions cost nearly three times as much.

And it’s not as though people are suddenly using significantly more healthcare. Among employers with comparable data across 2025 and 2026, hospital admissions were flat or down during the first seven months of this year. Overall utilization declined for many of our larger employers.

There are areas where we want to see more care being used. Outpatient behavioral health is one example. Helping people access treatment earlier can keep them healthier and reduce the need for more intensive care later. But paying several times more for the same lab test or infusion because of who owns the facility does nothing to improve someone’s health.

Now consider what happens when millions of Americans face higher insurance premiums or lose coverage altogether.

Others won’t have an employer plan to fall back on. And the reductions to Medicaid funding raise another set of concerns. Medicaid is a major source of funding for long-term care, skilled nursing and home-based services, which are already under pressure from staffing shortages and rising demand. As more people lose coverage, hospitals may look to offset uncompensated care costs by charging commercially insured patients more, putting additional pressure on employers already struggling with rising healthcare costs.

Employers have good reason to care about the stability of both markets. The individual market provides an important source of coverage for part-time workers, early retirees and employees’ families. When that coverage becomes harder to afford, the consequences reach well beyond the people buying insurance on the exchanges.

Congress should start with policies that address what care actually costs. Site-neutral payments are an obvious place to begin. If a patient receives the same treatment in a hospital-owned outpatient department or an independent facility, there is little justification for the enormous price difference we see today.

We also need better transparency around prescription drug pricing, pharmacy benefit managers and drugs administered in hospitals and clinics, where markups can be particularly difficult to see. Employers shouldn’t need a dedicated data team to understand what they’re paying for.

The No Surprises Act’s independent dispute resolution process also deserves another look. While the law has helped protect patients from certain unexpected bills, the payment dispute process has created opportunities for inflated demands. And Congress needs to provide a stable individual insurance market, whether by restoring enhanced subsidies or developing a better alternative.

Employers have options of their own, too. One of our clients redesigned its surgical benefits so eligible procedures at a designated center of excellence were free to employees. The same procedures carried 10% coinsurance at an ambulatory surgery center and 20% at a hospital outpatient facility.

In the first year, the share of eligible surgeries performed in hospital outpatient settings fell by nine percentage points. Other services that weren’t included in the redesign saw no comparable shift.

The employer didn’t achieve that simply by changing the plan. Employees received information during enrollment, targeted communications and personalized guidance from Collective Health when they began seeking care. They weren’t left to figure out the options on their own.

That’s the work employers should be doing with their benefits partners. They can manage where infusions happen, not just which drugs are covered. They can reach people before a procedure is scheduled rather than after the bill arrives.

In this industry, we tend to talk about “members,” but these are patients trying to do what’s best for their health while making ends meet. Asking someone dealing with knee pain or a serious diagnosis to research hospital prices and navigate complicated insurance rules on their own isn’t reasonable. If we want people to choose more affordable care, we have to make that choice easier.

Employers have spent years absorbing healthcare cost increases, often with little control over the prices behind them. They have the purchasing power and the claims data to push for something better. With a new Congress preparing to take up healthcare affordability, they also have an opportunity to influence the policies that determine what they’ll pay in the years ahead.

Your AI Strategy Has A Single Point Of Failure: Convenience Is How AI Concentration Begins

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

KPMG Partner | Global Data & AI Leader | Enterprise AI, indirect tax compliance, and finance transformation across global organizations.

Ask most executives where their AI risk sits, and they point to the model: bias, hallucination, privacy. Those risks are real, but another exposure can disable several well-governed systems at once: concentration.

A few months ago, I sat with the finance leadership team of a global company running more than a dozen AI use cases. I asked which model sat underneath each one. It took them two weeks to answer. Nearly all traced back to one provider, through three vendor products nobody had connected.

No one designed that dependency; it built up one sensible decision at a time. Concentration arrives as convenience.

Supply chain leaders know that two suppliers offer little protection if both rely on the same factory. I apply the same scrutiny to AI.

I trace each critical workflow through its application vendor, model and version, hosting platform, region, identity service, retrieval store and data sources. I record the owners, the contractual counterparty and the processing locations. I ask vendors to disclose their upstream dependencies and say plainly what they can’t see.

Then I ask which workflows could fail together. Different vendors may share a model provider. Different models may share a cloud region. A backup route that relies on the failed identity service is no backup.

Concentration becomes a single point of failure when one shared dependency can stop a critical process with no acceptable workaround. Counting vendors tells me very little.

I prioritize by business impact, not model popularity. A meeting summarizer and a regulatory filing workflow deserve different protection.

For every critical process, I want the business to agree on maximum tolerable downtime, recovery time, acceptable data loss and minimum service quality during disruption, along with thresholds for accuracy, latency, throughput and cost per transaction. An invoice workflow, for example, might require recovery within four hours, no lost approved transactions and human review whenever required fields fail validation. Those figures are illustrative. The process owner must approve the real limits.

I also make sure someone has the authority to suspend automation, authorize fallback and speak to affected teams. Recovery targets without decision rights are aspirations.

Outages are visible, but behavioral changes are harder to see. On one engagement, a document processing workflow began placing fields incorrectly after an upstream model update. Nothing crashed, and it took weeks to find the cause. When several workflows share that dependency, one update can spread errors across the business.

I pin model versions where supported, track retirement dates and record prompts, retrieval settings, tool definitions and model identifiers with each release. Pinning buys control, not permanent availability.

I also insist on a representative evaluation set with expected answers, difficult cases and business acceptance thresholds. We run it before releases and periodically against production to catch unannounced changes. We measure field accuracy and business-rule compliance, not just whether a response parses. Failures go to human review, and we decide in advance when to roll back, switch models or pause processing.

I monitor service errors separately from output quality because a healthy endpoint can still deliver unusable results. Where recovery targets can’t be met economically, I ask the business to accept the exposure explicitly, with its consequences and a review date. The chief data and AI officer (CDAIO) should make that trade-off visible rather than inherit it.

I keep prompts, evaluation sets, source documents, output schemas and business logic under our control, and I isolate provider-specific calls behind adapters. However, a common interface doesn’t make models interchangeable. An alternative may need different prompts, tool permissions or retrieval settings. Changing embedding models may require rebuilding the index, and fine-tuning assets or managed agent state may not transfer.

I test the whole workflow, including access controls, audit records, data residency, downstream integrations and capacity under expected demand. A demonstration with 10 documents doesn’t prove recovery capability.

The fallback must match the failure. Another region addresses a regional outage, and another provider addresses provider loss. Neither fixes a shared authentication failure. For some processes, a practiced manual queue is the best option, with defined staffing, backlog limits and reconciliation so transactions are neither lost nor processed twice.

I ask procurement, legal and engineering to review change and retirement notices, dependency disclosures, export formats, termination assistance and our rights to retain prompts, evaluation assets and tuning data. Then I test the export. Portability on paper without usable files and a working destination is weak protection. I also weigh resilience spending against the loss it reduces because blanket duplication can consume budget without removing the dependency that matters.

In the first 30 days, I work with business owners to inventory critical workflows, map shared dependencies and approve recovery tolerances. Undisclosed dependencies go on the log as risks, each with an owner and a deadline.

By the 60th day, engineering and operations set evaluation baselines, verify capacity, identify contractual barriers and write fallback runbooks with activation triggers, decision authority and reconciliation checks.

By the 90th day, we simulate provider unavailability and a failed quality threshold. We measure detection time, switching time, recovered throughput, output quality and backlog clearance. Then we assign remediation and repeat the exercise according to criticality and after material changes.

I give directors one page: critical processes exposed to shared dependencies, recovery targets against demonstrated results, unresolved exceptions and accountable owners.

​The revealing question is whether we’ve shown that essential work can continue when the first vendor fails or changes. The next stage of CDAIO leadership will be defined by what we can withstand. Resilience is credible only when it’s measured, rehearsed and owned.​

Cancer Cells Are Turning Antioxidants Into Weapons Against the Immune System

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

A newly discovered weakness in cancer cells could open the door to treatments that help the immune system overcome tumors that have resisted immunotherapy.

Cancer immunotherapy has helped some patients fight even advanced cancers, yet many tumors still find ways to escape the immune system. Researchers have now uncovered one possible reason why. Cancer cells can release a protein that removes chemical signals needed by the very immune cells trying to destroy them.

The finding, published in the journal Science, points to a potential new target for cancer treatment. It also challenges a familiar assumption about antioxidants, which are generally associated with protecting cells from damage.

At the center of the discovery are reactive oxygen species (ROS), often called free radicals. These molecules have long been linked to aging, DNA damage, and cancer. In small amounts, however, they serve another purpose. Cancer-fighting immune cells called T cells rely on ROS to activate and attack tumors.

Researchers found that some cancers exploit this dependency by releasing an antioxidant enzyme called peroxiredoxin-1 (PRDX1). The protein removes ROS from the surrounding environment, depriving T cells of signals they need to function.

“One of the surprising findings is that antioxidants aren’t always beneficial in the context of cancer,” said Robert L. Eil, M.D., a physician-scientist who co-led the research. “While reactive oxygen species sound threatening, T cells actually need them to perform their tumor-fighting job. What we found is that tumors can exploit the T cell’s dependency by removing the reactive oxygen species the immune system depends on.”

Eil is an associate professor of surgery in the Oregon Health & Science University (OHSU) School of Medicine and a member of the OHSU Knight Cancer Institute. The study was conducted alongside investigators at the University of Cambridge.

The researchers uncovered the mechanism by examining tumor interstitial fluid, the liquid surrounding cells within tumors. They found high levels of PRDX1, creating an antioxidant-rich environment that could interfere with the immune response.

To determine whether the protein was directly helping cancer survive, the team used CRISPR gene-editing technology to prevent cancer cells from producing PRDX1. Without the enzyme, immune activity increased and tumor growth declined across multiple experimental models.

In one melanoma model, the immune system rejected tumors lacking PRDX1 without additional treatment. In other models, removing the protein made previously resistant tumors respond to immune checkpoint blockade, a type of immunotherapy that helps T cells recognize and attack cancer.

The researchers also looked for evidence of the same process in humans. They analyzed published data from human cancer cell lines, studied gene activity across thousands of tumors, and measured PRDX1 in fluid collected from patient tumors. All three approaches provided evidence that human cancers release PRDX1 into their surroundings, where it can suppress T cell activity.

“We tend to think of reactive oxygen species purely as damaging byproducts of metabolism. But we are increasingly understanding that ROS have important functions within cells, and T cells require them to activate,” said Alexander J. Wesolowski, Ph.D., the study’s first author and a researcher in the Department of Pathology at the University of Cambridge. “Our study develops this picture, revealing that tumors can exploit this very dependency to evade elimination.”

The team also found that cancer cells increased PRDX1 production during a process called immunoediting. As tumors evolve under pressure from the immune system, they can develop characteristics that help them survive. Producing more of this antioxidant protein appears to be one way they may strengthen their defenses.

The researchers describe the mechanism as a previously unrecognized “redox checkpoint,” referring to the way tumors manipulate ROS levels to suppress immune activity.

Understanding this process could be particularly useful for improving immunotherapy, which remains ineffective for many patients despite its success against certain cancers.

“The immune system has already shown us that it can eradicate advanced cancers in some patients,” Eil said. “The challenge is that current immunotherapies don’t work for most people, and even when they do, complete responses remain relatively uncommon. The more we understand how tumors suppress T cells, the more opportunities we have to design therapies that reverse that suppression.”

Possible treatments could include drugs that neutralize antioxidants released by tumors, therapies that block PRDX1 activity, or engineered immune cells capable of functioning in antioxidant-rich environments. These approaches remain possibilities for future research rather than established treatments.

“What’s exciting is that we’ve identified a target nobody was really looking for before,” Eil said. “This doesn’t put a drug in patients’ hands tomorrow, but it gives us an entirely new pathway to pursue. Discovering new targets is how future treatments begin.”

Eil and his collaborators plan to continue investigating how tumors weaken immune responses and whether those defenses can be overcome with next-generation immunotherapies, including engineered T cell treatments.

“This is one more victory against ignorance in understanding why cancers are so difficult to treat,” Eil said. “Every time we uncover a new way tumors suppress the immune system, we gain another opportunity to fight back. That’s how better treatments are built.”

Revenue rose 103% to a record $54.9bn in the three months to 30 October. Analysts had expected $49.32bn on average, according to LSEG data cited by Reuters. It was Dell’s third forecast increase this year, Jaspreet Singh and Harshita Mary Varghese reported for Reuters. The shares rose about 5% in extended trading.

Net income rose 266% to $5.7bn, or $8.82 a diluted share. Adjusted earnings were $9.45 a share, against the $6.59 analysts expected. Operating expenses fell to 7% of revenue, which Dell said was the lowest in its 42-year history. The board approved another $25bn for share buybacks, after Dell returned a record $4.9bn to shareholders in the quarter.

“We are now a different company, with Infrastructure Solutions Group (ISG) driving the vast majority of our profit and growth,” Jeff Clarke, Dell’s vice chairman and chief operating officer, said in a statement.
