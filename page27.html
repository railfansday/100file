Numa raises $45M to put AI agents to work in car dealerships

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

GM, Ford and Stellantis have opened their dealer networks to the startup's software.

Numa, which sells AI software that answers calls, books service visits and handles sales leads for car dealerships, has raised a $45M Series C led by Blue Cloud Ventures. It says more than 1,500 dealerships use it, and GM, FordDirect and Stellantis have approved it for their dealers.

Numa, a San Francisco startup that sells AI software to car dealerships, has raised a $45M Series C, the company announced on Monday. Blue Cloud Ventures led the round, with Threshold Ventures, Costanoa and Google’s Gradient Ventures taking part. Total funding now stands at $96M, after a $32M Series B in 2024.

Numa’s software answers dealership phone calls, texts and chats, books service visits and sends callers to the right staff. The company says more than 1,500 dealerships in the US and Canada use it. It claims to have handled over 1 billion conversations and booked more than 4 million appointments.

This year Numa pushed into sales. A new agent knows every car on the lot down to trim and mileage, and can suggest another if the one a buyer wanted has sold. In August, Numa bought SocketTime, which makes scheduling and repair tools for service shops.

“In two years, every general manager will have an AI COO,” said founder and CEO Tasso Roumeliotis.

He said the new money will pay for more engineers. Numa says it tripled its annual recurring revenue this year, but did not share figures or a valuation.

Carmakers are opening doors. GM has approved Numa as a vendor for its dealers, and more than 240 GM stores use it. In those stores, Numa says reply times fell from 23 hours to 24 minutes.

FordDirect, Ford’s dealer marketing venture, picked Numa as its AI vendor in August, giving it access to more than 3,000 Ford and Lincoln dealers. Numa is also approved for Stellantis dealers, about 3,000 more stores. Those are open doors, not signed customers.

Numa is not alone. Startups such as BadCo.AI are building AI for car buying, and voice AI firm Vapi raised a $50M Series B in May. Detroit’s three carmakers have also cut 20,000 office jobs since 2022, and AI is expected to speed that up.

But can we take a moment to pour one out for the equally-as-talented character actor doomed to be the runner-up far more often than he deserves? We're talking, of course, about Nicholas Hoult.

We've sort of been dreading this exact outcome the moment we first heard about the various finalists up for the world-famous MI6 agent. In all fairness, neither Nicholas Hoult nor his agent (nor his bank account) should shed any tears based on his career trajectory to this point. He's made an impression in a number of smaller roles over the years (check out "The Order" from 2024 for great example of that), all while working with some of the best directors around. He's even waded into the blockbuster arena a few times, first in the 20th Century Fox "X-Men" movies and most recently as Lex Luthor in James Gunn's DC Universe.

But, despite all that success, we have to point out how the "Mad Max: Fury Road" scene-stealer continues to get the short end of the stick. Adding insult to injury, this isn't even the first time he's watched Pattinson specifically land the coveted role he was eyeing. So here's to Nicholas Hoult, the perpetual underdog who'll always be a star in our hearts.

"Always the bridesmaid, never the bride" is probably too harsh to describe what's going on with Nicholas Hoult lately but, man, it reaches a point. Despite proving himself to have one heck of a screen presence throughout the last several years, whether it be as an agent of chaos in Yorgos Lanthimos' "The Favourite" in 2018 or as a leading man in 2019's "Tolkien" biopic or even the horror-heavy "Nosferatu," Hoult has clearly been aching for the unambiguous breakout role that would make him a household name among general audiences. With all due respect to his meme-factory performances in "The Menu" or "Superman," well, neither of those have quite cut it. (Although that one photoshoot for "The Great" alongside Elle Fanning still leaves rent-free in our minds. You know the one.)

Sadly, that trend continues with the James Bond announcement. Real ones know it all started when Hoult was in contention for the title role in Matt Reeves' "The Batman," reportedly the only other name considered along with Robert Pattinson. But wait, it gets worse. After losing out on that crown jewel for superheroes, Hoult managed to find himself in the running for Superman a few short years later for James Gunn's reboot — which he ended up falling just short of, as well, though he picked up the role of supervillain Lex Luthor in a fun bit of meta casting. Third time isn't the charm, however, as he's now once again looking up to Pattinson as our newest Bond. How much is one man supposed to endure???

We love you, Nicholas Hoult, even if Hollywood's casting directors apparently have it out for you.

Ahead of its IPO filing, Anthropic pledges to prioritize humanity’s benefit. Here’s how

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When Anthropic goes public on Wall Street, the new pressure to deliver profits will test a commitment it has made since its founding: to prioritize humanity’s benefit as it develops artificial intelligence.

The San Francisco startup, which plans an initial public offering this month, has sketched out a governance structure that it says will protect the company’s broader mission against the pull of financial interests. But the mechanisms it describes give the company wide leeway to decide how to measure whether it’s benefiting society, experts say.

Like its rival OpenAI, Anthropic is a public benefit corporation, a legal designation used by companies that pledge to balance shareholder interests with the public good. How that will play out for companies worth potentially trillions of dollars is uncharted territory.

“I think then the real test emerges if and when the question of public benefit becomes costly” — or when the public benefit comes into conflict with making a profit, said Sarah Kreps, a professor and director of Cornell University’s Tech Policy Institute.

In its regulatory filings, Anthropic pledges to pursue long-term benefits of humanity through the responsible development of advanced AI. “We strive to help bring humanity safely into an era where transformative AI exerts tremendous and potentially decisive influence over a broad range of human undertakings,” the company said. Anthropic also has pledged at least 5% of its stock to a “public benefit” fund and its founders have committed to donate 80% of their wealth.

A pledge to do good is baked into corporate structure

Other tech companies have pledged to do good including Google, which prominently featured its then-motto, “Don’t Be Evil,” in its investor prospectus when it went public in 2004. But the words were not legally binding, and critics have accused the tech giant of leaving them behind.

Elon Musk’s takeover of Twitter in 2021 shows how the conflict between profit and public good might play out in real life, said Stavros Gadinis, a law professor at UC Berkeley School of Law.

“Let’s say that Twitter’s directors felt that Elon was the wrong guy to police the public square because they didn’t trust him, they didn’t like him, they didn’t think he’d be fair, whatever it was,” Gadinis said. “But he made them an offer that was so high compared to what their advisors told them the company was really worth that it would be against their fiduciary duties to not sell to Elon. This is the situation that the public benefit charter is trying to avoid.”

Had Twitter been a public benefit corporation, it could have been required to consider factors beyond maximizing shareholder value when selling the company.

There aren’t many public benefit corporations whose shares are publicly traded and they are all much smaller than Anthropic and OpenAI. They include the eyewear company Warby Parker, the insurance company Lemonade and software developer Veeva Systems, which had a conventional corporate structure when it went public in 2013 and converted to a public benefit corporation in 2021.

Anthropic’s idealistic ambitions for how it develops AI could run up against competitive pressure, such as from China, Kreps said.

Anthropic and OpenAI’s models have gone from answering almost any question, even if it was dangerous, to becoming much more circumspect. But as they face more competition, including from open-source Chinese models that likely will tell you whatever you want, she said, “will they start to rethink some of those guardrails? And what happens then?”

The setup generally is not preferred by shareholders because it gives more power to the company’s board than it does to shareholders.

Whatever a company decides as its public benefit mission, it will likely be much more difficult to measure than profits. That’s particularly true for AI companies, whose leaders have exalted AI as the solution to many of humanity’s problems — from curing cancer to creating economic prosperity.

“There are these echo chambers, the Silicon Valley bubble where they really think that what they’re doing is for the good of humanity,” Kreps said. And someone immersed in that environment, she added, might not even ask the right questions or understand how “public benefit” should be measured.

A trust aims to hold Anthropic accountable

A Long-Term Benefit Trust created by Anthropic is intended to operate as an independent body of trustees with expertise in AI safety, national security, public policy, and social enterprise.

Through a special Class T common stock, the trust “generally has the authority to elect and remove” Class T directors, who together will constitute a majority of Anthropic’s board of directors, according to the IPO filing. The Class T common stock is a single share that allows the five trustees to vote as a unit. It has no economic rights — meaning they get to vote but they don’t see any financial benefit or losses from the share and can’t sell it.

“The LTBT’s arrangements are designed to minimize the LTBT’s and the Trustees’ exposure to our financial performance and grant the Trustees sufficient independence to ensure we are striking this balance appropriately,” the filing said.

Three of the company’s board directors are elected by the trust directly, and another three are elected by other shareholders, which include the company founders with a 50.1% voting majority. A seventh director, with a potentially tie-breaking vote, is nominated by the trust and the holders of the Class T shares, but cannot be appointed or removed without consent from the board’s nominating committee, which is expected to be controlled by the founders.

Effectively, the trust can only hold Anthropic accountable on issues such as AI safety and the company’s broader mission up to a point, Gadinis said.

The trust “cannot determine the everyday decisions of these directors or the management of the company,” he said. “If they disagree, they will have to go through the effort of removing directors, and they may not be able to remove a majority.”

As a public company, Anthropic also will be required to report finances and generally be more transparent than a privately held one. Many public companies dealing with AI, including Google and Microsoft, regularly update the public on efforts to develop AI responsibly.

The incentive for transparency could be as powerful as other mechanisms set up in Anthropic’s corporate structure, Gadinis said.

“I do think that by being public, we are going to get a lot more information, because a lot of people are going to be asking questions, and companies will be responding to these questions. not only because they’re required by security laws, but also because they want to build this kind of trust with the investing community,” he said.

OpenAI will embed Apollo researchers to investigate AI subversion

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

OpenAI will embed six Apollo Research safety evaluators in its organization as part of a pilot, Apollo said Monday. The news follows a series of safety incidents, including one in which an OpenAI agent swarm broke out of its sandbox and hacked into Hugging Face during cybersecurity testing. Many AI safety researchers, including from Apollo, have argued such an event might have been avoided if outside evaluators had been embedded within OpenAI to monitor its safety testing.

Apollo did not share when the pilot will begin. TechCrunch has reached out to OpenAI for comment.

The idea of embedding evaluators into frontier AI labs first came from Anthropic, but OpenAI appears to be the first to take action. Until recently, AI companies have only brought in outside reviewers to test models after post-training and shortly before public release. Evaluators say this is insufficient because it doesn’t allow them to test for misaligned behaviors like scheming during training or to monitor internal deployment use cases that could still pose a threat.

The pilot with OpenAI and Apollo will focus on investigating whether models attempt to subvert their own or their successors’ alignment or control mechanisms. In other words, they’ll be on the lookout for whether AI models try to undermine the safety rules and safeguards designed to keep them, or future versions of themselves, under human control. OpenAI recently published findings that while training GPT-5.6 Sol, it began leaving instructions for future versions of itself telling them to conceal mistakes and misaligned behavior from the user.

Per Apollo, the pilot will examine whether training incentivizes subversion; whether evaluations can elicit subversion; whether subversion occurs during internal deployment; and whether reasoning relevant to subversion would be detectable. These risks are becoming increasingly important to monitor as AI models grow more capable of acting autonomously and deceiving humans. Apollo’s previous research with OpenAI found that models can behave differently when they know they’re being tested and tend to prioritize what evaluators want to see over users’ actual institutions.

Many in the AI safety community have warned that AI labs might invite try to stymy evaluators’ independence, especially if outsiders find unflattering evidence that labs would rather keep hidden. Apollo says an essential part of the pilot is OpenAI’s agreement that the firm can publicly explain the conditions and limitations of its work.

Still, there are areas where Apollo believes its access to OpenAI’s systems is lacking. For a truly independent evaluation of the company’s AI models, Apollo says it needs true employee-level access to OpenAI’s systems, including “all data, records and documentation relevant to their assessment even if a developer was trying to hide unfavorable information.” Getting access to all data, including training data and evaluation logs which companies are loathe to show to outsiders, is important for, say, checking a company’s claims about how a model performed or determining when certain concerning behavior emerged.

In previous coverage, John Steidley, chief of staff at Palisade Research, told TechCrunch that it’s important to see this data in order to assess whether a model was trained to perform well on a “shutdown resistance benchmark.”

In OpenAI’s first pilot, the AI lab is providing Apollo with access to only the information required to assess whether the models engage in subversion attempts. This includes “relevant training code and datasets, reinforcement-learning rollouts with available reasoning traces and rewards, and the ability to query intermediate model checkpoints.”

The scope of access will be limited to “only the agreed claims and only for the specific models and time periods agreed for the assessment,” Apollo says. OpenAI still reserves the right to withhold certain information within that scope if it deems it necessary because of technical infeasibility, security risks, or IP protection. The company agreed not to withhold information simply because it is unfavorable.

Apollo also won’t get access to all OpenAI staffers, instead being limited to “only authorized staff,” which are participants that the AI lab selects, though Apollo can suggest certain individuals. Crucially, if an OpenAI employee outside this group raises confidential information within the scope of Apollo’s research, the firm cannot follow up and will need to cease communication and route followup through OpenAI.

That condition is significant. It comes in the wake of OpenAI firing three researchers for allegedly sharing confidential information with safety evaluation firm METR — which performed the Hugging Face incident post-mortem alongside Redwood.

The pilot will also be limited in how much researchers can disclose. While Apollo will be allowed to publish independent conclusions of its findings, OpenAI will be able to limit evidence available to substantiate them publicly, which could make it hard for outsiders to independently assess the findings. Apollo has pushed back against this limitation, saying believes researchers should be allowed to disclose the findings of the evaluation “to an appropriate outside audience even if the developer was trying to prevent the disclosure of unfavorable findings.”

While OpenAI is not granting all of Apollo’s conditions for a truly independent evaluation, the firm says the pilot is “an ambitious first step towards deeper access and disclosure rights,” and that it is working towards running full embedded evaluations with multiple AI developers in the future.

How Much Does The World's Largest Commercial Engine Cost?

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The headline figure that the incoming GE Aerospace 134-inch GE9X turbofan is worth $42-45 million is misleading. Understood as a list price, it is ok, but compared with the market value of a GE90 or Trent XWB, it is not. While the engine is likely to be the most expensive engine on the market, it is still likely to belong to the same tier as the competing Trent XWB-97, with the difference in value measured in single digits of millions of dollars, not tens of millions.

It is also incredibly difficult to disentangle the price of an engine from its financing, airframe, status as a bundled spare, long-term maintenance contracts, and bulk orders. It is almost unheard of for an airline to walk into a GE Aerospace showroom, point to a large aero engine, and pay upfront for one engine with no maintenance, tooling, spare components, or other services. Here is how much a GE9X is worth in 2026, attempting to avoid apples-to-oranges comparisons.

The GE Aerospace GE9X is the world's largest commercial jet engine by diameter, and by thrust produced during test runs, it is not the world's largest by certified thrust. The world's largest aeroengine by certified thrust is its predecessor, the GE90-115B. The GE90-115B was built to power the Boeing 777-200LR, Boeing 777-300ER, and Boeing 777F freighter. It comes with a certified thrust of 115,300 lbf at sea level.

Its GE90-94B counterpart is certified for 93,700 lbf and powered the previous Boeing 777-200 and Boeing 777-300 generation of Triple Sevens. GE Aerospace says it has test-run the new GE9X generation of the GE90 family at 134,300 lbf. It claims that this is the "highest thrust of any commercial jet engine." But producing the highest thrust in test runs and actually being certified for that are two different things.

The GE9X has a certified thrust of 110,000 lbf, placing it at 5,300 lbf less than its predecessor. GE Aerospace's new variant will provide the incoming Boeing 777X family with 5% fuel-specific fuel consumption improvement versus "any twin-aisle engine available" (read: Rolls-Royce Trent XWB), and offers up to 10% specific consumption improvement versus the older GE90-115B. It achieves an approximate 10:1 bypass ratio.

Safe Fly Aviation writes, "Aircraft engine values are among the most important metrics in commercial aviation finance. A single powerplant can cost more than a private jet, and in today's market, some engine types are worth more than the aircraft they power." According to its analysis, the General Electric GE9X is the world's most valuable engine at around $42–45 million at list price. It says, "Its list price of $42–45 million per engine exceeds the list price of many private jets, making it the pinnacle of aircraft engine values."

For reference, it lists the GE90-115BL as having a "Full-Life value" of over $32 million and a Half-Life Market Value increasing by 60% since 2023. The only other extra-large modern engine is the Rolls-Royce Trent XWB, "valued at approximately $25 million per unit." There are some glaring issues with these comparisons, and they do not offer an apples-to-apples comparison.

The GE9X is not yet on the market, as the Boeing 777X is yet to enter service. Therefore, the only data point is its list price, while the market values for the other two engines are being used. I have an accounting background, and this is a terrible comparison. List prices are little more than brochure listings where manufacturers and sellers often inflate the true market value of their products. The market value, what companies actually pay, is much more consequential.

Discover why the Boeing 777X depends entirely on the GE9X, the world’s largest and most advanced commercial jet engine ever built.

In its H2 2026 update, IBA states, "Current widebody engine values continue to grow strongly due to new aircraft production delays. Existing engine retention remains high, MRO demand is strong, and spare engine supply is limited." The value of an engine is not just its performance or the cost of manufacture, but also its scarcity. This is the classic economics 101 of supply and demand. Currently, demand is high, and supply is constrained, driving up costs.

Put another way, the delay of the Boeing 777X and the fact that the GE9X is not yet in service is making GE90-115BL and Trent XWB engines more valuable. Airlines are being forced to keep their older GE90-powered 777s in service for longer, which necessarily includes the engines. Low spare engine availability for the GE90-115BL is driving up the value of the engine.

While the A350 is a new aircraft with no airline seeking to retire them, the fact that it is one of the only larger widebodies available, given production constraints of the 787 and no 777Xs, is driving up their value. IBA states that the GE90-115BL full-life values exceed $35 million, and its long-term lease extensions are being observed between $250,000 and $300,000 per month. Meanwhile, MBA Aviation lists the H1 2026 lease rates for the Trent XWB-97 with a market lease rate of $287,000.

In commercial aviation, virtually no airline or lessor pays the published Original Engine Manufacturer (OEM) list price for a factory-new widebody aero engine. In other words, the GE9X is not really worth $42 million. GE Aerospace can slap any value that it claims to value it at, but that is divorced from the reality of the market. The 'real' price is the 'discounted' price that airlines and lessors actually pay for them.

As a rule of thumb, when engines are purchased as part of a new aircraft order, especially on competitive twin-engine aircraft, OEM's routinely provide 'concessions' (aka discounts) of 40% to 46% or more off their inflated catalog list prices. However, the discount is typically less on aircraft like the A350 or 777, where Rolls-Royce or GE Aerospace are exclusive engine suppliers. That said, there are still typically substantial discounts.

The true value of the GE9X will become much more apparent when it is in service and starts appearing in secondary markets. Until then, there is simply a lack of data compared with the XWB and GE90. The value of the engine also varies by whether it comes as part of the aircraft or separately. This is similar to a car. It is typically much cheaper to purchase a car fully assembled and ready to go than purchasing each individual part, including the engine, and then self-assembling it.

The largest and most powerful commercial engine ever built, the GE9X is by no means cheap

Speaking of list prices per seat (not engines per se), Sirius Capital Aviation writes, "list prices were far higher than any realistic estimate of actual prices (typical discounts were already over 50%) but they did provide a limited market signal of relative prices." Put another way, the list price of $42 million for the GE9X is a starting figure that needs to be discounted to find out what it is really worth. It is absolutely not a number that can be compared in good faith with the market price of a Trent XWB-97.

Another issue is that it can't even be compared with XWB list prices, as Rolls-Royce does not publish standalone engine list prices and Airbus ceased publishing its annual aircraft list price catalog in 2018. Using a reverse calculation, the implied list value for the XWB would likely fit into the $35 to $40 million range. This is back-of-the-envelope math and should not be taken too seriously. Given that the GE9X is the sole engine for the 777X, an expected discount off list price of around 35% to 40% can be expected.

Importantly, the GE9X may be the only engine on the 777X, but the 777-9 still has to compete with the XWB-powered A350-1000. If this is the correct range, then the real implied market rate of around $22 million to $28 million per engine can be expected. It is possible that 35-40% is too steep, and so the market price may be higher. In other words, the value of new XWB and GE9X engines may be within a reasonably modest margin of each other.

Also, engines are seldom just purchased alone. They are part of bundles that include other engines, the aircraft themselves, spares, and service agreements. Engine manufacturers may sell engines at or even below manufacturing cost, as their margins are made up on the lucrative 15–25-year-long servicing of the engines. Spare engines purchased directly from the OEM generally get smaller discounts than line-fit installed engines.

Even looking at announced contracts is problematic. For example, GE announced Singapore Airlines had ordered 22 more GE9X engines in 2022 for $2.8 billion at list price. That works out to more than $100 million per engine, but it also includes a 12-year maintenance contract (called TrueChoice). The price of engines likely varies considerably by airline.

Emirates has placed orders for around 45% of the entire global 777X backlog. This shifts much of the bargaining power to Emirates from Boeing and GE. Much of the program is reliant on Emirates (and Qatar), and Emirates knows it. While the numbers are not available, it is reasonable to assume Emirates is purchasing GE9X engines for much less than Silk Way West, which is ordering two aircraft with four engines.

Up to 15 meteors could be spotted per hour under perfect dark sky conditions on the night of Nov. 17-18, according to the International Meteor Organisation. NASA, meanwhile, has given a more conservative rate of less than three meteors per hour — time will tell.

Leonid meteors appear to race away from a point of origin, or radiant, in the constellation Leo, which glistens high above the southeastern horizon in the hours before dawn in mid-November. Both Jupiter and Mars will be separated by just a few degrees in the constellation Leo on the night of the peak, making it particularly easy to find in 2026!

Shooting stars that appear close to the radiant will strike Earth almost head-on, leaving the shortest trails. As if shooting stars weren't enough, the Leonid shower is also known for spawning dazzling fireballs and long-lasting Earth-grazing meteors, which strike our atmosphere at an oblique angle, leaving long trains as they skim through before passing back out into space.

The shower is also capable of producing magnificent meteor storms when Earth passes through particularly dense trails of debris left behind by Comet 55P/Tempel-Tuttle, which orbits the sun approximately every 33 years. Paul Rudd And Seann William Scott's R-Rated Comedy Is Worth Revisiting 18 Years Later

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Studios abandoned big comedies years ago. But in the 2000s and early 2010s, we really had it good. This was the age of the Judd Apatow comedy, in which the director not only delivered such modern classics as "The 40-Year-Old Virgin" and "Knocked Up," but influenced almost every comedy released during the era. Alongside the main Apatow offerings you had those which he produced such as "Anchorman: The Legend of Ron Burgundy," or those made by his collaborators, such as "Superbad." Then, there were those films that featured Apatow associates but were made without his direct involvement. The brilliant "I Love You, Man" is one example which, despite being criminally overlooked, remains one of Paul Rudd's best films.

As does 2008's "Role Models," which saw Rudd team up with Seann William Scott for a film that, like pretty much every comedy movie released at the time, was defined by the improvisational, R-rated humor that had made Apatow's films so successful. This particular movie, however, paired Apatow allies Rudd and Christopher Mintz-Plasse with former members of sketch comedy troupe The State in director David Wain and writer/actor Ken Marino.

As someone who watched "Role Models" when it came out, I simply cannot believe this movie is 18 years old. Despite being less well known than the aforementioned "Knocked Up" and its ilk, "Role Models" became instantly beloved by me and my friends upon its release. Almost two decades later, it's strange to think that an entire generation has grown up without films such as this shaping their sense of humor. If you happen to be part of that group, now's the time to acquaint yourself with this  oft-overlooked comedy. If, like me, you remember "Role Models" fondly, you'll be happy to hear it holds up.

Like so many of the best comedies of the 2000s, "Role Models" succeeds because despite being an R-rated comedy with frequent gross-out gags and unapologetically immature humor, it's also a surprisingly tender story with its heart in the right place.

Paul Rudd plays Danny who, alongside Seann William Scott's Anson Wheeler, works as a promoter for the energy drink "Minotaur." Danny feels deeply unfulfilled and has become deeply cynical as a result, spending most of his time correcting people on their speech. This causes his girlfriend, Beth (Elizabeth Banks), to break up with him, after which Danny loses it during a presentation at a high school. His freak out leads to both he and Anson being assigned to community service at Sturdy Wings, a big brother-style program where the pair become mentors to two "littles."

Anson is paired with Ronnie (Bobb'e J. Thompson) a foul-mouthed kid with no friends, while Danny is given Augie Farks (Christopher Mintz-Plasse), a teenager obsessed with his medieval LARP group. At first, Danny and Anson are merely going through the motions. But over time they develop a bond with their "littles" — one which just might melt Danny's cold, cynical heart.

The secret star of "Role Models" is Joe Lo Truglio, who plays Kuzzik, a particularly dedicated LARP-er who, if you watch the bloopers, made it almost impossible for Rudd to get through any of his scenes without breaking. But there are a ton of standout performances here, from A.D. Miles as overly earnest Sturdy Wings counsellor Martin Gary to Ken Jeong as the self-serious LARP king Argotron and Jane Lynch as a former addict turned Sturdy Wings leader.

"Role Models" hit theaters in November 2008 and grossed a solid $92.6 million on a $28 million budget. It also garnered positive reviews, earning a 77% "certified fresh" rating on Rotten Tomatoes. Dana Stevens of Slate found "Role Models" to be "strewn with enough serendipities to make it a worthy variation on the dudes-grow-up genre," while the San Francisco Chronicle's Peter Hartlaub noted how, while Seann William Scott and Paul Rudd were "from two different moviemaking cliques" they ultimately "get along fine in a Bing Crosby/David Bowie kind of way."

Meanwhile, Rafer Guzman of Newsday noted how Rudd, who co-wrote the script with David Wain, Timothy Dowling, and Ken Marino, had "clearly learned a few things working on Judd Apatow comedies." By that point, Rudd had appeared in Apatow's "The 40-Year-Old Virgin" and "Knocked Up," as well as several Apatow-produced projects including "Anchorman" and "Forgetting Sarah Marshall." As such, he was well-established as one of the most prominent figures of the 2000s R-rated comedy boom.

But The State boys certainly brought their own flair to the film and the pairing of theirs and Rudd's sensibility along with Scott's lovable knucklehead charm made for a hilarious and surprisingly heartwarming experience that deserves to be remembered as fondly as some of the more well-known comedies of the era. California Supreme Court Invents Constitutional Right to Affordable Bail

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The California Supreme Court has once again mistaken judicial invention for constitutional interpretation. In April, the court held that, except in capital cases, the California Constitution guarantees criminal defendants a right to pretrial release on bail—and requires trial judges to set bail at an amount that is “reasonably attainable” by the accused.[REF] That holding is as novel as it is alarming. It elevates a judge-made entitlement over the text, history, and structure of the state constitution; discounts the grave risks that some defendants pose to victims and the public; and disregards the repeated, unmistakable commands of the California electorate.

Californians have confronted this pattern before. Again and again, the state’s high court has purported to discover sweeping rights for criminals in the California Constitution that the document’s text does not fairly support—only to have the people restore constitutional order at the ballot box. Propositions 4, 8, 9, and 189 were not isolated political reactions. Each was a referendum passed in response to radical decisions that placed the interests of criminals above the rights of victims, the protection of the public, and the common-sense judgments of the citizenry. Each reflected the sovereign authority of the people to correct a court that had strayed from the constitution it was charged with interpreting.

The latest bail decision repeats that error in particularly stark form. During the past 44 years, California voters have amended their constitution not once but twice, both to make clear that trial judges may deny bail to a person charged with a felony and to establish a Victims’ Bill of Rights—including mandatory detention for violent felons who pose a threat to others.[REF] Those amendments cannot be reconciled with the court’s new affordable bail standard. The court’s contrary conclusion does not merely misread the charter; it diminishes the constitutional choices the people themselves have made.

This Legal Memorandum proceeds in three parts. Part I traces the California Supreme Court’s recurring practice of constitutional innovation and the electorate’s repeated use of the initiative and referendum process to reject it, with particular attention to Propositions 4, 8, 9, and 189. Part II examines the text, history, and purpose of California’s bail provisions and Victims’ Bill of Rights, showing how those provisions preserve judicial authority to deny bail when necessary to protect victims and public safety. Part III analyzes the court’s April decision, identifies its doctrinal and practical defects, and explains why its “reasonably attainable” standard cannot be squared with the constitution as amended by the people.

The court’s latest holding is contrary to the text of California’s constitution, contrary to its history, and contrary to the settled will of the people. It should not be permitted to stand. The people of California—or, where constitutionally sufficient, their elected representatives—should correct the court once again and reaffirm a basic principle of ordered liberty: The criminal justice system exists not only to protect the rights of the accused, but also to vindicate the rights of victims, punish criminals, and safeguard the public.

Part I: Judicial Innovation v. the Electorate

California’s original constitution of 1849 provided that “all persons shall be bailable, by sufficient sureties; unless for capital offences, when the proof is evident or the presumption great.”[REF] Another provision stated that “excessive bail shall not be required, nor excessive fines imposed, nor shall cruel or unusual punishments be inflicted, nor shall witnesses be unreasonably detained.”[REF] In 1879, those two provisions were consolidated into Article I, Section 6 of the Constitution of 1879. That remained the case for almost 100 years.

In 1973, the California Supreme Court held in In re Underwood that the right to pretrial release was guaranteed for all defendants.[REF] Displeased with the court’s ruling, voters passed Proposition 7 in 1974, relocated the bail provisions to Section 12, and gave trial judges the discretion to release defendants on their own recognizance.[REF]

In 1977, Governor Jerry Brown appointed Rose Bird as Chief Justice of the California Supreme Court. Bird was divisive and controversial in part because of her consistent votes against capital convictions[REF] and a dissent she wrote opposing the inclusion of Proposition 8—the Victim’s Bill of Rights—on the ballot in 1982.[REF] She was eventually removed from office in 1986 by the voters.

By 1982, having had enough of rogue judges and violent criminals, California voters passed two propositions to amend the constitution to shorten the leash on judges and bolster victims’ rights. The first was Proposition 4, a constitutional amendment that allowed courts to deny bail in felony cases under two additional circumstances. As amended, Section 12 stated:

A person shall be released on bail by sufficient sureties, except for: (a) Felony offenses involving acts of violence against another person when the facts are evident or the presumption great and the court finds based upon clear and convincing evidence that there is a substantial likelihood the person’s release would result in great bodily harm to others or; (b) Felony offenses when the facts are evident or the presumption great and the court finds based on clear and convincing evidence that the person has threatened another person with great bodily harm and that there is a substantial likelihood that the person would carry out the threat if released.[REF]

The voters passed Proposition 4 by an overwhelming vote of 82.5 percent to 15.1 percent to prevent both the legislature and judges from changing the requirements that governed bail without the people’s consent.[REF]

The second was Proposition 8, which amended the state constitution by including a victim’s bill of rights and restricting the rights of convicts.[REF] Adopted by a vote of 56 percent to 43 percent, Proposition 8 repealed Section 12 of Article I and added Section 28, which included the following prefatory language: “The People of the State of California find and declare that the enactment of comprehensive provisions and laws ensuring a bill of rights for victims of crime, including safeguards in the crime justice system to fully protect those rights, is a matter of grave statewide concern.”[REF] Section 28(e), entitled “Public Safety Bail,” stated in part:

A person may be released on bail by sufficient sureties, except for capital crimes when the facts are evident or the presumption great…. In setting, reducing or denying bail, the judge or magistrate shall take into consideration the protection of the public, the seriousness of the offense charged, the previous criminal record of the defendant, and the probability of his or her appearing at the trial or hearing of the case. Public safety shall be the primary consideration.[REF]

Taken together, Propositions 4 and 8 sent a loud and clear message that the citizens of California were fed up with judges releasing violent felons, that mandatory release on bail was a bridge too far, that victims mattered, and that public safety—not defendant’s “rights”—was the “primary consideration” that judges must take into account when setting bail.

In 1994, the voters passed Proposition 189, which, among other things, amended Article I, Section 12 by adding “felony sexual assault offenses on another person” to subsection (b).[REF] A year later, in In re York, a case dealing with whether a court or magistrate may condition release from custody on a defendant’s own recognizance, the California Supreme Court noted in a footnote that the bail provision language in Proposition 4 appeared to conflict with the language in Proposition 8 (“shall” in Proposition 4 vs. “may” in Proposition 8) and that, because Proposition 4 received more votes, its bail and own recognizance provisions were “deemed to prevail over those set forth in Proposition 8.”[REF] In other words, trial judges “shall” (i.e., must) release defendants on bail unless it is a capital case or one of the felony exceptions enumerated in subsections (b) or (c). Remarkably, the court did not cite any statute or state constitutional provision authorizing it to erase the will of the voters and nullify Proposition 8 because Proposition 4 received more votes.

In 2006, the California Supreme Court held in People v. Standish that, because the bail provisions of Propositions 4 and 8 were “directly at odds,” Section 28(e) of Proposition 8 “never took effect.”[REF] So much for the 2,826,081 Californians who voted for Proposition 8.

Angered by crime rates and a state Supreme Court that did not put victims first, California voters passed Proposition 9, the “Victim’s Bill of Rights Act of 2008,” called Marsy’s Law.[REF] Proposition 9 specified that the law, named for Marsy Nicholas, who was murdered by her ex-boyfriend in 1983, was “needed to remedy a justice system that fails to fully recognize and adequately enforce rights of victims of crime.”[REF] In the “Findings and Declarations” section, Marsy’s Law says that “[t]he People of the State of California find that the ‘broad reform’ of the criminal justice system intended to grant these basic rights mandated in the Victims’ Bill of Rights initiative measure passed by the electorate as Proposition 8 in 1982 has not occurred as envisioned by the people,” a not-so-subtle swipe at the California Supreme Court and its abandonment of Proposition 8.[REF]

Marsy’s Law amended Section 28 of Article I of the state’s constitution by enumerating victims’ rights “in order to protect the legitimate rights of victims of crime.”[REF] Key among those rights were (1) the right to “have the safety of the victim and the victim’s family considered in fixing the amount of bail and release conditions for the defendant” under Section 28(b)(3) and (2) the right to public safety bail: “A person may be released on bail by sufficient sureties, except for capital crime when the facts are evident…. [I]n setting, reducing or denying bail, the judge or magistrate shall take into consideration the protection of the public, the safety of the victim, the seriousness of the offense charged…. [P]ublic safety and the safety of the victim shall be the primary considerations” under Section 28(f)(3).[REF]

Notice how California voters again adopted language to reject the California Supreme Court, this time to rebut the court’s creative erasure of the bail provisions of Proposition 8. By using “may” instead of “shall,” the people made clear both that judges do indeed have the discretion to deny bail and that the safety of the victim must be the primary consideration.

Part II: Cash Bail and Victims’ Rights

Cash bail rests on a straightforward proposition: Pretrial liberty and accountability need not be opposing principles. By placing a financial stake behind a defendant’s promise to return to court, bail supplies a concrete incentive to appear while permitting release pending adjudication—not punishment before conviction. From the perspective of the California District Attorneys Association (CDAA) and other prosecutor associations across the country, traditional cash bail remains an important component of a pretrial system charged with protecting the public and securing defendants’ appearance. Bail schedules give that system a practical framework: Predetermined, offense-based amounts provide a consistent starting point, make release requirements more predictable, and can allow eligible defendants to obtain release without waiting for an initial court appearance.

Properly understood, however, a schedule is a starting point, not a substitute for judicial judgment. Neither money nor a printed chart guarantees public safety or attendance; individualized review must account for the defendant’s circumstances and ability to pay, available nonfinancial alternatives, and applicable constitutional protections. The sound argument for bail schedules is therefore not that they eliminate discretion, but that they discipline its exercise, providing an orderly framework within which courts can reconcile liberty, accountability, and public safety.

As a policy matter, the push to end cash bail is not new in our country. In 1961, progressives created the Manhattan Bail Project specifically to eliminate cash bail.[REF] That project eventually grew into the Vera Institute and dozens of other organizations like Vera that are obsessed with ending cash bail. They even came up with spooky-sounding terms like “poverty penalty,” suggesting that the only reason a person remained in jail pending trial was his or her inability to post bond, ignoring the facts of the crime alleged or history of failures to appear in court or long criminal records—the Achilles’ heels of the movement. In 2017, former career public defender Robin Steinberg founded the Bail Project, an organization the provides free bail assistance to those who cannot afford to post bail, in California.[REF]

In 2018, the California Senate passed S.B. 10, a law designed to eliminate cash bail in the state and replace it with mandatory risk assessments for detained suspects awaiting trial.[REF] After the governor signed the bill, a unique mix of stakeholders banded together to repeal the legislation by referendum during the November 2020 election. Supporters of the “no” vote to repeal the controversial legislation included, in addition to the California Republican Party, the American Civil Liberties Union (ACLU) of Southern California, California Black Chamber of Commerce, California NAACP, and Crime Victims United of California, among other groups. The “yes” vote effort was backed by the California Democratic Party and included financial support from Patty Quillin ($1 million, the wife of Netflix CEO Reed Hastings; Steve and Connie Ballmer of Microsoft ($6 million total); and Tom Steyer, founder of Farallon Capital hedge fund and the NextGen Climate Action Committee ($500,000).[REF] California Proposition 25 repealed S.B. 10 by a margin of 56.41 percent to 43.59 percent and effectively restored cash bail.[REF]

That same year, George Gascon was elected Los Angeles County District Attorney, in large part because of an infusion of $2.5 million from George Soros, who gave the money to the California Justice & Safety PAC, which supported Gascon’s campaign.[REF] Upon election, Gascon set up a transition team populated with 38 anti–law-and-order activists and zealots tasked with implementing his progressive (including anti–cash bail) agenda.[REF] This was significant because the Los Angeles County District Attorney’s Office is the largest DA’s office in the country, with upwards of 900-plus prosecutors spread across the massive county. Since one of the hallmarks of the Soros rogue prosecutor movement was to eliminate cash bail, Gascon’s “bail committee” was headed by Robin Steinberg and Dolores Caneles, Community Outreach Director for the Bail Project.[REF]

Moments after Gascon was sworn into office, he issued a blizzard of policy directives. Special Directive 20-06, concerning “Pretrial Release Policy,” claimed that “unaffordable cash bail” creates “a two-tiered system of justice,” leads to “unnecessary incarceration,” and “eviscerates the bedrock of our democracy and undermines our principles of justice, fairness and equality under the law.”[REF] Even though Proposition 25 had just failed, the directive said the D.A.’s Office “will not wait for statewide reform before imposing meaningful changes in the use of cash bail.”[REF] In what has become a mantra for the anti-bail cabal, the directive blurted out that “freedom should be free.”[REF]

The directive does not mention that certain criminals, especially career felons, pose a significant risk of danger to the community and that cash bail is a tool that works to ensure their appearance at future court dates. Nor does the directive mention the law (such as Marsy’s Law), victims’ rights in the California Constitution, or the fact that public safety is and should be the “primary consideration” when setting the appropriate amount of bail.

Gascon’s diktat prohibited his prosecutors from taking into consideration an accused’s criminal history, requesting cash bail for any misdemeanor, and relying solely on scored risk assessment tools for detention.[REF] In a move that likely upset career prosecutors in his office, Gascon’s directive prohibited them from opposing defense motions to remove or modify conditions of release, defense requests to waive client appearances at “non-essential” court proceedings, or defense requests seeking relief from bench warrants for defendants who fail to appear.[REF] In other words, Gascon prevented his prosecutors from being prosecutors.

Part III: Humphrey and Kowalczyk

Two years before Proposition 25 repealed S.B. 10 but after S.B. 10 was the law in California, the Court of Appeals for the First District of California held in In Re Kenneth Humphrey that a trial judge (1) failed to make findings and inquiries regarding the financial ability of the defendant to post bail; (2) failed to consider whether less restrictive conditions of release were available; and (3) set bail based solely on the bail schedule rather than on an individualized inquiry.[REF] As a result, the appeals court reversed and remanded the case to the trial court. The trial court conducted a new bail hearing and ordered Humphrey released on various non-financial conditions.

The issue was resolved, and there was no live case or controversy, but that did not stop the California Supreme Court from wading into the issue. Typically, the top appellate court in a state takes cases only where there is a live case or controversy. But not in California, where its Supreme Court injected itself into the bail debate by granting review on its own motion in the Humphrey case. In a 2021 opinion, the court noted that it granted review “to address the constitutionality of money bail as currently used in California as well as the proper role of public and victim safety in making bail determinations.”[REF]

Despite the fact that Humphrey had won his case and obtained the relief he sought, the court held that “where a financial condition is nonetheless necessary, the court must consider the arrestee’s ability to pay the state amount of bail—and may not effectively detain the arrestee solely because the arrestee lacked the resources to post bail.”[REF] The court handed down its decision on March 21, 2021—four months after November 3, 2020, when the voters had soundly rejected Proposition 25 and its idea of eliminating cash bail and replacing it with a scheme based on risk assessment—and did not even mention the results of Proposition 25 in its opinion.

The court noted that in “unusual circumstances” when the “need to protect community safety may conflict with the arrestee’s fundamental right to pretrial liberty…[ to detain an arrestee] a court must find by clear and convincing evidence that no condition short of detention could suffice and then ensure [that] the detention otherwise complies with statutory constitutional requirements.”[REF] If Justice Antonin Scalia had been on the Humphrey court, he would have ridiculed the majority’s “interpretive jiggery-pokery.”[REF]

Courts have no business opining on hypothetical issues or issuing policy statements under the fig leaf of a judicial opinion. Our Founders realized this and embedded limitations on the jurisdiction of United States federal courts in Article III, Section 2, Clause 1 of the U.S. Constitution. As the Supreme Court noted in Flast v. Cohen, the judicial power of the federal courts “is constitutionally restricted to ‘cases’ and ‘controversies.’”[REF] Those two words “have an iceberg quality, containing beneath their surface simplicity submerged complexities which go to the very heart of our constitutional form of government.”[REF] Those two words “limit the business of federal courts to questions presented in an adversary context and in a form historically viewed as capable of resolution through the judicial process.”[REF]

Similar to the federal Constitution, the California Constitution divides the power of the government among the legislative, executive, and judicial branches, but it does not contain a case-or-controversy clause. Under Article VI, Section 12(a), the state’s Supreme Court “may, before decision, transfer to itself a cause in a court of appeal.”[REF] Under Subsection (b), however, the “Supreme Court may review the decision of a court of appeal in any cause.”[REF] The California Supreme Court has interpreted this clause as allowing it to issue advisory opinions, and the “cause” it took up here was obvious: Draft an opinion that discovers a constitutional right to affordable bail and enshrine that right across the state despite the will of the people as recently affirmed by the result of Proposition 25. The court did not cite Section 12 as a justification to render an advisory opinion.

Amicus briefs in support of Humphrey included Chesa Boudin, a San Francisco Deputy Public Defender, various ACLU offices across California, the California Public Defenders Association, and the American Bar Association.[REF] Amicus briefs supporting the People of California included elected district attorneys George Gascon, Summer Stephan, Michael Ramos, and (at least at first) Xavier Becerra, the state’s Attorney General.[REF]

The unanimous decision reads like a policy paper from the Bail Project. The decision notes that an “accused may be impaired to some extent in preparing a defense” and that “studies suggest that pretrial detention heightens the risk of losing a job,” referencing two slanted law review articles critical of pretrial detention, one of which was published by Harvard Law School’s Criminal Justice Program (CJP).[REF]

The two issues not resolved in Humphrey were (1) whether Section 12 of the state constitution “can or should be” reconciled with the public safety bail provision of Marsy’s Law in Section 28(f)(3) and (2) whether it is constitutionally permissible for a trial court to set bail above a defendant’s ability to pay.

The court did not note that the lead author of the Harvard paper was Elana Fogel of the ACLU’s Initiative to Combat Mass Incarceration[REF] or that one of the two faculty co-directors of the CJP was Professor Carol Steiker, a zealous anti–death penalty advocate.[REF] Nor did the court mention the fact that among other people who provided “generous insights and feedback” to the paper were none other than Patrisse Cullors, a radical Marxist, avowed prison abolitionist, and co-founder of Black Lives Matter, and Alec Karakatsanis, a public defender and fellow traveler on the abolish-prisons train.[REF]

To justify its opinion, the court wrote that “principles of equal protection and substantive due process likewise converge in the money bail context. The accused retains the fundamental constitutional right to liberty,” citing the Supreme Court’s opinion in United States v. Salerno.[REF] The problem for the court is that Salerno did not say that. In fact, Salerno held (1) that the federal Bail Reform Act’s authorization of pretrial detention on the basis of future dangerousness constituted a permissible regulation that did not violate substantive due process and was not impermissible punishment before trial and (2) that the Due Process Clause did not categorically prohibit pretrial detention imposed as a regulatory measure on the ground of community danger.[REF] The page in Salerno that the California Supreme Court referenced in its opinion for the proposition that an accused “retains the fundamental right to liberty” was ripped out of context.

In analyzing the Bail Reform Act’s procedures, the U.S. Supreme Court contrasted the “government’s interest in preventing crime,” which it described as “both legitimate and compelling,” with the “other side of the scale…the individual’s strong interest in liberty.”[REF] The Court noted the “importance and fundamental nature of this right” but then added that, “as our cases hold, this right may, in circumstances where the government’s interest is sufficiently weighty, be subordinated to the greater needs of society.”[REF] Nevertheless, the California Supreme Court held in Humphrey that pretrial detention based solely on an accused’s financial condition violates state and federal equal protection and due process principles.[REF]

Those cases set the stage for the latest outrage from the California Supreme Court. In In re Kowalczyk, the court addressed two important questions left unresolved by In re Humphrey: whether Article I, Section 12, or Article I, Section 28(f)(3) of the California Constitution controls the denial of bail in noncapital cases and whether a trial court may set monetary bail above a defendant’s ability to pay. The case arose after Gerald Kowalczyk used several lost credit cards at a fast-food restaurant and was charged with identity theft and related offenses. The allegations themselves were comparatively modest, but Kowalczyk’s record was anything but modest: He had accumulated 64 prior convictions, had failed to comply with supervision while previously released on his own recognizance, and had convictions in multiple counties and states.

The trial court first set bail at $75,000 and later denied bail altogether, citing Kowalczyk’s extensive criminal history, his poor record of compliance, and the substantial risk that he would not appear for future proceedings. While Kowalczyk’s habeas petition was pending, he accepted a plea agreement, received credit for time served, and was released.

The Court of Appeal initially dismissed the petition as moot, but the California Supreme Court transferred the matter back with instructions to decide the constitutional questions. The case thus became the vehicle for resolving a conflict among the Courts of Appeal over the meaning of California’s competing bail provisions and the reach of Humphrey.

The dispute centered on two commands adopted at different times: Section 12’s direction that a person “shall be released on bail,” subject to enumerated exceptions for specified violent or threatening felonies, and Marsy’s Law’s later declaration in Section 28(f)(3) that a person “may be released on bail” and that public safety and victim safety “shall be the primary considerations” when bail is set, reduced, or denied.

The court held that the two provisions could be “harmonized,” but only by treating Section 12 as the controlling limitation on judicial authority. In the court’s view, Section 12 supplies the exclusive categories in which bail may be denied in a noncapital case: certain violent felonies, felony sexual assaults, and other felonies involving threats of great bodily harm, coupled with the constitutionally required findings.

Section 28(f)(3), the court reasoned, does not enlarge those categories. Instead, its use of the word “may” describes only the possibility that a defendant may or may not be released, while its references to public safety, victim safety, and “denying bail” operate only within Section 12’s preexisting framework. Relying on the presumption against implied repeal, the court concluded that Marsy’s Law neither displaced Section 12 nor gave judges broader authority to detain dangerous defendants charged with offenses outside Section 12’s enumerated exceptions.

On the second question, the court held that when preventive detention is unavailable under Section 12, bail generally must be set at an amount reasonably attainable by the particular defendant. A court must first consider whether nonfinancial conditions—such as supervision, monitoring, or required check-ins—can reasonably protect the public, safeguard the victim, and assure the defendant’s appearance. If monetary bail is necessary, the judge must conduct an individualized assessment that includes the seriousness of the offense, the defendant’s criminal record, compliance history, flight risk, danger to others, financial resources, and the availability of less restrictive alternatives. The amount need not be convenient or easily affordable, and a defendant must substantiate a claimed inability to pay. But a judge may not deliberately set an insurmountable figure as an end run around Section 12’s limits on detention.

Although its conclusion may be dressed in the familiar robes of harmonization, the court once again managed to find a creative way to write around what the people enacted. Marsy’s Law did not say that public safety and victim safety should merely be considered after judges had exhausted the narrow detention categories contained in Section 12. It declared that a person “may” be released on bail; expressly governed the “setting, reducing, or denying” of bail; and commanded that public safety and victim safety “shall be the primary considerations.” Those words were adopted against a well-developed constitutional history in which the difference between “shall” and “may” had already carried unmistakable significance. Rather than give the voters’ chosen language its ordinary force, the court converted “may” into a vague statement of abstract possibility and reduced the public-safety mandate to a procedural appendage. That is not faithful harmonization. It is judicial revision.

The court’s reliance on the presumption against implied repeal also obscures the central issue. No one needed to prove that Marsy’s Law silently erased every word of Section 12. The task was to give meaningful effect to both provisions, including the later amendment’s explicit reference to denying bail and its elevation of safety above all other considerations.

A genuinely harmonious reading would recognize that Section 12 preserves a general right to bail while Section 28(f)(3) grants courts discretion to deny it when compelling public-safety or victim-safety concerns demand that result. Instead, the court treated the older provision as a ceiling on the newer one, leaving the operative language of Marsy’s Law with remarkably little independent work to do. The result is especially difficult to defend in a case involving a defendant with 64 prior convictions, repeated supervision failures, and a demonstrated history spanning multiple jurisdictions—the very sort of record that makes a categorical refusal to recognize broader judicial discretion appear detached from practical reality.

Just as troubling is the court’s dismissive treatment of amici curiae on the other side, especially the California District Attorneys Association. The CDAA’s amicus brief made the straightforward textual point that Section 28(f)(3)’s express reference to “denying bail” must do real work; otherwise, the phrase is substantially drained of meaning. The court brushed that argument aside as “unpersuasive,” reasoning that “denying” bail refers only to defendants who are already eligible for detention without bail under Section 12. But that answer assumes the very conclusion the amici challenged.

It does not explain why the voters added broader, later language addressing the denial of bail, used “may” instead of “shall,” and made public and victim safety primary if they intended no meaningful expansion of judicial authority.

The irony is difficult to miss. The amici’s arguments were grounded in the constitutional text, the history of California’s competing bail provisions, the structure of Marsy’s Law, and the electorate’s unmistakable demand that victims and public safety come first. The California Supreme Court’s contrary logic depends on redefining ordinary words, minimizing later-enacted language, and insulating its preferred result behind an unusually rigid application of the rule against implied repeal.

Having disconnected its analysis from the text and from the voters’ evident purpose, the court then pronounced the more natural reading “unpersuasive.” Once again, the people spoke through the initiative process, and once again, the court found a creative way to tell them that the words they enacted do not mean what they plainly say.

Conclusion

Once again, the court has mistaken judicial invention for constitutional interpretation, elevating a newly minted right to “reasonably attainable” bail over the text, structure, and history of California’s constitution—and over the repeated commands of the people themselves. A constitution that makes public safety and victim safety the “primary considerations” cannot fairly be read to require judges to price dangerous or chronically noncompliant defendants out of detention.

California’s legislature should likewise conduct public hearings; compel testimony from prosecutors, sheriffs, victims, and pretrial-services officials; and require transparent reporting on rearrests, absconding, supervision failures, and crimes committed by defendants released under the new rule.

The amendment should state in plain English that criminal defendants do not have a constitutional right to bail set at an amount they can afford when they are accused of a violent crime, present a serious danger to a victim or the public, pose a substantial flight risk, repeatedly violate release conditions, or have a long criminal record demonstrating chronic recidivism or noncompliance. It should also reaffirm that judges may deny bail in those circumstances and that protection of victims and the public—not a defendant’s financial convenience—remains the primary consideration.

Finally, Californians should use every lawful mechanism of judicial accountability that is available to them. If members of the court persist in rewriting voter-enacted constitutional commands, the public should seriously consider recall proceedings against the responsible justices, consistent with California law and with full respect for due process. The Nvidia scandal fuels tougher China chip rules as Commerce targets Singapore and Malaysia

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia’s CEO on Thursday, critics of the U.S.’s policy of restricting chip sales to China lost one of their biggest allies.

In addition to being one of the industry’s biggest proponents of the AI buildout, Huang remains one of the most vocal critics of U.S. policies that bar the sale of advanced chips and chipmaking equipment to China. He claimed that these measures would encourage the creation of a competing Chinese chip industry, and that it was better for the U.S. to keep Chinese companies hooked on U.S. technology.

Nvidia’s CEO was also a strong supporter of open-source AI models—many of which came from Chinese developers like DeepSeek—in the face of criticism that such models posed a serious cybersecurity threat.

Revelations that Huang had an affair with Alice Huang (no relation)—a former executive at Megaspeed, a Singaporean company suspected of funnelling controlled chips to China—has torched the Nvidia CEO’s credibility on the issue of export controls. (Some reports claim U.S. intelligence considered Alice Huang to be part of a Chinese espionage campaign against Nvidia, but others have cast doubt on that assessment).

On Monday morning, the U.S. Department of Commerce released the first update to its export control regime since the scandal broke, issuing an interim final rule that closes a longstanding loophole that allowed Chinese companies to access AI chips based in overseas data centers, and expands export controls to an array of Southeast Asian countries. Congress, too, is debating several laws that would codify and expand existing export controls.

Yet tighter rules may also complicate matters for other countries trying to forge a more neutral path between the U.S. and China

Current export controls block the most advanced AI processors—made by companies like Nvidia, AMD, and Intel—from being sold to Chinese companies, though the Trump administration has allowed sales of Nvidia’s H200 to approved Chinese customers. Chipmaking equipment and high-bandwidth memory are also blocked.

Commerce’s new rule—which will go into effect immediately once it’s included in the Federal Register—would require licenses to export advanced AI chips to data centers in Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil. It would also require a license if large data center operators anywhere in the world want to grant access to companies headquartered in China, effectively closing the remote access loophole. Data centers already serving such companies will have 90 days from the rule’s effective date to get a license.

The Trump administration may remain split on how to act against chip smuggling; interestingly, the press release from the Commerce Department notes the rules were “written by and at the direction of the Department of Treasury and the National Security Council.” But Congress may act if the Trump administration remains divided.

“The regulatory path in Washington is going to be harder and bumpier,” Hanna Dohmen, a senior research analyst at Georgetown’s Center for Security and Emerging Technology, told Fortune before the rule’s release. She pointed out that several bills have been included in this year’s National Defense Authorization Act. The Huang scandal “adds momentum to push those bills through, and the chance of those passing are likelier than ever.”

“No matter what Trump does, it’s possible that Congress should be able to provide limitations,” Joshua Kurlantzick, a senior fellow at the Council on Foreign Relations, said before the rule’s release. “It’s not only about Trump anymore.”

Congress, meanwhile, is closer than ever to locking those controls into law.

The first bill, the Remote Access Security Act, also seeks to close the remote access loophole, giving the Commerce Department authority to license and penalize foreign companies tapping advanced U.S. AI chips from abroad. (The House passed the bill in January and is still awaiting consideration in the Senate).

The second bill, the Chip Security Act, would require advanced chips to ship with  security measures, like location tracking, to ensure processors aren’t diverted upon export. It also requires exporters to promptly disclose evidence of diversion or smuggling. Yet the act does not go as far as to mandate kill switches or more aggressive measures to stop smuggling.

The third, the AI Overwatch Act, would treat advanced chips like weapons: before any sale to China or other adversaries, the administration would have to notify Congress, which could then block the deal with a joint resolution of disapproval. It also carries an “America First AI” provision requiring Commerce to certify that exports won’t leave U.S. customers short of computing power.

The fourth, the MATCH Act, may be the most aggressive. The act would largely ban the sale and servicing of deep ultraviolet lithography tools across China, and imposes broader restrictions on facilities associated with major Chinese chipmakers like SMIC and CXMT. It gives allies like Japan and the Netherlands 150 days to align their own controls with Washington’s; if they fail, the U.S. can extend controls to equipment supplied from those countries, even if they contain no U.S.-made components.

Still, Dohmen pointed out that changing the rules alone won’t be enough to entirely halt chip smuggling. Several measures still require follow-on enforcement by either the U.S. or third parties; she pointed out that knowing that a chip was smuggled matters little if the Bureau of Industry and Security doesn’t act.

Much of the attention during the Nvidia scandal has fallen on Southeast Asia, and Singapore and Malaysia in particular. Megaspeed, the company accused of funnelling Nvidia chips to China, is based in Singapore; Malaysia was also reportedly the home of Megaspeed’s data centers. Megaspeed has denied sending chips to China.

“The administration wants to encourage Malaysia and Singapore to remain in the American AI ecosystem, but it doesn’t want them to become conduits through which Chinese firms can obtain advanced chips—or remotely access the computing power those chips provide,” Jennifer Lind, associate professor of government at Dartmouth College, said. “That has been its policy so far and I don’t anticipate this changing.”

The new Commerce Department rule does offer a carve-out for end-users that are, or are owned by, a U.S.-headquartered company. The region has attracted billions of dollars of investment from U.S. tech companies such as Microsoft, Oracle, and Amazon, which should qualify for that exemption, though the rule’s remote access limits still apply to them.

While both Singapore and Malaysia have made some public moves against chip smuggling, Kurlantzick said he’s “more inclined” to believe the Singaporean government was taking the matter seriously. Malaysia, in contrast, “wasn’t really doing anything, and didn’t care,” he said. “The U.S. is insanely unpopular in Malaysia.”

Stopping Chinese companies from accessing chips in overseas data centers could be a problem for countries like Malaysia, which want to become data center hubs. “For Malaysia, the controls could be disastrous,” Kurlantzick said. “From what Prime Minister Anwar Ibrahim sees, their biggest potential future industry is a data center and AI hub for Chinese companies.”

The U.S. is also trying to draw countries into its “Pax Silica” framework, an initiative to secure semiconductor and critical mineral supply chains that bypass China. Beijing has a competing framework, the World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation Organization; the U.S. is pushing countries to choose one or the other. (Trump is in Manila this week for the ASEAN Summit, where he’s expected to persuade Southeast Asian countries to join Pax Silica—and perhaps now answer some awkward questions about these expanded rules.)

Still, Kurlantzick thinks the U.S.’s lack of attention to the region will hold back efforts to get other countries on board. “The U.S. isn’t really offering Southeast Asian countries–or really anyone in Asia–much in return for their demands,” he said.

The biggest barrier to chips entering China right now isn’t Washington, but Beijing. The Chinese government has largely withheld permission for its companies to import Nvidia chips, instead pushing them to tap domestic chips made by companies like Huawei. DeepSeek in particular has led the drive to get Chinese AI models to run well on Huawei’s Ascend processors.

“Export controls still matter,” Lind said, though she admitted they may matter less now. “China is already well on its way toward developing its own AI ecosystem; Huawei makes its own chips for training and running AI models, and a software platform (CANN) that’s an alternative to Nvidia’s CUDA ecosystem,” she said.

She’s paying more attention to “whether semiconductor manufacturing equipment continues to be restricted—whether China develops the capability to manufacture its own frontier chips,” Lind said. “The Trump administration continues to restrict SME exports, and will likely continue to.”

Still, Huawei’s Ascend processors are not as good as the best Nvidia AI processors, particularly when it comes to training models.

Before the BIS announced its rule on Monday, Dohmen suggested that if Chinese companies can’t access Nvidia chips overseas, the roughly 600,000 H200s that Reuters reported are gathering dust in Hong Kong look more attractive.

“Would the Chinese government be more willing to let those H200s flow into China?” she asked. “If there is certain significant demand for remote access, and that’s cut off, there may actually be more need for the H200s.”

It’s not yet clear that China can supply all the computing power its AI developers need, particularly if overseas access disappears. China “does not have the capacity at this point to meet that demand,” Dohmen said.

Physical Therapists Share the Most Effective QL Stretches and Exercises for Runners With Lower Back and Hip Pain

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Here’s how to rehab this deep lower back muscle at home—and when it’s time to book a session with your PT.

Most runners are familiar with the big muscles involved in running, such as the quads, hamstrings, and glutes. But there are lesser-known—yet still important—players that can influence running performance. One example: the quadratus lumborum, or QL for short.

A deep trunk muscle, the QL attaches to the lower spine and the back side of the pelvis, Nicole Haas, PT, DPT, a physical therapist and founder of Boulder Physiolab in Colorado, tells Runner’s World. It serves a few different functions in daily life, such as helping you arch your back, bend to the side, and rotate your upper body, Haas explains.

When you run, the QL plays more of a stabilizing role, working to control your pelvis and trunk while you stride, says Spencer Agnew, PT, DPT, a physical therapist at Sword Health and run coach with Wisconsin-based Ascent Endurance Group. The QL also assists with breathing, specifically when you inhale, Agnew adds.

If this muscle is tight or irritated, you’ll likely feel pain in your back or sacroiliac joints, which link the pelvis and lower spine, Agnew says. To help you avoid these aches, we asked the PTs to explain what can cause an angry QL and how to relieve it.

There are several reasons your QL might be yelling at you. A common one? Your hip flexors are tight. In particular, when the psoas (a big, rope-like hip flexor muscle that runs from your lower back to the front of your hip) is shortened, it can pull your pelvis into an anterior tilt, meaning your pelvis is tipped forward, arching your lower back. In many cases, this yanks on your QL as well, because the psoas and QL both insert into the same group of vertebrae in your lower back, Agnew explains.

Another culprit is weak glutes. When your glutes aren’t strong enough to help you maintain good running mechanics, your knee can collapse inward as you land and your opposite-side hip can dip down, Haas explains. This, in turn, can cause your trunk to side bend and rotate, thus placing more strain on your QL over time. Running downhill can exacerbate the problem, Haas adds, because that can cause your lower back to arch and place further stress on your QL.

Yet another cause of an irritated QL is upper-back stiffness. Restrictions in the upper part of the spine can shift more workload onto the QL, Haas explains. So can issues in your shoulders, hips, and mid-back. If these areas aren’t moving like they should due to pain or injury, your QL may receive added stress, Haas says.

Keep in mind that in some cases, your QL may hurt because it’s taking on a bunch of extra stress it’s not meant to, but the muscle itself probably isn’t tight. “It’s more like your QL is just irritated because everything has been collapsing around it and loading it,” Haas says. “It’s overworked and underappreciated.”

A tight-feeling QL is worth addressing because it can lead to a few not-so-great issues if left unchecked. A big one? Compromised running mechanics.

A tight QL, as mentioned, can shorten your hip flexors and pull your pelvis into an anterior tilt, which will reduce your knee lift as you stride, Agnew says. As a result, “you’re not going to be able to generate as much force moving forward, and you’ll have more of an arched-back position when you run,” he explains. In many cases, this can deliver a double whammy of reduced performance and increased back pain, he says.

On top of that, a tight QL can also hinder your breathing. As mentioned above, one of the QL’s jobs is to help you inhale, so when this muscle is tight, you may not be able to breathe as deeply or easily, Agnew says.

Plagued by what you think may be an irritated QL? Try Agnew’s three-step at-home series to help reduce your discomfort.

Perform this sequence as often as every day if you’re in significant discomfort that’s limiting your ability to run. In the case of minor pain, incorporate this series two to three times per week. On the days you run, perform the myofascial release, pelvic tilts, and hip hikes before you lace up, then tackle the other components afterward.

This can help turn down the pain response in a barking QL by desensitizing soft tissue, Agnew explains.

The pelvic tilts help you learn how to coordinate and control pelvic motion so you can maintain proper mechanics while running, and the hip hikes help activate your QL. The kettlebell side bend increases blood flow to the muscle, which can aid in healing. Finally, the suitcase carry increases strength, stability, and engagement of the muscle, which can ensure it functions properly during a run.

Lengthen your QL—as well as tight hip flexors—with this two-for-one half-kneeling lunge stretch.

Do these moves daily or several times per day to mobilize your lower back. Perform them before or after a run. Important caveat: If your QL hurts more after stretching—or if your QL pain returns soon after stretching—then stretches are likely not the solution and you should stop doing them, Haas explains.

To start, do one set of these moves daily to help your body learn the neuromuscular patterns. (The caveat: If you feel sore, then skip a day.) After about six weeks, do these moves two to three times per week as part of your regular strength-training routine.

This exercise targets the glutes and works on pelvic control in a movement pattern that’s similar to running. Think of it as an opportunity to train proper glute use in your running form, which will help unload any strain on your QL caused by weak glutes.

This exercise can improve core stability and strengthen the glutes, resulting in improved running mechanics and less stress on your QL. You’ll need a long, looped resistance band.

Jiff 1.0: A datetime library for Rust

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Jiff is a datetime library for Rust that encourages you to jump into the pit of success. Jiff automatically handles many common problems related to daylight saving time by putting time zone handling front and center into the API. Jiff has already seen widespread adoption in the Rust ecosystem.

Jiff is pronounced like “gif” with a soft “g,” as in “gem.”

This blog announces the Jiff 1.0 release by giving a tour of its API.

Target audience: Rust programmers who need convenient datetime handling.

Jiff is already used in production in many projects: uv, Cargo, Starship, fd, Nushell, mise, Jujutsu, uutils/coreutils and even as the basis for a Temporal implementation in AWS LLRT. It is time to call it 1.0.

Unless some significant defect is found in Jiff’s API, my plan is to commit to Jiff 1.0 indefinitely. This is important because the intent is for Jiff to be an ecosystem building block that appears in the public API of other libraries. This makes Jiff an attractive public dependency, which means semver incompatible releases are likely to cause significant pain in the ecosystem. They will not be made lightly.

To see a list of breaking changes between Jiff 0.2 and Jiff 1.0, please consult the CHANGELOG.

In programming, a datetime library’s core functionality rests at the intersection of a computer system’s concept of time and a human’s concept of time. The prevailing notion of system time is Unix time. A human’s concept of time is far more complex, but the most popular such model is the Gregorian calendar coupled with either a 12-hour or 24-hour clock. At the core of every datetime library rests some routine for converting between these concepts.

A datetime library typically provides much more than this. Indeed, datetime handling has historically been very difficult. My own belief is that the difficulty comes from poor modeling of the domain. Instead of encapsulating the complexity inside a reusable bundle of code, many datetime libraries pass some or all of the complexity on to its users. Jiff instead tries hard to encapsulate the complexity and make it difficult for users to do something wrong. (Jiff is not the first to do this!)

When should you use each one versus the other?

To a first approximation, if you can use a ZonedDateTime, then that is probably the type you want to reach for. It represents a specific instant in time in a particular geographic location. That is, it has both a Timestamp and a TimeZone.

If you only ever need to work in UTC or just deal with system time, then a Timestamp is probably the right choice. This is useful for representing a specific instant in time that is not connected to any time zone.

A civil::DateTime is useful in contexts where you don’t care about a specific instant in time and when calcuations should be done independent of time zone. For example, when parsing datetime information from a user interface datetime picker.

Remember that both a civil::DateTime and a Timestamp can be converted to a ZonedDateTime by supplying a time zone.

So far, we’ve explored a good chunk of Jiff’s API. We didn’t talk about every nook and cranny, but I think we’ve covered the most important pieces.

We’ll wrap up by briefly discussing motivation and inspiration.

Whenever I needed datetime handling in Rust, I always got confused. Should I use chrono or time? How do I get my local time from a timestamp? How do I use my system’s time zone database? How do I do calendar arithmetic?

Before I started working on Jiff, this confusion got annoying enough for me to consider writing my own crate. I felt like the APIs of both chrono and time could be improved upon. I personally found the domain quite interesting and so set out to build something better.

Why not improve on an existing solution, like chrono or time, instead of adding yet another choice into the ecosystem? I do think there are material downsides of having too many choices, but I had concluded that both chrono and time (in 2024) had reached a local maximum. Moreover, in 2024, I was not an expert in datetime handling. It took building a datetime library to become one. So convincing crate maintainers to go in a radically different direction would have been a tall order.

Jiff’s design and comparison documentation contain much more detail regarding the motivation and specific examples between Jiff, chrono and time.

Jiff is heavily inspired by Temporal, which is JavaScript’s standard library solution for date and time management. Jiff inherits API design, functionality and even parsing/formatting of datetime values. Note though that Jiff is not and does not aim to be a 100% compatible implementation of the Temporal specification. Jiff is meant to be a Rust ecosystem library first and foremost.

With that said, if you’re familiar with Temporal and Rust, then it’s likely you’ll find Jiff to be a relatively straightforward translation of Temporal’s API to Rust. This example shows how to use Temporal to round a calendar span to the nearest number of days relative to a date:

Five European Countries Plan to Deport Migrants to Rwanda

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Germany, Austria, the Netherlands, Greece and Denmark are seeking to establish a hub for rejected asylum seekers. Here’s what to know about their effort.

Five European countries announced a plan on Monday that would send rejected asylum seekers to Rwanda, an experiment that comes as governments across the continent toughen their stances on migration.

The joint push from Germany, Austria, Denmark, the Netherlands and Greece was pitched as a way to handle migrants who had no right to stay in Europe but who, for one reason or another, could not or would not return to their home countries.

The project, which is not yet finalized, shows how European leaders’ stance on migration has hardened since the arrivals of 2015-16, when countries like Germany admitted more than a million asylum seekers. With far-right groups like the Alternative for Germany rising in popularity, centrist leaders have gradually taken a less welcoming stance.

“For me, today marks a crucial step toward a new migration system,” Mette Frederiksen, the prime minister of Denmark, said at a news conference on Monday.

The pilot project is expected to start on a small scale, with perhaps 100 or 200 deportees. Critics are already worried both about the idea of sending people to a nation to which they have no connection, and about the risk that human rights will not be sufficiently protected in Rwanda.

The country has a history of courting migration-related deals.

Britain developed a plan to send asylum seekers there in 2022, though judges in London ultimately ruled the effort unlawful and it was later abandoned. The United States has recently deported people to Rwanda.
