‘Kamen Rider’ Studio Toei Frames Generative AI Rules, Sets First Video Games for 2027

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Toei, the Japanese studio giant behind “Kamen Rider” and the Super Sentai series that became “Power Rangers,” has drawn up rules for generative AI and started using AI in its film and video production, president and CEO Yoshimura Fumio said at the Taiwan Creative Content Fest (TCCF) in Taipei.

A dedicated team at the company is testing how far AI can be used in actual content production, and Toei is building an AI system that helps less experienced producers estimate budgets. “We have put in place rules for the use of generative AI, and from this year we have started using AI in our film and video production,” Yoshimura said. “We are already working on things like creating backgrounds with AI.”

Toei’s back-office departments also use AI for routine tasks, data processing and internal queries, and the company is looking at applying it to the creative supervision of IP merchandise. Yoshimura pointed to “Dreams of Violets,” a live-action feature made entirely with generative AI that was selected for the Tribeca Festival in June. The 75-minute film was reportedly made by two creators for around $2,000.

“I believe AI is not something that takes away jobs, but a tool that expands the possibilities of expression and business,” he said.

Toei also put a timeline on its move into games. Toei Games, the label launched this year to mark the company’s 75th anniversary, will release “Hino” in early 2027, with “Killa” and “Debug Nephemee” following from spring 2027. All three are original titles from outside creators rather than adaptations of Toei IP, and will launch on Steam before rolling out to Nintendo Switch, PlayStation and Xbox.

“We aim to bring game creators’ talent on board over the long term as a new vein of IP creation, and make games the starting point for stories in place of novels and comics,” Yoshimura said.

The keynote also mapped out how Toei is taking “Kamen Rider,” which turned 55 this year, beyond Japanese TV. Yoshimura held up the franchise as his main example of what the company calls the “evergreening” of IP. The first series premiered in 1971, and the Showa-era shows that ran until 1988 built its core fanbase. The Heisei-era series that began in 2000 handed the franchise down from parents to children, while events, merchandise, manga, anime and stage shows have since carried it to a third generation.

The current series, “Kamen Rider Zeztz,” was rolled out almost simultaneously around the world, including Taiwan, the rest of Asia and North America, which Yoshimura called a big step for the group. Toei is also pushing into India. “Kamen Rider” was introduced there at a Japanese IP event in 2025, and the strong response led to a deal with a local Disney-affiliated company.

“Considering the population size and rising incomes from a growing middle class, I think India can be called an extremely promising market in the medium to long term,” Yoshimura said. “We expect it will take time before this takes root as a business, but it’s important to build connections now and steadily grow our partnerships and fan base.”

In April, Toei launched The Kamen Rider Animated, an animation label. Its first project teams the studio with Aniplex, the company behind “Demon Slayer,” and Shirogumi, which worked on Yamazaki Takashi’s “Godzilla Minus One,” on a new “Kamen Rider” property with a scale and style of action only animation can deliver. A live-action label, The Kamen Rider Premium, will make full-scale hero action films for a wider audience, including international viewers and people who don’t follow the TV shows.

Under its Toei New Wave 2033 medium- to long-term plan, the company aims to raise the share of its sales that comes from overseas from 30% to 50%, with Asia the current focus. Yoshimura cited estimates that the global entertainment and media market is growing by an average of 3.4% a year. He added that streaming now lets K-pop, Japanese anime, Indian films and Chinese microdramas travel the world without being changed.

“Now that content rooted in local cultures and histories, like ‘Shogun,’ is crossing racial and national borders and captivating people around the world, I’d say the main battlefield for our business is overseas,” he said.

During the audience Q&A, Yoshimura said that before “Power Rangers,” Toei exported its Japanese series unchanged, and they struggled to win over Western audiences. The breakthrough came from keeping only the characters and rebuilding the cast and drama in the U.S.

“I believe this approach of localization is indispensable for spreading characters around the world,” he said. “We also understand that, unlike animation, live-action work in particular is not something that is readily accepted as-is in each country.”

“Power Rangers” has now run in the U.S. for more than 30 years. In 2025, the original “Mighty Morphin Power Rangers” was relaunched as “Mighty Morphin Power Rangers: Re-Ignition,” with newly designed toys from Hasbro, a “Fortnite” collaboration and a Lego line in the works. Toei used a similar remake model in the Philippines. “Voltes V: Legacy,” the 2023 live-action version of Toei’s 1977 anime “Super Electromagnetic Machine Voltes V,” peaked at a 58% rating there and was released in Japanese cinemas the following year.

The company is also building stories from scratch in other markets. In February, it signed a strategic co-production partnership with Thai producer-distributor M Studio. Together with M Studio and Korea’s Showbox, it is adapting Ito Junji’s “Long Hair in the Attic” as a premium Thai film for Asian audiences. The film will open in Thailand first before heading to Japan and other territories.

In co-production, Toei backed “Dear Stranger,” a Japan-Taiwan-U.S. film from director Mariko Tetsuya that stars Gwei Lun-mei and Nishijima Hidetoshi. It was shot entirely in New York and released last September. The project was selected for the Hong Kong Asia Film Financing Forum’s HAF and raised money abroad from the start, with Taiwanese investors including TAICCA’s TICP 2.0 program. Toei’s Kyoto studio is also supporting the Japan shoot of “Iris,” an action thriller from a U.S. independent producer.

Toei is the only company in Japan that owns and runs two studio lots, one in Tokyo and one in Kyoto. The Tokyo lot houses Japan’s only permanent 270-degree LED volume stage.

Toei is also making short-form content. “Everyday Host Club” is a short anime of 24 episodes that run about three minutes each. “Meshimori Onna,” produced at the Kyoto studio for a client, is a vertical period drama of 30 episodes that run one to two minutes. Yoshimura suggested that vertical formats could one day become the norm even in cinemas.

On Taiwan, he noted that in 2025 Japanese films were the most-released titles in the market and second only to U.S. films at the box office. Toei titles released there include “Midnight Diner,” “Shin Kamen Rider,” “Howling Village” and “Oshi no Ko.” Local broadcaster YoYo TV airs “Kamen Rider,” and Toei is expanding live character shows in Taiwan with a local partner. The studio also invested in the Taiwanese horror film “The Rope Curse 4.” Yoshimura said he wants long-term tokusatsu partnerships and international co-productions with Taiwan.

“We are looking for partners with whom we can plan, make and deliver compelling stories to the world together,” he said.

Yoshimura joined Toei in 1988. He worked in live events, licensing and digital distribution, including some of Japan’s early mobile content and subscription streaming services, and became president in 2023.

FA and WSL Football launch major study into ACL injuries in women’s football

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

To help deliver timely coverage of women's football, we may use AI tools to support content creation and editing. While we strive for accuracy and editorial quality, readers should independently verify important information.

The Football Association and WSL Football have teamed up with Imperial College London on a new study into ACL injuries, the knee problem that has cost the women’s game some of its biggest names in recent seasons. All clubs across the Barclays WSL and WSL2 are being asked to take part, from the players themselves to the medical, performance and coaching staff around them.

Announced on Thursday, the project is being run as a PhD programme funded by the FA. Imperial researcher Hanna Lehtovirta, based in the university’s Department of Mechanical Engineering, is leading the work, with Fortius Clinic knee surgeon Simon Ball also involved. According to the FA’s announcement, the aim is to find out why anterior cruciate ligament ruptures keep happening in the elite women’s game and to turn those findings into practical prevention advice.

The research is split into two stages. In the first, players complete surveys covering their injury record, how much they train and play, and hormonal, nutritional and psychological factors. Anyone who has ruptured an ACL since the 2018-19 campaign will have their answers set against those of players who have avoided the injury, in the hope of spotting patterns linked to risk.

The second stage goes back to the same group for interviews, and this time staff are brought in too. The FA says plenty of earlier research has looked at single factors, often in a lab, and that far less is known about how they combine in the daily routine of a professional squad. Lehtovirta said her team would be looking at everything “from minutes played and injury prevention practices at clubs to how players developed before they entered professional football.”

ESPN reports that data gathering began in June and that the study is set to run for three years.

The numbers explain the urgency. Research cited by ESPN suggests women are around twice as likely as men to suffer an ACL injury, and roughly two in every three cases happen with no contact from another player. England captain Leah Williamson and Beth Mead both missed the 2023 World Cup after rupturing the ligament, and Vivianne Miedema has also had to come back from the injury during her time in England.

Ritan Mehta, the FA’s Head of Women’s Medical, was clear about the scale of the problem. “ACL injuries remain one of the biggest challenges facing female footballers,” she said. “While significant progress has been made in understanding the factors that may contribute to these injuries, there is still much more to learn.”

WSL Football’s Medical Governance Lead, Dr Abosede Ajayi, pointed to the mix of football, healthcare and academia behind the project, saying the findings could “inform medical standards across the women’s game”. Ball put it more bluntly: “ACL injury is devastating for professional athletes,” he said, adding that “prevention is better than cure!”

"AI is the best thing to ever happen to Airbnb. It's changing both how we develop products and how we operate," Airbnb said in a letter to shareholders. "AI is helping our teams move faster, and in Q3 alone, we shipped more than 50 products and features — nearly 70 percent more than a year ago."

Now Airbnb intends to add an AI travel agent to that collection. While still under development, the company says its new AI agent is being designed to help users research, plan, and book trips on its platform.

"We’re developing a custom-designed, AI-native experience that builds the interface around your question," said Airbnb. "Rather than a chat window, our agent draws on a library of hundreds of travel-specific interface elements — maps, photos, comparisons, calendars, itineraries — and composes them in real time to match what you’re looking for."

CEO Brian Chesky elaborated during Airbnb's earnings call, stating that the AI agent will "feel a little bit like generative AI" due to its ability to respond to users' requests with relevant custom interfaces for specific travel needs. Chesky further stated that the agent will have a "post-chat-based interface," being less text-focused, though it will still retain a "chat element."

"If you want a car rental, the agent generates this little UI of a car, and it's [presented] beautifully in a custom-generated interface," said Chesky. "If you want to invite co-travellers, the agent helps you."

Airbnb's AI agent will also help resolve issues once a booking has already been made, including changing reservations or assisting with refunds and rebookings. Interestingly, Airbnb states that the AI will provide 24-hour safety support as well, though did not elaborate on exactly what type of assistance it will offer.

"Our agent will help guests explore options, make decisions with the people they’re travelling with, and take action directly in Airbnb," Airbnb wrote. "Over time, we want our agent to help guests plan their entire trip in one place, across homes, hotels, services, experiences, and more."

This won't be restricted to bookings that are available on Airbnb either. Chesky stated that the AI agent will be able to go "outside the walls of Airbnb," though noted it will stay within the realm of travel.

"Today's AI agents, like [Meta's AI agent] Muse and [invite-only AI assistant] Instinct, are good at simple routine tasks like making a restaurant reservation or scheduling an appointment," Chesky said. "But planning a trip is different. Travel is aspirational. Discovering where to go and where to stay is part of the experience… I don't think the general-purpose agents are the best to do that. That is why we are building our own AI agent."

Airbnb's new AI travel agent is expected to be released in the first half of 2027.

This upcoming AI travel agent isn't to be confused with Airbnb's AI customer support assistant, which is already in operation. Airbnb states that its AI assistant resolved around 40 percent of all customer support issues without any human agents in Q3, an increase from approximately 30 percent last quarter. In Q2, Airbnb's AI agent resolved nearly 45 percent of issues that specifically began in the tool.

Airbnb reports that its revenue grew 19 percent to $4.9 billion in Q3, bringing in a net income of $1.9 billion.

Lost audio recordings of Louisiana Creole give us a rare chance to hear the past voices of early New Orleans

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Recordings from 1935 reveal how Louisiana Creole speakers switched languages, capturing speech patterns that written accounts often missed.

To understand how Louisiana Creole sounded in the 1800s, scholars have had to rely on writing. But some writers changed what they heard.

One researcher wrote in 1933 that he had “purified the dialect” in his transcriptions.

Recordings made two years later, now analyzed for the first time, capture something that those written accounts often missed: people moving between Creole and French as they spoke.

Lorenzo Dow Turner, remembered as the first African American linguist, recorded those voices on 13 aluminum discs, way back in 1935.

Oliver Mayeux, a linguist at the University of Cambridge’s Faculty of Modern and Medieval Languages and Linguistics, tracked down the digitized recordings and worked out who was speaking.

The discs contain the earliest known recordings of spoken Louisiana Creole and Louisiana French. Both languages now have dwindling numbers of elderly native speakers.

“Turner’s lost recordings contain far more linguistic diversity than the texts we relied upon until now,” said Mayeux.

“This is the language of the levees and cane fields. The language that drifted out from French Quarter saloons along with early jazz music at the turn of the last century.”

Louisiana Creole shares almost all its words with Louisiana French, but the two languages use different grammar.

Creole developed on southern Louisiana plantations, where enslaved people, mostly from West Africa, came into contact with settlers who spoke regional varieties of French.

“Africans with little common language were forced onto plantations where French was the language of administration. Everybody is learning approximations of French and then approximations of approximations. Quite quickly, a new language is born,” said Mayeux.

By Turner’s arrival in July 1935, younger people and city residents were already moving toward English. Mayeux, who has family ties in Louisiana, knows that history through his own family.

“By the middle of the 20th century you see the end of intergenerational transmission. My grandfather wanted his children to speak English,” explained Mayeux.

“This was typical of many Louisianans who saw their future as American rather than Creole or Cajun,” he added.

Many children, therefore, grew up without learning the language their parents spoke.

Turner was known for studying Gullah, a creole language with English roots spoken by Black communities in Georgia and South Carolina.

He visited Louisiana to compare the two languages’ sounds, especially how speakers’ voices rose and fell.

“From casual observation I find that the intonation of Creole resembles very much that of Gullah,” Turner wrote to a funding body after the trip.

His recording machine weighed about 100 pounds (45 kilograms). He had to drive it to places with electricity, which limited where he could work.

“Turner relied on a network of Black doctors and intellectuals in New Orleans and out in the country. The physicians found him Creole-speaking interviewees and let him record in their offices,” said Mayeux.

“These were trusted spaces for the participants during the era of Jim Crow segregation and, crucially, were wired for electricity.”

In Lafayette and Opelousas, doctors Julius Blackburn Matthews and August C. Terrence hosted the sessions. One participant, Marie Raymond, said she had never known a doctor until she was 69.

A woman also helped Turner conduct many interviews. She seemed more comfortable speaking French than he did, and Mayeux thinks she was probably Turner’s wife, Geneva Calcier Townes Turner.

Turner never analyzed the Louisiana Creole recordings, and his applications for funding to continue the comparison were rejected. The Guggenheim Foundation turned down one application and awarded a fellowship to his former student Zora Neale Hurston instead.

Mayeux suspects Turner simply set the discs aside. Indiana University’s Archives of Traditional Music later digitized them, but their labels offered little information and sometimes got it wrong.

One clue came from a disc that a Turner biography described as two people singing. It actually contained solo piano music by Jessie Covington Dent, a New Orleans pianist and civil rights activist.

Dent’s family papers at the Amistad Research Center led Mayeux to Turner’s papers. An address book there named the doctors who had hosted him.

Another disc listed “Mme Emma Bronssard,” which Mayeux recognized as a misspelling of Broussard. The woman said she was 69, had been widowed for 20 years, and had raised 12 children of her own plus four orphans.

Census and death records matched those details to Emma Benoit, born Emma Broussard.

Her great-granddaughter, Valencia Pradier-Linton, later shared a magazine interview in which Benoit’s son said his mother “didn’t know how to talk English at all.”

Turner recorded three Creole speakers in New Orleans, where people shifted to English earlier than in the countryside. The city’s variety of Creole likely has no speakers left.

Mayeux identified two speakers: George Doyle, born in 1867, and Odelia Edmond, born in 1862. Doyle had worked as a police officer before the city segregated its police department.

Some of their speech matched the old texts closely. Doyle’s word for policemen, “lapolis,” came from the French “la police,” but the French word for “the” had become a permanent part of the noun.

Edmond used mo for “my” when she said “my mother.” French would use a feminine form of the word instead.

Other details challenged what scholars thought they knew. The speakers used rounded vowels, like those in the French words “peu” and “plus,” which researchers had treated as a later borrowing.

Edmond also moved between old and newer forms of the same word. While explaining that she was three days old when her mother died, she used a long form of “have” known mainly from 19th-century writing.

Just 15 seconds later, when talking about being nine days old, she used the short form. Hearing both from the same speaker suggested that New Orleans Creole was more varied than the written record made it seem.

Outside New Orleans, speakers often began interviews in Louisiana French, the more formal language. Others in the room then asked them to speak Creole.

Emma Benoit started with a polite French greeting. In Mayeux’s translation, a woman interrupted with “no no no! Creole!” and then “no no that’s French; that’s not Creole anymore.”

Benoit replied in Creole, “I forget that,” and said she would talk about her troubles. A few minutes later, she returned to French and received another reminder.

Anastasie Dumas switched languages too, answering in French when Turner addressed her in formal, European-sounding French. When his co-interviewer asked in somewhat stiff Creole how to make gumbo, Dumas returned to Creole.

Most participants could not read or write and did not own a radio. Speaking into a visiting professor’s recording machine would have been an unusual, formal experience.

The people in the room and the occasion helped shape how they spoke. Mayeux concluded that French signaled politeness and respectability, while Creole emerged in more relaxed moments of joking and storytelling.

For most speakers today, moving between the two languages no longer carries the same degree of social meaning.

Each recording lasts about two to five minutes, and static makes some speech hard to follow. The New Orleans speakers also came through one circle of doctors, educators, and musicians, so their voices cannot represent the whole city.

Mayeux describes his reconstruction as partial and interpretive, shaped by his position as a white researcher listening nearly a century later. He hopes others will offer their own readings.

He also cannot yet explain why the city recordings differ from the old texts. The language may have changed, writers may have removed variation, or both may have happened.

The next step is comparison with recordings that Turner’s former student Raleigh Morgan Jr. made in the 1950s. Researchers can study those voices alongside the 1935 discs and the Creole spoken today.

For some young learners, Creole offers a way to reconnect with their family history. Many are grandchildren of native speakers whose parents never learned the language, Mayeux said.

“Young people are learning Creole to connect with the richness of their heritage, helping them discover a distinct identity in an increasingly homogenized world,” said Mayeux.

The study is published in the journal Language Documentation and Description.

Most lithium comes from desert ponds or hard-rock mines, and a new lab method skips both

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Engineers pulled lithium from salty water in the lab using a solvent that reacts to heat, a step toward reaching brines that evaporation ponds can’t use.

The lithium in a battery usually comes from one of two places: a hard-rock mine, or a desert pond where the sun bakes salty water for up to two years.

Columbia University engineers have now pulled lithium out of salty water in the lab with neither one. Their solvent takes up lithium at room temperature and lets it go when the temperature rises.

Ngai Yin Yip, an associate professor of Earth and Environmental Engineering at Columbia, did the work with four co-authors. It’s a proof of concept. The team mixed salty water in the lab to copy the chemistry of the Salton Sea, a geothermal region in California.

About 40% of lithium production starts as brine, salty water that collects in large reservoirs under deserts. Nearly all of that lithium comes out through solar evaporation. Mining companies pump the brine into sprawling ponds and wait for the water to evaporate.

Evaporation ponds need a lot of dry, flat land, so they work in places like Chile’s Atacama Desert and parts of Nevada. They also use large volumes of water in places that can barely spare it. Hard-rock mining, the other main source, comes with steep tradeoffs of its own.

“There’s no way solar evaporation alone can match future demand,” Yip said. “And there are promising lithium-rich brines, like those in California’s Salton Sea, where this method simply can’t be used at all.”

The Salton Sea region holds enough lithium, by one estimate, for more than 370 million electric vehicle batteries.

Lithium-bearing brine turns up elsewhere in the U.S. too. In Arkansas, a U.S. Geological Survey-led study estimated 5 million to 19 million tons of lithium in brine beneath the southwestern part of the state.

In Pennsylvania, analysts who studied company data on wastewater from 515 gas wells found lithium in it as well.

The solvent is a liquid chemical that behaves differently depending on temperature. At room temperature, it attracts water. When it warms, it repels water.

The team mixed the solvent with brine at room temperature, and the solvent took up water and lithium together. Then the team warmed it a little.

The solvent now repelled water, so it released the lithium and the water as a purified stream. It returned to its starting state, ready to reuse.

The warming step needs only modest heat. “It can be powered by low-grade heat from waste sources or solar collectors,” Yip said.

In brine, lithium mixes with sodium and potassium, two metals that behave a lot like it, and separating the three is hard. The solvent still preferred lithium to sodium across versions with different chemical structures, by as much as about 10 to 1.

The solvent kept that preference even when lithium was 1,000 times scarcer than sodium or potassium in the water. In the simulated Salton Sea brine, the preference reached about 13 to 1 over sodium and 24 to 1 over potassium. So the solvent still separated lithium when lithium made up only a tiny share of the metals in the water.

Magnesium, another common contaminant in lithium brines, also dropped out as a solid, because the solvent is alkaline, the opposite of acidic. In the Salton Sea mixture, the team removed all of it.

The team reused one batch of solvent for four rounds. Together, the four rounds recovered nearly 40% of the lithium in the simulated brine. The solvent kept its preference for lithium throughout.

So about 60% of the lithium went unrecovered after four rounds. The team hasn’t yet optimized the system for yield or efficiency, so 40% reflects an early version of the method.

The team ran its tests in the lab, in batches, on brines it mixed itself. Real brine from the ground may hold more impurities than the lab mixes did, and the team still has to show how the solvent handles them. The same goes for its performance at the size of a working plant.

Whether recovery rises above 40% once the team tunes the system remains an open question. Columbia has invited companies that want to collaborate on the technology, or license it, to express their interest.

The full study was published in the journal Joule.

Last month I had to merge two exports from our billing system into one import file. The first export had 31,207 customer email addresses, the second had 17,706, and a few thousand lines overlapped. The CRM import endpoint rejects the whole file if it contains a single repeated email, so deduplication had to happen before upload.

There was a second constraint that tripped me up: row order drove import priority. Whichever line appeared first had to stay. So sort -u, the command half of my brain reached for, was off the table from the start. Here are three approaches that do work, tested on Ubuntu 22.04 with Python 3.11.1 and GNU awk 5.1.1.

The obvious one-liner is list(set(lines)). I've written it myself under deadline pressure. Don't. A set iterates in arbitrary hash order, so the output is a shuffled file, and the CRM import would run in whatever order the hash table happened to land on.

What you want is a seen set plus a counter:

Reading line by line means the file never sits in memory as one blob. The seen set held about 45,000 short strings, roughly 6 MB on my machine. Membership checks against a set are O(1) on average, which is why this stays fast as the file grows. The same pattern exists in JavaScript as a Set, so porting the script is a ten-minute job if your pipeline is Node-based.

When I just need the result and don't want to write a file, GNU awk does it in a single command:

$0 is the entire line. The expression seen[$0]++ returns 0 the first time a line shows up (falsy, so awk prints it) and counts upward after that. First occurrence wins, original order preserved. Same semantics as the Python script. The gawk manual has a whole chapter on how arrays and their uninitialized elements behave, worth a read if this idiom feels too magical.

One honest caveat: awk accumulates every line it has seen in memory, same as the Python set. For a 40 GB file on a 4 GB box neither will end well, and you'd reach for sort with a stable flag or a disk-based approach. For anything under a few hundred megabytes it's fine.

The second export came out of a Windows service. Every line ended in \r\n, and my rstrip("\n") left the carriage return in place. On screen the lines looked identical; in memory they weren't:

First run reported dropped 3203. After I changed the strip to line.rstrip("\r\n"), the same file reported dropped 3847. Those extra 644 "duplicates" were the same addresses, invisible until the repr showed the \r.

Reading a 2 km laser rangefinder module over UART with Python (LR2000E2, 55 AA protocol)

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A laser rangefinder module is one of the easiest long-range sensors to wire up and one of the easiest to misread. The wiring is four wires. The mistakes happen in the protocol: a frame header you skip, a checksum you do not verify, a unit you assume. This post walks through a small, complete Python reader for the ERDI LR2000E2, a 905 nm UART laser rangefinder module with a published range of 4 to 2,000 m, and shows where the traps are.

Everything below comes from the LR2000E2 user manual (v1.2, public edition 2026-09-06) and the product page at https://erdilrf.com/models/lr2000e2/. Data checked on 2026-11-06.

Two numbers matter for software. The rate is adaptive, so you should not build a 10 Hz control loop around it; treat it as a slow, long-reach sensor. And the "valid measurement rate" in the manual is 98 % or better up to 400 m and 80 % or better from 400 to 2,000 m, which means at long range one reading in five can legitimately be a failure. Your code has to handle failed readings as a normal case, not an exception.

Two practical notes. First, the manual states the 3.3–5 V supply range but does not separately specify the logic level of TXD and RXD. If you power the module at 5 V and talk to a 3.3 V-only microcontroller pin, measure TXD with a scope or meter first, or ask the manufacturer for the model-specific statement. A USB-serial adapter that supports 3.3 V levels is the safe starting point. Second, pin 6 is an enable input: the module expects it high to operate, so tie it according to the manual before wondering why nothing answers.

The inrush current is below 400 mA, so use a supply that can deliver it without sagging. A weak USB hub that browns out at power-on will look like a protocol problem.

Frames are 8 bytes. A command is 55 AA D1 D2 D3 D4 , where the checksum is the low byte of the sum of the function code and the four data bytes. The reply is also 8 bytes: 55 AA STA FF DIS_H DIS_L . STA = 1 means success; any other value means the measurement failed. The distance is DIS_H * 256 + DIS_L, and the manual states that all results are output as the actual value × 10, so you divide by 10 to get metres.

You can check the checksum column by hand: 0x88 + 4 × 0xFF = 0x484, low byte 0x84.

sync() instead of ser.read(8). Reading a fixed 8 bytes works until the first dropped byte, after which every frame is misaligned and you decode garbage forever. Searching for 55 AA first costs nothing and removes that failure class. Note the small gap: a data byte pair of 55 AA inside a payload would false-trigger, which is why the strict checksum option exists.

strict defaults to off. The manual text describes the reply checksum as the sum from the first header byte to DATA4. Enable strict=True on your bench first and log what you see; if your unit's replies agree with the formula, leave it on in production. If they do not, you have found a document or firmware difference worth reporting to the supplier before you design anything around it.

Failed readings are normal. ok=False is returned for a bad frame and for STA != 1. Keep the last good value with a timestamp and a staleness limit instead of passing None downstream.

Units. Dividing by 10 is specific to this model family. Other modules from other vendors, and even other ERDI families, report millimetres or centimetres. Never copy a divisor from a sibling model; take it from that model's manual.

You do not need a 2 km line of sight to test the software. Point the module at a wall at a known distance such as 5 m or 20 m, measure with a tape, and log 100 single shots. Plot the histogram. You are checking three things: the mean against the tape, the spread against the ±1 m accuracy band, and the fraction of STA != 1 readings. For targets that are well inside the range, the failure fraction should be very small. If it is not, check supply sag and the SW-SHOT line before suspecting the optics.

For the long-range claim, treat the datasheet range as a statement about a target and conditions, not a promise. The accuracy table does not specify target reflectivity and size, and real reach depends on weather, target colour, angle and sun. A low-reflectivity target at an oblique angle will fall short of 2,000 m. Plan for margin.

Each constant in it is cited to the model's manual, which is the point: the unit divisor, baud rate and frame length are per model, not per family.

A client, whom I’ll call Jon, came to me for treatment for anxiety symptoms. Jon was a highly paid manager at a healthcare company. He reported feeling sick to his stomach on Sundays as he dreaded the deluge of demands on Monday. Jon had lost his appetite for pleasurable foods, as he complained of indigestion and chronic gut discomfort. “I don’t enjoy the weekends anymore. It’s not enough time to relax and feel refreshed. I’m constantly stressed out. My wife is sick of me snapping at her and the kids,” Jon said.

A recent survey by the American Psychological Association found that 75% of Americans report feeling physical and/or emotional symptoms related to stress. Another study reported in Forbes found that 66% of Americans feel burned out, with younger workers feeling it the most.

The reasons for all this stress and burnout are varied but can be summarized here:

It's hard to feel like you can't control the major factors that impact your quality of life. Feeling like you have no control can leave you feeling defeated, unmotivated, and down.

We can’t control broader labor and economic trends, but we can improve how we respond to them. Science-based strategies can help reduce stress and prevent burnout. Here are three you can try now:

Take a morning walk to get the positive effects of sunlight, which helps regulate your sleep/wake cycle. You want a morning cortisol boost to start your day. This helps lower stress by increasing focus early in the day and improving sleep at night. Plus, you reap the numerous health benefits of daily exercise (Huberman, 2026).

Refrain from starting and ending your day on the cellphone

Those who grab the cellphone in bed in the morning and evening report disrupted sleep and mood, contributing to burnout. Instead of cortisol peaking in the morning and lowering at night, cellphone use flips the script, leaving you feeling more anxious, sleep-deprived, and burnt out (Kheirinejad, et al, 2023).

Eat five to nine servings (a half-cup) of fruits and vegetables daily. In a study of healthcare workers, like Jon, those who ate a nutrient-rich diet had lower burnout scores. Only 8% of 5,000 healthcare workers surveyed reported getting the minimum daily requirement of five servings of fruits and vegetables (Utter, et al. 2023). Most nutritionists recommend the Mediterranean diet. That diet is high in vegetables, legumes, and olive oil, and low in sugar and animal fat, like butter. Another study found adherence to the Mediterranean diet reduced the risk of burnout (Amiri et al., 2025).

Jon was so eager to feel better that he tried all three strategies immediately. He got up a few minutes earlier to take an early morning walk before work. He talked to his wife about cutting out sugary sodas and sweets, and they agreed to move toward the Mediterranean diet. The hardest one for Jon was the cellphone. He had a habit of checking his phone and work emails immediately each morning, before he even got out of bed. I explained how this habit disrupted his cortisol regulation and contributed to his sleep issues. Because he couldn’t resist grabbing his phone first thing in the morning, I suggested he keep it out of the bedroom and check it only after his morning walk.

After three weeks implementing these new habits on most days of the week, Jon reported a meaningful improvement in mood. “My wife says I’m much nicer to be around. My gut isn’t waking me up at night anymore. I notice that I’m able to focus better at work too.”

If you’re feeling burned out, or like you’re headed in that direction, it’s always good to get a physical exam with bloodwork first, to determine whether there is a physical cause for the low mood and other symptoms. Sometimes a vitamin deficiency, a thyroid problem, or another medical condition can produce the same symptoms as burnout. If your overall health is good, try the three strategies above. Small changes over time can reap big benefits.

Un bot Telegram è un comodo telecomando per uno script o un agente IA sulla tua macchina. Funziona su qualsiasi telefono, non richiede una tua app e non richiede un server pubblico. Il problema è che un bot è pubblico. Chiunque può trovarne lo username e scrivergli, e il token dimostra soltanto che il tuo script è il bot; non dice nulla su chi sta scrivendo. L'app desktop osFoundry ha un ponte del genere per i suoi agenti, scritto in Rust (telegram_bridge.rs). Qui sotto trovi lo stesso schema in circa 70 righe di Python con la sola libreria standard, testato contro un sostituto locale della Bot API, insieme all'errore di una riga che ha trasformato un bot tranquillo in 183.389 richieste al minuto.

Telegram consegna i messaggi in due modi. Un webhook richiede un URL HTTPS pubblico che Telegram possa chiamare. getUpdates non richiede di esporre nulla: lo script chiede i nuovi messaggi e, con timeout=25, Telegram tiene aperta la richiesta fino a 25 secondi e risponde appena ne arriva uno. Su un portatile è la scelta giusta. Nessuna porta da aprire, nessun tunnel, nessun certificato.

Il timeout HTTP (40 s) deve essere più lungo dell'attesa del long poll (25 s). Se è più corto, ogni ciclo tranquillo finisce con un timeout lato client che sembra un'interruzione. L'app conserva il token nel portachiavi del sistema operativo, non in un file. Da Python, keyring.get_password fa lo stesso, e per un test va bene una variabile d'ambiente.

In 60 secondi di inattività, il ciclo di long poll ha fatto 3 richieste e uno short poll ogni secondo ne ha fatte 60. Un messaggio arrivato mentre una richiesta era tenuta aperta ha ricevuto la risposta da 1,5 a 6,3 ms dopo nel test locale. Su una rete reale si aggiunge il tragitto di andata e ritorno verso Telegram.

Il bot deve sapere quale persona serve prima di fare qualsiasi cosa. L'app lo fa con un codice monouso. Clicchi "Pair a phone" sulla macchina, che mostra otto caratteri casuali, e dal telefono invii /pair CODE al bot. Chi invia un codice valido entro 10 minuti è il proprietario.

Il codice viene da secrets, non da qualcosa di indovinabile come l'ora o il nome del bot, perché il codice è l'unica cosa che autorizza un mittente. Viene anche rimosso quando è usato, quindi funziona una sola volta.

Quello che viene salvato è il from.id numerico, non l'@username né il nome visualizzato. Entrambi possono essere cambiati, e uno username liberato può essere registrato da qualcun altro. Accanto viene salvato l'id della chat, per un motivo che compare più sotto.

Tre regole reggono la sicurezza, e ciascuna nasce da un errore specifico annotato nel sorgente dell'app.

I mittenti sconosciuti ricevono silenzio. Una risposta come "you are not paired" dice a un estraneo che il bot è collegato alla macchina di una persona reale e lo invita a continuare a provare. Nessuna risposta sembra un bot che nessuno esegue.

Il pairing funziona solo in una chat privata. Un bot aggiunto a un gruppo riceve i messaggi del gruppo, quindi fare il pairing lì permetterebbe a ogni membro di dare istruzioni all'agente.

A una persona associata si risponde solo nella chat da cui ha fatto il pairing. I commenti dell'app descrivono una versione precedente che controllava il mittente ma rispondeva alla chat da cui arrivava il messaggio. Un utente associato che aggiungeva il bot a un gruppo si ritrovava con un agente che rispondeva in quel gruppo, dove tutti potevano usare strumenti sulla macchina del proprietario. L'autorizzazione è per mittente e la risposta va a una chat, quindi i due controlli vanno fatti insieme.

/unpair viene gestito prima di quel controllo sulla chat, così l'accesso si può togliere dal telefono, che è dove sei quando ti serve.

offset è il modo in cui lo script dice a Telegram quali aggiornamenti ha visto. Tutto ciò che sta sotto è confermato e scartato, e tutto ciò che è pari o sopra viene inviato di nuovo. A New Era of Bionic Vision? AI Helps Scientists Unlock the Brain’s Visual Cortex

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Researchers have used artificial intelligence to improve how a brain implant stimulates the visual cortex of a blind person, potentially advancing the development of bionic eyes.

Artificial intelligence could make future bionic eyes more precise and responsive by improving how electrical signals communicate with the brain. In a study published in Neuron, researchers used deep learning to control brain stimulation in a blind man, producing more accurate neural responses with less electrical current.

The findings could advance visual prostheses that bypass damaged eyes and optic nerves, stimulating the visual cortex directly to generate perceptions of light.

Researchers from UC Santa Barbara, ETH Zurich, and Miguel Hernández University (MHU) tested the approach in a 27-year-old man who lost his sight following a traumatic brain injury. At a hospital in Spain, a temporary implant containing 96 electrodes stimulated his visual cortex, producing phosphenes, perceived as flashes or shapes of light.

Led in part by UCSB professor Michael Beyeler, the team trained a deep neural network to predict brain responses to different stimulation patterns. The AI model also incorporated measurements of resting brain activity, allowing it to account for changes in the brain’s condition before each test.

The AI-designed stimulation patterns produced targeted neural responses more accurately and required less electrical current than comparison approaches. Researchers also found that recorded brain activity predicted the participant’s visual perceptions better than the electrical settings alone.

“Engineers naturally want to treat phosphenes like pixels: stimulate more electrodes, and you should get a more complete image,” Beyeler said. “But the brain does not work that way. Electrodes interact, neural responses fluctuate, and what we put into the brain is not necessarily what the person perceives. The challenge is to learn more about that transformation.”

The implant could both stimulate neurons and record their responses, enabling the AI to learn from actual brain activity. The participant also described perceived flashes, including their brightness, size, shape, and color.

The technology could eventually help people who lose their sight through injuries or neurological and inherited conditions. Beyeler emphasized those “who have been able to see for part of their life, but then, because of an inherited eye disease or an accident, have lost their vision,” adding, “For those people, the desire may be very strong to get some vision back.”

Describing the international research effort as “a true collaboration,” Beyeler said the study demonstrated how computational models could influence experiments involving human participants.

“Building a model in the abstract is one thing,” he said. “Seeing it shape an experiment with a person is something else entirely. That ability to go from theory to something that may one day help people is what drives much of the work in our lab.”

Although the research remains an early proof of concept, it suggests that future visual prostheses may work better by continuously adjusting stimulation to an individual’s neural responses.

“A useful visual prosthesis cannot rely on a fixed recipe,” Beyeler said. “It has to learn how an individual brain responds and adapt the stimulation accordingly. Ultimately, the device should adapt to the person, not the other way around.”

For Natural Volume, Wash Your Hair The French Girl Way

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

We all know that French girls have that special je ne sais quoi (so much so that it’s inspired countless essays on this very site). When it comes to their hair, the style is nonchalant—and yet, somehow also skillfully cultivated. It’s hair that’s air-dries, falls just right, and retains that slightly tousled movement that gives no hint of effort or styling. So how exactly do Jeanne Damas, Brigitte Bardot, Jane Birkin, and more do it?

Hairstylist and French girl herself Daphné Narcy has an idea: It all starts how you wash your hair. “You may be damaging your hair without even realizing it while in the shower,” Narcy says. “When you’re hair is wet, it’s at its most fragile. But habits like washing hair too quickly, scrubbing too hard, or using water that’s too hot may be causing breakage and frizz.”

She offers up eight simple shower-time adjustments to turn your hair into a French girl dream.

The first—and by far the most common—mistake: People wash their hair instead of their scalp. “We rub the lengths against each other, which damages them, and we neglect the roots, where sebum, pollution, and residue accumulate,” says Narcy. Her philosophy can be summed up in one sentence: A healthy scalp is the foundation of healthy hair. It’s also what determines how light the roots feel once the hair is dry.

The heat from a scalding shower lifts the hair cuticle, which can be bad for shine. “The hair loses moisture, becomes more porous and duller, and the color fades faster,” Narcy says. Hot water hurts the scalp, too, making it drier and more irritated. “Water that’s too hot also softens the hair follicles, which can lead to hair loss.” Your new rule of thumb here is disarmingly simple: “If the water feels pleasant on your wrist, without feeling hot, it’s the right temperature for your hair.”

Use your fingertips in small circular motions, without ever scrubbing. “It’s meant to be an invigorating massage, not a scrub,” insists Narcy. It’s also a moment of pleasantness. When you enjoy it, washing your hair becomes a ritual rather than a chore.

If you’ve ever read the back of a shampoo bottle, you know the instructions: Lather, rinse, repeat. And Narcy says that isn’t just to get you to use more product; but instead the double-shampoo is key to keeping hair clean—though only use a walnut-sized amount each time.

“When you use too much shampoo, residue can remain on the scalp, weighing it down, potentially irritating it, and making your hair look dirty faster The result: you end up washing your hair more often, and you get caught in a vicious cycle.”

The lengths should only be lightly touched when it comes to shampooing. “Press your hands together and slide them down, using very light and gentle friction, all the way to the ends. While rinsing, the lather that runs down finishes cleansing the lengths.” Less friction means fewer tangles, less breakage, and mid-lengths that not only stay hydrated but also dry smooth.
