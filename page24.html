How long will your NVIDIA GPU be supported?

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

NVIDIA can support GeForce GPUs for years, but Game Ready updates, security patches and OS support don't all end at once.

Graphics cards do not have an expiration date on the box. That would make it easier to upgrade, but much more depressing.

NVIDIA also does not guarantee a specific number of years of driver support for all GeForce GPUs. Rather, support is largely dependent on the architecture your card uses. At time of writing, Turing, Ampere, Ada and Blackwell GPUs are still on the normal Game Ready Driver track, which includes new features, game optimizations, performance improvements and bug fixes. That means that even though the RTX 2060 was released in 2019, it continues to receive regular driver updates.

Older cards have started moving down the ladder. In October 2025, Maxwell, Pascal and Volta GPUs stopped receiving Game Ready updates, but NVIDIA will continue to offer critical security updates until October 2028. Pascal and Volta had nine years of Game Ready support, whereas Maxwell lasted for 11 years.

That's a pretty respectable run, but what comes next and how could it impact your system?

NVIDIA does have a neatly defined three-phase software support lifecycle. Unfortunately, it's about NVIDIA vGPU software; not about the GeForce card in your gaming PC.

NVIDIA vGPU is a virtualization solution, mostly for enterprise use (data centers and professional deployments), that requires an active software or support subscription to receive software support. Phase I, also known as Full Support, includes new features, performance enhancements, security patches and bug fixes. Phase II is the next three years of Extended Full Support, with the same coverage, and Phase III is the final three years of Maintenance Support, where the GPU remains on its last supported software branch and receives maintenance, security fixes and bug fixes as NVIDIA sees fit.

GeForce support is far less ceremonial. NVIDIA has moved older architectures off its primary Game Ready branch, but doesn't publish a single lifespan for all generations.

That can also make model names confusing. The GTX 750 Ti is a Maxwell card and will continue to receive security updates until October 2028, whereas the more powerful GTX 780 Ti is based on the older Kepler architecture, whose published security-update window ended in September 2024. A good rule of thumb: bigger number equals older architecture. GPU naming is weird that way.

The end of Game Ready support sounds a lot more dramatic than it normally is.

Your graphics card does not suddenly refuse to boot up Windows or stop running all of the games you already have installed. Existing drivers continue to work. What goes away is the constant flow of optimizations for new games, feature additions, performance tweaks and regular bug fixes, which can become more noticeable as newer software expects newer drivers.

Security-only support is still meaningful though. NVIDIA has already issued dedicated Maxwell, Pascal and Volta security drivers during 2026, so these cards haven't simply been abandoned.

Another clock is ticking too: your operating system. The last full Game Ready and Studio driver for Windows 10 will be available in October 2026, according to NVIDIA. Windows 10 systems will get quarterly security updates and critical bug fixes until October 2029. So even a newer RTX GPU might lose the normal Game Ready update stream due to Windows and not the hardware.

For an RTX 2060 owner, driver support alone is not much of a reason to start looking for a new GPU. Performance and VRAM limits or a lack of access to newer features may become bigger issues first. NVIDIA hasn't confirmed a new GeForce generation for 2027 either, despite reports previously pointing to late-2027 mass production for its next architecture.

It wasn’t the plane’s first flight. The CE-25A had already completed 19 autonomous test flights with nobody on board since February 2, and China’s aviation regulator is now reviewing it for a type certificate.

The CE-25A is a T-tail commuter plane with 10 electric ducted fans on the wings, five per side, instead of propellers. COMAC lists a 300 km/h cruise speed and a 400 km (250-mile) range.

The CAAC accepting COMAC’s type certificate application starts the airworthiness review. It doesn’t mean paying passengers are close, and COMAC hasn’t given a date for commercial service.

The program has been quiet for a long time. COMAC showed a CE-25A model at the China International Import Expo in Shanghai in November 2023, with a placard listing 400 to 500 km of range and a 400 km/h top cruise speed. The full-size aircraft, registered B-0EFH, only became public when photos leaked on Chinese social media on October 28.

The obvious battery candidate is CATL. In July 2023, COMAC, CATL, and Shanghai Jiao Tong University formed a joint venture with CNY 600 million in registered capital to develop electric aircraft, as we reported when CATL launched its aviation unit.

CATL had just unveiled its “condensed” battery at the time, claiming up to 500 Wh/kg at the cell level, and said it was testing the cells to aviation standards. Neither company has confirmed what’s inside the CE-25A, though.

~500 Wh/kg has long been seen as the target for battery energy density to enable electric flight on a commercial scale.

COMAC isn’t alone in flying a large electric demonstrator. In August, Heart Aerospace flew its X1 in Plattsburgh, New York, for 27 minutes. It’s a 25,000-lb aircraft with a 106-foot wingspan, which Heart calls the largest battery-electric aircraft ever flown.

Eviation flew its 9-passenger Alice back in September 2022, then laid off most of its staff in early 2025.

Heart is the closest Western comparison. It dropped its 19-seat ES-19 for the 30-seat ES-30 back in 2022, and the ES-30 now relies on reserve turbogenerators to stretch its range, with BAE Systems working on the battery. Heart is targeting 2031 for service and claims “more than 40%” lower operating costs.

For years, the go-to excuse in the West for doing nothing about emissions was China. Why should we bother, the argument went, if China won’t? Now China is pulling ahead, and it’s doing it by cutting its dependence on fossil fuels.

Look at trucking. Electric trucks hit 50.3% of China’s heavy truck sales in September, and they’re already eating into the country’s diesel demand. In the US, electric models were about 0.3% of heavy-truck sales last year, according to the ICCT.

Now it’s planes, starting where batteries make sense today: short regional routes. The CE-25A’s 400 km shrinks once you keep the reserves regulators require, and a Beechcraft 1900 turboprop in the same class covers well over 1,000 km. So it’s not replacing turboprops anytime soon. But that’s how trucking started too. Short-haul first, then long-haul tractors once the batteries caught up.

And this couldn’t happen fast enough. We’re in the middle of a diesel and jet fuel crisis. Jet fuel averaged $3.50 a gallon in early August, up 63% from a year earlier, according to Heart Aerospace. Every truck and plane China moves to batteries is one less exposed to that.

China also has the batteries to pull it off, with CATL in the joint venture. Meanwhile, Heart has pushed to 2031 and added a fuel-burning backup, and Eviation is a cautionary tale.

Why do birds flap before landing? It may be about more than slowing down

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

New calculations suggest birds flap during landing not just to control speed, but to stop within limited space.

Most birds flap their wings as they come in to land, but those final wingbeats may do more than slow them down. New calculations suggest they also help birds stop within a tight space.

A bird the size of a pigeon could glide to a complete stop with its wings held still.

To manage it, though, the bird would have to begin its landing maneuver at about 80 mph (36 meters per second). It would also have to climb roughly 65 feet (20 meters), the height of a six-story building.

Flapping, the researchers propose, lets a bird stop inside the space it has.

Tom Rottier carried out the study at the University of Manchester and the University of Toronto Institute for Aerospace Studies. He worked with Philippe Lavoie of Toronto and Ben Parslew of Manchester.

No birds were flown for the work. Instead, equations were written for a bird gliding on fixed wings.

Only gravity, lift, and air resistance acted on this model bird. The team then solved the equations for the slowest possible touchdown.

The forces on a wing depend on how fast the wing moves through the air. Those forces shrink as a bird slows down. Its control over where it goes shrinks with them.

Arriving fast is no better. Whatever speed is left at touchdown is usually absorbed by the legs. A bad landing can damage muscle and bone, or it can kill the bird.

The difficulty, said Parslew, an aerospace engineering lecturer at Manchester, is that “landing isn’t as simple as just slowing down.”

A wing also has a minimum working speed. In level flight, the bird’s whole weight has to be carried by lift. A wing that moves too slowly cannot make enough of it. Engineers call that minimum the stall speed.

The stall speed is not fixed, however, as the team’s equations show. A bird that climbs more steeply puts less of its weight on its wings. The stall speed drops as a result. In a climb straight up, it is zero.

A climb that ends straight up is how a gliding bird could stop without flapping. The bird comes in fast and pulls up. It then climbs more and more steeply while gravity and air resistance slow it down.

At the top, the bird is pointed straight up and has no speed left.

More lift also means more drag, and drag takes away the speed the climb depends on. For that reason, the tilt of the wings has to keep changing on the way up. By the end, the wings are turned broadside to the air.

The team calculated what such a landing would take for a rock pigeon weighing 14 ounces (400 grams).

Starting from level flight, the bird would need that 80 mph speed. This is more than double a pigeon’s typical cruising speed.

The bird would travel about 200 feet (60 meters) horizontally, roughly two-thirds of a football field, while climbing 65 feet.

City pigeons fly between buildings. A landing of that size is not feasible in many of those places, the team wrote. Bigger birds would need even more room.

The researchers therefore propose that flapping is not simply a way to slow down. A glide can do that. Flapping does it in a small space.

A perch can be approached from below, but the ground cannot. That changes the answer.

The researchers then tested landings in which the model bird could not climb at any point. No path brought it to a stop.

The slowest landing allowed by the equations came in at an angle of about 34 degrees below level.

A bird that glides down to the ground therefore arrives with some speed left. That speed has to be removed by flapping, by braking with the legs, or by both.

The legs need enough muscle and bone to absorb the impact. But that adds weight the bird must carry in flight.

The researchers suggest this trade-off may have shaped how birds evolved. The need to withstand landing impacts may have set a minimum strength for their legs.

Leftover speed may also help explain why ducks, geese, and swans land differently on water than on solid ground.

More of a bird’s energy is absorbed by water than by the ground. The bird can therefore arrive faster.

Some birds of prey do finish a landing without flapping.

A 2022 study observed Harris’s hawks flying between two perches. They learned to dive low and then glide up to the perch.

A wing that is tilted very steeply is stalled, meaning air no longer flows smoothly over it.

The authors of that study proposed an explanation for the swoop. In their view, it keeps the distance a hawk flies with stalled wings as short as possible.

Rottier’s team tested the idea by giving their model bird one goal at a time. For this test, the model bird was allowed to flap during the dive, as the hawks did.

First, it was told only to spend as little time stalled as possible. The model bird climbed and then came down onto the perch. It arrived at speeds that would harm a real hawk, the team wrote.

Next, the only goal was to land as slowly as possible. This time the model bird swooped deep.

A mix of the two goals generally matched the real hawks’ paths best. Touchdown speed did more than stall time to produce the swoop.

Diving builds speed, and speed allows a steeper climb. A steeper climb ends in a slower arrival.

The pigeon figures come from equations, not from a bird in the air. In its main form, the model has no wind, no body or tail, and no flapping.

A wing gets a brief boost in lift and drag when it tilts up quickly. The wings of landing birds do this, yet the boost is also left out of the model. To see how much it could matter, the team doubled the model’s maximum lift.

For a slow touchdown, the starting speed the bird needed fell by 44%. The forward distance fell by 65% and the climb by 55%. When the maximum drag was doubled instead, the speed needed went up.

A living bird may therefore be able to glide to a stop in less room than the pigeon numbers suggest. How much less cannot be said from the model.

The equations were written so that wind can be added later. The team also described the model as a base for studies of single species.

The same physics may apply to animals that never flap, such as flying squirrels and flying snakes. It may also apply to drones built to land on perches.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the Royal Society A.

Legendary hospitality brand Waldorf Astoria has chosen a neighborhood ten minutes from downtown Denver for its first foray into residential living in Colorado.

With architecture by SAR+, interiors by BAMO, and conceptual design by Carlos Ott, prices start at $1.5 million for a studio and cap out at $13 million for the largest penthouse.

While the luxury leader could have set down in a high-end ski haven like Aspen, Vail, or Telluride, the company instead went with Cherry Creek, a dense, walkable retail corridor five miles from the center of Denver.

The neighborhood's median price per square foot is $293, according to Realtor.com data, compared to $334 for the overall Denver metropolitan area.

Featuring two major commercial destinations—Cherry Creek North and Cherry Creek Shopping Center—the global brand felt this was the place to make its residential debut in the Rocky Mountain state.

The brand's housing portfolio is set to nearly triple, reaching 34 properties and 3,498 units by 2030, according to the company.

Residences are particularly clustered in Florida, with condo buildings in Pompano Beach, St. Petersburg, Sarasota, and Miami. Other locations include New York City; Atlanta, GA; Chicago, IL; Las Vegas, NV; Park City, UT; and Maui, HI.

Cherry Creek, in a small, dense 16-block radius, may seem an unexpected choice to join in this world renowned group of cities, but the developer says it as an easy decision.

"We recognized early on that Denver was ready for a world-class, branded luxury residential offering, and Cherry Creek was the natural fit," Ryan Shear, managing partner at developer Property Markets Group (PMG), told Realtor.com.

"The neighborhood’s established luxury market, vibrant retail and dining scene, and distinctive character aligned perfectly with the Waldorf Astoria brand and the kind of project we aimed to deliver."

"Cherry Creek has long been one of Denver's most prestigious neighborhoods," Dawn Raymond of Kentwood Real Estate, who is heading up sales in the complex, tells Realtor.com.

She touts the district's central location, walkability, eye-catching architecture, high-end shopping options, art galleries, and a restaurant scene that has "exploded" in the past five years.

According to Denver's tourism website, the neighborhood boasts 300 stores, 75 cafes and restaurants, 50 spas and salons, and five hotels.

"It's really the walkability that appeals to everyone," she says. "It's like a large village. To be able to live in the neighborhood, and walk out the door in the morning, grab a cup of coffee or go work out before going into the office, it's very much a live-work place."

Raymond, who has long represented top tier properties in the area, says that there are only three other luxury condos in the neighborhood—and none of them offer the level of service and amenities that the Waldorf does.

Affluent state residents were ready to pounce: An impressive 75% of the complex's units, or all but seven, are already sold. This includes four of the seven penthouses, as well as the most expensive penthouse, priced at $13 million.

Monthly HOA dues will be estimated about $1.50 per square foot, which will cover most amenities and services.

Raymond says that the developer, PMG, "leaned on" the local brokerages, asking them what well-heeled buyers in the area wanted.

"We had a whole list of things people were looking for," she says. Top of the list for amenities was a fitness center—and the complex will have a wellness center with massage, sauna and steam rooms, as well as the now-requisite cold plunge.

"A lot of people were asking about that," she says with a laugh. "It's like taking two shots of espresso."

There's also a resort rooftop pool, signature restaurant (still being negotiated), owners' lounge, and the Waldorf's signature Peacock Alley-inspired lounge and bar.

These affluent buyers are also busy—and crave as frictionless a lifestyle as possible.

"They value time, convenience, personalized service," she says. "That's really a hallmark of the Waldorf Astoria name."

To that end, the concierge services will include valet, housekeeping, pet services, laundry, parcel reception, doorman and bellman, security, automotive detailing, chef services, and more.

Personal concierge services are "a la carte," and could include requests such as finding a hotel for visiting family members, or procuring tickets to a hot theatrical production. An initial concierge meeting with a buyer will help pinpoint services that might be needed, says the broker.

Ownership also comes with discounts at both Waldorf Astoria and parent company Hilton hotels worldwide.

"What surprised me is how many local people were buying," says the agent. "I was thinking we'd be getting people from all over the country—which we did—but the initial run was all local people."

She attributes the intense local interest to elite Colorado residents—who already own a ski chalet, mountain estate, or sprawling ranch—also wanting a "city place," as their second or even third abode.

But until a complex came along with a bold name like Waldorf Astoria, they couldn't find a place that met their high standards.

"A lot of the buyers had looked at the other condo buildings," she says, but found them lacking. "They were waiting for something truly spectacular to come along."

The Waldorf Astoria was "literally the perfect brand to come in," she says. "It's just brought so much excitement."

Beyond generally being locals, the condo's buyers are a "diverse group," from CEOs to techies to "oil and gas people" to ranchers. "It's quite a mix of people," she says.

Since most of the buyers will be using the pads as pieds-à-terre, they wanted to know that everything will be taken care of for them awhile they were away, says the broker.

The 8 Best Soft Sci-Fi Movies in Film History

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The label 'soft sci-fi' refers to sci-fi stories that shift the focus away from strict scientific plausibility and instead place a premium on people, culture, and society. They're not that interested in explaining the nitty-gritty of their advanced tech or alien creatures. The speculative elements exist to bring out the themes or reveal something about human nature.

With that in mind, this list looks at some of the best movies in this subgenre, from intimate romances grand to space operas, the wacky time-travel of Back to the Future to the existential cybernoir of Blade Runner. Bangers, all.

“Meet me in Montauk.” Although this one has a speculative premise involving fictional technology, it's really a story about relationships. After discovering that his former girlfriend Clementine (Kate Winslet) has undergone a procedure to remove her memories of their relationship, main character Joel (Jim Carrey) decides to do the same. Most of the film then unfolds inside his collapsing memories as the procedure progresses, which writer Charlie Kaufman and director Michel Gondry (both masters of the offbeat) cleverly present in non-linear order.

Joel revisits arguments, disappointments, tenderness, embarrassment, and ordinary moments that seemed insignificant when they happened. In the process, he begins to realize that losing the painful parts of the relationship also means losing everything meaningful about it. As a result, Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind winds up being a madcap but surprisingly touching psychodrama.

“The heart's not like a box that gets filled up. It expands in size the more you love.” This is one of the most perceptive movies ever made about AI, precisely because it avoids the usual Skynet-like 'evil robot' storyline. Instead, we get something more wistful and intimate. Joaquin Phoenix leads the cast as Theodore Twombly, a lonely writer living in a near-future Los Angeles who installs an advanced operating system that names herself Samantha (voiced by Scarlett Johansson).

Samantha learns extraordinarily quickly, developing curiosity, humor, insecurity, desire, and an increasingly complex relationship with Theodore. Director Spike Jonze refuses to treat that romance as a simple joke about a man falling in love with his computer. Ultimately, Her paints a compellingly prescient picture of isolation in the digital age, as well as a cautionary tale about the inability of any machine to truly replicate human connection.

“Roads? Where we're going, we don't need roads.” Back to the Future devotes remarkably little effort to making time travel scientifically credible. Doc Brown (Christopher Lloyd) invents the flux capacitor after hitting his head on a bathroom sink, installs it inside a DeLorean, and discovers that traveling through time requires reaching 88 miles per hour. That's essentially all audiences need to know, because director Robert Zemeckis understands that explaining the physics would only slow down the endlessly entertaining, ingeniously constructed plot.

The solution requires our likable hero Marty McFly (Michael J. Fox) to play matchmaker for two teenagers who will eventually become his own mother and father, while simultaneously finding enough power to return home. The time travel itself makes no real sense and functions more like something out of a fairy tale than a typical sci-fi flick, but that's all part of the charm.

“Everything that comes true is an expression of your true wishes.” This cryptic gem from Andrei Tarkovsky bears some sci-fi trappings, but approaches them with a meditative sensibility. In Stalker, three men (Alexander Kaidanovsky, Anatoly Solonutsyn, and Nikolai Grinko) are sent into an eerily abandoned landscape known as the Zone, where the normal rules of reality may no longer apply. The Stalker makes his living illegally guiding people into this forbidden territory.

His latest clients are known simply as the Writer and the Professor, both of whom want to reach a mysterious Room supposedly capable of granting a person's deepest desire. Their journey gradually becomes less about reaching a physical destination than confronting what each man actually believes. In other words, this is philosophical sci-fi where clear answers are in desperately short supply. It's poetic and elliptical, leaving the viewer to draw their own conclusions.

“Dreams make good stories, but everything important happens when we're awake.” The original Dune novels are landmarks of soft sci-fi, almost science fantasy, really, and Denis Villeneuve did a great job of carrying that over to his film adaptations, while still making them feel modern. He weaves a colorful, thematically rich tale of prophecy and personality cults, focused on Paul (Timothée Chalamet), the scion of House Atreides, ordered to take control of Arrakis.

Although the story takes place in a far-flung future, the vibe is mythological rather than coldly high-tech. (Indeed, we don't see any digital devices at all). We get grnithopters, shields, spice harvesting machines, and interstellar travel, but the movie doesn't get bogged down in explaining how it all works. Instead, the emphasis is on imperialism, political manipulation, ecological exploitation, religion, and Paul's growing awareness that the visions surrounding him may have horrifying consequences.

“Unfortunately, no one can be told what the Matrix is. You have to see it for yourself.” The Matrix contains enough philosophy, martial arts, cyberpunk imagery, religious symbolism, and leather coats to fuel decades of interpretation. Keanu Reeves turns in perhaps his most iconic performance as Neo, a programmer who learns that humanity has been imprisoned by machines inside a simulated reality, all so they can be exploited for the machines' ends.

It's a killer premise that draws on real problems from philosophy, which the Wachowskis gleefully fuse with ideas borrowed from anime, Hong Kong action cinema, cyberpunk literature, and mythology. So much of the movie has become world-famous (like the red and blue pills), and its vision of a future where people live entirely in an online, digital world manipulated by bots now feels uncomfortably close to home.

“Do. Or do not. There is no try.” Few sequels build so well on their predecessor's foundation. A New Hope gave us a galaxy of Jedi, droids, smugglers, and impossible spacecraft, and The Empire Strikes Back gave those ideas a darker, deeper treatment. Here, the heroes lose repeatedly, and those failures make their eventual journey more meaningful. After the Rebel Alliance is driven from its base on Hoth, the central characters are separated.

Han (Harrison Ford) and Leia (Carrie Fisher) flee the Empire aboard the Millennium Falcon while Luke (Mark Hamill) travels to Dagobah to train under Yoda. Again, the aesthetic verges on science fantasy. Faster-than-light travel, giant walking tanks, artificial intelligence, and inhabited asteroid fields exist alongside the mystical Force. Enterprise blockchain is going to come back from the dead

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

With scalability, privacy tools and onchain liquidity improving, businesses may finally have the infrastructure to make enterprise blockchain work.

Opinion by Paul Brody, founder and chief executive, Nightfall Networks

Do you remember Pets.com? It raised $110 million from Amazon and others to build a business where people ordered pet toys and dog food online.

The company crashed and burned, and everyone learned an important lesson: you can’t profitably ship a 50-pound bag of dog food to a customer’s door.

As online retail entrepreneur Ed Baum asked in a 2001 postmortem: “Did Pets.com’s business plan assume the invention of transporters to magically move bags of pet food without shipping costs?”

Please, whatever you do, don’t share this with the people at Chewy; they might not realize their business model doesn’t work. Chewy reported $12.6 billion in sales and $223 million in net income in its latest fiscal year.

The real lesson of Pets.com wasn’t that shipping dog food was a bad idea. It’s that the idea arrived before the logistics, customer habits and unit economics required to make it work.

As it was with chew toys and dog food, so it may be with enterprise blockchains.

Collectively, the crypto industry probably incinerated just as much money trying that between 2017 and 2023 as Pets.com. Maybe more. But the dozens of companies that went after enterprise blockchain were not delusional; they were just too early.

It’s not hard to understand the appeal of enterprise blockchain solutions, at least superficially. Nearly all enterprise systems today tend to fail at the boundaries between companies. There are two failure modes: mode 1, the OG failure mode, where collaboration information is constantly out of sync.

In this world, enterprise IT systems are islands, communicating asynchronously with each other by digital text messages and emailed spreadsheets. It’s cheap and easy to set up, if you don’t mind always having slightly wrong information.

Related: Privacy is missing ingredient for business blockchain adoption: EY exec

Failure mode two is the costly, real-time proprietary integration. Powerful industry leaders politely request their key suppliers and partners to join their closed but always-on networks.

Chewy reported $223 million in net income in its latest fiscal year. Source: Chewy.

These are very costly, but they work well and leave everyone with a kind of business version of Stockholm syndrome, where you come to appreciate the thoroughness and efficiency of the digital choke-hold you are in.

Blockchains offered a better path forward: shared information and business logic across an open ecosystem. All the benefits of a proprietary system in terms of real-time visibility and efficiency, without the lock-in.

Sometime around 2017, this started to look like a very obvious good idea. Investment poured in, consortia launched, press releases were drafted and released, and then pretty much everything crashed and burned. TradeLens, We.Trade, Contour, B3i, ASX Chess, Everledger. All came and went in just a few years.

Though there is a lot of nuance to the details of each of these stories, they all largely have one thing in common: they had to do unnatural things to make up for the lack of privacy available natively in blockchains.

No company wants their competition to see what they’re doing in detail. Mostly, this meant building a private/permissioned chain and then doing lots of non-blockchain-like things to it and in it to try to keep information private. The results were uniformly slow and expensive and failed to gain traction.

It’s hard to understate the bloodbath of companies and startups that tried and failed to make enterprise blockchain work. A quick web and history search turned up 18 different ones, some of them well-known.

Many had big-name backers, from major banks like Deutsche Bank and HSBC to technology companies like IBM. Pedigreed parents and big brands made no difference — privacy didn’t work and the results were dismal.

What few survivors of this are left have “de-chained” themselves — giving up the blockchain theater and reverting to a classic software-as-a-service (SaaS) model. Many companies that tried and tested these permissioned enterprise systems as users came away from their experience with a clear learning: we tried blockchain, it didn’t work.

That’s unfortunate because everything is different now.

Blockchains have scale. Ethereum in 2020 handled 1 million transactions a day and costs per transaction periodically spiked to $30-50 when demand was high. Today, most transactions get done under a penny and capacity is ramping toward billions of transactions a day. Nobody talks about the “trilemma” anymore. Ethereum is decentralized and it is scaled.

Related: Ethereum’s Glamsterdam upgrade launches on Sepolia testnet

Privacy is different now, too. While privacy is still not the default setting, a huge range of privacy tools and systems are now available, from privacy pools to zero-knowledge roll-ups and full privacy chains.

On top of that, Ethereum is building a range of tools that will add basic elements of privacy at multiple levels in the network. The result: it’s now possible to run business operations at scale with a high degree of privacy.

Finally, there’s the money. It’s all moving onchain. Regulators around the globe have blessed stablecoins and the result is a tsunami of liquidity, with transaction volume reaching almost $1.8 trillion in June.

If we combine the operational benefits of blockchains with the financial ones, we get something that enterprises have never really had before: a single ecosystem where you can not only buy, sell, and move assets, but get paid for them as well.

There was just under $1.8 trillion in adjusted stablecoin transaction volume in June. Source: Visa

The return on investment from every single component of this equation is big. Combined, they can yield colossal advantages. McKinsey partners even found that digital supply chain tools could make inventory reductions of 35-75% possible through better planning.

More conservatively, from my experience in the field, just switching away from the existing cascades of spreadsheets and electronic data interchange (EDI) messages can allow firms to run most supply chains with about 20% less inventory at the same service level. That’s worth billions in working capital in most industries.

It gets better from there. Smart contracts reduce the time required to handle many business contract rules from days to minutes and cut the costs of administration in half.

Microsoft began moving its Xbox video game network onto smart contracts for handling royalties and cut the time to access monthly royalty statements from 45 days to four minutes. All that while handling more than 2 million transactions a day.

And once you have inventories, contracts, purchase orders and invoices onchain, you can go after working capital directly as well.

When you can prove your purchase order originates from a top-quality buyer, you can access credit for less. And when you have full visibility into inventory and delivery status, you can factor receivables immediately and efficiently.

The possibilities and the returns are big. The use cases are obvious. It’s time to try again.

These are some of the strongest 3D printer filaments for durable parts

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Stronger filament doesn't automatically mean a stronger print, and the toughest materials can require better hardware too.

If you're looking for one winner, it's not exactly convenient that a 3D print can be "strong" in a few different ways. A rigid bracket should resist bending, while a part that gets knocked around may last longer if it can flex and recover instead of breaking. In this article, we're going to hone in on rigidity and the types of materials that work well for structural parts.

Carbon-fiber-reinforced nylon (PA-CF) and polycarbonate (PC) are two of the strongest practical materials for the kind of filament-based 3D printers that are accessible to the average maker. Both materials can handle demanding functions, higher temperatures and considerably rougher treatment than everyday polylactic acid (PLA) or glycol-modified polyethylene terephthalate (PETG).

Sure, there are stronger, more expensive specialty materials that could vie for the title of heavyweight filament champ. But let's stick to the pros and cons of carbon-fiber nylon versus polycarbonate, so you can decide which material packs the most punch for your projects.

Carbon-fiber-reinforced nylon (PA-CF) combines nylon with short carbon fibers — hence the name. Nylon already handles demanding functional parts well, and the added fibers make the finished material stiffer and more resistant to bending under load. How resistant, you ask?

Bambu Lab's dry-state test puts its carbon-fiber-reinforced nylon 6 (PA6-CF) at 151 megapascals (MPa) of bending strength. Its heat-deflection temperature is 186 degrees Celsius, meaning it resisted deformation under a specified load until around that temperature. Those figures help explain why carbon-fiber nylon is used for brackets, fixtures and other working parts instead of for items that simply sit pretty on a shelf.

A strong filament, however, does not necessarily make a strong print. How you orient the object on the print bed, plus wall thickness, infill and part shape, can significantly change how much abuse the project can take. Bambu Lab does not claim the 151 MPa result applies to every finished print. Pricing is another factor to note, as Polymaker currently lists a 1kg spool of its Fiberon PA6-CF20 at $52.99 at time of writing

There is some hardware housekeeping to contemplate too. Carbon fibers are abrasive, so your old faithful brass nozzle may not last long. Bambu recommends a hardened steel nozzle, requires PA6-CF to be dried before use, and lists a nozzle temperature of 260 to 290 degrees Celsius with an enclosed printer when using this material.

The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) says fused-filament desktop printers can emit respiratory irritants and ultrafine particles, so good ventilation or filtration is important to consider.

Unlike carbon-fiber composites, polycarbonate (PC) produces strong parts without chopped fibers. It's a stiff engineering plastic with good heat resistance, making it suitable for housings, brackets and other parts that need to withstand more than light use.

Once again, you'll need to consider your equipment and workspace before deciding if PC is the best and strongest filament for you. 📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Last year, researchers from Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon University revealed startling evidence about the impact of using AI on how—and how hard—people think, finding that among more than 300 knowledge workers, leaning too much on AI tools like ChatGPT was associated with diminished critical thinking skills.

The study, mirrored by results from MIT-led research, suggested that even using AI for low-stakes tasks such as proofreading “can lead to significant negative outcomes in high-stakes contexts,” like writing legal documents, the study authors wrote.

For the young generation of digital natives navigating AI anxiety around keeping up with peers using the technology and AI displacing them from jobs, the fear of the technology making people dumber is dominant. But that hasn’t stopped them from using AI—even when they’re explicitly told not to.

A Wharton-led survey published earlier this year, conducted in partnership with Gallup and the Walton Family Foundation, found young people are ramping up their AI use, even as their concerns about it causing lazy thinking persist. A survey of nearly 2,500 U.S. adults between the ages of 18 and 28 completed in October 2025 found 79% of respondents believed AI makes people lazier, and 62% said they had concerns it makes people less smart.

“What we find is deep ambivalence on how Gen Z is thinking [about] using AI,” Benjamin Lira Luttges, a postdoctoral scholar at Wharton who led research for the report, told Fortune.

Despite these fears, Gen Zers have increased their AI usage. Pew Research Center data published in June found 66% of people ages 18 to 29 have ever used an AI chatbot, such as ChatGPT, up from 55% in 2024. However, 37% of those young adults said they believed the impact of AI on them over the next 20 years would be negative, the most pessimistic attitude of any age group surveyed.

The paradox of Gen Z’s willingness to use AI at the office, even amid persistent worries about the technology’s impact on critical thinking, lays bare the young generation’s complicated feelings toward AI, according to the report’s authors. After all, Gen Z’s fraught relationship with AI runs deep. Nearly one-fifth of the generation is worried about AI displacing them at work, yet they lead the workplace in AI adoption.

Though they require some decoding, Gen Z’s tangled attitudes toward AI can be critical in designing a path forward for the technology, more broadly, to be best integrated into the workplace, Lira Luttges suggested.

“Young people lead the adoption of new technologies, and a lot of things that are often seen as fringe, as not mainstream, are adopted by young people and eventually become part of the mainstream,” he said. “So in a sense … looking at Gen Z is a way of looking towards the future of work.”

Lira Luttges speculates the biggest mental factor informing Gen Z’s attitudes toward AI is simply a bias toward immediate gratification, a disposition more prominent in younger, developing minds.

“There’s a legitimate tradeoff between benefits and costs that you get from using AI,” he said. “Our brains are wired to prefer smaller, immediate rewards versus long-term, delayed rewards.”

As Gen Z grapples with finding or keeping jobs, as well as scaling their career ladders, job performance bolstered by an AI boost may hold more appeal than the less tangible threat of critical thinking skills loss. Similarly, even if an employer does not want an employee using AI for certain tasks, those workers, particularly if young, might consider getting their tasks done efficiently as more important, particularly if the risk of getting caught is slow, Lira Luttges noted.

Gen Z survey respondents, for example, may see themselves as AI power-users, Lira Luttges said. Sure, AI could atrophy critical thought capabilities and make other people lazy, but not those filling out the survey.

To maximize how AI is used in the workplace, employers should not ban AI, but rather embrace ambivalence toward it, the report authors argued. According to the survey, respondents who reported using AI more frequently worried less about its impact on intelligence and motivation, indicating AI anxiety may resolve over time.

Some future-of-work experts, including Mark Beasley, professor and director at North Carolina State University’s Poole College of Management, believe a critical thinking gap, not an AI skills gap, will pose a serious threat to organizational pipelines and business operations. Beasley told Fortune the threat AI poses to entry-level jobs could mean insufficient training and experience for middle- and eventually upper-tier positions in the near future.

“The biggest risk organizations face is just being stagnant,” he said.

“You should outsource the crap, not the craft,” he added.

Scientists Just Challenged a 50-Year-Old Theory About How Evolution Works

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

In bacteria and yeast, a gene’s importance did not predict its mutation rate, challenging the idea that evolution gives vital DNA preferential treatment.

Not all genes are equally important. Some are essential for survival, controlling basic functions such as copying DNA and keeping cells alive. Others can be lost with little or no effect. But does that mean evolution has found a way to shield its most vital genes from potentially harmful mutations?

For decades, scientists have debated whether essential genes are naturally less likely to mutate. Now, researchers at the University of Bath have put that idea to the test, with results that challenge a long-standing evolutionary hypothesis.

Dr. Sofia Radrizzani and Professor Laurence D. Hurst, from the university’s Centre for Evolution, examined spontaneous mutations in the bacterium Escherichia coli (E. coli) and baker’s yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae). Their study, published November 12 in PLOS Biology, found no evidence that essential genes receive extra protection and offers a new explanation for why earlier research may have suggested otherwise.

“We’d expect natural selection to rapidly remove mutations when they occur in more important genes, because these mutations are more likely to be harmful to the organism. This can create the illusion that important genes have fewer mutations when, in reality, some mutations have simply disappeared before we have a chance to analyze them – what you can think of as ‘ghost mutations’,” Hurst said.

To reduce that problem, the team used data from “mutation accumulation” experiments designed to minimize the effects of natural selection. Three separate tests found no evidence that selection had substantially skewed the results.

“This looks like the largest and cleanest sample of mutations assembled to date,” Radrizzani said.

The researchers compared mutation rates with experimental evidence showing what happens when individual genes are removed. Deleting an essential gene kills the cell, while losing other genes has little or no effect. This gave the team a way to test whether genes a cell depends on most were less likely to mutate.

“Analyze the data any which way and we found nothing. In neither species did these key genes have a lower mutation rate,” Hurst said.

The team also assessed importance through the amount of a gene’s protein the cell produces and the strength of natural selection acting on the gene over evolutionary time.

“We found that genes producing more abundant proteins don’t have lower mutation rates. Nor does the way genes evolve over thousands of years predict how frequently they mutate,” Radrizzani said.

These findings address a debate that began in 1972 and resurfaced as recently as 2023. At stake is the relationship between the two steps in the conventional account of evolution. Mutations first generate genetic variation. Natural selection and other forces then act on that variation, favoring some traits over others.

Darwin’s finches, which Charles Darwin encountered on the Galápagos Islands in 1835, offer a familiar example. Random mutations contribute to differences in beak size, allowing birds to use different food sources. When conditions favor particular foods, birds with beaks better suited to eating them are more likely to survive and reproduce. Their traits consequently become more common over generations.

The disputed proposal suggests that a gene’s importance also influences how often mutations arise in it. Vital genes would receive greater protection, while genes where change could help adaptation would mutate more frequently. Examples of the latter include genes in which mutations give bacteria antibiotic resistance or alter birds’ beak dimensions.

There is a theoretical difficulty with that proposal. Gene mutation rates are already so low that reducing them further for individual genes would offer very little benefit, making it difficult for natural selection to favor such a protective system.

“The claim that mutation is slower for important genes is a triumph of hype over substance,” said Radrizzani, who co-wrote the paper as part of her PhD. “It’s a really old suggestion and is reinvented in various forms routinely.”

Genes copied later during DNA replication did tend to mutate more frequently in the team’s analysis, showing that some features of the genome can help explain differences in mutation rates.

“While we see known causes of variation in mutation rates, we found no evidence for the effects that have been proposed as a challenge to the conventional model. None predicted which genes would have high or low mutation rates, and there are certainly no grounds for a shift in paradigm,” Hurst said.

Gen Z’s job crisis has reached the ultrarich. Wealth advisors say billionaire parents are now planning for kids who may struggle to land and keep jobs

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Even the wealthiest parents in America are worried about their kids’ financial futures. The unemployment rate for recent college graduates has held at about 5.6%, and 42.5% of recent grads were underemployed at the end of 2025, the highest share since 2020. A college degree no longer guarantees a steady paycheck, and economists warn AI could upend the entry-level work that recent grads have historically done.

In a world where seemingly no one is immune to the impacts of AI on the workforce and an increasingly stiff job market, ultra-high net worth people are starting to have the same concerns as middle-class and low-income families: Will my kid be able to get a job and support themself?

“Billionaires have the financial resources to support their children, but they sometimes struggle to determine what else is needed for their children to succeed,” Tom Thiegs, managing director of leadership and legacy at Ascent Private Capital Management with U.S. Bank, told Fortune earlier this year.

Millionaires and billionaires “are recognizing this is not the same game they had to play,” wealth manager Patrick Dwyer told CNBC earlier this year. “Families have to rethink … what it means to support their children. And we’re not talking about spoiling your kids. We’re talking about: What if your kid needs retraining at 33?” Dwyer is managing director at Aligned by NewEdge Wealth, a boutique wealth management firm based in Miami. He works with clients with net worths between roughly $100 million and more than $1 billion.

Dwyer said his clients are concerned their children, typically between age 22 and 35, are struggling to secure and hang on to jobs that are historically associated with security and status, including technology, law, and health care. That means the wealthy will have to plan for a future in which they’re passing on more of their fortune to their kids.

“[They’re] realizing that if they don’t pass on more meaningful wealth to their children, or their children are not able to accumulate wealth … their kids could have [less] agency over their lives than they did,” he told CNBC.

While this may sound like an irrational fear from America’s wealthiest, it’s a reality more wealth managers recognize.

“This is a very real concern I’m hearing from ultra-affluent families right now,” Thiegs said. “On the surface it can sound irrational: ‘Why would a billionaire worry about their child getting a job?’ But realistically, no matter how much money you have, parents still want their children to succeed and lead fulfilled lives.”

The job market jitters Gen Zers face directly impacts how their parents can help them plan for their financial futures.

But the crux of the problem isn’t that parents fear they won’t financially be able to help their children, it’s more that they’re worried they won’t have the same career outcomes and sense of fulfillment as past generations.

“They’re not usually worried about the financial security of their children; rather they worry that the job market will impact their child’s sense of purpose, identity, and confidence,” Thiegs said. “They also worry that significant wealth will dampen their drive or desire to work.”

But that doesn’t mean Thiegs encourages his clients just to bankroll their kids for the foreseeable future. Instead, estate planning, investing, and other long-term financial planning is a must.

“When parents are worried about their children’s job security, we recommend creating a system that provides opportunities for growth and development rather than just a financial safety net,” he said.

It’s more important to implement plans that support a child’s self worth than just their net worth, he added.

Trent Von Ahsen, a certified financial planner and managing partner at Cedar Point Capital Partners, also says his ultra-high net worth families view the risks of today’s job market as less of a financial stability concern, but more about whether they’re setting their kids up to be indefinitely dependent on them.

“This cohort of parents seem more concerned about over-supporting their children, than under-supporting them,” Von Ahsen told Fortune earlier this year.

The shift away from historically high-paying, white-collar jobs is already evident in the choices Gen Z is making. Facing mass layoffs in white‑collar sectors and anxious about AI, many young workers are peeling away from traditional corporate routes in favor of jobs they think might offer more control or faster cash—from becoming content creators to opting for blue-collar jobs in manufacturing, electrical work, and other technical work.

In some cases, college‑educated Gen Zers are even competing for six‑figure nanny and tutor roles in elite households, chasing financial “freedom” outside of a traditional office career. A 2026 Deloitte global survey found just 6% of Gen Z and millennial respondents said reaching a leadership position is their primary career goal. Instead, many say they favor stability, skill-building, and well-being over climbing the ladder quickly.

This all means, though, billionaires and other high-net worth individuals have to financially plan differently from how they might have in the past. They have to design financial plans that “encourage growth and responsibility” instead of only making large inheritances all at once, Von Ahsen said.

“We see more emphasis on education funding flexibility, mentorship, and phased wealth transfers,” he said. It’s “really an attitude moving toward providing opportunity without removing initiative.”

Why did Google Pixels stop using Snapdragon chips?

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google ditched Snapdragon in 2021 to build phones around its own AI silicon.

It seems like it was just yesterday when Google dropped Qualcomm's Snapdragon chips and launched the Pixel 6 on its proprietary Tensor platform – but it's not. We've just passed the five(!) year anniversary in October, so what better time than now to look at why Google did it. The decision saw Google swap out the mobile gaming pedigree of Snapdragon in the Pixel 5a for better AI capabilities (like transcribing a rambling voice note) in the Pixel 6 with Tensor silicon shaped around Google's machine learning models.

Tensor spent its first four generations as a heavily reworked Samsung Exynos design. Google only recently moved to a chip of its own base design with last year's Tensor G5, fabricated in Taiwan by TSMC. The Tensor G6 in the new Google Pixel 11 continues that lineage, while the Pixel Watch 5 that launched beside it still carries a Qualcomm chip.

In August 2021, Google's SVP of Devices and Services, Rick Osterloh, wrote in an announcement that the company had "run into computing limitations that prevented us from fully pursuing our mission" in AI, so it built its own chip. Its centerpiece was the tensor processing unit (TPU), a custom application-specific integrated circuit dedicated to running Google's speech recognition, translation and computational photography models on the phone instead of in the cloud.

Five generations on, the workload has changed but the overarching design hasn't; Google says the Tensor G6 packs 50 percent more TPU compute than the G5. Paired with the latest Gemini Nano model, it also claims up to 3.5 times faster on-device AI tasks that use up to 3.5 times less energy.

Testing by Android Authority measured Qualcomm's Snapdragon 8 Elite at more than twice the Tensor G5's multi-core CPU performance and around 2.5 times its graphics performance. The publication puts this discrepancy down to Google's financial priorities rather than any failures in engineering, but there's no dancing around the fact that a flagship Snapdragon or an Apple A-series chip beats Tensor on things like raw power and sustained gaming loads (and probably battery life under heavy use), and that's been true of every Tensor Pixel since 2021. Google is a services company before it's a silicon specialist after all, and it has accepted deficits in benchmarks as the price of running its Gemini features the way it wants. That's great if you're a Gemini power user, but not so great if you're any other type of power user.

The Pixel Watch 5 was announced alongside the Pixel Tag and Pixel 11 series, and runs on Qualcomm's Snapdragon W5 Gen 2. Indeed, the company hasn't yet made any wearable Tensor products and still relies on Qualcomm's silicon wherever it doesn't use its own.

Tensor itself began as a Samsung co-development codenamed Whitechapel. A 2021 analysis of the first chip found that it shared clock management, power management architecture and memory controllers with Samsung's Exynos processors. Samsung was responsible for manufacturing every Tensor through the G4 and supplied the modems, and its processes were blamed for the line's thermal issues and its Exynos modems for the connectivity problems. The Tensor G5 in last year's Pixel 10 was the first one built without Samsung, and the G6 is understood to have swapped the Exynos modem for a MediaTek part. That's according to leaked specifications that have since been partly confirmed, thus removing the last major Samsung part from a platform it helped build.

Qualcomm's flagship phone chips are said to cost manufacturers $240 to $280 per unit in the current Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 generation. That's an increase of $40 to $80 compared to the 8 Gen 3. In designing Tensor, Google has managed to dodge that markup and divert more budget to refining the TPU its AI features are built around. Interestingly, Samsung has gone in the other direction: Its Galaxy S26 Ultra uses that Gen 5 chip across all regions. On top of that, all North American S26 models use Snapdragon. Alas, while the switch from Snapdragon to Tensor didn't make Pixels cheaper than their rivals, it did give Google what it wanted: its own chip roadmap.

Vitamin C could help block cancer-linked chemicals in your stomach, study finds

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Vitamin C may do more than support everyday health. New research from the University of Waterloo suggests that it could also help prevent potentially cancer-causing chemicals from forming during digestion. Using mathematical modeling, scientists investigated how vitamin C influences chemical reactions in the digestive system that have been linked to cancer risk.

Exposure to nitrates and nitrites has increased in North American diets over the past several decades. These compounds are found in cured meats such as bacon and salami, as well as in fruits and vegetables grown in soil or water affected by pollution. Although nitrates and nitrites contribute to important functions in the brain and cardiovascular system, they can also participate in potentially harmful reactions once they reach the stomach.

One of these reactions, called nitrosation, can produce substances that researchers suspect may contribute to cancer development. Understanding what causes these chemicals to form, and what might prevent their formation, has been a longstanding challenge.

Why Vitamin C Could Change the Cancer Risk Equation

"Since at least the 90s, researchers have been studying the link between cancer and these compounds, with conflicting results," said Dr. Gordon McNicol, a post-doctoral researcher in applied mathematics and the first author of the study. "Our work suggests that the presence of dietary Vitamin C may help explain these inconsistencies."

To investigate this possibility, the researchers developed a mathematical model that simulates how nitrates and nitrites travel through the body and undergo chemical changes. The model incorporated several interconnected systems, including the salivary glands, stomach, small intestine, and plasma, the liquid portion of blood.

Their simulations revealed that vitamin C consumed alongside nitrates and nitrites may interfere with the formation of potentially harmful compounds. This could help explain why foods containing nitrates do not necessarily carry the same risks.

For example, leafy vegetables such as spinach naturally contain both nitrates and vitamin C. According to the model, the presence of vitamin C could limit the chemical reactions associated with cancer risk, potentially helping explain the protective effects suggested by some dietary research.

The researchers also explored whether vitamin C supplements might offer similar benefits when taken after eating.

Their findings suggest that taking vitamin C after each meal could moderately reduce the formation of nitrosation products associated with cancer risk. This effect could be particularly relevant when consuming foods containing nitrates and nitrites, including processed meats such as bacon and salami.

However, the results come from mathematical simulations rather than clinical trials. Although the model predicts that vitamin C can reduce the production of potentially harmful chemicals, further research is needed to determine whether this translates into a measurable reduction in cancer risk in humans.

New Clues for Future Cancer Prevention Research

The researchers believe their model could help scientists better understand the complicated relationship between diet, digestive chemistry, and cancer risk. It may also provide guidance for future experiments examining how nutritional choices influence the formation of potentially harmful compounds.

"This work provides a mechanistic roadmap for future clinical and laboratory studies by identifying the key interacting drivers of these potentially harmful chemical reactions, including nitrite exposure, antioxidant intake, meal timing, gastric conditions and oral microbiome activity," said Dr. Anita Layton, professor of applied mathematics and Canada 150 researcher chair. "This model can help researchers design more targeted experiments and interventions, focusing on when and in whom nitrosation is most likely to occur."

By identifying how factors such as vitamin C intake, meal timing, stomach conditions, and bacteria in the mouth interact, the model could help researchers develop more precise strategies for studying and potentially reducing cancer-related chemical reactions during digestion.

The study, "Vitamin C as a nitrosation inhibitor: A modelling study across dietary patterns and water quality," was published in the Journal of Theoretical Biology.

Like my hunting partner John Yenter, the guides were convinced that I was entirely too meticulous in my trophy specifications.

The only encouragement I had came from Kim Clark, who had become a very important part of our expedition. His full name was Webster Kimberly Clark, and he was a refuge biologist employed by the U. S. Had it not been for Kim encouraging me to stick to my specifications, I might have given up. He encouraged me because accurate field data on a big male brown bear just out of hibernation is apparently very scarce.

By the third day my partner John was adjusting his own specifications to suit the bear that was in sight. We were glassing a brownie on the shoreline across the lake, and through the glasses it looked good — perfect hide, pleasing color, but slightly under my requirements as to size. John wanted it.

In a few minutes we were on our way across the lake, approaching very quietly so as not to alarm the bear. We landed several hundred yards from the bruin, and John started stalking the animal with his guide. In spite of the skill they displayed, the bear caught the man smell. He was on his way up the mountainside before John could shoot. He and Nick stood on the shore watching the occasional motion of a bush which indicated that the bear was going higher and away.

The bear was 150 yards from the lake shore when he came into a little opening in the brush where we could see him. At that point he made the biggest and last mistake of his life. He stopped and looked back. At that moment John fired. The bear fell. Two more shots finished him completely. John’s performance was a credit to him, to his .375 H. & H. Winchester, Model 70 rifle, and its Bausch & Lomb scope sight.

It was without question a big bear, the color was excellent, the 8½-feet-square hide was prime and perfect. John was a thoroughly happy hunter.

With his bear hide strung up to dry, John concentrated on the wonderful trout fishing near camp, with time out now and then to think up some new criticisms of my ideas about a proper trophy.

At the end of the ninth day we sized up the situation and found that we had glassed 40 bears, killed one. I was still holding out for a bigger and better one.

The weather was perfect for the first three days, which was probably why we saw so many bears. There was rain every day after that, and visibility was often too poor for good work with binoculars. There’s no climate on Kodiak, just a vast assortment of unexpected and undesirable weather.

When we started out the 10th morning, we found that what had been rain at the lake camp during the night had been snow above the 1,500-foot level on the mountain. In spite of that, our hunting began early, and at 8:30 we saw a bear that looked very interesting. He was a mile from the lake shore, 1,600 feet higher, just above the new but temporary snow line.

Trying to get a better look at the bear, we moved up the bed of Cottonwood Creek. This took us out of sight of the bruin momentarily, but we knew exactly where he was because the snow line established his elevation and the pass made in the mountains by the creek marked his location the other way. Yet, an hour’s looking from our new position failed to locate him.

We then climbed up high on the right bank of Cottonwood Creek, and from there we glassed constantly until noon. We felt sure that the bear was there because we certainly would have seen him if he’d walked away.

At noon we abandoned that position and moved to a spot across the lake, a mile farther away, which gave us a better perspective and a view of more of the ground area. We took turns eating and resting, but we were constantly looking. The rain returned in full force and it was a very uncomfortable situation.

We never saw a sign of the bear until 4 o’clock, when suddenly he stood up and was in plain view again. It was now evident that we’d first seen him when he finished his morning feeding, and he’d bedded down for the day. Now he was up feeding again.

After a few minutes of careful observation, the guides decided that this bear would meet every point in my specifications. This was a fine display of skill on the part of the guides and is something I could never hope to duplicate. To the inexperienced, a bear two miles away through a 20X Bausch & Lomb telescope is just a bear, but my guide was able to classify him very accurately as to sex, size, and condition.

I knew enough by now to trust this guide’s judgment, and in a few minutes we were on our way back across the lake to start the stalk.

Before we reached the far shore the clouds had dropped so low that the mountains were completely obscured.

Since John had killed his bear, we decided that he’d stay with the boat and the two guides would accompany me on the climb up the mountain. It took a few minutes to strip for action, which meant down to heavy underwear, a sweat shirt, rain suit, and hip boots. This sounds like unusual mountain-climbing equipment, but the first mile of our climb would be accomplished by wading up the creek. It was much easier that way than climbing through the murderous alder thickets on either side.

By 5:30 we’d accomplished the most difficult part of the climb and were at about the same height as the bear and 500 to 800 yards to the right of him. At this point we climbed out of the creek and dived into the alder thicket. All alder thickets are nasty, but this one was the worst that I’d ever seen.

Soon we picked up a prominent bear trail, a path so worn that one of the guides called it “a bear highway.” It was obviously the main bear trail on that particular mountainside, and we felt sure that my bear was on it. We had no idea which way he was traveling, so it was possible that we’d meet him at any moment. It was also possible that he’d park a few yards off the trail to observe and properly take care of anything that might be using the bear highway. It’s this kind of thinking that prevents daydreaming on brown-bear stalks.

Grishka, who was in front of me, explained that he’d lead and watch ahead. Nick would bring up the rear and watch behind us and to the sides of the trail. I was free to watch any angle that suited my fancy. I think this was a polite way of telling me that they didn’t trust my powers of observation.

I was planning what I’d do if certain things happened. I realized that any shooting would be at very close range because of the thick alders and the heavy fog. While my guides were both carrying .348 Winchesters with open sights, I was carrying a .375 Winchester with a Bausch & Lomb scope. No scope is ideal equipment for a quick shot at 10 yards, and I wished for an old-fashioned V notch and front post.

Nick must have been reading my mind. He said, “Bill, if we meet the bear and you have trouble with your rifle, just throw it down and grab mine. I’ll be right behind you.”

Nothing could have done me more harm than that speech. I knew now that he had no more confidence in my equipment than I had, and I was scared stiff. I had enough acid in my stomach to pickle a ton of iron castings.

We soon came out into the little open spot on the mountainside that we’d marked as the location of the bear when last seen almost two hours before. This clearing was about 100 feet in each direction — no alders, just dead grass almost knee high. We spent 20 minutes searching the open area and forcing our way into the brush around the edges, trying to pick up a trail or some sign of the bear.

Suddenly my head was gripped by Grishka, a powerful man but only five feet tall. He pulled my head down to his level, slapped his wet face against mine. Pointing to the right, he whispered in my ear, “Bear there. Shoot!”

About 75 yards away, through a little opening in the brush, I could see a dark spot. It could have been a bear. As I dropped to one knee, my perfect guide braced his knees to my back to steady my position. His 9X binoculars were tight to his eyes and just above my head as he watched the bear.

When I picked up that brown spot in my 2½X scope, I couldn’t tell whether it was a bear or not. If it was, I couldn’t for the life of me see which way he was pointing.

In a whisper, I asked Grishka, “Which end is the head on?”

After a full minute’s careful observation through his glasses, he put his mouth to my ear and said, “Other end. Shoot!”

That concise data from Grishka was fed through my fire-control center down to the trigger finger, and my gun fired. I was so excited I didn’t hear the report of the rifle, nor was I conscious that there was any recoil. But I did hear the bullet hit something, and then a fraction of a second later I knew it was a bear. He roared his complete disapproval of the whole situation.

Then it seemed that the brown bulk went up in the air several feet. An instant later it was on the ground and for a split second was completely still. At that moment I fired my second shot, and the bear’s reaction to that was a quick lunge to the right, toward the alders. I fired my third shot as he disappeared.

Grishka had said the first shot was good, but I didn’t see how he could possibly know. The bear had disappeared into the brush just as though he hadn’t been touched.

Both guides were now running as hard as they could to the right — in the same general direction the bear was going when we last saw him. It was quite obvious that they were attempting to get ahead of the bear before he reached the creek bed, which was 600 yards away. I caught them quickly and stayed right with them, not due to any bravery on my part, but simply because it was obviously much safer to stay close to them and their two rifles.

Our travel in the next few minutes was parallel to that of the bear, and we could follow his location by the noise he made. I thought — and hoped — that the amount of noise was due to the fact that he was badly wounded, running blind and knocking brush to one side.

A few seconds later we were on the edge of the creek where we could look down a steep bank to the water 100 feet below. Then I realized that the noise of the bear was not to my left, as it had been, but almost back of me and getting closer. I turned to face the noise. A second later that bear came into sight less than 20 yards away.

It was the most stirring sight a thrill hunter could ever hope to see. That bear was coming at us at top speed, and it looked as though he knew exactly what he was going to do when he got there.

I slammed the gun to my shoulder and looked at the bear in the scope sight. The contour of the ground was such that I could see the back of his neck, so I selected a spot where the neck joins the shoulders. The crosshairs settled on that spot. I tightened on the trigger, adding pressure and waiting for the recoil.

But the bear rolled dead as Caesar before my rifle went off. I lowered the weapon still cocked and loaded. I never have felt so cheated in all my life. In another 1/10th of a second I could have been the Man Who Stopped the Charging Brownie.

Later examination showed that the first shot had gone very effectively into his side just back of the ribs, forward under the spine, just grazing it, cutting the main blood vessels just back of the heart. Clearly the bear had been too far gone to make a determined charge. He was only making a blind run with his last spark of life, and we happened to be in his path.

That bear was coming at us at top speed, and it looked as though he knew exactly what he was going to do when he got there.

I examined the bear closely, and was glad that I’d stuck to my specifications. It was a male, the color dark brown, the hide in perfect condition, and the size was enough to flatter any desk hand. It was the 41st bear we’d seen on this trip.

Suddenly I realized that it was beginning to get dark. We’d have to leave the bear and return the next day to do the skinning. I was worried about what might happen to the hide overnight, but Grishka said, “We leave strong man smell here. Nothing bother.” With a grin he added, “The strongest thing here is white-man smell of your sweat shirt. We leave it.”

I peeled off the shirt and he tied it to a limb above the bear. When we returned the next morning the shirt and the bear were there and nothing had bothered it.

The guides, Kim Clark, and I left camp early next morning and climbed the mountain to skin the bear. John remained in camp. He and the cook, Pete Unger, had planned a fishing trip.

Kim was keenly interested in the bear from our description so we went in loaded with camera equipment, scales, surgical instruments, test tubes, and specimen bottles. Very few Kodiak bears have ever been accurately weighed and examined, and this is especially true of big bears just out of hibernation.

My bear was an ideal specimen for studying the effects of hibernation, because he evidently had just come out of that lethargic state. The stomach was found to be about 1/10 full, and the contents weighed 16 pounds. Carefully separated and weighed, we found that the contents were 55 percent blue-joint grass, 20 percent nettle, 20 percent wild celery, and 5 percent poque, which is a parasitic growth on the roots of the alders. There was no animal matter present.

The intestine was then examined and the length was accurately determined to be 77 feet 6¾ inches. It was then split from end to end for internal examination. There was no food in it. This indicated two things: first, that the bear had not been out of hibernation long enough for food to pass through the stomach into the intestine, and second, that he had gone into hibernation with the intestine completely empty, which conformed to the generally accepted theory. Further examination revealed there wasn’t a sign of ulcer, scar, scab, or scale in the stomach or intestine.

We also observed that there were no parasites of any kind in the entire digestive tract. This is one of the strange results of hibernation, because this bear during the six months of feeding eats carrion of all kinds, specializing in dead fish. He also digs up and eats worms, insects, and roots. When winter comes, he’s literally and actually a fur coat full of worms. There are thousands of them, including 10-foot-long tapeworms. The bear knows instinctively that he cannot go into hibernation with any food in his body; it would ferment and cause serious trouble before spring.

So when winter approaches he goes on a bedtime diet of certain plants and berries. Then, after a few days of fasting, he’s completely empty. The worms go with the food, and he goes to sleep physically clean. His stored body fat keeps him going till spring.

Every piece of this bear was carefully weighed. He weighed 840 pounds at the time he was killed. Kim Clark figured his weight when he was fattened for hibernation in the fall would have been about 1,300 pounds.

His age was estimated at ten years. The rough field measurement of the skull indicated a Boone and Crockett rating of 28½, against the world record of slightly above 30 inches. Unless the skull shrinks more than an inch during the drying period that must precede official scoring, the trophy should rate a place on the Boone and Crockett record list. This was in every respect an outstanding bear. I learned a lot from him, and much more from Kim Clark.

The skull and all the data collected were sent to Dr. Robert Rausch, Arctic Health Research Center, Anchorage, Alaska, where it became a part of their records.

A fireplace should be a source of warmth, comfort, and connection in your home, not a source of worry, says Chris Maxson, owner and CEO of Acucraft Fireplaces in Big Lake, Minnesota. If your fireplace hasn’t been cleaned or professionally inspected in years, don’t light a fire until you’ve had it evaluated.

“Just because a fireplace looks fine on the outside doesn’t mean it’s safe to operate,” warns Maxson. Even when a fireplace sits unused, the chimney can develop obstructions from animal nests, leaves, and other debris. In addition, moisture intrusion, deterioration, and damaged components can create potential safety concerns. Whether you have a traditional wood-burning fireplace or a modern gas fireplace, you’ll want to understand how best to clean and maintain it.

It’s not safe to use a fireplace that has been neglected for an extended period. In general, you should clean a wood-burning fireplace and the chimney once a year before you start using it. Likewise, gas fireplaces need cleaning once a year or as recommended by the manufacturer. Here are the reasons to clean fireplaces, according to Maxson:

Wood-burning fireplaces produce soot, ash, and creosote as wood burns. Creosote is highly combustible, and accumulated deposits can increase the risk of a chimney fire. Both gas and wood-burning fireplaces also rely on properly functioning venting systems to direct combustion byproducts away from occupied spaces. Obstructions or damaged venting can contribute to dangerous conditions, including carbon monoxide exposure inside homes.

A clean, properly maintained fireplace is more likely to operate as designed. On the other hand, excessive buildup, restricted airflow, and neglected components can interfere with ignition, combustion, and draft.

Cleaning a fireplace isn’t just about making it look good. It’s about making sure the entire system—including the components you can’t see—is functioning properly. Preventive maintenance protects both your home and your investment.

Maxson recommends that homeowners have their fireplace professionally inspected at least once a year, even if they don’t use it very often. “Cleaning removes buildup, while an inspection helps identify hidden issues that cleaning alone won’t solve,” he explains.

For wood-burning fireplaces, annual chimney inspections are essential. “A qualified chimney professional can determine whether sweeping is necessary based on creosote accumulation, soot, debris, and other conditions,” explains Maxson. “Homeowners who burn wood frequently may require additional cleaning during the heating season” for their fireplaces. Women who have longer reproductive life spans or use menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) may have better brain health as they age, according to a new study.

The research, published November 4 in Neurology, found that longer lifetime exposure to estrogen was associated with fewer signs of damage to small blood vessels in the brain.

The findings could offer new insights into the relationship between estrogen and brain aging, particularly as women account for approximately two-thirds of Alzheimer’s disease cases worldwide.

Researchers also found that certain associations between hormone therapy and brain health differed depending on whether women carried a genetic variant associated with an increased risk of Alzheimer’s disease.

However, the relationship between estrogen and brain health is complex. While the findings suggest that longer estrogen exposure may be associated with healthier brain aging, they do not establish that hormone therapy can prevent Alzheimer’s disease or dementia.

The researchers drew data from the Gothenburg H70-1944 Birth Cohort, a longitudinal study of 1,203 individuals born in 1944 who resided in Gothenburg, Sweden.

The final cohort included 352 cisgender women, with an average age of 70, who had no neurological disorders and reliable self-reported data on their estrogen exposure.

The researchers found that those with a longer reproductive span had a lower risk of small vessel disease (SVD). Participants with a longer reproductive life span had an 8% lower risk of having two or more signs of SVD. “As estrogen levels decline during menopause, some of these protective effects may diminish. Trinh agreed that an individualized approach to menopause management is important.

“This does not mean that every woman should start hormone therapy to prevent dementia. The BeyondTrust bug was used before the patch

Posted on Nov 15 • Originally published at circuits.fit on Nov 15

CISA listed BeyondTrust Remote Support CVE-2026-102764 on October 16 with forensic triage required. BeyondTrust says it was exploited as a zero-day on August 29.

Edited by Emilio Santos. October 21, 2026.

On Thursday, October 15, 2026, BeyondTrust published security advisory BT26-05 for its Remote Support product. The flaw is CVE-2026-102764, scored 9.3 on CVSS version 4 and rated Critical. BeyondTrust describes it as improper origin validation in an internal messaging component that could let a remote attacker perform file operations on an affected appliance. Every Remote Support version up to and including 26.2.3 is affected. The next day, October 16, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency added the CVE to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog. The catalog row carries a federal due date of October 19 and marks forensic triage as Yes.

The patch is the easy part. BeyondTrust’s own account says the bug was used as a zero-day in late August, nearly seven weeks before the advisory. If you, or a supplier that reaches into your network, run a self-hosted appliance, the question this week is not only “are we patched?” It is “was the box used before we patched, and could we tell?”

BeyondTrust’s blog post on BT26-05, last updated October 16 at 12:00 p.m. EDT, gives this timeline:

As of the October 16 update, BeyondTrust says it knows of only those two affected customers and has seen no adversary activity since August. It assesses with moderate to high confidence that the actor is the China-nexus cluster Mandiant and Google Threat Intelligence Group track as UNC5221, which Mandiant now calls ARMORY CASTLE. It also warns that this actor tends to use a zero-day selectively and then, once the flaw is public, move to broad exploitation of every instance still unpatched.

Two details matter for buyers. Privileged Remote Access shares the affected code; BeyondTrust found no way to exploit it there but released the patch for it anyway. And self-hosted Remote Support customers without automatic updates must patch by hand, after first upgrading if they are older than 24.3.1.

The catalog entry links to Binding Operational Directive 26-04, which CISA issued on June 10, 2026. It binds only federal civilian agencies, but it is the clearest public statement of how the government ranks urgency. It asks four questions: is the asset publicly exposed, is the CVE in the catalog, can an attacker automate the exploit, and does the attacker gain partial or total control. For the top tier, an agency must remediate within three days and carry out a forensic triage to check whether the system was already compromised.

CISA’s implementation guidance sets recommended, not mandatory, targets: scoping within two hours of the catalog addition, evidence collection and patching within 24 hours, and an escalation decision within 48 to 72 hours. Evidence comes first, because patching can destroy it.

BeyondTrust’s account explains why that order is right here. It says the actor planted a web shell, escalated to full administrative control within minutes, installed persistence designed to survive a reboot, read the appliance’s stored secrets, and then deleted logs and its own tools before leaving. Evidence kept only on the appliance “may not survive,” the company writes, and what is there “cannot be implicitly trusted.” Logs forwarded off the box are what count.

BeyondTrust was one of nine additions to the catalog between October 13 and October 16. CISA added Ivanti Connect Secure CVE-2026-104935 and Windows CVE-2026-96951 on October 13; Palo Alto Networks PAN-OS CVE-2026-0322, Cisco Secure Email Gateway CVE-2026-76519 and two AhsayCBS backup-server flaws, CVE-2026-105133 and CVE-2026-105134, on October 14; an Atlassian Data Center file-access flaw, CVE-2026-21589, on October 15; and Fortinet CVE-2025-61624 alongside BeyondTrust on October 16. Six more, four of them Plex Media Server, followed on October 20. Every one of the nine October 13 to 16 rows, apart from AhsayCBS CVE-2026-105133, is marked for forensic triage.

As in the October 1 catalog briefing and the NetScaler note, most of the list sits at the network edge: VPNs, firewalls, email gateways, remote access and backup servers.

A remote-support appliance is privileged access by design. BeyondTrust says so plainly: the appliance “stores and brokers privileged credentials across an estate,” which makes it both a credential source and a map of who connects to what. Treat a compromise of one as a compromise of every credential it injected.

The second lesson is about your detection stack. BeyondTrust says its sensors fired in real time when the attacker escalated privileges, but the automated triage feature in a third-party detection platform closed those alerts as false positives. The company is now working with that vendor so automated triage cannot close high-severity signals on its own. Ask your managed detection provider, in writing, which high-severity alerts its automation can close without a person.

The exposure is often not your own appliance. Managed service providers, outsourced IT desks and software vendors use remote-support tools to reach their customers. If one of them runs self-hosted Remote Support, your systems sit one hop away from a box that was exploited as a zero-day. That is a third-party risk question with contract consequences: notification clauses, audit rights and who pays for forensic work.

Forensic triage costs money even when it finds nothing; budget for it as part of patching. If you carry cyber insurance, check now whether the notice clause is triggered by a suspected incident or only a confirmed one, and whether the insurer requires its own forensics firm. For a public company, a confirmed compromise also raises the Form 8-K question. The four-business-day clock under Item 1.05 starts at the materiality determination, not at the advisory date; the SEC clock briefing and the 8-K materiality worksheet cover how to record that decision.

BeyondTrust says it is withholding technical details while self-hosted appliances are still being patched. When those details are published, expect wider exploitation of anything left unpatched, which is the pattern the company itself predicts for this actor. Watch the BT26-05 blog and the catalog row for changes. Under BOD 26-04, federal agencies’ Phase III deadline, 180 days from issuance, falls on December 7, 2026 by our count; agency patch reporting will keep edge-device rows in the news through year-end.

On Wednesday, October 21, 2026, the PHP project released PHP 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36 and 8.2.35. Sophos and Amazon have both reported exploitation attempts. Neither has confirmed a successful compromise: Sophos says the attempts it saw failed, and Amazon says PHP2Shell is “now seeing broad exploitation attempts in the wild” but that it is unclear whether any would have succeeded on real-world systems. For most companies, PHP is not something they bought on purpose. It runs under the marketing site, the help desk, the vendor portal and the appliance in the rack. That changes what this week’s work looks like.

PHP 8.1 and older get no updates from the PHP project. Because of the severity, the security team published minimal source backports for 7.4, 8.0 and 8.1, and some distributions and long-term-support vendors ship their own fixes. PHP 8.2 reaches end of life on December 31, 2026.

As of this writing on October 22, neither CVE is in CISA’s Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog. We checked the catalog JSON feed, version 2026.10.21 with 1,754 entries, this afternoon. NVD lists both records as “Undergoing Analysis.”

Two working assumptions break here. The first is that the disclosure date is the start of the risk window. Amazon’s advice is direct: “Start investigations from the earliest known exploitation, not from the disclosure date. For PHP2Shell, investigate from at least starting on October 17.” No successful compromise has been confirmed, but a system patched this week was exposed from at least the 17th, and the log review is how you show it was not entered.

The second is the patch service level. Amazon’s CISO writes that for critical bugs in internet-facing systems, teams should “measure patch and mitigation timelines in hours,” because “a 7-day or 30-day SLA assumes a delay that no longer exists.” Whether you adopt that is a policy choice with a staffing cost, and it should be made on purpose rather than discovered during the next incident.

The harder problem is visibility. CISA notes that PHP “is often a component of other software and prevalence may not be directly observable.” CERT-EU makes the same point and points to a list maintained by the Dutch NCSC of third-party products that embed PHP. Your scanner may find the servers you run. It will not tell you which SaaS vendor’s customer portal runs mod_php on 8.1.

This is a supplier question with a paper trail. The useful output this week is a dated list: which vendors run PHP in the service you use, what version, and when they patched. That list is what your auditor, insurer and board will ask for if anything turns up later, and “the vendor said we are fine” is not an answer you can defend without a date and a name.

Budget for the tail. Systems on PHP 8.1 or older either get upgraded, get a paid backport from an LTS vendor, or come offline; CISA and Sophos both recommend taking unpatchable internet-facing systems offline or behind network-layer authentication. If the business refuses, record a dated, owned exception rather than an informal one. The 8.2 end-of-life date on December 31 means a second round of the same decision is ten weeks away. If a compromise is confirmed at a public company, the Form 8-K question follows; the SEC clock starts at the materiality determination, not at the advisory.

Watch the KEV catalog. If CISA adds either CVE, the row will carry a federal due date and may mark forensic triage, which would make the October 17 lookback a formal expectation for agencies. Watch Sophos and Amazon for any confirmed successful exploitation; so far both report attempts, and the payload Sophos analyzed, a likely Mirai variant, was blocked before delivery. Watch vendor advisories, which tend to arrive later than the upstream fix. And watch NVD, where analysis of both records is still under way.

How to Build an IT Risk Register in Excel

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 15 • Originally published at circuits.fit on Nov 15

Write the scales first, then look scores up from a 5 by 5 grid. Columns, formulas, severity bands and a worked example.

Edited by Emilio Santos. October 25, 2026.

The IT or security manager who needs a working risk register in a spreadsheet, and the finance or audit lead who has to read it.

An IT risk register is only as good as the agreement behind its numbers. The spreadsheet part takes an afternoon. Getting two people to give the same risk the same score takes longer, and it is the part that matters.

This guide builds a register in Excel (it works the same way in LibreOffice Calc and Google Sheets) around a 5 by 5 likelihood by impact matrix: the columns, the lookup formulas, severity bands that set how often each risk is reviewed, and a worked example with the arithmetic shown.

A risk register lists the things that could go wrong with your technology, how likely and how damaging each one is, what you are doing about it, and who is accountable. It is a decision tool: it tells leadership where to spend money and attention, and it shows an auditor, insurer or board that risks are known and owned.

Keep it separate from two neighbouring lists. A vulnerability or findings tracker holds individual technical issues with fix dates. A risk exception register holds approved, time-limited departures from a control. A risk register sits above both: "a critical system is compromised through an unpatched internet-facing service" is a risk; a specific missing patch is a finding; an approved decision not to patch one server until March is an exception. The three can reference each other by ID, but merging them makes each harder to read.

Calculated columns follow: inherent score and severity, residual score and severity, next review due, and an overdue flag.

Write risk statements in the form "because of [cause], [event] could happen, leading to [consequence]". "Phishing" is a topic. "Because staff can approve payment changes by email, an attacker impersonating a supplier could redirect a payment, leading to a direct financial loss" is a risk someone can score and treat.

Before anyone scores anything, write a one-line definition for each level, and put them on a settings tab next to the matrix. For example:

Where you can, attach numbers to impact levels that fit your company: a dollar range, hours of downtime, number of customer records. "Major" means something very different to a 30-person firm and a 3,000-person one. If you use a published method such as NIST SP 800-30, follow its structure, but the definitions still have to be written for your organization.

The middle levels are the hardest. If Possible and Likely cannot be told apart in writing, most risks will end up scored 3 by 3, and the register will look tidy while saying very little.

Put the matrix on the settings tab as a grid: likelihood 1 to 5 down the rows, impact 1 to 5 across the columns, and the score in each cell. The default is likelihood multiplied by impact, so the grid runs from 1 in the top left to 25 in the bottom right. Say the grid occupies Settings!C12:G16.

This was a breathtaking feat; a brilliant technical achievement that required the best engineering minds to think through a million different, difficult problems. And on Saturday evening, following a handful of practice runs into the Indian Ocean, they did it before the entire world.

Watching Starship launch from here a little less than a day earlier, and knowing it had orbited the planet multiple times, added to the sense of unreality in seeing this spaceship return to Earth under its own power, and be captured by the tower.

It felt like stepping into the future. SpaceX has dazzled us before, by landing rockets on barges and returning humans to space with Crew Dragon. But this flight took things to another level. Starships are huge. They carry a huge amount of energy coming back from orbit. And until a few years ago, it seemed beyond fantastical to think about capturing a rocket with what are essentially outsized chopsticks.

Humans have built reusable spacecraft that flew into orbit before. In the 1970s, flush with confidence after the triumphant Moon landings, NASA designed and built several Space Shuttles. They had solid rocket boosters that were fished from the ocean and reused. The large orange external tank was not recovered after each flight, but the orbiters went to space for two weeks, or even longer, before returning to Earth and landing on a runway.

The shuttles were engineering marvels. But during the return to Earth they acted as gliders, not igniting their engines. And their heat shields were delicate flowers, requiring painstaking refurbishment after each flight. At the outset of the shuttle program, the space agency contemplated sending dozens of humans into space at a time, and carrying cargo at a cost as low of $25 per pound.

Reality never matched these rosy projections. The crew space capped out at seven astronauts, and the cost-per-pound was closer to $25,000. Each orbiter cost an extraordinary amount of money to build; NASA only ever built five that went to space.

SpaceX has five Starships in an advanced state of development at Starbase today; and its factory houses the components for a dozen more. The company can currently manufacture a Starship upper stage in its South Texas Starfactory every three weeks. The factory was sized to produce a ship every three days. When SpaceX founder Elon Musk discussed these build targets in an interview five years ago, he sounded mad. But he no longer does.

The Space Shuttle was nearly a fully reusable space launch system, but it fell far short of low-cost, rapid launch. Over 30 years the vehicle made 135 flights. Within a few years SpaceX aims to fly 135 times a year, then thousands of times.

Such was the significance of Saturday. SpaceX aspires to fundamentally change the relationship between humanity and the heavens. The first step is to get the entire rocket back. SpaceX has now returned both elements of a Starship rocket safely to its launch tower. It will take time to reach a high rate of success with such landings, but Saturday’s safe return offered a proof of concept.

The landings will come with increasing frequency; and so will the launches. Over time SpaceX will perfect the heat shield that is likely to be the most difficult aspect of rapid reuse. And eventually, as difficult as it may be to believe, what we experienced with amazement this weekend will become routine. One day, we will take this all for granted.

Born April 24, 1943, in Camden, New Jersey, Sterban began singing at six in Sunday school as a soprano. He sang tenor in his seventh-grade glee club, and upon his eighth grade, his voice dropped to bass.

He studied music at Trenton State College (modernly known as the College of New Jersey), and began singing in a gospel quartet where he met Joe Bonsall, his future Oak Ridge Boys bandmate.

Originally recorded by Dallas Frazier, the Oak Ridge Boys’ version of the track went to No. 1 on the country chart and No. 5 on the pop chart, sold more than two million copies and won a Grammy. Sterban’s bass refrain was a major touchpoint on the song.

The Oak Ridge Boys went on to also become members of the Grand Ole Opry, the Gospel Music Hall of Fame and the Country Music Hall of Fame.

Sterban released his memoir From Elvis to Elvira: My Life on Stage, written with Steven Robinson, in 2012.
