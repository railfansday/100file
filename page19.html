Apple HomePod mini 2 review: A necessary, but underwhelming update

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I'd be willing to forgive you if you forgot about the new HomePod mini. The overhauled smart speaker played a supporting role at best during Apple's Welcome Home event last month, behind the HomeView, and probably, in some minds, behind the updated Apple TV 4K. Still, the company provides a few upgrades in the HomePod mini 2 ($149), ensuring that it's ready for Siri AI and the assistant's newfound ability to offer personalized context for your queries. The main problem? The speaker doesn't yet support the recently upgraded virtual assistant Apple spent years improving.

The most notable update on the HomePod mini 2 is on the inside. Apple swapped in its S10 chip from the Apple Watch Series 10, kicking the nearly antique S5 silicon to the curb. For a Watch comparison, that's like going from a Series 5 or first-gen Watch SE, which debuted in 2019 and 2020 respectively, to the 2024 edition. It's a big leap, and it's one Apple says was necessary for the HomePod mini to work with Siri AI.

The recently updated assistant isn't all the S10 is helping with here. Apple says the component can run real-time music evaluation "to optimize dynamic range, loudness and acoustic performance," with a hand from "advanced computational analysis." When connected to the newly introduced fourth-gen Apple TV 4K, you can use four HomePod mini 2 speakers as a surround sound setup. That S10 chip helps the speakers recognize their positions in a room and adjust the lag, convey that info to Apple's streaming box, and audio levels accordingly.

I only have two HomePod mini 2s, so I wasn't able to test this configuration, but I did hear it during Apple's event in October. Even with that brief experience, I can tell you using two second-gen HomePods as the front speakers and two HomePod minis as the rears is a much better setup. The HomePod mini doesn't muster enough bass to be a great home theater speaker on its own, and those full-size HomePods bring the boom when an action scene demands it.

Before now, you could send audio from a Mac to a HomePod mini, but Apple has expanded the functionality so that the second-gen model can serve as persistent default speakers. This means, in theory, they'll stay connected to your Mac as the primary output rather than just relying on a temporary AirPlay link. This allows you to use the HomePod mini 2, or a pair of them, for everything on your computer — from FaceTime calls to games — without having to think about connecting each app or session to the speaker(s).

The HomePod line has always had lights on the top, but they haven't really done much. Before now, the top touch panel would light up if audio was playing or display an animation when you chatted with Siri. With a new feature called Sound Glow, those lights now show hues that compliment a song's album artwork. The lights move in real time, reacting to the bass and treble in each tune. If you opt for a soundscape, Sound Glow reacts to that too, showing an animation that resembles the pitter patter of rain drops, for example. It isn't a game-changing feature, but it's a nice use of lighting that was barely utilized previously.

Lastly, there are the new colors. The HomePod mini 2 comes in pink, green, blue, white and midnight (dark grey), with those first three coordinating well with Apple's iMac colors. That list means the company retired orange and yellow from the previous generation, both of which looked great in their time. As you can see, my review units are green, and I've enjoyed the pop of color they've added to my desk over the last few weeks.

Given that Apple saw fit to bestow a "2" on this HomePod mini, I expected some upgrades to overall sound quality to be in the mix. Unfortunately, I can't notice a difference between old and new models. Sometimes I think there's some variation, but it's not substantial enough that I'm sure, and it's not consistent across various musical styles. I do like the audio performance of the HomePod mini for its size, but it still has a sound profile of crisp highs and prominent mids offset by bass tone that lacks dynamics and depth. It's good enough for casual listening or background tunes, but not for anything more than that.

Despite the lack of Siri AI, I was hoping the S10 chip would provide a performance boost for the old Siri experience you have to settle for right now. However, the assistant's responses to basic questions took the same amount of time as they do on the first-gen model. To be clear, they're quick on both devices, I'm merely pointing out there's no difference in this regard. Just don't try to ask the old assistant to dive into Apple's core apps for you, or try to have an on-going conversation — neither request will bear any fruit.

The biggest issue with the HomePod mini 2 right now is its biggest omission: Siri AI. The recently updated assistant isn't available on the speaker yet, and Apple hasn't provided a timeline for its arrival either. The upgraded HomePod mini is equipped to support the now-current-gen Siri, thanks to the S10 chip. However, you'll have to wait for Siri's newfound contextual awareness to dive into your Messages, Calendar and other apps on your iPhone or iPad. And to me, that's a huge miss for Apple on an overhauled smart speaker. You'll also have to wait for the more conversational chats with Siri as the HomePod mini 2 doesn't yet support those either.

I like the idea of using a HomePod mini 2 or a stereo pair of them as a persistent computer speaker with a Mac, but it's not a great experience in practice. Any time you do anything — start a song, pause a song, skip tracks — there's a 1-2 second delay in the audio responding. The lights on the top of the speakers, including the Sound Glow effect, will stop shining immediately, but the audio has to catch up. There's also a slight delay between speakers if you're using a stereo pair, which sounds like every song has added reverb. For these reasons, I found the Mac speaker feature more frustrating than enjoyable, and I quickly moved back to AirPlay over a nearby Sonos speaker for my productivity tunes. And I haven't even mentioned the fact that I can't get the persistent connection to stick with my MacBook Air either. I'm constantly reselecting the HomePod mini 2 as the audio output.

Another item that's disappointing is something else that's not here: a substantial overhaul. The original HomePod mini is six years old at this point, so I expected a much bigger redesign for the second-gen version (until the rumor mill squashed that). If you have a first-gen HomePod mini, you'll miss out on the updated Siri, whenever it arrives, and new features like Sound Glow and the home theater option. And if you're hoping to create a stereo pair, you'll need two of the same generation HomePod minis to do that; you can't pair a first-gen speaker with a second-gen model for that purpose.

Lastly, there's the price. The previous version was $99 before Apple raised it to $129 in June when the company hiked the prices on pretty much all of its hardware. Now, I'm well aware of the global economic factors at play, especially the AI-fueled cost increase on RAM and other components, but asking $50 more than the first-gen model for incremental updates is tough. Had Apple kept the price at the June rate, the HomePod mini 2 would be a much easier recommendation.

It's baffling to me that Apple would announce an updated HomePod mini without support for the latest version of Siri ready to go. The speaker's primary use is a hands-free conduit to the company's voice assistant, after all. The only decent conclusion I can come to is that Apple wanted to announce what it considered was a full suite of home products around the HomeView debut, and it felt like the Apple TV 4K, HomePod mini and a collection of smart home devices were the best way to do that. I would be interested to know what the hold up is, because the Apple Watch Series 10 supports Siri AI already, and those wearables are also running the same S10 chip as the HomePod mini 2. But, of course, Apple isn't talking specifics.

After spending a few weeks with the HomePod mini 2, I mostly wish Apple had just waited. At a higher price, this isn't as compelling as it used to be in terms of getting Siri in your home. And with Siri AI missing, and some of the new audio features proving to be unreliable, it's clear a bit more polish was needed here.

Has he raised their standard of living? Is the opposition right in alleging that the BJP is systematically hollowing out Indian democracy? Increasingly, those questions are being answered in a way that makes it seem like Modi has peaked.

At a Diwali party last month, a young Indian researcher in her mid-20s warned me against attending a protest rally. “The police will pick you up,” she said, her eyes wide open. In a recent protest, the Delhi police had locked down major parts of the capital and cracked down on protesters. The police cut off the internet, shut down local trains in key areas, and detained thousands, including journalists. Even those hanging around weren’t spared. “Our lawyer friends who went to the protest site to help the protesters were also beaten up,” she said. “Because our friends were detained and beaten, now we fear we could be next. You could be next.”

This is starkly different from India in 2011, when tens of thousands flocked to Jantar Mantar, a historical protest site near Delhi’s power center, for an anti-corruption demonstration. That protest paved the way for the Congress-led government’s defeat and Modi’s entry into national politics in 2014. Modi’s government, however, appears to have drawn a very different lesson about how to deal with protests.

BJP leaders suggested removing protesters because they inconvenienced passersby and businesses. That line was parroted relentlessly in TV studios. In my travels in and around Delhi, I found that message landed with certain people—mostly those who also believed the BJP’s insinuation, which came without any evidence, that protests in India were a foreign ploy to destabilize the country. Protests in India unfolded at the same time as in France and elsewhere in Europe, and this was portrayed as a sign of collaboration.

However, there appears to be a pattern in how the current Indian government discredits and responds to protests. A few months ago, when Indian students marched against an exam paper leak and jobless growth, the same police thrashed the students, even firing pellet guns. Some TV anchors claimed the police were the victims.

These are unusual scenes in India, a democratic country where the right to protest is guaranteed under the constitution. India won its independence through peaceful protests against the British.

On Nov. 1, despite the police’s reported highhandedness, thousands of people from different parts of the country gathered for the mega protest at an alternative and government-approved protest site. By noon, as I reached Connaught Place in central Delhi, Congress workers were marching on the streets and calling for the removal of Chief Election Commissioner Gyanesh Kumar. They accused Kumar of working to benefit the BJP.

But Kumar’s role and the discrepancies in electoral rolls weren’t the only reasons that pushed people to protest. Three Gen Z protesters stood in a corner, two of them wearing masks to avoid being identified by the police. “They picked up people after the protests last time, from their homes,” said one of the masked boys. All three had joined the protests for very different reasons but the same cause—saving Indian democracy.

The masked boy recounted the story of how his friend, a child, was terrified for his mother’s security when Hindu-Muslim riots unfolded in Delhi early 2020. He blamed the BJP for instigating the riots. The young man standing to his left hailed from West Bengal and was furious that his grandparents, both octogenarians, were knocked off the voters list and denied the right to cast their ballot. “[The government] wanted to exclude a certain community, Bengali Muslims that they falsely say are Bangladeshis, but everyone was affected,” he said. “They are dividing us on the basis of religion; this is not okay.”

Mihika Omseema refused to cover up her face. She said her parents supported her and that gave her the strength to reveal her identity and take part in protests. She said she joined to protest against growing Hindu-Muslim polarization, falling economic standards, exam paper leaks, and a whole host of other issues. In Delhi, some neighborhoods showed a mood where parents might join their children—if not at the protest grounds, then at the ballot. A senior gentleman and an upper-caste Hindu said, “Modi will not win Delhi, no chance,” but he confessed that Modi still had many supporters in the country. An 80-kilometer ride to Meerut has been shortened from more than 3 hours to less than 2 hours via a new flyover completed under Modi’s government, which has also built several other key highways linking cities in western Uttar Pradesh. Meerut, for the first time, has a fast train that reaches Delhi in less than an hour and thus expands employment potential. (Uttar Pradesh has the maximum number of electoral constituencies and plays a decisive role in forming the government in Delhi. Although central elections are still more than two years away, the state itself will go to the polls next year.)

In Meerut, I heard a different set of complaints against the BJP. Younger people acknowledged infrastructure development but said there was still no work. Only Muslims were worried about growing polarization; Hindus were more upset with slow economic growth and a demolition drive to bulldoze illegal shops and other structures. “Of course, this will affect the BJP in the elections; people are, we are, very angry with the government,” said Aditya Kumar, who sat on a stool in his emptied and recently destroyed shop in a market where rubble had still not been cleared. He sold nuts, bolts, and other construction material. “The structures were illegal, but there is no other employment, so what were we supposed to do?” he said, highlighting the lack of industry and compulsion to resort to small retail businesses.

“This is dictatorship,” said Kumar, a staunch Modi supporter until the demolition drive. “And no one raises our voice because the media doesn’t broadcast anything critical of Modi.”

About a dozen other upper-caste Hindus in the city who professed to be pro-Modi said the BJP was unlikely to win Meerut even if it retains the state. That could change if there were credible opposition, but they did not see a match. Congress scion Rahul Gandhi marched alongside protesters and promised to seek justice for those wrongfully detained or beaten up. In Delhi, Gen Z protesters spoke in his favor. But in a state deeply divided along caste lines, despite being upper caste, he is still not seen as an alternative.

Akash, a 26-year-old from Bulandshahr, said that the BJP will return to power because it had ensured quick payment for sugarcane to farmers like his father. “There are also more roads and police stations,” he said, emphasizing improvement in law and order under the BJP government led by Modi acolyte Yogi Adityanath. He said that Indians like him would rate Modi on economic development over the protests in Delhi or the state of Indian democracy. But he also didn’t want to give up his own right to protest or question the government.

“If the choice is between a better economy and democracy, I choose the economy, but if it is between the economy and my ability to question the government, then I choose democracy,” he said.

Like Meerut in Uttar Pradesh, in the Shekhawati region of Rajasthan, local factors were adding to a sense that all was not well under the Modi government. But here, too, the protests were not the reason that people said the government could change. “Farmers are not being paid on time,” Bhawani Singh, a tourist taxi driver, said. “Our children will never go and protest like those in Delhi. But we are upset about the Agnipath scheme and the shortening of military service,” he said, referring to government plans to cut down the length of service below the rank of officer to four years and retain only a quarter for a full term. “Most kids here want to join the army or the police,” he said. (The government appears to have noticed the grievance, announcing earlier this month that two more years of service could be offered and a second chance at joining longer-term,, for those who meet the selection criteria.)

Local factors remain paramount for Indians deciding how Modi has fared in his third term. The protests have had some effect on their overall assessment—distancing those affected by other government policies, while many remain ambivalent or supportive. The weakening of institutions isn’t yet a factor in how they vote, but it has been registered, as former supporters say the media is largely pliable, and now the Election Commission is suspected of being subservient.

The fence-sitters are the upper-caste Hindus who form the backbone of the BJP’s voter base. And while many are still firmly behind the party and backing Hindu dominance, at least some appeared uncomfortable with a slide away from democracy. “He wants to be Putin,” an upper-caste Hindu said  . “Now with the delimitation bill, the BJP is trying to ensure it always has enough seats to keep them in power,” he added, referring to legislation that will come up for voting in parliament next month and intends to expand the number of seats based on population. The same exercise in Jammu and Kashmir, Chief Minister Omar Abdullah said, helped the BJP.

The recent protests are occurring in the middle of Modi’s current stint in office. He still has time to assuage concerns. But the opposition will also have a chance to highlight its case about the weakening of democratic institutions and the threat to Indian democracy. And just as a political wave pushed Modi into office 12 years ago, another one could be building that will eventually sweep him out.

What Are Perpetual Futures? How Crypto's Most Popular Derivative Works

What Are Perpetual Futures? How Crypto's Most Popular Derivative Works

Perpetual futures are the engine room of crypto trading. Centralized exchanges processed $85.3 trillion in perps trading volume in 2025 alone, according to CoinGecko's State of Crypto Perpetuals Report 2026, and the U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) describes perps as the dominant form of crypto derivatives trading.

They aren't only for crypto, either. Exchanges now offer perps on stocks, commodities and currencies, and Decrypt has become the first news publisher to offer perps trading, powered by Solana exchange Phoenix.

This guide explains what futures are, what makes perpetual futures different, how funding rates and liquidations work, and what to know before trading them.

To understand perpetual futures, it helps to start with ordinary futures.

A futures contract is an agreement to buy or sell an asset at a set price on a set date in the future. Futures have been used for centuries: farmers used them to lock in prices for crops before harvest, and today they are traded on everything from oil and wheat to stock indexes and Bitcoin, on regulated exchanges such as CME.

Traders use futures for two main reasons. Hedgers use them to protect against price swings; an airline might buy oil futures to lock in fuel costs, for example. Speculators use them to bet on price movements without owning the underlying asset.

Traditional futures have an expiry date. When a contract expires, it settles, either by delivering the asset or, more often, by paying out the difference in cash. Traders who want to keep their position open must "roll" it into a new contract, which can add costs and complexity.

Perpetual futures, often shortened to "perps" or "perpetual swaps," work like futures, but with one key difference: they never expire. Traders can hold a position for as long as they like, provided they have enough collateral to keep it open.

The idea was proposed by Yale economist Robert Shiller in a 1993 paper as a way to create markets for assets that are hard to trade directly. Perps took off in crypto. Crypto exchange BitMEX, widely credited with bringing perps to crypto, launched a perpetual contract on Bitcoin in 2016. BitMEX itself closed in September 2026, but the product it pioneered has become the most traded instrument in crypto.

A perpetual contract never actually involves owning the asset. Traders deposit collateral, or "margin," and their profit or loss depends entirely on how the price moves.

When opening a perp position, a trader chooses a direction. Going long means betting the price will rise; going short means betting it will fall. Being able to short as easily as going long is one reason perps are popular: it lets traders profit from falling prices, or hedge assets they already own.

Perps are usually traded with leverage, which lets traders control a larger position than their collateral alone would allow. With 10x leverage, for example, $100 of margin controls a $1,000 position. If the asset's price rises 5%, the trader makes $50, a 50% return on their margin. But if it falls 5%, they lose $50, half of their margin.

Exchanges track two prices to manage this. The index price is the asset's price on spot markets, usually averaged across several exchanges. The mark price, based on the index and the perp market itself, is used to calculate profits and losses and to decide when positions must be closed. Using an averaged price makes it harder for a sudden spike on one exchange to trigger liquidations.

Because perps never expire, nothing forces their price to converge with the underlying asset, as traditional futures do at expiry. Instead, perps use a funding rate.

At regular intervals—most commonly every eight hours, though some platforms use shorter or longer schedules—traders on one side of the market pay traders on the other:

Decentralized exchange Hyperliquid, for example, pays funding every hour, at one-eighth of the rate calculated for an eight-hour period, to stay consistent with centralized exchanges.

Funding payments go directly between traders, not to the exchange. For traders holding positions over long periods, these payments can add up, and they can either boost or erode returns.

Funding rates also offer a window into market sentiment. Strongly positive funding suggests traders are crowded into bullish bets, while negative funding shows that bears are paying to stay short.

Rates for the same asset can also differ between exchanges. On November 19, 2026, Bitcoin's funding rate was 0.01% per eight hours on Hyperliquid, compared with 0.0037% on Binance, according to Hyperliquid's funding comparison tool.

Some traders aim to earn funding payments rather than bet on prices. In a "cash and carry" or basis trade, they buy an asset on the spot market and short the same amount through perps, collecting funding while their positions offset each other's price moves.

Leverage is a double-edged sword. Every perp position has a maintenance margin, the minimum collateral needed to keep it open. If losses push a trader's margin below that level, the exchange automatically closes the position, a process called liquidation. The trader typically loses most or all of the margin they put up.

The higher the leverage, the smaller the price move needed to trigger a liquidation. At 50x leverage, a 2% move against a position can wipe it out.

Liquidations can also feed on each other. When prices fall sharply, liquidated longs are forced to sell, pushing prices lower and triggering further liquidations, a so-called liquidation cascade. On October 10, 2025, the crypto market saw its largest liquidation event on record, with more than $19 billion in leveraged positions wiped out in a single day.

To cover losses when positions can't be closed in time, exchanges typically keep an insurance fund. In extreme cases, some use auto-deleveraging, closing profitable positions on the other side of the market to keep the system solvent.

Crypto perps trade on two main types of venue.

Centralized exchanges, such as Binance, Bybit and OKX, run their own order books and hold users' funds. They account for the vast majority of perps trading: Binance alone held about a third of centralized perps volume in early 2026, according to CoinGecko. Many are unavailable to U.S. customers.

Decentralized exchanges let traders use perps through smart contracts, while keeping control of their funds. Some, such as Hyperliquid and Solana-based Phoenix, run order books onchain, matching buyers and sellers much like a traditional exchange. Others, such as GMX and Jupiter, let traders trade against a shared pool of liquidity provided by other users.

Decentralized perps are growing fast. Their trading volume jumped from $1.5 trillion in 2024 to $6.38 trillion in 2025, led by Hyperliquid, CoinGecko found. Decentralized exchanges' share of perps trading volume peaked at 13% in late 2025 before slipping to 10% in April 2026, while their share of open interest, the total value of open positions, reached 13.5%.

In the U.S., perps developed largely on offshore exchanges, outside the reach of the CFTC, but that has started to change. In July 2025, Coinbase introduced CFTC-regulated "perpetual-style" futures for U.S. customers, with five-year expiries and up to 10x leverage. Two months later, CFTC-regulated exchange Bitnomial launched what it called the first U.S. perpetual futures, with 25-year terms and eight-hour funding. In May 2026, the CFTC approved a Bitcoin perpetual futures contract from prediction market Kalshi and set out a framework for listing more, and in June, Kraken rolled out perps to eligible U.S. customers through Bitnomial, which its parent company had acquired.

Some of these contracts, including Kalshi's and Bitnomial's original design, have long but fixed terms of 25 years, so are not strictly perpetual. The regulator says perps can be offered on CFTC-registered exchanges under federal oversight, and has advised U.S. traders to avoid offshore venues.

The shift has been contested. CME Group, the largest U.S. futures exchange, has sued the CFTC, arguing that perps are swaps rather than futures under U.S. law. The CFTC has nonetheless continued to approve more perps platforms, starting with Backpack.

Perps are now spreading beyond crypto. Exchanges have begun listing perps tracking stocks, stock indexes, commodities such as gold and oil, and currencies, giving traders around-the-clock exposure to markets that normally close in the evening and on weekends.

Those products carry the same mechanics, and the same risks, as crypto perps. They track an asset's price without giving traders ownership of the asset itself, so a perp on a company's stock doesn't come with voting rights or dividends.

Ben Zartman Splices Climbing Slings In His Garage. They’re the Best I’ve Ever Used.

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Ben Zartman at work splicing slings in his garage. (Photo: Courtesy Ben Zartman)

When Ben Zartman learned to climb near Mexico City in the early 1990s, no one he knew could afford climbing gear.

“All of us were poor, and all the gear was imported and so expensive,” he says. “Most of the guys at the crag were sewing their own shoes or making their own cams.” Backpacks, harnesses, quickdraws—if they could sew it themselves, they did. At the time, Zartman was in high school. He didn’t have a job, but he did have a knack for making stuff. It never occurred to him that doing so might be sketchy until he arrived in Yosemite for the first time in 1995 and partners refused to climb on his rack. They were astonished he’d made his own gear. He was astonished that they’d bought theirs.

Later that year, when a government shutdown forced Zartman out of Joshua Tree, he hitchhiked to San Diego and bummed a ride to Baja with some sailors. Over the next few years, he split his time between big walls and boats, getting better at both kinds of rigging all the while. Eventually he got married, had three daughters, and spent five years sailing around the world on a boat he’d built himself. Eventually, he got work in a rigging shop in Rhode Island. He spliced custom slings for workaday sailors, then for open-ocean racing boats.

“At one point, I was sitting in the shop, splicing these high-end Dyneema loops, and I thought, man, if I had these in Yosemite, I would have been the coolest kid at the lodge cafeteria,” he says. And he likely would have: the spliced, covered loops used in sailing are seamless: no bar tacks or stitching. Instead, one end of the loop is woven into the other like a snake eating its tail. When you pull, the ends suction against each other (Zartman compares it to a finger-trap toy), making the loop stronger under load than bartacked Dyneema.

“The idea fomented for a while in my mind, and in 2021 I started reaching out to every climbing gear manufacturer I could think of,” Zartman says. Letters and samples went out to Black Diamond, Metolius, Sterling, and others, asking them if they’d be interested in partnering on a product. Hardly anyone responded.

So Zartman did what he’d always done: he took the DIY approach. In 2023, he launched his company, Zartman Rigging. He joined the UIAA, mailed in slings for lab testing, and registered for UIAA labels. Once his gear was certified, the renowned gear shop IME, in North Conway, New Hampshire, started selling it. Rock and Snow in New Paltz, New York, and IME in Salt Lake City, Utah, were next. As his following grew, so did his offerings: a featherlight personal anchor system, seamless rappel back-ups, a rope-carabiner alternative called the Dreamlink, and a tiny rappel plate designed for 6-mm cord. The ideas come from Zartman’s experience climbing Yosemite walls; the materials come from his time on boats.

“The research and development budget in the [sailboat] racing scene is so big,” he says. “It’s a great place to take technology from. I’m at that edge—I make ropes for the fanciest racing boats in the world, so I’m able to bring that into the climbing world pretty quickly.”

The results are wild. The Dreamlink has a breaking strength of 43 kN (or, rather, he tested it up to 42 kN in a UIAA lab, and that’s when it broke their machine). The seamless runners are tagged 24 kN but break at 27kN. A fancy UV coating makes them essentially sun-proof. They don’t expire like other soft goods; you only have to retire them if the core becomes exposed. I have a double-length seamless runner that I use for building anchors and extending rappels. No bartack means easy setup, it racks well, and it’s pretty light. (Most traditionally manufactured Dyneema slings are tagged at 22kN. Mammut’s 120-centimeter Contact Sling weighs 28 grams, while Zartman’s version is 32 grams, the same as Black Diamond’s Infinity Cord.)

Zartman has a strong social media fan club, but he has plenty of critics too. Mountain Project commenters, he said, can be particularly vicious. Many scoff at the idea of a soft shackle or a seamless runner as gimmicky or unsafe. He’s not too bothered.

“Ray Jardine got scolded by all the Valley locals when he showed up with Friends,” he says, citing the first-ever cams. When other companies started making camming devices in the ‘80s, Jardine’s critics were forced to bite their tongues. Zartman is seeing a similar arc with his own gear. Several other climbing brands have started marketing soft shackles similar to Zartman’s Dreamlink. And in 2025, Black Diamond released its Infinity Cord, a Dyneema sling that uses the same bartack-free construction as Zartman’s and the same covered-loop splicing technique that sailors have used for decades. Black Diamond called it a “breakthrough in the evolution of cordelette engineering.”

Zartman says he’s unaffected by the new competition. “If there are people copying the concept, that’s good because that means the concept is good,” he says. In the meantime, he’s keeping his head down, focused on fulfilling orders and prototyping his next batch of inventions.

Zartman Rigging is gaining recognition, albeit slowly. Zartman does no paid advertising; about 80 percent of his business comes from Instagram. His top-shelf pricing likely tempers growth as well. For context, a 120-centimeter Zartman Rigging sling is $63, versus $40 for Black Diamond’s Infinity Cord or $14 for a basic Dyneema Trango runner of the same length.

“Because I make everything out of the nicest materials and by hand, it’s the most expensive gear on the market. It’s a luxury brand,” he says. “Which is ironic given that I started in Mexico with my own homemade climbing shoes.”

Still, he produces enough to keep busy. Recently, he was able to hire one of his daughters part-time to help him splice, tag, and quality-test each product. He’s now a member of the UIAA safety commission, helping the organization design new testing protocols around skinny ropes and other novel forms of cord. He’s shipping to Alaska and Hawaii. People have started recognizing him in gear shops and at crags. Things are taking off—despite what Mountain Projectors may say.

“To a lot of people’s surprise,” he laughs, “I’ve sold a lot of gear.”

You won't find this fan favorite feature in GTA 6

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Replaying missions is possible, but not easy.

Some of the more hardcore fans, running on little-to-no sleep, might be working their way through the missions in the Grand Theft Auto 6. Unlike in its predecessor though, GTA 6 has no option to replay completed story missions. That marks a major change from the previous game, which allowed players to run back finished mission from the pause menu, mostly to retry its skill challenges. (Those haven't reappeared, either.)

Even after more than 100 hours playing the game for his review, GamesRadar editor-in-chief Josh West said that he found no replay option. Other early-access reviewers are reporting the same. So, the only way back into a mission, at least for now, is to load a manual save made before it started, and before autosave overwrites itself.

Mission replay was first added to The Ballad of Gay Tony, the second Grand Theft Auto 4 expansion, back in 2009. It also introduced a scoring system that graded players on secondary mission goals like completion time, damage taken and headshots. Replay was introduced essentially so players could go back and get a better score. Then, with GTA 5, those goals became bronze, silver and gold medals, and achievements tied to attaining them. Rockstar's Red Dead Redemption 2's also had a "Gold Rush" trophy, which also asked for 70 gold medals out of the 104 available via its story missions.

GTA 6 has no gold medal objectives at all, at least as things stand now. Instead, challenges can be found in their own section of the progress menu. They cover broader goals rather than being mission-specific, and mostly unlock cheats once the story is completed. Rockstar North's co-studio head Rob Nelson told IGN that Rockstar had made "a very conscious effort to open things up where possible" in GTA 6's missions. Players will now find that some missions suggest an optimal route that players can ignore, for example, with the job turning into a police chase rather than ending in failure.

Red Dead Redemption 2 players long complained that straying from set paths could fail missions. That, along with GTA 5's mission medals enforcing a linear playstyle, seems to be the reason why Rockstar left them out.

GTA 6 gives us 15 save slots. That might seem generous on the face of it, but it's not all that many for a campaign that has more than 90 main missions. Sharper minds than I have worked out that keeping a route back to everything would mean a fresh manual save roughly every 6.6 percent of the way through the game.

The missing mission repeat feature probably comes down to how sprawling GTA 6's story is. Entire missions can play out differently based on decisions players made earlier, sometimes without knowing they were making them. A heist, for example, can be buttery smooth or turn into an awful mess depending on gameplay completed hours earlier. Story choices also shape the wider world, down to which characters live and die. Reloading a mission and playing it in isolation would ignore the choices that set it up originally.

It's not all bad news: Races, stunts, pool and other minigames are repeatable, as are shop robberies and the bank jobs as the story unlocks. Robbed locations gray out on the map and reset after a cooldown.

And obviously GTA 6's story was designed to support repeat playthroughs, but that's a big commitment. Nelson himself — the game's lead dev — has said his playthrough took 80 hours, covering the main story and some optional goals. In GTA 5, you could jump back into a favorite mission in a few minutes, but now, playing one differently means planning saves in advance or committing to another full run. Whether that changes remains to be seen.

The annoying GPU support bracket from the original was so integrated into the motherboard that even to add a new SSD in, you had to disassemble the entire graphics card and faceplate first. This has been improved so it's more practical this time around.

Furthermore, it's going to be very difficult not to disrupt Maingear's very neat and tidy PC assembly job when you upgrade, so it feels like there would be a risk of messing things up if you go to upgrade your machine later. What Every Home Needs for an Extended Power Outage

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Americans are “pantry-loading” following a cascade of technology outages that have disrupted email, grounded flights, and interrupted banking services—while feuling fears that the failures could be signs of something bigger.

Costco and Walmart have reported increased purchases of emergency supplies, from food and water to batteries and generators. As of Nov. 15, 21 of the 26 high-priced generators offered on Costco's website were sold out, according to Bloomberg.

"Consumer anxiety following the Microsoft Outlook outage, the ground stoppage for all U.S. airline flights and apocalyptic concerns flaring up across social media have all contributed to increased emergency prep sales," David Bellinger, an analyst for Mizuho, a Japanese investment bank, wrote in a recent research note.

Federal officials say they've found no evidence that the outages are connected or resulted from coordinated cyberattacks. But while the fears driving the rush to stockpile may be unfounded, the threat of prolonged disruptions is all-too real.

In August, severe storms knocked out power to nearly 375,000 customers across Northwest Indiana, leaving some without electricity for nearly two weeks.

A historic ice storm in January caused more than 330,000 outages across Mississippi and Louisiana, where some residents faced similarly long waits.

Both emergencies stretched far beyond the familiar three-day supply of food and water—and exposed how quickly households can lose access to basic necessities.

“A three-day emergency kit is still a good starting point, but I’d consider it the minimum rather than the actual goal,” says Brian Armentrout of Chariot Energy. “In Texas, we’ve seen severe weather events where power outages and other disruptions can last well beyond 72 hours.”

But preparing for the next emergency means more than filling a pantry. It means knowing how long your home can function without the systems that keep it safe—and having a plan for when it can't.

The familiar three-day kit is still a vital part of emergency preparedness, but the American Red Cross recommends that amount for an evacuation kit.

For households sheltering at home, the recommendation is much longer: two weeks of food and water.

Daniel Kilburn, an emergency-planning consultant and retired U.S. Army senior infantry drill sergeant, makes a similar distinction.

“The three-day emergency kit is still good if you're actually evacuating. It should not take more than three days to get to where you need to go,” he says. “However, based on current power outages happening around the country, eight to 10 days is not unreasonable."

But most households aren't prepared for that: 69% of respondents said they had assembled emergency supplies, according to the Federal Emergency Management Agency's latest National Household Survey. Among that group, just 34% said their supplies could last more than two weeks.

Kilburn recommends having enough food and water for every member of the household to last a full two weeks. But that doesn't mean buying two weeks of emergency supplies all at once.

Instead, he recommends gradually adding extra shelf-stable foods and water during normal shopping trips, then rotating those foods through the kitchen so the emergency supply functions more like a well-stocked pantry.

“Water is one of the biggest things people tend to overlook,” Armentrout says. “During an extended outage, water service can also be disrupted, so it’s important to have enough bottled water on hand for drinking and basic needs.”

Winter Storm Uri showed how those failures can overlap. The 2021 storm caused power losses for 69% of Texans and water service disruptions for 49%, according to the Texas State Auditor's Office.

The Red Cross recommends one gallon of water per person per day. For a family of four, that works out to 12 gallons for three days—or 56 gallons for two weeks.

And while food may be more intuitive to store than water, it presents a different problem: how to cook it without power.

A closed refrigerator generally keeps food cold for only about four hours without power, according to FoodSafety.gov. A full freezer can maintain its temperature for about 48 hours; a half-full freezer, about 24 hours.

That makes shelf-stable foods a good choice. But Kilburn warns that households should also have a safe way to boil water or prepare food if the power stays out.

A cellphone may become one of the most useful tools in a blackout, and useless within hours without a way to recharge it.

“Backup power is another area people often underestimate,” Armentrout says. “A portable battery bank, generator, or small solar charger can help keep phones and other essential devices operating when the power is out for several days.”

The Red Cross also recommends a battery-powered or hand-crank radio, backup batteries and chargers, and a paper copy of important phone numbers.

But phones are only one electrical dependency inside a modern home. Kilburn recommends mentally walking through an ordinary day and asking what stops working without electricity.

“Let's say you decide it's time to evacuate and your car is in the garage, how are you going to get the garage door open?” he says.

Medical equipment is also a vital consideration. More than 3 million Medicare beneficiaries rely on electricity-dependent durable medical and assistive equipment, according to the Department of Health and Human Services.

For these households, the Red Cross recommends establishing a backup plan in advance.

Generators can provide backup power, but they carry their own risks: carbon-monoxide poisoning from misuse. Nearly 100 Americans die each year in poisonings caused by portable generators, according to the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission.

To stay safe, never operate a generator in an enclosed space like your home or garage—even with doors or windows open. Keep them outdoors and more than 20 feet from windows, doors, and vents, and make sure you also have a battery-powered or battery-backup carbon-monoxide detector.

Eventually, the biggest question during a prolonged outage may not be how many supplies remain, but whether the home itself is still safe.

“Before an outage, close blinds or blackout curtains to reduce the amount of heat coming into the house,” Armentrout says. “If the power goes out, stay in the coolest part of the home, which is typically a room on the lower floor away from direct sunlight.”

Battery-powered fans can help, but only to a point. The Centers for Disease Control says fans should be used indoors only when temperatures are below 90 degrees Fahrenheit. Above that, using a fan can actually increase body temperature.

That means one of the most important parts of a blackout plan can't be stored in a closet: somewhere else to go.

Kilburn recommends finding out in advance where cooling centers would operate during a major outage. And Armentrout says households should establish their threshold for leaving before conditions deteriorate.

“It becomes unsafe when your home can no longer meet your basic needs,” he says. “That could be caused by a variety of things, from temperatures becoming too hot or too cold, to running low on water, necessary medications, or having the ability to run critical medical equipment.”

So preparing for two weeks without electricity doesn't mean planning to stay inside a powerless home for two weeks no matter what.

It means having enough food, water, and backup power to preserve your options—and a plan for where to go when staying home is no longer one of them.

Stedelijk Museum appoints Krist Gruijthuijsen as new director

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Krist Gruijthuijsen is currently the director of the Espoo Museum of Modern Art (EMMA) in Finland

Photo: Joel Karppanen. Courtesy Stedelijk Museum, Amsterdam

The Stedelijk Museum in Amsterdam has announced that the Dutchman Krist Gruijthuijsen will take over as its new director from July 2027.

Moving from Finland, where he has since 2025 been director of the Espoo Museum of Modern Art (EMMA), he said he wants to bring an outsider’s perspective and a focus on how the modern city connects with the world.

“The Stedelijk museum’s strength is in bringing large and Modernist exhibitions to the Netherlands,” he tells The Art Newspaper. “People go to the Rijksmuseum for Rembrandt and to the city [Stedelijk] museum to understand how Amsterdam has developed, to understand Modernism through the lens of art and design.”

The Stedelijk Museum enjoyed success last year with an Anselm Kiefer show—organised with the neighbouring Van Gogh Museum—and an exhibition of work by the Dutch photographer Erwin Olaf, increasing annual ticket revenue compared to 2024 by almost a quarter, to €10.8 million. Its current Yayoi Kusama show has been extended to the end of January due to its popularity.

This success stands out against a backdrop of Dutch museums reporting financial pressure and a fall in visits compared to pre-pandemic levels. The Rijksmuseum’s most recent annual report describes internal discussions that took place about the fact visitor numbers fell 4% in 2025 to 2.3 million, while the Rembrandt House Museum recently warned that rising tourist taxes will damage cultural tourism.

But Gruijthuijsen said he is more interested in variety than visitor records. “I’m not a visitor numbers director,” he says. “It is quality rather than quantity: what we need economically, and the rest is a plus. It is very difficult these days because people only choose what they know. My goal is first and foremost to support artists and art.”

Gruijthuijsen takes over from Rein Wolfs, who has been the director since 2019.

From one of the first exhibitions to establish Van Gogh’s work in 1905 to experimental shows of the 1960s, the Stedelijk Museum created an international reputation for contemporary art, which Gruijthuijsen wants to make relevant today. “The DNA of the city museum is a modern day and global perspective—trying to find connections,” he says.

From the Finns, he will also bring an important lesson in terms of improving diversity and tackling under-represented subjects, he says: “Less talking, more doing.”

‘Aurora’ Short Film Team Angad Aulakh And Guy Kent On Tapping Into The Emotional Weight Of Self & Outer Space

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Angad Aulakh’s lifelong fascination with space inspired Aurora, a sci-fi short about an astronaut left stranded after a mission goes catastrophically wrong. Freddie Ballard (Guy Kent) is sent to investigate a mysterious signal, but an accident makes him the sole survivor, with no way home and growing doubts about what his superiors have told him.

Shot in black and white, the film draws on German Expressionism and retro-futurism to create its unique visual world. Aulakh and Kent talk to Deadline about shaping Freddie’s journey, building the film’s cosmic scale on a short-film budget and their plans for what lies beyond the vast reaches of the galaxy they created. DEADLINE: How did you come up with this concept? Watch on Deadline

ANGAD AULAKH: It really started with a lifelong obsession with space and the idea that we are not alone in the universe. It’s one of the most compelling ideas for storytellers and people in general. When we look at how short our lives are, how small Earth is and how vast the universe is, the possibilities for what’s out there are so exciting. It’s the idea that we might not even be asking the right questions yet. We don’t know which questions to ask because there are concepts so grand and profound. Exploring them through storytelling felt like a wonderful opportunity.

With short films, you have more leeway to be a little avant-garde. I often liken short films to haikus, while features are full-fledged novels. So I wanted to focus on the emotion, the moment, the visuals and the wonder of space. Technology has evolved so much that it’s why we can make a film like this on our budget today.

I’m happy to say we didn’t use any AI on the film. I’m not throwing shade at AI, by any means, but I know it’s a big part of the conversation right now. It started with looking up at the stars and wondering what’s out there and what stories I could explore in the vastness of space.

DEADLINE: Guy, I read that the two of you have partnered on several projects. Is this one the most ethereal? When you heard about the idea, what went through your mind?

GUY KENT: I was thrilled because I also have a lifelong fascination with space and our place in [the cosmos]. As Angad said, we have so many questions and no answers. Finding some faith that there’s meaning despite not having the answers is profound to me. When this idea came up, it was an exciting adventure to embark on.

Angad and I have been deliberate about budget and scope, especially with short films. We prioritize quality and want to understand what we can and can’t achieve. With this film, the next question was, “How are we going to pull this off? How do we make it feel like the work we want to do, while making sure it works, resonates and lets the emotional moments land?”

Our pre-production work and the team we’ve worked with in the past gave us a built-in shorthand. That was especially true with our cinematographer, but finding a stage where we could create a set and build on it was also a huge part of making this feasible.

DEADLINE: The short film rests on you, Guy. You have a lot of scenes on your own. Your character is not only lost in space, but lost to himself, or somewhere in between. How did the two of you work on that story? What would you like audiences to think about through that juxtaposition?

KENT: Angad and I had a lot of discussions that began with isolation. Isolation isn’t something you show; it’s something you sit in. It’s revealing to see what happens when all distractions are removed and there’s no barrier between you and your own thoughts.

For Freddie, there’s a chance to find himself again. He’s an astronaut trained for crisis, someone who understands how to solve most of the problems he faces. Here, he’s confronted with something very personal and existential that he has no answers for.

It would be interesting if audiences were left wondering not only how they would react to meeting something beyond Earth, but also considering the hope that there’s something greater. As Angad said, even if we don’t have answers to these questions, there can be meaning in an unknowable answer. That makes you feel found in a way.

DEADLINE: Angad, what was the hardest thing about putting this story together?

AULAKH: The biggest challenge was looking at short films as a whole and thinking about how much time we had and how long the film would be. There are practical considerations, too. A lot of festivals won’t accept a short film longer than 15 minutes.

AULAKH: There are practical considerations around the film’s life after it’s released, but audiences also expect a short film to be short. Ideally, you’re not making a 30- or 40-minute short, because then you might as well make a feature. We had to think about the real estate available to us and the most effective way to make audiences leave having felt something and gone on an emotional experience. That’s what I’m interested in as a filmmaker: moving people. A big part of that is considering how the music, sound design, performance and production design work together to convey something cinematic and effective.

You also want to tell a compelling story. Weaving Freddie’s journey into a cohesive narrative in 15 minutes was always going to be a challenge, but I’m pleased with how it turned out. We focused on the emotion and on the fact that a mission has gone wrong. This astronaut is either going to be killed aboard the spacecraft or find his way home. I hoped the quieter moments would sustain the audience because they’d want to stick around to see whether Freddie makes it.

DEADLINE: Let’s talk about the scope of the film. The cinematography is great. Why black and white as your color scheme?

AULAKH: German Expressionism was on my mind as I wrote the screenplay and thought about the aesthetic I wanted. The other reason is that we don’t see many black-and-white films set in space or black-and-white science-fiction films. More than anything, I think it looks gorgeous on screen. The light on the set, shining through the top grates, was beautiful.

We had scouted locations beforehand, so we knew what the set would look like. But the first image that came to mind was Freddie Ballard lying on the floor, screaming and tumbling. I saw it in black and white. I hadn’t shot a black-and-white film since film school, and I thought this was the perfect opportunity. The stars look beautiful in black and white. There was also a practical reason. Black and white is more forgiving when you don’t have a huge budget for production design. You can hide things in the shadows, and it worked well with our special effects and the 3D modeling. We also made sequences in Unreal Engine with Stuart Lippincott, who’s an absolute genius. His work was a huge inspiration to me. I thought the lighting, images, and compositions would be tremendous in black and white.

KENT: On an emotional level, I think black and white subconsciously creates a sense of melancholy. That’s the basis of German Expressionism, at least in my experience. There’s an emotional texture it brings.

AULAKH: Yeah, it’s very melancholic. Black and white also has inherent cinematic associations. Audiences think of films from the 1930s and ’40s, most of which were made in black and white. I love the idea that Aurora can feel like a product of the past, something you might have seen in those bygone eras. That was exciting because retro-futurism, which is used extensively in science-fiction films and video games, is powerful and interesting. I gravitate toward it, so I loved being able to use that aesthetic in Aurora.

DEADLINE: What were the biggest technical challenges, and where did you have to get especially inventive? You’ve talked about using black and white, but is there another example? How did you get inventive with the production design?

AULAKH: A lot of the credit goes to our visual effects artist, Ian Mills Miller. The entity you see in the film was originally going to be an actor, Ethan [Daniel Corbett], wearing a latex bodysuit. We were going to use his movements and digitally map them onto a being, the way they did with Gollum in The Lord of the Rings. Instead, we used Ethan as a reference, and Ian created the magnificent 3D being you see in the film.

That’s a hard shot to pull off because it’s expensive and time-consuming, and you have to be careful about how much you show. Black and white allowed us to hide the entity in the shadows. He’s a being of light at the end of the film, but throughout it he looks dark and mechanical. He’s influenced more by machinery than by alien life.

It was tricky. Having the ability to hide him in the shadows and take a less-is-more approach was effective. If you hold an effects shot too long, the audience may start to look at it differently. Even in major motion pictures, it can be disconcerting and create an uncanny valley effect. We wanted to avoid that with our digital effects.

AULAKH: I’ll take the first part, and Guy can talk about where we’ve been and what’s coming up. We premiered at HollyShorts this August, which was a lovely experience. The festival was so well put together, and the audience response was wonderful. There were events throughout the week, so you had a chance to meet other filmmakers and talk with people watching your film. As a filmmaker, you get real-time feedback from the room. Unfortunately, we can’t attend many festivals because they’re all over the world. The few opportunities we get to sit with an audience and see the film on the big screen are phenomenal. It’s been wonderful so far. Our last few shorts had great festival runs, and we hope Aurora will top both of them. We’re just getting started. Guy can tell you more about where we’re headed next.

Drawing on the work of artists and theorists alike, the art historian traces how generations of Black painters have deployed color and how color, in turn, shapes our understanding of Blackness.

Sometime in the 1980s, when I was still a fledgling curator of 20th-century art at the Metropolitan Museum of Art, I was invited to the studio of a rather prominent artist. During our conversation, the work of Jacob Lawrence came up somehow, and the artist mentioned that he was impressed by what he described as Lawrence’s use of “Black” colors. Did I mention this artist was White?

I was gobsmacked by the comment. Not that I wasn’t attuned to stereotypical societal associations of color — most notably, of bright, loud colors with Black people. In the 1950s, a full three decades before Nancy Reagan practically made red her official color, my Aunt Ruth was appalled that my mother made red coats for my sister and me, rather than using more acceptable pastel colors. Then a friend who grew up in Northern Ireland told me that Protestant Irish factions associated Catholic Irish with loud and bright colors.

But how did such racially associative ideas translate to the realm of the so-called “fine” arts? I was aware of the place of color theory in the practice of art and knew the names of important theorists, from Johann Wolfgang von Goethe to Johannes Itten to Josef Albers. But otherwise, I presumed artists’ palettes were the result of personal predilection, technical explorations (i.e., the opening up of brushstrokes and form in the 19th century and the introduction of a wall of color in the 20th century), or cultural orientation. So it was therefore with great interest that I approached reading Richard Powell’s latest publication, Colorstruck!: Painting, Pigment, Affect (2026).

Chicago-based Muszynski, who oversees decision-making on what Publicis estimates to be 40% of the U.S. video marketplace inventory, is arguably the last of the old-school heads of investment that have seen the upfronts morph into the byzantine bazaar it is today.

A vanished bird from Haiti may help solve an ancient Pacific mystery

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

DNA reveals how snipes and woodcocks reached distant islands, stopped migrating, changed physically, and became vulnerable to extinction.

The sandpiper family includes some of the most extreme long-distance migrants among birds.

Two branches of that family, the snipes and the woodcocks, have behaved quite differently. These birds reached islands again and again, and migration was then abandoned.

Nearly every living species was examined in a new DNA study.

The results suggest that island settlement was accompanied by changes in body size and flying ability. Island species later proved vulnerable when people arrived with rats and cats.

The work was led by Min Zhao, then a postdoctoral researcher at the Florida Museum of Natural History. Colleagues at Virginia Tech and the Field Museum in Chicago also took part.

According to Zhao, the whole project grew out of one extinct bird from Hispaniola. That Caribbean island is shared by Haiti and the Dominican Republic.

The dates reported in the study were estimated from family trees built with DNA. The uncertainty attached to them can run to millions of years.

Florida Museum researchers dug the bird’s bones out of a cave in Haiti in 1984.

In 2015, those bones were described by two scientists as a new species of woodcock. Woodcocks are long-billed birds that probe soft soil for food.

One of the two scientists was David Steadman, who is now an emeritus curator at the museum. He is also listed as a co-author of the new study.

Zhao’s team extracted DNA from two of the fossil specimens. The researchers then read the mitochondrial genome, a small set of genes passed from mother to offspring.

Both specimens were placed with the snipes, which form the sister group of the woodcocks. Their closest living relative turned out to be the giant snipe of lowland South America.

The analysis suggested a South American origin for the Haitian bird’s ancestors. The two lineages are thought to have split between 5 and 12 million years ago.

Why, then, did the bones resemble those of a woodcock? The authors think the bird’s build may have been altered by island living, which could explain the mix-up.

The outer wing bone of the Haitian bird was unusually short when compared with its upper wing bone. The authors wrote that it could fly but probably not well.

The team sequenced 45 samples in total. Two were the Haitian fossils and 19 were toe pads taken from old museum specimens. The remaining 24 were fresh tissue samples.

All but one of the 29 living species of snipes and woodcocks were covered by these samples. Specimens were supplied by ten museums and research collections.

A family tree was built from the data, and both groups were traced back to Eurasia.

The ancestors of today’s American birds crossed from the Eastern Hemisphere to the Western Hemisphere four separate times.

One of those crossings took place roughly 20 million years ago. It was then that the ancestor of the American woodcock reached North America.

The researchers could not tell whether the bird arrived across the Atlantic or by way of Alaska.

The Austral snipes are found on the outlying islands of New Zealand. Scientists have long placed these small brown birds in a group of their own.

Some snipes from Eurasia spend each winter in Australia and on nearby islands. The DNA showed that those visitors are not close relatives of the Austral snipes.

Instead, the Austral snipes share a branch with several large South American snipes and the extinct Haitian bird.

Two DNA datasets placed the split between the Austral snipes and their relatives at about 14 million and nearly 18 million years ago, respectively.

The southern continents had broken apart long before either estimate. The snipes’ ancestor most likely crossed the Pacific.

How the crossing happened remains an open question. The authors suggested that birds straying far outside their normal range may have played a role. Such straying was perhaps caused by extreme weather.

The authors also raised a route through Antarctica. Patches of treeless tundra are thought to have lasted there until at least 12.8 million years ago.

Several living snipe species still breed on tundra in the Arctic.

The analysis indicated that the ancestors of both snipes and woodcocks were migrants. Their island descendants are not.

Ten of the 29 living species are found only on islands, and none of those ten migrates.

The habit was lost several times. Woodcocks gave it up when they settled the Malay Archipelago in Southeast Asia. Snipes did the same when they settled South America and Madagascar.

Body size changed as well, although not in a single direction.

The Amami woodcock of southern Japan is about 45% heavier than the Eurasian woodcock, its closest mainland relative. Madagascar’s snipe is about 91% heavier than the common snipe of Eurasia.

The opposite change is seen in the Austral snipes. Their average weight is 3.4 ounces (96 grams), which is about the weight of a deck of cards.

South American snipes on the same branch average 8.6 ounces (243.5 grams). That is more than twice as much.

The smaller size may be explained by scarce food, the authors suggested. Competition from kiwis and other birds that probe the soil for worms was also considered possible.

Ten species of snipes and woodcocks are known to have died out in the recent geologic past. Nine of them lived on islands.

The Austral snipes suffered the heaviest losses. Three of nine known species survive. All three are ranked as Vulnerable or Near Threatened by the IUCN Red List.

The South Island snipe died out in 1964, and introduced black rats are regarded as the likely cause.

No resident snipe or woodcock is left in the Caribbean. At least three species have been lost there.

The youngest dated bones of the Haitian bird are about 600 years old. Rats and feral cats brought by people are suspected of causing its extinction.

Snipes nest on the ground, where they can be reached by those predators. Island species had given up migration, and in some cases strong flight as well.

The authors wrote that these changes made such species especially vulnerable to human impacts.

Overhunting was named as one of those impacts.

Jessica Oswald, a co-author, also worked on the study at the Florida Museum.

“People will eat anything,” said Oswald. She said a crew of hungry explorers or whalers could wipe out an island’s birds in a single weekend.

Island woodcocks in Japan and Southeast Asia appear to have fared better. They live in mountain forests, which may be harder for people to reach.

New Zealand may have been reached first by the Austral snipes.

From there they could have spread to Norfolk Island, New Caledonia and Fiji. The opposite path cannot be ruled out, because no genetic data exist for those islands’ extinct snipes.

More snipe species may once have lived in the Pacific and are now extinct. In that case, today’s birds would be the last survivors of a larger group. Direct fossil evidence of that link across the Pacific is still missing.

The Haitian bird’s place on the tree rests on mitochondrial genes from two specimens. A possible close relative from Puerto Rico is also extinct and has not been tested genetically.

The authors suggest that DNA from fossils yet to be found on Pacific islands could help trace how snipes spread across the region.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.
