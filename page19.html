Microsoft's SideWinder Strategic Commander was a failed attempt to reimagine RTS controls. 23 years later, why are some players still using it?

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

How an unconventional peripheral became an equally unexpected survivor

PC players have been conditioned for decades to accept the keyboard-and-mouse setup for RTS games. One hand has to handle movement and commands through a keyboard, while the other points, drags, and selects with a mouse. Peripheral makers have, usually with the addition of more buttons, made many minor variations to this time-honored arrangement, but there's only ever been one real attempt to rethink it entirely for the PC: the Microsoft SideWinder Strategic Commander.

Unveiled on March 1st 2000 at Microsoft's Gamestock event, the Strategic Commander was one of three additions to their SideWinder line, with a scheduled release that October at a pricey $64.95. At E3 later that May, Microsoft demonstrated the Strategic Commander for the first time with SimCity 3000, while promoting its potential for other such games as Diablo II, Warcraft III, and Baldur's Gate II. This was all taking place during the so-called 'Golden Age of Real-Time Strategy Games,' which spanned roughly from the mid-1990s to the mid-2000s. It was when the RTS genre was at the peak of its mainstream popularity, and we fans were devoted to mastering its intricacies in any way we could to have a competitive edge.

Microsoft, all the while, were taking notes. "The team conceived the Strategic Commander in the late 1990s," Carl Ledbetter - then Industrial Design Manager for Microsoft's PC Hardware Group, now Head of Design for Xbox Devices - recalls to me. "As RTS games grew in popularity, we saw an interesting opportunity to design a new physical interface around a new interaction model. The idea was for it to be used left-handed in concert with right-hand PC desktop mouse control. We wanted to enable players to perform faster with more fluidity, comfort, optimised control placement for multiple hand sizes, simplicity of set-up, and ease of programming."

For Ledbetter and co. to realise this ambition, the Strategic Commander was designed as a unique combination of mouse, joystick, and gamepad. To manage the increasing complexities of the RTS games of the day, it came equipped with six programmable buttons, three shift buttons, and a three-position profile switch. Back then, these atypical features provided an unprecedented 72 possible key combinations across various configurations, all at the tip of our stubby little fingers. Better yet, it allowed us to toggle between three pre-configured profiles at a time, so that we could switch up our configurations on the fly depending on which stage of an RTS match we were in.

On release, contemporary reviewers acknowledged its originality, with critics like ActiveWin's Julien Jay describing it as "probably the most innovative (with the GameVoice) and revolutionary" peripheral designed specifically for strategy players. Others, like Neoseeker's Arnel Lim, suggested that it might’ve gone overboard with its customisation options, saying "you won't use anywhere close to all of them," in reference to the controller's programmable key combinations. Either way, the Strategic Commander failed to supplant conventional peripherals in the hands of most RTS players, and it was discontinued in 2003. As was the rest of the original SideWinder line, owing to declining sales and the industry-wide shift toward standard M+K setups for all PC games and genres.

However, unlike so many of its Microsoft-made siblings, this was not the end for the Strategic Commander. Niche as it was, it went on to enjoy a surprisingly long post-shelf life, one that was stretched out by a dedicated cult following - and that continues to this day. But why? Why, after all these years, is this forgotten peripheral from a discontinued line maintaining such a enthusiastic fanbase, years after the industry around it had written it off as a failure?

The answer lies in software and OS compatibility, as much as the hardware's own appeal. Andreas Weis was the developer of one of the first unofficial patches for the Strategic Commander, allowing players to keep on using it even after the original software stopped being compatible with newer versions the Windows. By that point, the device was a decade old, and well into 'official' obsolescence, yet Weis still answered the call of the Strategic Commander's righteously frustrated - if numerically few - fans. "There was a lot of interest back in the early 2010s in software patches to keep the Strategic Commander functional when the original software for the Strategic Commander stopped working," he explains. "Users were asking around on internet forums about how to get it to work on Windows 7 and Vista machines. Since I was studying computer science at the time, I was quite interested in reverse-engineering, and I hacked together a simple tool that would cover my use cases for the device."

In the years that followed, other patches were released to keep the Strategic Commander operational, the most notable of which was Philip Merwarth’s Strategic Engine software in 2013. Unfortunately, they weren’t open-source. Worse was that the protocol for programming the Strategic Commander wasn’t documented anywhere online in a way that was accessible for users. This is why Weis realized that he could make a unique contribution.

"I polished my old prototype code for the Strategic Commander software patch and released it as a library, so that other people could use it to write their own applications for programming the tool," he says. "As for me, I released one application that made use of the library publicly, the vJoy feeder application, which allows the device to be used with games that support joystick inputs." Curiously, Weis, by this time and like many others, had found other applications for a device with such a specific selling point, and even continues to use the Strategic Commander sparingly in 2026.

"The production quality of Microsoft hardware at the time was just top-notch, and I love the design of the old SideWinder devices, which is why I did not just throw the thing out when the software stopped working," he tells me. "It was worth keeping around to find new uses for it over the years, and I eventually realized that it makes quite a nice addition to a HOTAS (Hands-On Throttle And Stick) setup, where I could put actions that aren’t time-critical and don't fit well on the throttle on the Strategic Commander instead. For example, when I’m playing Elite Dangerous in VR, I’d put actions like deploying the landing gear on the Strategic Commander to free up buttons on the throttle for actions that were more relevant for combat."

When looking back at the history of Microsoft’s SideWinder line, it’s easy to call the Strategic Commander a failed experiment, given the fact that most of us are still using the same standard mouse-and-keyboard setup to play RTS games. However, the central premise behind its design philosophy was that the interface between a player and a game could be redesigned to cater to specific genre quirks, and decades following its release, it’s still maintained a coalition of loyal users: some RTS players who believed the Strategic Commander really pulled it off, and those found other uses for the device in other flavours of PC game. And with the issue of modern compatibility, a roadblock for so much retro hardware, bypassed by homemade patches, both camps can continue twisting and clicking their Strategic Commanders unimpeded.

All the same, I wonder if it might have basked in more conventional success, had Microsoft made more forward-thinking decisions. The Strategic Commander has proven over the years that its unprecedented customisation had applications far beyond what Ledbetter’s team had envisioned, and once the popularity of RTS games settled down, other, similar click-heavy subgenres like MOBAs and Real-Time Tactics got big as well. Would a more officially maintained, potentially refined Strategic Commander model have served those crowds? I think it might. The narrow focus on RTS games may have also hurt the device's since-demonstrated broader appeal; if Microsoft had fully capitalised on this customisation selling point by communicating its benefits to everyone, not just those playing the single most popular PC genre at the time, it could have provided the blueprint for a proper, long-lived alternative to controllers, mice, and keyboards.

Alas, gaming history is littered with revolutionary peripherals that failed to actually revolutionise the industry surrounding them. The Strategic Commander is no different, but for the fact that it’s had this surprisingly long post-shelf life - and, as a result, the opportunity for insight into what could have been done to salvage the its failures. Still, that unexpected longevity can provide come comfort to the Strategic Commander's creators, as well as its lingering users.

"I think the design made sense for its time, especially with the rising popularity of RTS games," Ledbetter reflects. "Looking back, I would have liked more time to test and refine the Strategic Commander. I’m proud of what we brought to market, and I’m glad to know that it remains beloved by many players to this day."

A $25 million MEV ruling exposes a dangerous blind spot in Wall Street’s tokenization push

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An Ethereum exploit case has brought renewed attention to transaction-ordering risks as the SEC allows tokenized stock venues to set their own safeguards.

A federal judge on Nov. 16 acquitted Anton and James Peraire-Bueno in a $25 million Ethereum maximum extractable value (MEV) exploit case because prosecutors had failed to prove a material misrepresentation.

The decision leaves a separate question for tokenized US stock markets about what protects investors when transaction ordering works against them.

The SEC had already opened a conditional route for those markets in September on its framework, which preserves applicable securities antifraud and manipulation laws, but requires venues to disclose their policies for MEV even when they have none.

MEV concerns the value captured through transaction ordering. Four operators' notices show how much can differ within that framework, from transaction privacy and planned sequencing controls to the prospect that investors bear their own losses.

In her 46-page opinion, US District Judge Jessica Clarke found insufficient evidence to sustain wire fraud and the related wire-fraud and money-laundering conspiracy charges. The missing element was a material misrepresentation in the brothers' Omakase operation.

The opinion describes a 2023 operation that used lure transactions to attract sandwich-trading bots and exploited a relay-code vulnerability, producing about $25 million in 12 seconds. The targeted bots themselves sought to profit by placing transactions around someone else's trade, a practice that can leave that trader with a worse execution price.

The case was more specific than a simple claim that someone profited from transaction ordering, since prosecutors had to establish the charged fraud.

Clarke found that the trial evidence did not establish a material misrepresentation in the Omakase strategy, and frustrating MEV-Boost's intended design could not supply that missing element.

The court also distinguished precedents involving spoofing, where orders create a misleading impression before being canceled. Here, the lure transactions executed and also were not placed on a regulated market with rules prohibiting the conduct relied upon in those precedents.

That reasoning does not establish a universal requirement that wire fraud occur on a regulated market, nor does it determine whether all forms of MEV extraction are lawful.

In a March 30 letter, the Securities Industry and Financial Markets Association (SIFMA) argued that exposing pending trades to sandwiching and front-running can conflict with securities-market expectations. It called for technical protections or fair-sequencing rules when automated market makers handle securities.

An automated market maker (AMM) lets participants trade against assets committed to a pool using software-defined pricing. The ordering of those trades can affect who receives a favorable price and who captures the resulting profit.

Keeping an investor's pending instruction private and deciding who processes it first are economically significant choices.

The SEC's Sept. 17 order created temporary, conditional relief from the definition of an exchange for qualifying tokenized National Market System stock venues using permissioned AMM pools. Certain liquidity providers trading proprietary capital received separate relief from the dealer definition until Sept. 17, 2031.

The order retains applicable securities antifraud and antimanipulation provisions, including Exchange Act Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5. Its conditions include permissioned access, public notices, records, and specified trading stoppages.

A venue must describe policies and procedures designed to address it, or state that it has none. It must disclose known material risks, explain any mitigation and compensation arrangements, and describe trading-abuse monitoring or acknowledge its absence.

A venue's published account of its protections can be detailed while leaving investors exposed to risks the operator expressly acknowledges.

The SEC's public list, reviewed Nov. 16, contains four notice filers. Their submission dates range from TSV LLC's Sept. 23 notice to Formation Securities' Nov. 9 filing.

TSV LLC's current notice says Canton's architecture generally avoids broadcasting readable pending transaction contents to unrelated participants, aiming to reduce opportunities for conventional public-mempool front-running and sandwich attacks.

The operator still acknowledges risks of information leakage, sequencing, compromised infrastructure, and collusion.

OKXICE's Nov. 5 notice disclosed that it has no policies and procedures designed to address MEV. That sits alongside general protections, including permissioned participation, participant minimum-output settings, surveillance and the ability to revoke access for manipulative trading. OKXICE says those features mitigate, rather than eliminate, MEV risk.

Its planned pools use Uniswap v4 on XLayer, where the notice says the sequencer generally orders transactions according to offered network fees.

Perpetuals.com's notice proposes generally first-in, first-out sequencing, with priority for trading-halt instructions and signed arrival receipts intended to make ordering verifiable. Yet its affiliated sequencer would receive nonpublic transaction information.

A private pending-transaction route can reduce exposure to outsiders while leaving a concentrated information advantage to manage.

Its October notice describes safeguards that were not fully implemented and surveillance intended to precede trading. Formation's notice likewise describes planned safeguards for its Solana venue.

Preventing an abusive trade, detecting it afterward, and compensating its victim are different functions.

TSV says participants generally should not expect compensation for market or impermanent losses, compromised participant systems or losses caused by unaffiliated third parties, unless it expressly agrees otherwise or the law requires it.

Perpetuals.com reports no established compensation policy for the risks described in its notice. Formation specifically excludes compensation for lost or compromised private keys or wallets, which does not establish a general recovery guarantee for other losses.

While campaigning the week before the midterms, health secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. visited Sheepgate, a Christian addiction recovery center in Colfax, Iowa. Sheepgate, Kennedy said, is a model for the type of faith-based center that doesn’t receive federal funding but that he believes should.

“We took a wonderful tour today and it was really impressive and really touching,” Kennedy said in a video about the visit.

The Trump administration has repeatedly emphasized the promise of faith-based addiction recovery programs, including in its 2026 National Drug Control Strategy. The Great American Recovery Initiative, co-chaired by Kennedy and Kathryn Burgum, extols the power of faith. And in late August, Kennedy tapped Monty Burks, who leads the Center for Faith at the Department of Health and Human Services, as interim executive director of the initiative. Like Kennedy and Burgum, Burks is in recovery from addiction.

Treatment and policy experts largely agree that faith communities — and a belief in something bigger than oneself — can play an important role in a person’s recovery. But some are concerned that federal grants will be directed to faith-based initiatives that don’t provide evidence-based treatment.

Sheepgate, for example, explicitly prohibits those who join its yearlong programs from using methadone, a drug approved by the Food and Drug Administration for opioid use disorder. They can only use another addiction medication, Suboxone, if they’ll be fully tapered off of it within the first three months, according to the center’s student guidelines — a quick timeline that doctors generally recommend against. It’s not alone: Research has found that faith-based treatment centers are less likely than secular ones to offer medications for opioid use disorder, and more likely to refuse clients who may be taking them.

Refusing to accept people on FDA-approved medications “is so irresponsible,” said Ayana Jordan, an addiction psychiatrist at NYU Langone who works with faith organizations on recovery efforts. “I would not want to provide federal funds to that type of recovery center because they’re actively working against things that we know can save lives.”

But Michael Hunsberger, Sheepgate’s regional president, said the issue isn’t so black and white. Sheepgate would accept people taking addiction medications, he told STAT, if the center received more funding and was able to build out the medical staff. He argued that faith-based organizations are often excluded from public funds regardless of the level of care they provide.

“We could literally allow multiple of those prescriptions and bring on different medical staff and still be excluded from certain dollars due to our faith-based beliefs,” Hunsberger said.

Medications for opioid use disorder are just a few on a long list of drugs that Sheepgate doesn’t have the capacity to distribute or monitor the use of, Hunsberger said. When somebody isn’t a good fit for Sheepgate due to any medication use, the staff tries to find another organization where they could seek help.

While the Trump administration has continued to broadly support medications for substance use disorder — which can be highly effective at preventing overdose deaths — Kennedy has repeatedly cast doubt on existing systems for treating addiction. He’s also expressed support for unconventional (and scientifically dubious) approaches to treatment, including “wellness farms” where people reside in nature and engage in manual labor. Kennedy’s own path to long-term recovery from drugs and alcohol leaned strongly on groups like Alcoholics Anonymous and his Catholic faith.

But what worked for Kennedy won’t work for everyone, experts say. As Trump’s tax-cut bill goes into effect and more people lose health coverage, “what really concerns me is that there seems to be an expectation from the Trump administration that these faith-based groups are going to fill those gaps,” said Libby Jones, associate vice president of the Global Health Advocacy Incubator’s Overdose Prevention Initiative.

The administration has not made official regulatory changes to the federal grant process, but it has gestured that the bar may be lowered for faith-based groups, Jones said. A “Dear Colleague” letter from the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration stressed that faith-based groups should receive “equal treatment” in the grants process. The Justice Department has deemed unconstitutional Biden administration regulations that aimed to prohibit the use of federal funds for “explicitly religious activities” and protect people from discrimination on the basis of faith.

Experts believe there should be clear stipulations that faith-based organizations receiving federal funding follow evidence-based care, and that federal agencies should ensure that funding goes to a diverse array of religious groups, not only to Christian organizations.

“I am always heartened to know that there is money that is being put into faith-based institutions,” Jordan said. “But we have an obligation to taxpayers — this is taxpayers’ money — to say what actually works.”

Sheepgate would welcome any oversight and regulations that may come with federal funding, Hunsberger said, as long as it does not interfere with the organization’s core identity.

“If those federal dollars change our identity as a faith-based organization, then that would change us accepting those dollars,” Hunsberger said. But, “the faith community — the majority of it — is not against the medical side. It’s just, a lot of times, we’re pitted against each other.”

The Department of Health and Human Services did not respond directly to STAT’s questions about how it may assess the efficacy of faith-based recovery organizations for future funding opportunities. “For generations, these organizations have provided hope, accountability, mentorship, housing, workforce opportunities, and the relationships that help people sustain long-term recovery, often with limited resources and little government support,” HHS deputy press secretary Lizzie Harvey said in a statement.

This year, HHS has given supplemental funding awards of $500,000 each to Alabama, Kansas, and West Virginia to pursue faith-based prevention work. Kennedy has also said that SAMHSA’s STREETS grants — which send $12 million over four years to communities in support of work on the intersection of homelessness and addiction — will also have a “new emphasis on faith-based recovery,” according to the Iowa Capitol Dispatch.

Both of those grant notices included language directing recipients to employ evidence-based practices. But another “Dear Colleague” letter written by SAMHSA leadership earlier this year warned against relying on medications alone for addiction treatment, so as to avoid what it calls “a default sentence to life-long medication use.” That guidance, which has now been baked into SAMHSA’s terms and conditions for all grant recipients, created some confusion about the agency’s support of FDA-approved medications.

“There’s an ambiguity here that just needs to be clearer,” Jordan said, adding that the administration appears to be focusing largely on white, Christian organizations.

“The methods by which we define ‘faith-based’ and ‘evidence-based treatment’ need to be clear,” she said.

In the past four months, Kennedy and other HHS leaders have visited or hosted roundtables with at least half a dozen other Christian recovery centers. It’s unclear how many encourage or allow the use of FDA-approved medications for substance use disorders.

Without strict guidance from federal agencies, funding decisions may be left to states or more local jurisdictions. The Montgomery Alcohol, Drug Addiction and Mental Health Services (ADAMHS) Board, a county-level authority in Ohio, was tapped last month for one of eight federal STREETS grants. For years, the board has funded and coordinated with both faith-based and secular recovery homes, a housing agency, behavioral health facility, street outreach and medicine teams, and peer-support services.

The Montgomery ADAMHS board only funds recovery homes that are certified with the state, the board’s housing coordinator, Kaylyn Drodge, told STAT. That includes six Christian recovery homes and two secular ones. (They’re open to non-Christian faith groups, but have not received any such applications, Drodge said.)

Ohio law requires certified faith-based recovery homes to accept people with different religious backgrounds, as well as those who do not want to engage in any faith activities. But there are no requirements about accepting people on medications for opioid use disorder. Out of the six Christian recovery homes the board funds, two turn those people away.

Recovery homes in Ohio are not allowed to provide medical care, Drodge said. But in many places, those lines are blurred.

“For the general population, they are equating ‘recovery’ with ‘treatment,’” Jordan said. She believes organizations that don’t provide clinical treatment directly should still be eligible for federal funding, but the government should consider how they encourage or support patients to receive proven medical care.

Opinion: The problem with ‘zero tolerance for bad behavior’ signs at hospitals

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

By Autumn Fiester, Aliza Narva, and Stacy C. Smith

Fiester is professor of medical ethics and health policy at the University of Pennsylvania’s Perelman School of Medicine and director of the Penn Program in Clinical Conflict Management. Narva is the director of ethics at the Hospital of the University of Pennsylvania. Smith is a registered nurse, certified health care ethics consultant, and qualitative researcher.

Enter any hospital today and you’ll see signs warning patients and visitors that abusive language, disruptive behavior, or aggression will not be tolerated. Some threaten removal from the facility. Others warn that security or police may be called for infractions. Almost all of them declare “zero tolerance” for any of these behaviors.

The motivation behind these signs is understandable. Workplace violence against health care workers is a serious problem in U.S. hospitals, with research demonstrating an increase in frequency over the past five years.

In response to this widespread problem, both state governments and hospitals have made a concerted effort to demonstrate that they are taking this problem seriously. They have enacted a range of anti-violence measures that include reporting systems, de-escalation training programs, weapon-detection portals, increased security presence, and posted behavior signs.  Our own health system has implemented similar measures.

While these efforts are laudable in intent, not all of them are evidence-based with demonstrable efficacy in curtailing workplace violence. That includes the now-ubiquitous posted hospital behavior signs. Multiple studies that have tested these signs do not deter patients or families from committing acts of violence or reduce the frequency of physical threats.

If posted hospital behavior signs were merely ineffective at reducing workplace violence, they would be pointless, but not harmful.

But we believe that behavior signage is more problematic than it appears. The content of the signs sends clear messages to patients and families about their tenuous status and value in the institution. These signs telegraph to patients and their loved ones that they are on notice, warned that their care can be withheld and their behavior policed — even punished. In the urgency to reduce violence against health care workers, hospitals have failed to consider the message these signs communicate and whether that message aligns or conflicts with the core values of medicine: trust, care, compassion.

In our review of posted behavior signs, we found that they target only patient and visitor conduct. They rattle off a list of patient and family “do nots,” the hospital’s mechanisms of behavior enforcement, and the dire consequences of behavioral infractions. Even when the signs give a nominal nod to the institution’s commitment to reciprocal expectations for respectful communication between patients and the clinical staff, the list of “do nots” applies only to patients and families. They list no parallel behavioral standards for nurses or physicians. That makes behavior signs ignore the relational aspect of conflict. Research on lapses in clinical professionalism points to a more nuanced explanation for some of the challenging behaviors exhibited by families and patients. The Joint Commission has even named such clinical behavior as one of the causes of violence against health care workers.

Behavior signs also display from a type of institutional amnesia, forgetting why patients and families have come to the hospital in the first place: They are in pain, anxious, frightened, grieving. They may have or soon receive a life-altering diagnosis or a heartbreaking prognosis. They are vulnerable. Is this a group of people who should receive stern warnings and threats of removal as they enter the building?

When signs espouse “zero tolerance” for “noncompliant,” “disrespectful,” or “demanding” behavior, they signal to patients and their families that raising concerns about their care can be interpreted as misconduct. In a setting where patients and families already feel profound vulnerability, voicing a complaint about their clinical care can feel too risky.

That silence risks patient safety. Data show that patient and family feedback can reveal genuine safety concerns and quality issues that would fly under the radar if they remained unvoiced. Without patients and families feeling welcome to express concerns, hospitals can miss the exact information that would improve patient care and prevent patient harm.

Hospitals do not have to choose between protecting hospital staff and preserving patient/family trust in the institution.

Imagine signs that convey not threats, but shared expectations and a willingness to help when things go wrong:

“Our staff is here to help you during what may be one of the most difficult days of your life. We ask everyone, including staff, patients, and families, to treat one another with respect. If something is not going well, please let us know so we can help resolve it.”

Such messages still make clear that violence will not be tolerated. But they also clearly convey that health care is a partnership, that the experience of illness can be challenging, and that civility helps everyone. They express behavioral standards, while also indicating that the hospital’s response to challenging interactions is to help, not punish. Some hospital systems have already replaced their “zero tolerance” signs with civility signs that project care and concern.

Protecting health care workers from violence must continue to be an institutional priority, but that effort requires evidence-based prevention programs, including efforts to understand and address the root causes of conflict.

Messages about patient, family, and clinical staff behavior can be delivered in a way that acknowledges the concerns of hospital employees while also preserving patients’ and families’ sense of belonging and their ability to ask hard questions, voice concerns, and participate fully in their care.

The words we post on hospital walls must communicate more than rules. They must communicate medicine’s values.

The latest product from the tobacco industry is a flavorful nicotine tablet that melts in your mouth, allowing users to get a buzz as discreetly as popping a mint — and amping up concerns that products to replace cigarettes are too appealing to kids and teens.

In the United Kingdom and Switzerland, Philip Morris International recently introduced Zyn Melts, mint-flavored tablets that provide a nicotine hit as quickly as smoking a cigarette. Japan Tobacco International launched a similar product called Perlz in the U.K. in May.

And in the U.S., Nic Nac Naturals, a nascent nicotine company based in Spokane, Wash., has been selling flavored lozenges called Nic Nac since 2022; they’re now available in about 3,000 stores nationwide.

Users tuck these tablets next to their gums, similar to how they use nicotine pouches. Like pouches, the new products come in colorful packaging and flavors such as mint and berry. But with tablets, there’s no wet sachet to discard.

That makes them more appealing than pouches, said Erwin Henriquez, a global analyst for the marketing research firm Euromonitor. “It’s much more friendly to chew gum or a dissolving tablet than to put something in your gums,” he said. “You have vitamins and supplements that consumers are already used to.”

Public health advocates and researchers are concerned about nicotine tablets for a similar reason. Their resemblance to mints or lozenges “may make them appear harmless and more appealing to children and adolescents,” said Magnus Lundbäck, a cardiologist, nicotine researcher and professor at the Karolinska Institutet in Stockholm.

Tobacco companies have introduced e-cigarettes, heated tobacco, and nicotine pouches as less harmful alternatives to cigarettes. Tobacco-related diseases are the leading preventable cause of death worldwide, responsible for 7 million deaths a year.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration, which influences tobacco policy worldwide, has authorized these products because they’re less harmful than cigarettes and could help smokers quit.

But nicotine is tied to health problems even when it’s not inhaled through smoke, and the long-term health risks of these new products aren’t clear. Public health advocates say there’s little evidence that smokers are switching to pouches and heated tobacco entirely. More often, they say, people use those products in addition to cigarettes.

“The tobacco and nicotine industry is continuously developing new products that may attract new generations of users, rather than reducing nicotine dependence,” Lundbäck said. “The ultimate public health goal must be fewer people addicted to nicotine, not simply more ways to consume it.”

Henriquez, the Euromonitor analyst, said dissolvable tablets are a “natural evolution” of nicotine pouches and serve consumers’ growing appetite for stimulants as they “feel stress all the time.”

Nicotine pouch sales reached $9.2 billion globally in 2025, according to Euromonitor. The volume of pouches sold has grown 50% annually for the past five years and is expected to continue at 20.5% a year, according to a July report by Henriquez.

On its U.K. site, Philip Morris International tells consumers that Zyn Melts “offer a completely new way for adult users to enjoy tobacco-free and smoke-free nicotine moments on the go.” It compares tablets to pouches, saying tablets are for a “fast nicotine experience” while pouches have a “longer lasting nicotine and flavor experience.”

Japan Tobacco sells Perlz through Flavour Warehouse, a U.K. vaping company it controls. Its website states, “First came vapes. Then pouches. Now there’s Perlz — the ingenious new nicotine experience for existing adult nicotine users.”

Tablets contain less nicotine than most pouches, with Zyn Melts and Perlz available in 1 and 2 milligram doses.

Nic Nac lozenges are available in 3 and 6 mg doses. Nic Nac Naturals co-founder and Chief Executive Officer Nicco Magnotto said the lozenges are different from Zyn Melts and Perlz because they release nicotine over about 30 minutes, more like a pouch.

The most popular nicotine pouches, in comparison, range from 3 to 17 mg. Philip Morris International recently introduced a 1.5 mg pouch for users “new to nicotine pouches.”

Public health officials have been engaged in a yearslong balancing act with new nicotine products, weighing the potential benefits to adults looking for less harmful alternatives to cigarettes against the potential for youth to abuse or accidentally ingest nicotine.

Vapes exploded in popularity in 2018, causing the FDA to declare teen use an epidemic. Then nicotine pouches caught on, with surveys showing increased use among teens over the past few years. The industry, however, says the percentage of youth using pouches remains low.

Meanwhile, poison control centers in the U.S. reported that calls involving nicotine poisoning, many involving pouches, nearly doubled from 2024 to 2025. Three-quarters of cases last year involved children under the age of 6.

Nicotine tablets represent a new area of concern.

The industry says it’s not looking to sell to kids. In an email, a Japan Tobacco spokesperson said the company does not “design, market or promote Perlz to appeal” to minors and that the containers are child-resistant, requiring three buttons to be pressed simultaneously to be opened.

“We take steps to ensure that our advertising is directed towards adult tobacco and nicotine users only and does not have particular appeal to minors,” the spokesperson said.

Magnotto said his company does not advertise or market Nic Nac to youth and that the company’s research indicates they don’t find lozenges appealing. “It comes across as medicinal,” he said. “Pouches are the rage. Kids aren’t even aware of us.”

Asked about the potential for abuse by kids, Magnotto said his company’s container has a scientifically tested, child-resistent seal and will soon have a more secure mechanism.

Phillip Morris International did not respond to a request for comment. The company says on its U.K. website that Melts are meant for adults.

The U.K. doesn’t require regulatory approval for nicotine products, making it an ideal test market. The tablets were introduced shortly before regulations imposed by the Tobacco and Vapes Act 2026 became effective in October. That law imposes stricter rules on tobacco and nicotine products, including prohibitions on giveaways and big discounts.

Philip Morris International is looking beyond the U.K. and Switzerland. It has filed for trademark protections for Zyn Melts in the U.S. and Europe, both as a consumer nicotine product and a pharmaceutical aid to stop smoking.

The trademarks are among many the company has registered this year for Zyn, including “House of Zyn” and “Zyn Club” — signs that the company is positioning Zyn as a lifestyle brand.

Nicotine tablets have been available in the U.S. for years as smoking cessation aids, which requires a special FDA approval as a pharmaceutical product. The agency would need to do a separate review in order to authorize the sale of tablets to consumers.

Nic Nac Naturals submitted an application in 2023 to sell its nine mint and fruit-flavored tablets to the general public in the U.S.

Mitch Zeller, former director of the FDA’s Center for Tobacco Products, said nicotine tablets “could possibly be helpful” to smokers looking for alternatives in a “properly regulated market.”

First, he said, the product needs to undergo a complete scientific review, with studies showing how different users respond to it. He noted that a new FDA rule allows nicotine products to be sold while their application is pending, which means Zyn Melts and Perlz could be on shelves without any research on their appeal to adolescents.

That’s not how it should work, he said: “All of the intended and unintended consequences of something like this need to be sorted out on a pre-market basis.”

But for decades, astronomers have suspected that the internal dynamics of massive galaxies like ours play an outsized role in regulating star formation.

As observations by NASAâs James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) now reveal, massive galaxies have largescale gaseous outflows that greatly quench their star production.

Outflows are large-scale flows of gas, from inside to outside the galaxy usually caused by either supernovae or energetic outbursts from supermassive black holes.

These JWST observations have revealed that outflows are more abundant and more powerful than we thought, suggesting that the evolution of galaxies is strongly impacted by outflows, Sirio Belli, an associate professor at the University of Bologna, Italy and the paperâs co-author, told me via email.

Normally, galaxies are rich in gas, and when the gas is sufficiently cold, it collapses under its own weight and forms a dense cloud from which new stars form. But such outflows likely remove a large quantity of gas from galaxies, which are then unable to form new stars.

Growing supermassive black holes at the centers of galaxies drive powerful winds, blowing away gas that would otherwise form stars, Rebecca Davies, the paperâs lead author and an astrophysicist at Australiaâs Swinburne University of Technology in Melbourne, told me via email. With JWST, we can measure the amount of cold star-forming gas being ejected, Davies told me.

But JWST has also revealed that outflows powered by accretion onto supermassive black holes are linked to the rapid shut-down or âquenchingâ of star formation.

Quenching can happen if the galaxy runs out of gas or the galaxy stops being able to turn the gas into stars because the gas is too hot or turbulent to collapse, says Davies.

One possibility is that the supermassive black hole at the center of the galaxy releases a large amount of energy which removes and/or heats the gas and prevents the formation of new stars, says Belli.

As such massive galaxies age, their AGN become relatively quiescent, not unlike Sag A* --- our own Milky Wayâs supermassive black hole in our galactic center.

The Milky Wayâs black hole had a major flare just a few million years ago, smaller outbursts only a century or two ago, and it still flickers almost every day as hot gas swirls just outside it, says Davies.

A key open question is what triggers outflows powerful enough to quench galaxies.

Outflows are very common in massive galaxies at cosmic noon, but most of these galaxies are not quenching: the outflows simply keep the galaxies in equilibrium and prevent them from converting all their gas into stars too quickly, says Davies. Possible triggers of the most powerful outflows include galaxy mergers, or radio jets launched by the central supermassive black holes, she says.

As for how this stellar quenching might impact the formation of molecular clouds that spawn solar type stars and habitable planetary systems?

When galaxies are quenched, the formation of new stars and planets is shut down, with strong repercussions on the probability of developing habitable systems, says Belli. If the Milky Way had been quenched at Cosmic Noon, it would likely not have formed many new stars in the last 10 billion years, and our solar system which is only 5 billion years old would not exist today, he says.

But star formation can become too much of a good thing.

When star formation is too strong there are other problems, such as the large number of stellar explosions that could negatively impact the habitability of planets, says Belli. The connection between galaxy-scale processes and the habitability of planetary systems is only now being investigated, he says.

Judge Acquits MIT Brothers in $25M Ethereum MEV Case, Ending Prosecution

A federal judge in Manhattan has acquitted Anton and James Peraire-Bueno, the MIT-educated brothers accused of exploiting Ethereum's transaction ordering system to take $25 million from automated trading bots.

U.S. District Judge Jessica Clarke granted the brothers' motion for a judgment of acquittal on Monday, clearing them of wire fraud, conspiracy to commit wire fraud and conspiracy to commit money laundering, according to her ruling. The decision closes the criminal case without a second trial.

While the brothers' scheme "may have frustrated the spirit" of MEV-Boost, software that lets Ethereum validators outsource the ordering of transactions to specialized block builders, that was not enough to make out fraud, Clarke said. In a footnote, she added that the prosecution "raises serious due process concerns," though she declined to rule on that question.

Because the money laundering charge relied on the proceeds of the alleged wire fraud, it fell with the fraud counts.

The case stemmed from an April 2023 exploit targeting so-called sandwich bots, automated traders that profit from maximal extractable value (MEV) by placing their own trades on either side of other users' transactions.

According to the ruling, the brothers placed bait transactions designed to lure specific bots, then exploited a bug in a relay's code to see the bots' bundled trades before committing to a block. They then swapped the bait transactions for trades that sold the tokens instead, leaving the bots with about $25 million in losses in roughly 12 seconds. Authorities arrested the pair in May 2024, saying they spent months planning the attack and took steps to hide the proceeds.

At trial in Manhattan last year, an insider testified that the brothers had allegedly planned the attack, and the defense had earlier sought to block evidence of their Google searches. Clarke declared a mistrial in November 2025, after the jury deadlocked.

The case was closely watched in the crypto industry. Clarke's ruling described it as a "first of its kind" prosecution, a label the government itself used in its indictment.

Astronaut Loads 35mm Film Into Analog Camera While in Space

German astronaut Rabea Rogge flew on the Fram2 spaceflight mission in April 2025, bringing her trusty second-hand analog camera.

Rogge is an Arctic roboticist, currently studying for a PhD at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology in the Department of Marine Technology. She is also Germany’s first-ever female astronaut.

Last week, Rogge shared a video on Instagram as she loaded up a roll of 35mm film while onboard the SpaceX Crew Dragon Resilience spacecraft.

While many celluloid shooters have encountered difficulties loading film on terra firma, Rogge deftly loaded a roll of Kodak Portra 160 into the camera that she calls a “sturdy companion.”

A post shared by Rabea Rogge (@rabearogge)

“Analog pictures from space,” Rogge captions the post. “My old second-hand analog camera has been a sturdy companion on all my expeditions from under -20°C [-4°F] in the high Arctic to the humid climate at sea around the equator. On Fram2 it got to launch on a rocket as well – and it still lives on so far!”

PetaPixel identified Rogge’s camera as a Minolta X-700 with a 45mm lens attached. The X-700 was the flagship SLR for the now-defunct Japanese company for the best part of 20 years. In 1982, it won the European “Camera of the Year” award, and it is still popular in the second-hand market today.

With the film loaded in the camera, Rogge was afforded stunning views of Earth thanks to the Crew Dragon’s 360-degree cupola. At 2,000 square inches, it was the largest single window ever flown to space. Rogge, along with her crewmates Chun Wang, Jannicke Mikkelsen, and Eric Philips, were also the first people to ever orbit over Earth’s north and south poles.

“Can’t imagine the face of the people from the film lab when they scanned your roll,” comments Thibaut Everaerts on Instagram.

Rogge’s camera is far from the first Minolta to ever fly from space; in fact, the first American to ever orbit the Earth, John Glenn, took a Minolta Hi-Matic (repackaged as an Ansco Autoset) with him into space.

Despite buying the automatic camera for just $40 from a drugstore in Cocoa Beach, Florida, NASA engineers modified the camera with a pistol grip so that Glenn could use the camera while wearing his bulky astronaut gloves.

The photos that Glenn took in 1962 and their resulting popularity are credited with helping NASA realize how important it was to document Earth from space.

Humana is continuing its push to reform prior authorization with the launch of a national provider gold card program.

Through the program, eligible providers will no longer need to secure a prior authorization approval and can instead use a streamlined process.

Humana estimates that about 50,000 providers will qualify for the program. Eligible providers must be able to demonstrate a proven track record of high-quality care and patient outcomes.

Traci Granston, M.D., vice president of clinical innovation and trend at Humana, told Fierce Healthcare in an exclusive interview that prior authorization and other utilization management approaches are key in ensuring that patients receive the appropriate care.

However, lengthy decision times and other barriers can create a painful friction. So for providers that consistently demonstrate that they are aligned with clinical guidelines, the gold cards can remove that guardrail.

"If they're following those guidelines and providing appropriate care, prior auths don't really serve a purpose for them," Granston said.

The insurer conducted a pilot earlier this year with about 400 provider organizations and approximately 8,000 clinicians. Through that process, the team conducted interviews with clinicians and office staff who shared their desire for fewer administrative burdens and a simpler experience.

Those insights shaped the broader launch, Humana said, and the insurer intends to continue to iterate and evolve the gold card program over time, including by adding new service lines. It's not uncommon for a program to roll out that aims to support providers, but their voice is less often at the table in shaping what the experience looks like to ensure that their pain points are being addressed.

Granston said that the team is examining more time-sensitive services that could fit within the program, for example.

"For our top-performing providers, we would like to essentially get out of the way and let them continue to care for our members," she said.

The gold card program builds on Humana's existing work to reform prior authorization, including a streamlined approval process for electronic submissions that took effect in January of this year. Under this initiative, Humana responds within one business day in 95% of electronic prior authorization cases.

Whale snot helps scientists sequence an entire genome

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 17, 2026 5:00 AM EST

Whale vomit can be turned into expensive perfume. Whale carcasses will sink to the bottom and become marine life habitats. Whale pee nourishes the entire ocean. But how about whale snot? The microbes and other biological material emitted from their blowholes when the giant cetaceans breathe has helped scientists sequence an entire whale genome: Take that whale vomit. The findings are detailed in a study published today in the journal Molecular Ecology Resources.

Whole genomes are crucial for animal conservation. With genomic data, researchers can measure population health, to see how much genetic diversity and inbreeding is present. It can also help identify individual animals for monitoring, help determine an animal’s sex, and estimate the population’s size.

To obtain genetic data from whales, marine biologists historically have had to conduct a skin biopsy. They’ll take a dart, stick it into the whale’s blubber, and take a small sample of the skin and blubber. However, during sampling, boats must get close to the whales, which can disturb them. In some locations and for some endangered species, getting close enough to take a biopsy isn’t permitted or possible.

That’s where whale snot—or blow—comes in. For this study, a team from Scotland’s University of St. Andrews, the University of Copenhagen in Denmark, the North Coast Cetacean Society, and Fisheries and Oceans Canada used several small drones off the coast of British Columbia. The drones were equipped with sterile petri dishes to collect the samples.

The team flew the drone through the exhaled breaths of one North pacific humpback whale (Megaptera novaeangliae kuzira) at a time, but still sampled several individual whales. The humpbacks generally took one to six breaths every time they surfaced—covering the drone and the dishes in whale snot.

The team collected 58 blow samples from 26 individual humpback whales. They also took repeated samples from the same whales, collected in different encounters and across two years to match the genomes. They then cross-referenced the genomes collected from the whale blow with genomes sequenced from traditional blubber biopsies taken from the same animals.

To keep the DNA from mixing, the team targeted the whale most upwind in the pod, since the blow downwind might mix with the blow from the whales up ahead. Resting whales were never sampled, and the team stopped the drone flights if a whale showed any reaction. More than one third of samples were collected by flying the drone directly from land—no boat required.

The DNA was sequenced to produce two types of genomes. Low-coverage nuclear genomes, which are inherited from both parents, and high-coverage mitochondrial genomes, which are inherited from the mother only.

The team found that the genetic diversity estimated from the blow samples was very similar to the estimates taken from traditional biopsies. Fortunately, the humpback population in the study shows little evidence of recent inbreeding. There is a small proportion of the genome that has long stretches of identical DNA, but the team believes that most likely reflects distant shared ancestry or past population bottlenecks.

According to the team, this research marks one of the first whole genome datasets for a group of North Pacific humpback whales. Previously, only one North Pacific humpback whole genome was publicly available.

“We will use these blow-derived genomes to not only assess population structure and health, but also assess kinship and family ties among humpback whales foraging in northern British Columbia,” Dr. Éadin O’Mahony, a study co-author and marine mammal ecologist who was at the University of St. Andrews at the time of the study, said in a statement. “The aim is to understand how their social bonds form: Are the whales that forage together family, or long-term friends?”

As expected, the North Pacific humpback whales were genetically distinct from their counterparts living in the North Atlantic. This confirms that data taken from whale blow works as well from data taken from conventional biopsies. The drone-captured genetic analyses also correctly determined every whale’s sex, and revealed three previously undescribed mitochondrial DNA variants, which could help show how some of these whales are related. The blow from whales swimming in groups also did not have any sign of contamination from neighboring whales.

About 16 percent of the DNA in each blow sample were also primarily from the microbes living in the whale’s airways. According to the team, this indicates that a single breath could tell us about the individual whale and its respiratory microbiome, opening up a potential new window into cetacean health.

That’s a whole lot of data from just a little bit of snot.

Just over half of the country’s nonprofit hospitals have charity care and community benefit investments that fall short of their tax savings, according to the latest of the Lown Institute’s analyses on more than 3,000 hospitals’ tax filings.

In 2023, the most recent year in which Internal Revenue Service Form 990s were available for review, the think tank identified $38.8 billion of industry-wide tax exemptions, or $12.8 million per hospital and 3.8% of total expenses on average. About $37.1 billion was collectively spent by the same hospitals on various community benefits, as reported in the IRS forms matched to other filings such as Medicare cost reports.

While the total exemption handily outpaces the benefits investments by Lown’s measure, the group underscored that these "fair share” deficits or surpluses also vary widely between facilities, and that some of the industry’s biggest names are on the wrong side of the divide.

Here, Mayo Clinic Hospital led the pack with a $366 million deficit, according to Lown’s analysis, followed by Tisch Hospital (-$294 million deficit), Stanford Hospital (-$255 million deficit), New York-Presbyterian Hospital (-$248 million deficit) and Massachusetts General Hospital (-$219 million deficit).

"Some of America's wealthiest hospitals fall hundreds of millions short of giving back what they receive from their communities, leaving those with fewer resources to pick up the slack," Vikas Saini, M.D., president of the Lown Institute, said in a release on the new analysis, released Tuesday.

Among those leading the latter group were North Shore University Hospital ($167 fair share surplus), Rochester General Hospital ($146 million surplus) and Englewood Hospital ($142 million surplus). Lown’s analysis includes a searchable hospital-by-hospital breakdown of the tax exemptions and community investment totals.

When splitting the 52% of deficit hospitals apart from the surplus hospitals, the former group logged about $15.5 billion above their reported community benefits—a deficit Lown said could wipe the medical debt of 12 million Americans. Fair share surplus hospitals outpaced their exemptions by $13.8 billion.

Total tax exemptions were also “very evenly distributed” among the hospitals, Lown said. The 30 hospitals, or about 1% of all hospitals, with the largest tax exemptions collectively avoided $6.8 billion, or about 18% of total exemptions. The 607 hospitals, or about 20%, accounted for three-quarters of the country’s hospital tax exemptions.

Lown’s measure of nonprofit hospitals’ tax breaks includes exemptions from federal and state corporate income taxes, state and local sales taxes, property taxes, tax-exempt bonds, tax-exempt charitable donations and federal unemployment tax exemptions. Not included are other benefits provided to many nonprofits, like Medicaid disproportionate share payments, 340B discounts or profits from indirect tax arbitrage.

Its tally of community benefit includes spending on patient financial assistance, subsidized care, payments to other nonprofits to improve health or various community health or social determinants of health investments.

Of note, it excludes Medicaid shortfall—the difference between hospitals’ costs and revenues when treating Medicaid patients—and spending on professional education and research. The hospital industry typically points to these billions of dollars of exclusions when discussing their tax breaks, with Medicaid shortfall alone accounting for nearly half of their preferred community benefit spending totals. Lown noted in its methodology that these accounting losses are often offset by other discounts and supplemental benefits and don’t always have a direct impact on community health, among other objections.

Lown said the disparities between hospitals and the outsized benefits being delivered by some are a call to amend, not eliminate, nonprofit status exemptions. The think tank accompanied its analysis with calls to increase reporting transparency and set spending targets for hospitals tied to variables such as size, exemption value, financial health and prior spending.

“Tax exemptions aren't a gift—they're an expectation, and regulators need to make sure all hospitals hold up their end of the bargain," Saini said.

Lown acknowledged the impending financial pressure hospitals will face with upcoming H.R. 1 policy changes, and said such changes should be accompanied by “short-term” financial protections.

Still, the group isn’t alone in drawing scrutiny to the tax breaks. Lawmakers and policy advisors have pointed to, and questioned the justification for, nonprofits’ exemptions when questioning health system leaders on collections policies or high prices.

Academics and researchers have also called for greater transparency or other overhauls of how community benefits are reported or calculated. Of note, a research article published earlier this month in Health Affairs reviewing hospital filings between 2013 and 2022 outlined a disconnect between different hospitals’ listed charity care eligibility thresholds and their relative spending, which more closely aligned with community need than their stated policy.

Modern physics rests on two frameworks: general relativity and quantum mechanics. General relativity describes very large things, like stars; quantum mechanics describes very small things, such as atoms. Both are astonishingly good at predicting how the universe behaves, but they’re mathematically incompatible. Finding a theory that unites them—“quantum gravity”—is one of the field’s biggest goals. Black holes are a natural testing ground, because they’re one of the few areas where both relativity and quantum mechanics matter—they pack immense mass into a very small space.

One of the most useful tools for studying quantum gravity is called holographic duality. The real universe has three dimensions of space and one of time, which relativity combines into four-dimensional spacetime. But physicists studying quantum gravity often work with simpler model universes that they can understand in more detail.

The basic idea of holographic duality is that some of these theoretical universes can be described in two ways: as a complete universe with gravity and spacetime, and in quantum terms, with no gravity at all, “written” on the universe’s outer boundary. Just as a real hologram encodes a 3D image on a 2D plane, the quantum model encodes all the information of the universe in one dimension fewer (a universe with four dimensions of space, for instance, can be fully described by a quantum theory with three). This lets physicists turn hard questions about gravity into questions about quantum physics, which are sometimes easier to answer.

The first problem Claude solved concerns what happens when you heat up one of these theoretical universes. At low temperatures, the universe fills up with a gas of particles. Above a certain temperature, a black hole forms instead. In 1983, Stephen Hawking and Don Page showed that this switch is abrupt: it’s a phase transition, similar to how water suddenly boils at 100°C. But Hawking and Page’s calculation relies on general relativity, which holds only when the universe is vast, compared with the tiny scale at which that description breaks down. As the theoretical universe shrinks toward that smaller scale, spacetime loses any definite shape and becomes blurry, and nobody knows how to do the calculation on the gravity side of the duality. On the quantum side, though, the same situation can be calculated, at least in principle.

In 1999, physicists found that there was a phase transition in that regime as well. But they didn’t know whether it was abrupt, like Hawking and Page’s, or smooth. In 2003, a group of physicists set out the calculation that would settle the question, but it was extremely complex and was never completed. Claude did it by running a small research group of copies of itself, in which it worked out almost every piece of the calculation independently and checked its math against known results. It showed that the phase transition is abrupt. The same sudden switch appears at both extremes, suggesting that black hole formation isn’t just a feature of relativity but is actually built into the underlying quantum theory.

The second problem concerns a simplified theoretical universe with two dimensions of space instead of three. This universe is straightforward enough to study in detail, but still has black holes and other mysteries similar to those of our own universe. Even in this simpler universe, physicists don’t know how to create a full theory of quantum gravity, so they approach it from the other direction: rather than building a single, correct theory, they try to rule out wrong ones by showing that they are internally inconsistent. This method is called bootstrapping.

Since 2009, physicists have used a version of this method to set a ceiling on how heavy the lightest black hole in such a universe could be. Any theory that breaks the ceiling is ruled out, and each lowering of the ceiling narrows the search. Results from numerical calculations hinted at where the best possible ceiling lay, but they couldn’t prove it, and the best proven ceiling was well short of what the numerical results suggested.

Claude Fable 5.2 noticed a pattern in the numerical results that physicists had missed and borrowed a method from elsewhere in math and physics to calculate the precise ceiling for the simplest version of this technique and prove that it could not be lowered. It doesn’t identify the correct theory, but it may point physicists in the right direction.

Neither result describes our own universe directly, and neither has practical applications, but both contribute to a long-running theoretical effort to understand how quantum mechanics and relativity fit together.

In the rest of this post, we explain in more detail what each problem was and what Claude did to solve them.

Weeks after one of the agents reached a solution, a physicist, who had independently used Claude to tackle the same problem, shared his result, and a colleague realized that Hongbin’s agent had reached the same answer earlier. The two solutions used different methods, so they were a useful check on each other.

The modular bootstrap result came from several agents. The one that was assigned the problem spotted the pattern in the numerical results when surveying the bootstrap literature, and another one—which was working on a mathematically related problem about how tightly spheres can be packed into a high-dimensional space—turned that pattern into a method. In September, the results were formally verified in Lean, a computerized proof checker. Claude then wrote the paper with minimal guidance, and a physicist checked every main result.

To understand the first problem, we need to start with something much more familiar: a walk through New York City.

Imagine that you’re heading from Penn Station to the High Line entrance on West 30th Street and 10th Avenue, and there are two equally good routes. You could walk west along West 33rd Street and turn south, or head south first and then walk west along West 30th Street. Either way, you have to pick one. In the quantum world, though, that’s not true: a particle can take both routes simultaneously.

This is most clearly demonstrated in the famous double-slit experiment. In this test, you fire particles, one at a time, at a barrier cut with two narrow slits, and record where, on a screen behind it, each one lands. Each particle lands at a particular point on the screen, as a particle should. But as you fire more of them, they build up a pattern of stripes that would only be possible if each one had passed through both slits, like a wave.

To predict what a quantum particle will do more generally, physicists have to add up every path it could possibly take, known as its possible histories. Not every history counts equally; some contribute far more to the sum than others. It’s an extravagant and mysterious way to describe how a single particle behaves, but it’s also one of the most successful ideas in physics.

This approach, known as the “sum over histories,” is well established for particles and anything made of them. But what about gravity? Einstein showed that gravity is a consequence of curved spacetime: objects with mass bend space and time around them, and we feel that bending as gravity. In a quantum theory of gravity, therefore, what gets summed over is the shape of spacetime itself, rather than just the paths of particles moving through it. To make predictions in a quantum theory of gravity, we would have to add up every shape that the universe could possibly take, and nobody yet knows how to do that. Much of modern quantum gravity research is an attempt to work it out.

Hawking and Page found a case where this sum is simple enough to handle but still interesting. To explain Hawking and Page’s work, we need to imagine a different kind of universe, called anti-de Sitter spacetime. Imagine you’re standing in a giant bowl. No matter how hard you throw an object, it will always roll back to the place you threw it from.

Our universe isn’t like that. The difference comes down to a quantity called the cosmological constant, which describes the energy of empty space. In our universe, this energy is positive, which makes space expand ever faster. In anti-de Sitter spacetime, the energy is negative, so everything is pulled back to where it started. Hawking and Page chose this universe for their calculation because of how it behaves when it heats up: the bowl keeps heat in so it can stay at a steady temperature.

At any given temperature, two histories matter far more than all the others. Here, a “history” means an entire version of the universe: a particular shape of spacetime, along with everything in it. In one, the bowl is filled with an ordinary gas of particles—including gravitons, the particles believed to carry gravity. In the other, it contains a black hole. Whichever contributes more to the sum is, in effect, what we would observe—it “wins.” At low temperatures, the gas history wins, and the universe is full of gas; at high temperatures, the black-hole history wins, and the universe contains a black hole. At one particular temperature—which physicists now call the Hawking-Page temperature—the universe switches abruptly from one to the other. This is the phase transition.

It’s a rare case where we can see two very different shapes of spacetime competing in the sum over histories, and can say which one wins.

This is where the holographic principle enters the story. In the 1970s, Jacob Bekenstein and Hawking found that the amount of information a black hole can hold is proportional to its surface area, not to its volume. That’s very different from ordinary matter, where every bit of volume holds roughly the same amount of information.

In the early 1990s, Gerard ’t Hooft and Leonard Susskind proposed that this idea applies to gravity in general—that everything inside a region of space can be fully described by the information on its boundary, just as a flat hologram can display a three-dimensional image. In 1997, Juan Maldacena formalized the idea further. Gravity inside the anti-de Sitter bowl, he argued, is equivalent to an ordinary quantum theory of particles, with no gravity at all, existing on the rim of the bowl (the universe’s outer boundary). These are the gravity side and the quantum side of the duality we described in the introduction—they’re two languages for describing the same underlying reality.

In the case Claude studied, the quantum theory on the rim is called N=4 super Yang-Mills theory. It’s related to the theories that describe how particles interact in the real universe, but it has extra symmetries that make it much easier for physicists to work with. (Claude has worked with this theory before, calculating how particles interact within it to a new level of precision.) Since the gravity side predicts two histories competing with each other, the obvious question is: does N=4 super Yang-Mills theory (the quantum side) show the same thing?

For now, physicists can analyze this theory in detail only when its particles barely interact with each other, a condition known as weak coupling. This is the opposite end of the spectrum from where Hawking and Page’s gravitational calculation works. Weak coupling on the quantum side corresponds to the small, blurry universe on the gravity side. So there was no guarantee that any of Hawking and Page’s findings would hold there.

Remarkably, they do. Bo Sundborg in 1999, and after him Ofer Aharony, Joseph Marsano, Shiraz Minwalla, Kyriakos Papadodimas, and Mark Van Raamsdonk, showed that even at weak coupling, the theory on the rim goes through a transition as it heats up. This is its counterpart of the switch from gas to black hole inside the bowl. But it was still unknown whether this transition is abrupt, like Hawking and Page’s, or smooth and gradual.

When particles barely interact, physicists have to calculate how they behave one step at a time. They start with a simplified version of the theory in which particles don’t interact at all. Then they add a series of corrections, each more precise and harder to compute than the last. Physicists call these steps loops. In 2003, Aharony and his colleagues realized that whether the phase transition was an abrupt jump, as Hawking and Page predicted, or gradual, hinged on a single number that appeared only three loops in. Computing that number involves many thousands of terms, known as diagrams—far too many to work through by hand. In 2005, the same group completed the calculation for a simpler cousin of the theory and found an abrupt transition. But the version for N=4 super Yang-Mills, more than 10 times larger, was never completed.

The scale of the computational problem, while daunting, wasn’t the only obstacle to completing the calculation. There was a conceptual issue, as well: naively adding up all the parts of the calculation gives an infinite answer, and those infinities have to be tamed carefully to extract a meaningful result.

To solve the problem, Claude ran a small research group of agents, all copies of itself. Almost every piece of the calculation was done twice, by agents acting independently. For extra consistency checks, parts of the calculation could also be compared to previous results in the literature.

The result Claude found is an abrupt jump as the temperature rises, exactly analogous to what Hawking and Page had found using the sum over spacetime histories. The emerging picture is very simple: the transition is abrupt at both weak and strong coupling—the blurry and the smooth ends, in the gravity description. The most natural possibility is that it stays abrupt everywhere in between.

This is a beautiful outcome. It shows that a calculation using the sum over histories in gravity qualitatively matches the behavior of an ordinary quantum theory with no gravity at all.

Many open questions remain. The abrupt transition Claude found is very much like the one we see whenever we boil water. One interesting feature of abrupt transitions is that the losing side of the competition can survive for a while: for instance, water can be superheated above 100°C without boiling, as you could see for yourself if you were to put a cup of extremely pure water in the microwave. Something similar happens here. Above the Hawking-Page temperature, the hot gas of gravitons and other particles can survive for a long time in a superheated state. But at a higher temperature it can no longer survive: this is another transition, the Hagedorn transition, which is a characteristic signature of string theory. On the gravity side, that transition has many interesting properties of its own, and it would be valuable to check in detail whether they also appear in N=4 super Yang-Mills theory.

Another open question concerns the other possible histories of spacetime. Hawking and Page considered three: one in which their model universe is filled with gas, one that contains a large black hole, and one containing a smaller black hole that never comes to dominate the sum over histories. In principle, there are many more possible histories. As far as anyone knows, none of them ever dominates. But given certain changes to the starting conditions—if the universe is set spinning, for instance, or given a fixed amount of energy or charge—then some of these histories do take over. Working out exactly which ones win, and when, is an interesting problem for the future.

As we saw earlier, one useful model universe has just two dimensions of space and one of time: it’s called “3D gravity” because it describes gravity in three spacetime dimensions. According to the principle of holographic duality, 3D gravity inside the anti-de Sitter bowl is equivalent to an ordinary quantum theory living on the bowl’s rim.

As a model, 3D gravity is much simpler than the real thing, but it still allows for black holes and many of the same mysteries. It provides a useful laboratory for studying the sum over spacetime histories. It’s also a good place to test the idea that spacetime isn’t a basic ingredient of the universe, but emerges from something deeper. In general relativity, black holes look smooth. But in quantum gravity, if you zoom in to the smallest scales—the Planck length, around 10-35 meters, where quantum effects on gravity become impossible to ignore—black holes are made of an enormous number of tiny, individual building blocks. Physicists believe the same is true in our own, four-dimensional universe, but the 3D version is much easier to analyze with holographic duality.

Claude’s results build on a method called the bootstrap, a general strategy for solving quantum theories. Counterintuitively, the strategy doesn’t try to solve a theory directly. For 3D gravity, that isn’t even possible, because nobody knows how to define the theory precisely. Instead, the bootstrap rules theories out by showing that they contradict themselves. Physicists scan over all possible quantum theories and try to eliminate them one by one.

Remarkably, in many cases this method makes it possible to pinpoint a single quantum theory and calculate precisely how it behaves. The requirements of relativity and quantum mechanics are so demanding, and so delicately balanced, that together they rule out every alternative. In 2012, the bootstrap was used to solve a quantum field theory called the 3D critical Ising model, a simple model of magnetism that also describes water at the point where liquid and vapor become indistinguishable. Since then, it has been applied to many different quantum systems.

In 2009, Simeon Hellerman introduced a technique called the modular bootstrap, which applies the bootstrap method to 3D gravity. Its goal is to rule out inconsistent theories of quantum gravity in three spacetime dimensions. By combining it with other bootstrap methods, physicists hope to eventually eliminate every inconsistent theory. Whatever is left must be consistent. There could be no consistent theories, or just one, or whole families of them. Any of these outcomes would be interesting, because it would help us understand what spacetime looks like at the Planck scale in this simplified universe, and give us hints about the Planck scale in our own.

Here’s how the modular bootstrap works. 3D gravity in anti-de Sitter spacetime has a quantity called the central charge (c), which roughly counts how many microscopic building blocks the universe is made of. When general relativity holds, c is very large, because large black holes contain an enormous number of building blocks. A second quantity, M₁, is the mass of the lightest black hole. To run the bootstrap, physicists scan over every possible theory, with every possible value of c and M₁, and look for inconsistencies.

To understand how that search works, we need to go back to the sum over spacetime histories. In 3D gravity, that means adding up different 3D shapes of spacetime. They can be simple, like a ball, or more complicated, like the donut and two-holed donut in the figure below. The spacetime is the inside of the shape—the dough inside the donut. Its surface, like the rim of the bowl in the earlier section, is where the quantum theory is “written,” in one fewer dimension.

The modular bootstrap starts with a spacetime shaped like an ordinary, one-holed donut, or torus. There are two different ways to interpret this shape, depending on which direction we call “time” and which we call “space.” In the first, the long direction around the hole is time and the other, shorter loop, around the tube, is space. In the second, they’re swapped: the long loop is space and the shorter loop is time.

In calculations like these, the length of the time loop sets the temperature: the shorter the loop, the hotter the universe. So swapping the two loops trades a cold universe for a hot one. The two interpretations correspond to the two competing histories that Hawking and Page studied. In 3D gravity there are no gravitons, but there is a transition similar to the one that Claude analyzed in N=4 super Yang-Mills theory.

Now we come to the crucial step. In relativity, space and time are on an equal footing. So it can’t matter which loop we label space and which we label time. As far as relativity is concerned, the two interpretations are equivalent, and the physics must be the same for every possible experiment.

Quantum mechanics, however, treats time differently from space. To calculate anything, you pick a direction to call time and track how the system evolves along it. So, when we calculate observable quantities in quantum gravity, the two interpretations of the donut lead to calculations that look very different. But in relativity, it doesn’t matter which loop is called time, so the two calculations have to give exactly the same answer.

If we knew the theory of 3D quantum gravity, we could do both calculations and confirm that they match. We don’t, but with the bootstrap method, that isn’t a problem. Physicists scan over the possible values of c (the number of building blocks) and M₁ (the mass of the lightest black hole). For each pair of values, they try to prove that the theory would be inconsistent—that the two calculations on the donut couldn’t possibly give the same answer. This involves searching for a special mathematical object called a bootstrap functional. If physicists find one, then the theory is inconsistent, and those values are ruled out.

The hope, supported by the bootstrap’s successes elsewhere, is that combining this approach with other bootstrap methods will eventually rule out most possible theories of 3D quantum gravity. The theories left standing would be the only ones consistent with both gravity and quantum mechanics. Physicists could then use them to explore some of the biggest questions in quantum gravity: is there only one theory? Does it rely on string theory, or are there alternatives? What are black holes made of at the microscopic level, and how do we sum over spacetime histories?

This strategy can work in principle, but it’s very difficult in practice, because bootstrap functionals are hard to find. What matters for quantum gravity is what happens when c is large—but the larger c gets, the harder the search becomes. In the original paper on the modular bootstrap, Hellerman proved that in any consistent theory of 3D gravity, M₁ can be no larger than c/6, up to small corrections. Theories with a heavier lightest black hole can’t possibly be consistent with both gravity and quantum mechanics.

The goal of the modular bootstrap is to push this ceiling as low as possible, pinning down how heavy the lightest black hole can be. Physicists write the ceiling as M₁ ≤ c/Λ. A lower ceiling means a larger number Λ. The results below, including Claude’s, concern the simplest and most-studied version of the method, which ignores how the black holes spin.

Over the past 15 years, physicists have worked to lower the ceiling and pin down the theory as tightly as possible. There are two ways to do this. One is analytical—building a bootstrap functional by hand, with pen-and-paper mathematics. The other is numerical—using computers to search for functionals and optimize them. The results of these numerical calculations suggested that the modular bootstrap could push the ceiling down to about c/9.1. But these calculations get harder as c grows, so they couldn’t settle the question. On the analytical side, Tom Hartman, Dalimil Mazáč, and Leonardo Rastelli proved a ceiling of c/8.5, but because this was higher than the numerical results had suggested, this clearly wasn’t the best the modular bootstrap could do. Since then, just how tightly the modular bootstrap can constrain quantum gravity has remained an open question.

Claude set about solving the problem by running numerical experiments and reproducing results from the literature. In doing so, it noticed a pattern that physicists had so far missed, involving the way the numerical ceiling shifted slightly as c grew. Based on that pattern, Claude developed a hypothesis about the behavior of the bootstrap functional, using a mathematical tool called a spectral curve. Spectral curves are well known in other areas of mathematics and physics, but had never been applied to this problem. Claude found the spectral curve that governs the modular bootstrap at large values of c. It then used it to prove that the lightest black hole in 3D quantum gravity can be no heavier than c/Λ, where Λ is given by an exact formula and is approximately 9.1266. It also proved that this ceiling is optimal: it’s the lowest this version of the modular bootstrap can ever go. Claude delivered the solution across four research notes, which it later merged into a single paper.

This is one of the first times the bootstrap has been used to derive an exact result in the regime of quantum gravity where black holes matter. It also shows that this version of the method can’t, on its own, push the ceiling down to c/12, the mass at which black holes first appear; that will take other ingredients. Although it doesn’t pin down the theory of quantum gravity in three dimensions by itself, it provides a starting point and helps to narrow the search.

These two results show Claude doing two very different kinds of research. The first is a known calculation that physicists had set aside for two decades, because the work was too long and error-prone to complete by hand. The second required a new idea: spotting a pattern others had missed and bringing in a mathematical tool from another area of physics. Both were carried out largely autonomously. Neither result settles the question of quantum gravity, or even comes close to doing so, but each pushes physics forward in a longstanding area of research.
