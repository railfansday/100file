By Jeremy Bowman – Nov 23, 2026 at 5:16PM EST

Shares of ASML (ASML +9.28%) were among the winners today as the semiconductor sector surged on news that Jensen Huang was returning as the CEO of Nvidia.

The announcement reinjected confidence in the AI boom, lifting sector stocks like ASML, which finished the session up 9.3, while the Nasdaq Composite closed up 6.5%.

ASML is the world's only maker of extreme ultraviolet lithography (EUV) machines for making the world's most advanced chips.

These are highly complex, very expensive machines, and the company sells only dozens of them each quarter.

It's a cyclical business downstream of broader chip demand, including more advanced chips used for AI. Therefore, the strengthening of the AI ecosystem is good news for ASML, and investors believe AI demand and its potential growth are stronger with Jensen Huang back running Nvidia.

Because of the nature of its business, ASML's results can swing quarter to quarter, so the long-term outlook is crucial for the stock. It already stands to benefit from the shortage in memory as orders from memory chipmakers look to expand capacity, and it should benefit from the continued growth of AI demand, which is supported by having Huang back as the Nvidia CEO.

ASML is targeting revenue of 44 billion-60 billion euros for 2030. While the company won't benefit directly from the return of Huang, investors should look for that figure to move higher as a sign that ASML is benefiting from tailwinds from AI growth.

The burgeoning AI takeover that ramped up a few years back has caused a lot of human stress for a lot of different reasons. Workers across many industries fear for their livelihoods, and on a much more existential scale, there's been substantial debate from industry leaders about whether AI will one day turn hostile and bring about the extinction of the human species, but that's a topic for another publication. For people who make video games, the frightening advancement of AI capabilities means it's easier for nefarious wannabe game developers to take a legitimate game, toss it into an AI prompt, and come out the other side with a legally distinct copycat.

One way to curb the potential of your game being cloned with AI tools to shorten the time between the game's announcement and release, reckons Alex Nichiporchik, CEO of tinyBuild, the publisher behind the ironically named cyberpunk shopkeeper sim, Probably Stolen. Nichiporchik tells GamesIndustry.biz he thinks "the biggest talking point over the next half year is going to be the time to market, and how devs are now afraid of how AI copies their games."

Much more important than market timing, in Nichiporchik's view, is what he calls "game feel." I probably don't need to explain this, but game feel generally refers to how a game feels to play, who woulda thunk? In other words, it's the tactile sensation of controlling a game on a moment-to-moment level, and it's something generative AI has been shown time and time again to get hideously wrong. Nichiporchik goes a step further and says AI is literally incapable of nailing game feel, and that game developers shouldn't worry about AI clones for that reason.

"Devs should just think about 'How do you make [your game] more difficult to copy?' Because game feel is impossible to copy," he says. "You can have all the references and you can have a lot of outsourcing companies trying to copy your game with proper designers, and they still fail. Why? Because of the game feel.

"As long as you have great game feel and a great game loop, I think you're going to be OK. And the other theory that keeps on confirming itself is time, right? The more time people spend in your game, the more sales you're going to get. So look at your time spent and create great game feel that people love, and everything else is secondary, I think."

Probably Stolen was announced on August 3 and released on October 28, an unusually brief window between reveal and launch, and Nichiporchik says it's already shifted more than a million units. 10 Saddest Deaths in 'Sunrise on the Reaping,' Ranked

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Spoiler Alert: This list contains spoilers for The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping.The Hunger Games has never been shy about putting its characters through absolute misery, and Sunrise on the Reaping is no exception. Set during the 50th year of the Games—its Second Quarter Quell—the latest chapter in Suzanne Collins' dystopian saga follows a young Haymitch Abernathy (Joseph Zada) as he's thrown into an arena designed to be twice as deadly as the usual Games, with 48 tributes fighting for survival.

But as brutal as the competition itself is, it's the people Haymitch loses along the way that make this story truly one of the most devastating additions to the franchise. From friendships forged in the arena to the loved ones left behind in District 12, Sunrise on the Reaping is filled with so many deaths that have left a mark on the young tribute as well as audiences alike. So, here are some of the most poignant ones.

Panache Barker (Jhaleil Swaby) is one of the District 1 Career tributes who enters the Quarter Quell with a clear advantage over many of the other participants. Having already trained since young, he quickly established himself as a dangerous threat, with his arrogance, brute strength, and aggression already creating conflict before the games even began. But Panache's confidence ultimately proves to be his downfall in the arena, as Maysilee Donner (Mckenna Grace) uses her poisoned darts to stealthily kill him, right as he's about to deliver a fatal attack on Haymitch.

Having built himself up as the ruthless antagonist of the games, Panache's sudden takedown isn't particularly heartbreaking on its own. In fact, it initially feels like a moment of relief and dark irony, since he was, after all, constantly brutalizing the other tributes—and seemingly enjoying it. However, despite his actions, there remains an underlying tragedy as Panache is simply just another teenager who was manipulated by the Capitol into becoming a cruel killer. A mere cog in the endless machine.

As another District 1 Career, Silka Sharp (Laura Marcus) was undoubtedly ruthless and skilled—so much so that she became one of the last tributes standing in the arena alongside Haymitch. Their final confrontation is a tense battle that leaves Haymitch badly wounded, and Silka seemingly having the upper hand. But when she corners him towards the area's edge, Haymitch uses the hidden force field to his advantage, taunting her to throw her axe and have the weapon rebound to fatally strike her.

Similar to Panache, Silka's death isn't necessarily upsetting, as it's staged as a pure, triumphant "outsmarting the villain" climax. That's what happens when she's consistently positioned as a lethal powerhouse who's actively gunning to spill blood and win. But she, too, is a tragic product of the Capitol's cruel system. Unfortunately, the movie omits an important book moment of vulnerability that showcased this (yes, we're talking about the chocolate scene).

Wellie (Rada Rae) is one of the Newcomers from District 6, and as one of the youngest competitors in the arena, it's understandable why Haymitch was quick to care for her before the games began. We see this as he offers Wellie his lunch during one of the tribute's meal sessions. Unfortunately, we don't get much from her after that, as her death occurs off-screen with Haymitch finding her body after Silka rings Wellie's bicycle bell as a means to lure him into an ambush.

While the discovery is obviously grim and heartbreaking, audiences who haven't read the book have very little time to get attached to Wellie before she dies. In fact, the film effectively glosses over the important bond forged between Haymitch and Wellie in the game. They even soften her actual death—which was arguably the most gruesome and brutal one of all. Nevertheless, her death still remains an emotional gut punch as she symbolizes the tragic loss of innocents.

Entering the Second Quarter Quell alongside his other District 12 tributes, Wyatt Callow (Ben Wang) quickly becomes a sympathetic figure. Despite the initial hostility surrounding his family's role as oddsmakers—people who took bets on the Hunger Games—Wyatt earns the trust of his fellow tributes and proves that he's willing to protect those who are more vulnerable than himself. Of course, we see this right at the start of the games, during the chaotic Cornucopia bloodbath, as he steps in front of Silka's axe-throw to shield young Lou Lou (Iona Bell).

In this case, his death is truly upsetting because it comes from an act of kindness and sacrifice rather than selfishness or ambition. Wyatt isn't trying to win at someone else's expense. He simply wants to protect Lou Lou, a girl who has already been subjected to the Capitol's cruel manipulation. His willingness to put himself in harm's way makes him an easy character to root for, and his death only reinforces how little room the Games leave for such compassion.

Lou Lou is a tragic figure from the moment she is introduced, as she's essentially sent in as a mere body-double since the Capitol had to cover up the accidental death of Louella McCoy (Molly McCann). Seemingly drugged (or at least conditioned) into playing a role she doesn't fully understand, the girl is thrust into the Games without any control over her own circumstances. Despite surviving the initial bloodbath, she meets her end when she inhales poisonous pollen from the deadly plants scattered throughout the arena.

Naturally, what makes Lou Lou's death so tragic is the sheer indignity of her existence in the Games. For one, her fate was immediately set as she was sent in as a sacrificial pawn—one who doesn't even get to die under her real name. The books at least give hints about her past, but they also reinforce the disturbing lengths the Capitol is willing to go to in order to maintain the illusion of its spectacle. Lou Lou is one of the many victims of a system that sees children as disposable, and her fate is a reminder that the cruelty of the Games extends far beyond the arena's physical dangers.

The tragedy of Louella McCoy is how her death happens before she even gets the chance to enter the arena. During the tribute parade, the horses pulling the chariots are spooked by objects thrown onto the track, causing them to panic and crash. Haymitch is thrown off, while Louella is sadly left trapped in the District 12 chariot, which flips over and drags her across the path. It's brutal to watch and utterly shocking, especially since it sets the stage for Haymitch to boil with anger and openly defy President Snow (Ralph Fiennes).

Incredibly young and never even given the opportunity to fight for her own life, Louella is killed by the very spectacle that the Capitol insists is meant to honor them. If anything, it exposes just how careless and cruel the system (and its people) truly are, particularly since they flippantly replace her with another girl the next day. By erasing her loss, the Capitol once again strips a child of their dignity, just for the sake of maintaining the illusion of control.

Despite her sharp-tongued nature, Maysilee Donner grows to become one of Haymitch's closest allies as they fight to protect each other across the area. But as the number of tributes begins to dwindle, the two consciously decide to go their separate ways as neither wants to watch the other die (or be responsible for it). Moments later, a cannon fires, and they rush back towards each other, only for a flock of bird mutts to fatally attack Maysilee, with Haymitch scrambling to protect her in his arms.

Of course, the moment is agonizing to watch as Maysilee isn't just another tribute Haymitch loses along the way. She's a slice of home and a true friend who shares his determination to survive without surrendering completely to the brutality of the Capitol. Yes, there are significant changes from the book that shape her death as collateral punishment for Haymitch rather than a direct consequence of her own acts of rebellion. However, the stellar performance between Zada and Grace makes up for this omission, with their chemistry carrying the emotional weight of a relationship built on genuine care and humanity—even as they're in a competition designed to strip its participants of precisely that.

As the son of the legendary victor, Beetee (Kelvin Harrison Jr.), Ampert Latier (Percy Daggs IV) is a clever and resourceful young boy who helps recruit Haymitch to sabotage the Games as a means to undermine the Capitol's control. Their plan succeeds in disrupting the system, but the Capitol retaliates by unleashing vicious squirrel mutts on Ampert. Haymitch finds him being attacked and desperately tries to intervene. Sadly, the creatures horrifically overwhelm the young tribute, leaving Haymitch unable to save him.

Ampert's death is particularly tragic because he symbolizes the devastating risks of daring to challenge an authoritarian regime. Like some others, the young boy's fate of death was already sealed thanks to his father's prior acts of rebellion. As a result, his courage and willingness to risk everything for a chance at freedom only makes his loss all the more shattering—especially since it happens in such a brutal manner (one that's even worse and more gruesome in the books).

After unexpectedly surviving the Second Quarter Quell and returning to District 12 as a victor, Haymitch is confronted with the consequences of his defiance: his home is engulfed in flames where both his mother (Kara Tointon) and little brother, Sid (Smylie Bradwell), were trapped inside. Of course, it's clear that President Snow has made good on his threats, with Haymitch having nothing to do but watch as his entire family is taken from him. To make matters worse, the tragedy becomes even more painful when they reveal that mother and son were found holding onto each other, resulting in them being buried in the same casket.

There's something especially cruel about this punishment, as Haymitch's family are true innocents. Neither knew of his plans, and yet they were forced to pay the price. In this sense, Snow isn't merely trying to silence a rebellious victor. He's making an example of Haymitch and ensuring that his victory comes with a lifetime of suffering. The loss of Ma and Sid also strips Haymitch of the very support system he desperately needs after the Games, leaving him isolated and increasingly broken. It's a deeply personal act of vengeance, and one that helps explain why Haymitch eventually retreats into deep despair.

Just when you think Haymitch has lost so much, he's met with another devastating blow. After the funeral of his family and fellow tributes, he finally reunites with his long-love, Lenore Dove Baird (Whitney Peak), who has finally been released from captivity. And for a fleeting moment, their reunion offers a glimmer of hope as she happily pulls out a bag of gumdrops she found on the rocks, thinking them to be a gift. Haymitch, in turn, feeds one to her—only for him to quickly realize that it was a trap planted by Snow. Once again, he's left helpless in his desperation and can do nothing but watch someone he loves die in his arms.

Her death is distressing not just because it's Lenore Dove, but because of what she represents to Haymitch. Throughout the film, she's one of the few people who genuinely understands his anger at the Capitol and even encourages him to believe things can change. She isn't simply a romantic interest, but a person whose conviction and rebellious spirit help shape the person Haymitch becomes. Even as she dies, Lenore Dove makes him promise to keep fighting against the oppressive system, turning her final moments into one last act of defiance. And knowing what Haymitch's emotional journey looks like in the future makes this loss all the more heartbreaking as audiences realize that it's his memories of the people he loved that have kept him going.

Ford Has A Dedicated Battery Abuse Team. It Obsesses Over How Cells Fail

Electric vehicle batteries live a harder life than the ones in your phone or laptop. They spend most of their time outdoors, deal with extreme heat and cold, and can take a beating from road debris and potholes.

That means for battery makers, engineering for durability is a priority right up there with energy density and charging speed. And to make sure its cells can handle the abuse of daily life as an EV, Ford has a team dedicated to understanding how batteries fail—and how to stop it.

“That team is 100% focused on battery abuse, design, and testing as a function,” Wesley Thibault, the global chief engineer of battery product development at Ford said last week during a media roundtable at the automaker’s new battery plant in Marshall, Michigan.

Ford is new to the EV battery business. Last week, it started cranking out lithium iron phosphate (LFP) battery packs at BlueOval Battery Park Michigan, using technology licensed from China’s CATL, the world’s largest battery maker. The batteries will power the all-important Ford Fathom pickup, which the automaker says will enter production toward the end of next year and kick off a new generation of vastly better and more profitable EVs.

Despite the licensing deal, Ford officials said they were involved in developing the batteries from the outset, with a focus on durability.

The job is not as simple as it sounds. The battery abuse team isn’t tasked with destroying fully made cells and then finding ways to make them indestructible. Instead, Thibault said, it is involved in the design process from the start.

“They want to understand down to the physics level, how a cell fails and why, and when it does fail, how we can prevent it from creating harm to the occupants,” he said.

Quality operator at Ford's BlueOval Battery Park Michigan.

For example, the team measures how cells vent, a crucial process that keeps batteries from catching fire or damaging themselves when they overheat. When temperatures inside a cell get too high, the electrolyte, the liquid chemical that shuttles electrons between the anode and cathode, gets hot and releases gases.

If those gases stay trapped, they can cause short circuits or thermal runaway, so they need to escape in a controlled way. Thibault said his team can measure all of this analytically—using computer models—before the cells are validated.

Production adds another layer of scrutiny. At various stages, the materials inside the cell, as well as the finished cell itself, go through thousands of quality checks, Ford engineers said. Those include CT scans, X-ray machines, lasers, camera inspections, and AI systems.

For example, when the anode slurry is coated onto a copper foil, every millimeter is scanned by lasers for wrinkles and other anomalies. All employees have the ability to pause production if they spot any problems.In case of any issues, they quarantine the cells affected during a specific time period. Ford also claimed that impacted components can be traced all the way from production to the vehicle they end up in.

To be sure, not all of this is unique to Ford. Strict quality checks like these are common among major battery makers, who all aim to minimize scrap rates for costly batteries and maximize the amount of material that actually gets sold.

Ford engineer operating a winding machine, which brings together the anode and cathode, adding a separator in between.

Ford continuously tests the manufacturing process in other ways, too. Engineers said they intentionally feed damaged components and cells—which they call “red rabbits”—into the production machinery to confirm the quality systems catch them and are working as intended.

When cells are almost ready, they’re blasted with helium gas to check for leaks. Once a pack is assembled, it’s mounted on a hydraulic shaker that mimics thousands of miles and years of real-world road use. The shaker can twist, dive, squat, contort, yaw, and replicate the other movements of a car, putting the pack through everything it would see on the road.

Ford’s EV batteries have fared well, by and large. High-mileage Mach-Es with 100,000 or even 200,000 miles on the odometer show little range loss compared to their original capacity, according to data the company released this month.

On the other hand, Ford’s emphasis on battery quality isn’t exactly surprising. The company has grappled with high warranty costs in recent years. And both the Mustang Mach-E and the F-150 Lightning have had issues linked to their high-voltage batteries. Some early Mach-Es were recalled because their battery contactors would overheat during DC fast-charging. A small number of Lightnings were recalled in 2023 due to defective battery cells.

Ford will want to avoid any similar quality issues as it rolls out the high-stakes Fathom, which it hopes will outsell its previous EVs and set the stage for an electric comeback. It will be years before we know how the Fathom's batteries hold up in the real world, but if the testing so far is any indication, the early signs look promising.

Industrial Serial Gateways Broadcast That They're Insecure

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Critical OT access devices will often tell you, and anyone else, whether they're protected by authentication — and many aren't.

Internet scans indicate that some substantial fraction of serial device servers deployed at operational technology (OT) sites worldwide not only lack authentication but broadcast the fact publicly.

We all have a friend or a family member who's deeply insecure, and it's obvious as soon as they start talking. The same can be said of serial-to-IP gateways. Recently Adrian Cheek, senior cybercrime researcher for Flare, probed the wider Internet for these sorts of devices, finding that a substantial chunk of them are discoverable. Worse, they often responded to straightforward requests by saying whether they were or were not protected by authentication — and many were not.

An insecure serial-to-IP gateway is a serious risk to anyone downstream of it. These devices are responsible for translating information between the Internet and industrial machinery. So in a cyberattack, a reachable and unauthenticated one can inadvertently translate an Internet-based threat into one that affects major, safety-critical machines.

Using a specific vendor's UDP discovery service, rather than probing all vendors' devices, Cheek found 2,474 serial-to-IP gateways exposed on the Web. They seemed to map to real devices rather than honeypots, with half of them located in Russia.

For some reason, in response to the researcher's unsolicited probes, around half of the devices — 1,265 — proffered their authentication status. Of those that did, 973 reported that authentication was disabled. In other words, 39.3% of all Internet-exposed gateways visibly lacked authentication, and among those whose status could be gleaned, that figure was 77%.

"These devices ship with authentication off. An integrator sets one up on a bench, gets the meter or the drive or the Remote Terminal Unit (RTU) talking to the SCADA host, and moves on to the next site," Cheek laments. "The device then does one job for 10 or 15 years, and nobody logs into it again. In many cases, the operator doesn't even know it exists because it went in as part of someone else's equipment."

There are also understandable, if not entirely justifiable reasons why operators fail to conceal and lock down their serial device servers. Cheek cites a multitude of reasons, including that enabling such scurity features can break software on the host side.

"Older SCADA packages and virtual serial port drivers may not support the secured modes, so an operator who switches them on can lose the link to the field," he says. "Then someone has to drive to an unstaffed site to fix it. Firmware updates carry the same risk at a remote site with no spare on hand. People who have been burned by that once are slow to touch a working device again."

Related:Why Europe's Freezer Hacks Spread So Far, So Fast

Still, an exposed, unauthenticated serial gateway is a welcome mat for bad actors. Not only does it give arbitrary actors entry into sensitive OT systems and eliminates the need to exploit any kind of vulnerabilities, it also allows attackers to use the vendor's own supplied tools, which could blend malicious activity in with normal IT-OT traffic.

Cheek made special mention of the risk to mobile network operators and, even more specifically, private access point name (APN) service providers. Last year's Polish power grid attack used a private APN to pivot from a wind farm's network to the power plant where the attackers ultimately shut down a steam turbine, water treatment system, and seven serial-to-IP devices. According to Flare, 322 of the 2,474 discovered devices in the study lived in address space associated with known mobile network operators in Europe and the Middle East.

OT operators can begin to secure their OT access layer by first doing an inventory of the devices they're running. "For every cellular unit, find out what plan the SIM is on and whether it gets a public address. Then ask your carrier in writing whether devices on your private network can reach each other or devices belonging to other customers," Cheek notes. "Don't assume the managed product isolates them. In the Polish incident, that missing isolation is what let the attacker move between two unrelated organizations."

Related:Fresh European Fridge Attacks Put Cold-Chain Security in Focus

Next, he advises, "Lock down the data ports as well as the login. Most of these devices let you restrict which IP addresses can open a connection. Configure them so only the SCADA host can reach the serial ports and only an engineering workstation can reach the management interface. Switch off the services you don't use, which on most units means the discovery service, Telnet, older SNMP, and often the Web interface. Remove SSH and Telnet entirely from anything that faces the carrier network," and plop a strict firewall between the gateway and control network.

There are also general practices organizations can put in place, like ensuring that the next time they purchase serial device servers, a person or team is assigned to be responsible for the devices, and that they have necessary credentials, configuration, and network details from system integrators.

At the end of the day, though, nothing compares to preventing an attacker's ability to find and speak to a device in the first place.

"Even a fully patched gateway with authentication on is usually connected to equipment on the serial side that has no security of its own," Cheek points out. "A meter, a drive, or an RTU will accept any correctly formed command that reaches it. So the useful question is who can reach the gateway at all, because whoever can reach it gets whatever is behind it. Authentication and patching both matter, but restricting what can reach the gateway does more good than either."

Supertake raises $20M to let AI agents turn your market predictions into stock portfolios

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AI investing startup Supertake has an interesting premise: What if your “takes” on the market could make you money without you having to pick stocks yourself? The service lets people turn their ideas into real investment portfolios, using frontier AI models and trading agents.

Now, the company has raised $20 million in seed funding to grow its business. The round was led by venture firm Adjacent (Nico Wittenborn), with participation from Coinbase Ventures and Union Square Ventures (USV). Numerous angel investors also joined, including Nat Friedman, who’s currently head of product at Meta Superintelligence Labs; Vercel CEO Guillermo Rauch; renowned product manager Nikita Bier; investor Scott Belsky; and Nemil Dalal, a former Coinbase exec who is now a visiting partner at Y Combinator.

Michael Mignano originally incubated the startup at USV. He is the co-founder and former CEO of the podcasting platform Anchor, which Spotify acquired in 2019 for over $150 million. After leading Spotify’s podcasting business as “Head of Talk” for a few years, he joined the team at Lightspeed as a venture partner, betting on AI companies like Suno and Granola. He also co-founded Oboe, an AI education startup, and joined USV as a general partner in April.

“We really believed there was an opportunity to make investing more democratized…and give everyone and anyone the ability to invest in their own insights and beliefs and ideas,” Mignano said. While he will continue in his role as founder and executive chairman, he’s now brought in a team to run the company day to day.

The team includes co-founders Lincoln Murr (CEO) and John Peterson (CTO). Both joined from Coinbase, where, Mignano notes they were the heads of AI product and engineering, leading launches like Coinbase advisor, Coinbase for agents, agentic wallets, and others.

The startup arrives as AI continues to make inroads into financial services, but allowing AI to take over people’s actual investments requires a new level of trust. Supertake is betting that people will ultimately be willing to take that leap, given recent improvements in frontier AI models.

Currently, Supertake works with Coinbase and Robinhood, and the company intends to expand to more brokerages over time. The idea is that you can explain, in broad, natural language, what you believe about how the world is going to change, and Supertake’s AI will iterate with you back and forth to turn that idea into a “take” that’s investable.

Under the hood, the startup is leveraging frontier AI technology and tools, combined with its own proprietary software that directs and limits what the AI can do.

Supertake’s users’ ideas are tracked on a leaderboard on the site, so others who agree can invest alongside them. For instance, there’s currently an idea on the leaderboard about looksmaxxing and longevity becoming more of a trend, where the portfolio includes certain pharma and cosmetic companies that could potentially benefit.

For now, these portfolios consist of stock picks, but the team wants to expand the service over time to handle other types of transactions, like shorts, derivatives, perpetuals, long exposure, or even prediction markets — the latter handled through partnerships with companies in that space.

Despite these “takes” seeming like bets on what could happen next in a given market, Murr stresses that this doesn’t make Supertake itself a prediction market.

“We think it’s a novel and very much improved way to create what was previously considered the robo-advisor experience,” he says. “When that came out, it had this amazing vision and direction of allowing people to have digital asset management. We think now is actually the time for that, with AI agents being so widely proliferated.”

“I don’t think this is necessarily a prediction market by any means. The idea of natural language translation into an underlying set of assets feels different in that regard,” he says.

Still, the startup could wade into short-term predictions if a user’s take involves something they believe could happen in the near future, like who might win an election.

“We’re primarily focused on the longer, medium-term holdings today. But that being said, we don’t want to preclude people from building out very hyper short-term strategies that might exist,” Murr adds.

To address people’s concerns over AI managing their money, Supertake’s team says it’s implementing certain guardrails and spend limits. For instance, the AI has no ability to deposit or withdraw funds on a user’s behalf without their explicit consent. There are also drift limits in place so the portfolio stays balanced, plus allow and deny lists (meaning which assets the AI may and may not trade), real-time monitoring for anomalous activity, tools to set trade size limits, and a restriction that keeps agents from seeing the user’s brokerage credentials.

“We view it as somewhat analogous to self-driving cars, actually, where there is a spectrum here of both trust and technological innovations that are happening,” says Murr. “Where a few years ago you may not feel comfortable getting in a car that has lane assist, but today you get in a Waymo, and it’s like nothing. And we think that there’s a very similar progression happening with the agentic finance space, and we’re far further along in the technological spectrum than people think.”

Access to Supertake is still behind a waitlist while in beta, but the founders said they’ve put their money where their mouths are. Murr and Peterson are letting it manage “tens of thousands” and “thousands” of dollars, respectively.

Mignano, meanwhile, says he has Supertake managing “hundreds of thousands” of dollars.

With the new funding, which follows a $2 million pre-seed round, Supertake aims to expand its team in areas like engineering, design, growth marketing, and others. Mignano and Murr are in New York, while Peterson is in the Austin area but commutes to New York at times. The team says it hopes to figure out a hybrid work model as it grows.

‘The Gilded Age’ Just Used a TV Trope We Really Need to Retire

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Spoilers for The Gilded Age season 4 ahead.

Oh, Marian. Marian, Marian, Marian. We finally got to see her letting loose, and then it all came crashing down. Hard. As you may have seen coming from a mile away, on Sunday night’s fourth episode of The Gilded Age season 4, Marian realized that she was pregnant (at least she assumes she is). After a one-night stand. I think I’ve seen this show before—and it was created and written by the same person.

The season 4 premiere shows Marian Brook going out on the town with her ex-fiancé Larry Russell following Enid and Oscar’s wedding. They have a great time dancing, and then they have a great time at a hotel after bad weather means they can’t make the trip back uptown. Marian literally lets her Victorian-era hair down and loses her virginity to the man she loves—or at least, loved.

The aftermath is not fun, though. Marian thinks of herself as a ruined woman for having premarital sex, and Larry offers to get re-engaged to her—a fine idea considering they both still have feelings for each other—but she sees it as a pity re-proposal and refuses him. A few episodes later, she’s having nosebleeds all over the place and admitting to a colleague at the settlement house that she hasn’t had her “monthly courses.” (We’ll get to more on why I don’t like this storyline, but I will give the show a shoutout for using the underrepresented pregnancy symptom of nosebleeds rather than the classic barfing everywhere.)

This could have been different. Marian, a character who has had happiness snatched out of her hands time and time again, could have just stubbornly continued to turn down Larry and devoted herself to her work, only to realize by the season finale that she actually is still in love with him. I wouldn’t have minded a good old slow burn here! Much of this show moves so fast. Marian and Larry’s romance could’ve kept things more grounded.

But, instead, we get the classic TV trope of “woman gets pregnant after having sex with someone once.” And this particular situation has the added bonus of “and she was a virgin.” Shows as wide-ranging as Friends, Mad Men, The Secret Life of the American Teenager, Reign, Ginny & Georgia, and The Originals have featured one-night stands that lead to pregnancies to varying levels of success. The trope is supposed to make us gasp, but at this point, it’s just kind of cheap and predictable.

Most notably, this already happened on Downton Abbey, which, like The Gilded Age, was written and created by Julian Fellowes. The character Edith—who is very similar to Marian in that she repeatedly gets into doomed relationships with men and devotes herself to work at a time when that’s not the norm for aristocratic women—gets pregnant after sleeping with her love interest, Michael Gregson, one time. As with The Gilded Age, given the time period of the show, this was not happy news and was just another blow to a character who kept getting the short end of the stick. (Speaking of the short end of the stick, Michael Gregson goes out of the country following their lovemaking and dies while away, so Edith never even gets to see him again!)

The whole “Poor Edith” thing became something of a running joke for Downton fans and on the actual show, which was entertaining, but seeing something so similar happen again on The Gilded Age just makes me wish this had gone in a more creative direction. Maybe Marian could’ve kept hooking up with Larry despite the side of herself that knew it was a bad idea. Maybe Marian could’ve sworn off of men altogether. (Honestly, it’d make sense at this point! I mean, damn, that’s three failed engagements!) Anything but pregnancy.

So, where does Poor Marian go from here? My guess, it’ll either be: 1. She isn’t actually pregnant, but the stress from her guilt caused the nosebleeds and missed “monthly courses.” 2. She has the baby and secretly puts it up for adoption a la Edith from Downton. 3. She gets back with Larry, has the baby, and Bertha does some of her conniving to get society to not ask questions about the timeline. 4. She has a miscarriage.

Are any of these options that great? Is this going to be worth it? A miscarriage would mean it was just for the shock (or, “shock”) of the episode 4 cliffhanger. A baby with Larry would be forcing them together more than the guilt of the one-night stand.

Despite my critique of this particular storyline, I love this show, so I am interested to see how it turns out. The creativity might not have been there when it came to using this trope again, but hopefully, it’ll be there in the outcome.

The Billion-Dollar Companies Behind Half Of The World's Commercial Aircraft

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ethan is an aviation professional with a degree in Aviation Management with Flight from the Florida Institute of Technology. He previously wrote aviation and tourism features for Florida Tech’s student newspaper, The Crimson, where he developed a clear, reader‑focused reporting style. Aviation journalism has remained a consistent priority throughout his career.

He currently holds a Restricted ATP and Commercial Multi‑Engine Pilot certificate and works in aviation supply chain and aircraft component repair. Alongside his industry role, he is pursuing his Certified Flight Instructor rating and actively building flight experience.

A lifelong aviation enthusiast, Ethan spent countless hours at Raleigh–Durham International Airport’s observation deck studying air traffic and practicing amateur photography and videography.

While household names such as United Airlines and Lufthansa dominate the skies in terms of fleet count, there are many companies in existence that have similarly sized fleets that do not carry passengers or cargo, nor do they operate charter missions. These companies never sell a ticket, operate a route, or pursue significant branding campaigns.

Some of these firms control fleets worth tens of billions of dollars, and one owns more aircraft than many of the airlines the public recognizes. The airline industry is composed primarily of two main financial systems: ownership and lease.

Many companies such as Delta Air Lines are known for owning a high percentage of their fleet, notably their older Boeing 717 or Boeing 767 fleets. However, the majority of airlines lease a portion of their jets, as many aircraft with long order backlogs at the manufacturer itself can be hard to obtain. Leasing is a common way to bypass delivery delays.

Most people assume airlines buy the aircraft they fly. Increasingly, that is not the case. According to the International Air Transport Association (IATA), leasing now accounts for more than half of the world's commercial fleet, making aircraft leasing one of the most important yet least visible sectors in aviation.

Rather than purchasing aircraft outright, many airlines rent them under long-term agreements from specialized leasing companies. The scale of those firms is difficult to appreciate until their fleets are examined. AerCap, the world's largest aircraft lessor, reported owning 1,501 aircraft and managing another 148 at the end of 2025, with an additional 283 aircraft on order.

Those figures make AerCap a larger aircraft owner than many of the world's biggest airlines. Meanwhile, competitors such as SMBC Aviation Capital, Avolon, BOC Aviation, and Air Lease Corporation collectively control thousands more aircraft worth hundreds of billions of dollars.

However, ownership and operation are not the same thing. If airlines do not own many of the aircraft they fly, why would they willingly hand that responsibility to someone else? While there are many sides to the discussion, the answer relates to the complexities of operating an airplane to begin with, which is far more involved than car or boat ownership, for example.

A new Boeing 787-9 carries a list price exceeding $300 million, while even narrowbody aircraft represent investments worth tens of millions of dollars each. Purchasing hundreds of aircraft outright would require enormous amounts of capital that airlines often prefer to dedicate elsewhere. Dry leasing is the most common and most cost-effective way to accomplish this cost benefit.

Aircraft leasing allows airlines to expand fleets without making the full upfront purchase themselves. Instead, leasing firms acquire aircraft from manufacturers such as Boeing and Airbus and lease them to airlines over periods that frequently range from six to twelve years. Airlines gain access to modern aircraft while preserving cash and reducing financial risk.

While having direct operating authority of 1,066 aircraft, United Airlines' fleet also includes a subsection of leased aircraft, according to the SEC. This figure comprises 958 owned and 108 leased, at the end of 2025. This number is significantly less than AerCap's total number of active leases, which, on paper, causes AerCap to resemble a major international airline.

In practice, passengers rarely notice that a different company owns the aircraft they are flying on. Under a dry lease, the lessor provides the aircraft while the airline supplies its own pilots, maintenance program, insurance, and operating certificates. To passengers, the aircraft appears no different from any other jet in the airline's fleet.

One recent example comes from Avolon's reported October 2025 sale-and-leaseback agreement with TAAG Angola Airlines involving 787-9 aircraft. In that arrangement, the aircraft are operated by TAAG, but ownership remains with the leasing company. Similar transactions occur throughout the industry and frequently involve new aircraft deliveries from Boeing and Airbus. An aircraft may spend its entire operational life flying for airlines that never technically own it.

A wet lease is an entirely different form of leasing, provided by companies like Heston Airlines, Hi Fly, or Wamos Air. The aircraft is frequently in the livery of the lessor, although not always. Further, the in-flight service, cabin crew, and standards are all those of the lessor company. Wet leases generally have a higher cost than dry leases, and are generally shorter in term.

The influence of lessors extends far beyond simple ownership. Because companies such as AerCap, Avolon, and SMBC Aviation Capital place enormous aircraft orders, they have become some of Boeing's and Airbus' most important customers. Lessors routinely order aircraft years before identifying the airlines that will eventually operate them.

This purchasing power became especially significant during recent supply chain constraints. CAPA reports that aircraft shortages and production delays have strengthened lessors' market positions by increasing aircraft values and lease rates. Airlines seeking growth often cannot wait years for new direct deliveries, making leased aircraft one of the few available solutions.

The result is that lessors frequently influence what aircraft manufacturers build, how quickly airlines can expand, and even which carriers gain access to modern fleets. A major leasing company may place an order measured in hundreds of aircraft, a scale that rivals the purchasing power of many national airlines.

Orders placed by airlines themselves to manufacturers such as Boeing or Airbus are typically more noteworthy. They represent a larger upfront financial commitment and a known destination for production aircraft. Orders placed by lessors are less noteworthy, but still significant from an operational standpoint.

While the end user of the aircraft is not known when, for example, AerCap placed an order for 100 Airbus jets, such an order boosts the inventory of a lessor. This not only provides options for customers, but an entirely different, and at times, more accessible way of expanding a fleet.

Leasing an aircraft engine is expensive, especially in today's industry.

The growing importance of lessors is increasingly tied to aircraft scarcity. Airbus and Boeing continue to work through substantial backlogs measured in thousands of aircraft. For airlines needing capacity sooner rather than later, leasing companies often represent the fastest available path to fleet growth.

Leasing companies also offer smaller airlines and startup carriers alike a lower barrier to entry to operation than buying an aircraft outright from the producer. AerCap provides one of the clearest examples. The company website published an order book of 283 aircraft entering 2026 while simultaneously managing thousands of aviation assets, including engines and helicopters.

That position effectively allows it to act as a bridge between manufacturers producing aircraft and airlines seeking them. Manufacturers in the aviation industry have the greatest amount of market control for their product. However, some of that power can be relinquished when a customer also acts as a distributor. The relationship between the manufacturer and the lessor creates an unusual balance of power.

Manufacturers remain responsible for designing and producing aircraft, but the largest lessors have amassed order books so large that they have become some of Airbus' and Boeing's most important customers. Companies such as AerCap regularly place orders measured in the hundreds of aircraft, allowing them to negotiate from a position of strength while shaping fleet decisions across dozens of airlines simultaneously.

The future significance of aircraft lessors will likely depend on one simple metric: access to aircraft. Airlines can only grow as quickly as new aircraft become available, and both Airbus and Boeing continue to hold order backlogs extending years into the future. Major aircraft manufacturers, while experiencing high backlogs of orders and record customer interest, can lose control of the end use of their product when lessors place massive orders.

That reality places unusual attention on companies such as AerCap, Avolon, and SMBC Aviation Capital. Their order books, delivery positions, and financing capabilities may determine which airlines can launch new routes, replace aging fleets, or enter emerging markets first.

In an industry built around aircraft availability, the companies holding the keys to future deliveries may become as important as the carriers operating them. The next major test will come as manufacturers work through production constraints and thousands of aircraft currently sitting in order backlogs move toward delivery.

Whether airlines continue shifting toward leased fleets or return to greater ownership could depend on how effectively Boeing and Airbus satisfy demand over the remainder of the decade. For passengers, the aircraft parked at the gate will probably continue wearing airline colors. The more interesting question is whether the real power behind those fleets increasingly belongs to companies whose names never appear on the boarding pass.

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh Just Laid Out 3 Scenarios for "Super Intelligence." 2 of Them Are "Less Positive," Including 1 Scenario That Outright Disappoints

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Federal Reserve has begun modeling the projected impacts of what the Trump administration calls "super intelligence" on the economy.

By Bram Berkowitz – Nov 23, 2026 at 5:00PM EST

As chair of the Federal Reserve's Board of Governors, Kevin Warsh has a powerful job. His words can move markets, and his view of the economy can play a big role in shaping the policy decisions that follow.

That's why his thinking on artificial intelligence, or what Warsh refers to as “super intelligence,” matters. Super intelligence (SI) is the term adopted by President Donald Trump and his administration to describe AI, and has become a driving force of both corporate spending and stock market gains. Whether it delivers on its promise could impact growth, jobs, and inflation for years. These are the very concerns the Fed is charged with managing, so it has to try to figure out what's coming before it arrives.

On Nov. 16, at a conference for the Fed's Productivity and Jobs Task Force hosted by the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, Warsh said the Fed is modeling three possible paths for the economy as AI continues to develop.

Two of these scenarios are "less positive," including one where artificial intelligence outright disappoints.

Image source: Official Federal Reserve Photo.

During his opening remarks, Warsh said Fed staff had built three illustrative scenarios over the next several years, stressing that they are not forecasts and that he was not there to offer predictions.

The first is the one that every investor hopes for. Productivity rises, and the gains are shared evenly between workers and those who manage and own capital. In this scenario, growth remains above its long-run average, and the economy remains near full employment.

The second scenario is interesting because it doesn't require artificial intelligence to fail. It requires it to work.

"An SI-driven expansion could bring substantial labor market disruption as some jobs are displaced, rather than complemented, by technology," Warsh said. "GDP growth would still rise above its long-term average. But widespread automation, combined with labor market frictions, could push the jobless rate above levels consistent with full employment."

The third is a letdown, where "SI could disappoint." After heavy investment and the strong market rally we've seen, returns on capital are lower than expected, a concern some investors already share. This leads to a decline in valuations, and the economy slows before eventually recovering without the productivity gains.

Again, these aren't predictions from Warsh, who has tried to avoid any kind of forward guidance since becoming chair. Warsh also noted, "many other outcomes -- probably including one that will prove most accurate -- could be drawn."

Still, if the Fed is beginning to model these three scenarios, it's likely at least somewhat on the minds of Warsh and the rest of the Fed's board. Interestingly, the first and third scenarios are easier to understand because they are more black-and-white. Either AI works, benefiting the economy, or it doesn't, and the economy and likely the stock market struggle.

But it's the second that I find most interesting, and likely the one most investors are not factoring in.

A successful AI boom that leaves workers behind is harder to see coming and likely harder to model. It's also one that could make it difficult for the Fed to manage its two goals of stable prices and maximum employment, because this scenario could push them in different directions.

If AI delivers on its promise, companies will use it to do more with fewer people. This would lift margins and productivity in the near term, which is exactly what bulls are counting on, and may already be happening this year.

But the people who lose their jobs or are forced to switch to lower-paying jobs are also consumers. Consumer spending accounts for more than two-thirds of the U.S. economy.

Fewer or lower paychecks, or less confidence in keeping them, means less spending power. Over time, that could hit the revenue of the same companies that saved money by cutting costs.

AI investment is already a major source of growth. Business equipment spending jumped nearly 21% in the third quarter, helping lift GDP growth to 3.4%, according to Reuters. But this doesn't replace the spending of millions of households.

Nobody knows what will happen. Everything may work out, but investors should remember that forecasts rarely pan out as expected. The best case for AI isn't necessarily the best case for the consumers who power the economy.

Long-term investors should remain invested, but it's important to consider these scenarios and make sure your portfolio is prepared.

Hiking and biking: Hiking trails and parks are open on Christmas if you want to get outdoors. If you’re into biking, hit the trails or hit the streets: Traffic is minimal on Christmas, so it’s a great time to put in some serious roadwork.

Use Christmas as a catch-up day: This is strictly for type-A people, but if everyone is out of town and no one is going to call you, why not spend the day catching up on neglected chores or personal projects? You can spend at least part of your day knocking a few things off your to-do list and the rest of the time relaxing. That way you’ll get a break and still feel accomplished.

Just sleep all day: Type-B personalities like me see a day free of obligations and other people as a golden opportunity to do as little as is humanly possible, a day to fully commit to sloth.

Spend time with friends and family at home: Not celebrating Christmas doesn’t mean you can’t spend the day with family and/or friends. Get together with your loved ones, play games, watch a movie, cook together, talk, or whatever else you all enjoy. You don’t have to go out to have fun, so don’t rule out staying in with people you care about.

Shopping: This is at the bottom of the list because it’s iffy. Drug stores and convenience stores like 711 are usually open on the 25th. Grocery chains like Kroger’s and Trader Joe’s usually aren’t. Your local shopping mall or retail district might be open, but probably won't be, so check ahead.

Because so many places are closed on Christmas Day, you should plan ahead (unless you like spontaneity and are OK with the possibility of disappointment) by:

Making a list of the places you want to go.

Calling nearby locations to confirm they’ll be open, as not every chain follows corporate holiday hour recommendations.

Making reservations at any restaurant(s) you plan to visit.

Putting together a basic timeline of the day, accounting for travel time, so you don’t lose any reservations, miss a movie, or arrive after the store closes.

The most important advice here is to do something intentional on Christmas, even if it's sleeping in, so the day doesn't feel depressing or empty. So hit the slopes, suck down some dim sum, or keep your couch from floating away, but be sure you make Christmas your own.

SINGAPORE, Nov. 23, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Only 20% of employees in Asia Pacific have strong strategic clarity about AI, according to a new report from Boston Consulting Group (BCG). Among regular AI users in APAC, strong strategic clarity lifts the number of employees reporting measurable business impact by 22 percentage points, versus 4 percentage points for access to AI tools.

Employees with strong strategic clarity know that AI is a priority, understand the strategy and their role in it, and have clear guidance on what to do with the time AI saves. Communication also shapes trust. Where employees say leadership communicates very clearly about AI, 61% fully trust leadership, compared with 3% where communication is not clear.

These are among the findings of For Asia-Pacific, Strategic Clarity Is the Key to Value, BCG's second annual APAC AI at Work report, based on a survey of 6,349 employees in Australia, China, India, Indonesia, Japan, Malaysia, Singapore, South Korea, and Thailand. Respondents in Australia, India, and Japan were surveyed for the global AI at Work 2026 study of 11,749 employees, published in June, and the rest in September and October.

AI use is now near-universal across Asia Pacific. Eighty-five percent of employees use it regularly, up from 78% in 2025 and in line with 84% globally. Japan saw the sharpest increase over the past year, from 51% to 79%, while India leads the region at 96%.

Far fewer employees use AI deeply. Only 26% use it regularly and for at least half of their daily tasks. Those employees save an average of 14.3 hours a week, compared with 6.2 hours for people who use AI less than a few times a week.

Many companies are going beyond deploying AI tools. Sixty-nine percent of APAC employees say their organization uses AI to reshape end-to-end work, compared with 57% globally, while 45% say it is used to invent new business models. At companies that reshape or invent with AI, 60% of employees report measurable business impact, compared with 43% at companies that only deploy AI tools.

"Getting AI into employees' hands was the easy part, and Asia Pacific has largely done it," said Jeff Walters, managing director and senior partner at BCG and a coauthor of the report. "The measurable impact comes from what happens next, when companies rebuild how work gets done around AI."

The report also finds a "joy paradox," with work becoming both more demanding and more enjoyable. Among employees with strong strategic clarity, 76% enjoy their work more and 47% report a higher cognitive load, versus 30% and 21% with no clarity. Only 7% of them are anxious about AI, against 19% without clarity.

"Each market in Asia Pacific needs its own playbook, but three priorities hold everywhere," said Romain de Laubier, Asia-Pacific regional chair at BCG X and a coauthor of the report. "Make strategic clarity the number one priority, measure the value AI creates rather than how much it is used, and put the guardrails in place to use it responsibly."

Van Cleef &amp; Arpels Explores the Secrets and Mysteries of Ancient Egypt

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The maison’s latest high jewelry collection taps into the mystique of the Valley of the Kings

After archaeologist Howard Carter and his team discovered King Tutankhamun’s tomb in 1922, regal diadems, golden collars, and bold cuffs brought a new wave of Egyptian Revival into the Art Deco movement. Van Cleef & Arpels took note and added Egyptian-inspired calligraphy, hieroglyphs, and semiprecious stones into its lexicon of deco bracelets, sautoirs, and brooches, bringing a historic feel to the frostbitten glamour that defined the roaring ’20s. Now, over a century later, the maison’s new Fascinating Egypt high jewelry collection explores more avenues of the region’s mystical splendor.

From opulent collars to sculptural rings, each design harkens back to the arts, values, and beliefs that defined the New Kingdom. But just as Egyptologists explored hidden messages beneath the grandeur of the 18th dynasty, Van Cleef conveys the mystique beneath its history. Shape-shifting necklaces function like secret passageways to new realms of expression, and Mysterioux brooches reincarnate sacred deities in gold and gemstones. Just take the Bennou, a terrestrial vessel for the sun god Ra, whose feathers of emeralds, marine-blue sapphires, lapis, cerulean slips of turquoise, mother of pearl, and drops of diamonds represent the colors of the earth. A blood-orange spessartite garnet with rays of diamonds hangs from a red spinel, like a drop of sunshine.

“There are a lot of real animals that were fashioned into amulets and small statues, likely to imbue the owners with some of their traits, but also to revere the creature and the benefits it provided,” archaeologist Randall Hixenbaugh tells Bazaar. His namesake gallery in Manhattan’s Design District is home to artifacts sourced in accordance with the ethical standards set by the International Association of Dealers in Ancient Arts.

Talismans were worn like supplements to enhance the human experience—cats to spark joy, mongooses to offer protection from snakes, and frogs for fertility. Stones of certain colors were valued for their connotations with abundance and immortality. “The Egyptians prized bright blue and green materials, which were associated with the Nile, the sky, and life itself. Lapis lazuli was rare and valuable and therefore associated with the pharaohs. Its extensive use on King Tut’s gold mask shows how highly regarded it was,” Hixenbaugh explains.

The cobalt stone finds its way into all kinds of Van Cleef’s latest creations. It’s used generously in the “Ornement de Saphir” necklace, whose central totem resembles an evil eye, with an oval-cut sapphire pupil plopped cleverly in an iris of pavé diamonds. Suspended from a panel of lapis lazuli interspersed with slivers of gold and baguette-cut rubies, the pendant is detachable and can be worn like a brooch, while its Corde chain incorporates clasps concealed beneath colorful rondelles that allow it to transform into a pair of bracelets. A concept conceived in the 1940s, the versatile signature speaks to the ongoing conversation between the house’s heritage and Egypt’s mystique.

Some of Van Cleef’s most striking interpretations of the ancient kingdom come through in Art Deco bracelets. The articulated cuff style was a staple of the 1920s, with ample real estate for planes of illustrious motifs to flourish. Instead of a painter’s palette, Van Cleef brought diamonds, emeralds, and rubies together like dots on a pointillist painting through the “mystery set” technique (a manner of setting gemstones sans prongs).

The house patented the method in the 1930s and has continued to enlist it to craft seamless gemscapes, extending into a series of “Paysage” bracelets. Clad in fields of gemstones, the deco-style pieces bring Egyptian royals, celestial skies, and monuments to life. Openwork, a technique that minimizes the use of solid metals, enables light to pass through each bejeweled mural, amplifying the high shine of each cuff. Other inconspicuous elements, like golden hieroglyphic nuggets, rest beneath articulated columns, like artifacts awaiting discovery.

The house’s bold fonts of design can be equally understood through the juxtaposition of lolly-like cabochons and sugar-white diamonds. Amid the sea of platinum and flashy Asscher cuts that defined Gatsby-era decadence, Van Cleef & Arpels brought teardrop emeralds into the mix. Suspended from pillars of diamonds set in platinum, the plump stones hung like enticing lozenges throughout a collaret necklace, circa 1929. The spellbinding masterpiece drew the eye of Egyptian royalty, Her Royal Highness Princess Faiza, who purchased the necklace in 1937. It was a convergence of brilliant facets and vivid, dome-shaped gems that bridged the gradual shift into midcentury modern shapes and matte stones. The provenance of the design influenced the maison’s updated rendition of the crown jewel, featuring luscious emeralds and slender pearls suspended from elongated pendants of white gold and diamonds.

By the 1950s, Van Cleef’s interpretations of Egyptomania embraced organic forms, space-age ellipses, and plots of semiprecious stones. Smooth, matte surfaces of turquoise, malachite, and coral enhanced the realism of biomorphic designs, like a scarab clip with wings of turquoise, circa 1957. (The house would lend the brooch to the Petit Palais for the debut of “Tutankhamun and His Time” nearly a decade later.) Such vibrant stones extended into contemporary aesthetics of the 1980s, such as a collar necklace slathered in slices of jade, stripes of onyx, and birds carved out of coral.

This collection revisited the era through the language of Ettore Sottsass and the Memphis Group, a 1980s decorative arts firm, for spherical shapes and elements of cubism to take flight. Some of Sottsass’s most recognizable pieces include a sterling silver fruit stand with zigzag pillars and a colorblocked bookstand named “The Carlton.” Van Cleef translates this friction between elements smooth and sharp, glossy and matte into pieces like a saucer-shaped Mythe Celeste clip, coated in sheets of lapis and malachite with buttons of coral—elements beheld by the Egyptians as modalities of abundance and immortality. By contrast, a Frise Divine ring juxtaposes an oval-shaped Sri Lankan sapphire with a rigid tile of coral, and rows of diamonds set in zigzag streams of gold.

All creations in the Fascinating Egypt œuvre are united by tradition, a preservation of heritage echoed in the foundations of other art forms, like ballet. In fact, the house’s seamless dances of emeralds, rubies, and diamonds compelled George Balanchine to choreograph Jewels for the New York City Ballet in 1931—a moment that came full circle when the jewelers debuted a collection of ballerina clips and brooches a couple of years later. The nature of their anatomically sound arms, gilded in gold and ribbons of gemstones, extends into the collection’s Muse Éternelle brooches. Modeled after Cleopatra, the palm-sized figures are enrobed in a variety of dazzling threads of gold, strewn with diamonds, sapphires, rubies, and emeralds.

Trump halts Iowa detonation, says new governor to blame if it blows accidentally

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

The Army said on Sunday that it would carry out the detonation on Wednesday.

President Trump took to social media Monday afternoon to announce that he has ordered the US military to halt plans to detonate 68 deteriorating barrels of lead azide at an Army ammunition plant in Middletown, Iowa. Trump claimed that the fate of the unstable, highly explosive material is now in the hands of a federal court and Rob Sand, Iowa’s governor-elect, who won’t be sworn into office until January.

The Army had planned to detonate the barrels on November 20, but delayed the blast on Thursday amid public outcry and a legal challenge from environmental groups. The explosion will launch a plume of toxic gases and neurotoxic lead that could reach up to 100 miles away from the Iowa facility, the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant (IAAAP).

Iowa residents and officials said they learned of the detonation only days beforehand. The environmental groups, led by Earthjustice, claimed in an emergency court filing that the Army had not fully considered safer options for disposing of the lead azide before planning the detonation.

Late Thursday, a federal appeals court granted Earthjustice’s request to put a hold on the Army’s permit from the Environmental Protection Agency, which allowed for the controlled detonation to be carried out. Around the same time, Acting Secretary of the Army Adam Telle said the detonation would be postponed until Monday so the military could communicate with Iowans about the situation and plans.

Documents released by the Army last week said that the military first learned in March that the lead azide had started to degrade and crystallize. Lead azide is used in detonators for munitions, but in crystallized form it becomes highly unstable and sensitive to physical movement or friction. The IAAAP is said to contain around 9,700 pounds (4.4 tons) of lead azide in barrels held in earth-covered containers.

In late March, the Army awarded a contract to MuniRem Environmental, a Georgia-based firm that uses chemicals to neutralize munitions to safely dispose of the lead azide, according to a New York Times report. MuniRem uses a sulfur-based solution that reportedly breaks down explosives and immobilizes toxic chemicals, eliminating the need for detonation. The company had proposed spraying the inside of the earth-covered containers housing the barrels as well as the barrel surfaces to neutralize risks of spontaneous explosions.

But two months later, the Army canceled the contract without explanation. An Army contracting officer told MuniRem in an email at the time that the cancellation “was not based on deficiencies” in MuniRem’s plans, the Times reported. The company’s founder said MuniRem filed public records requests to get more information on why the contract was scrapped, but it has not received a response

Now, the Army claims that the lead azide has only degraded further since March and it cannot move the barrels or even open their lids without risking a catastrophic explosion.  Further, it claims that winter temperatures in the coming days increase the risk of spontaneous detonation.

“As the temperatures drop, the risk of an uncontrolled explosion increases because the formed crystals can break more easily in freezing temperatures,” lawyers for the Trump administration wrote in a court filing over the weekend. “If the crystals break, this could cause a chain reaction detonation that could cause an uncontrolled dispersal of lead azide into the environment.”

The Army claims that the only choice now is between a controlled, mitigated explosion or an uncontrolled, unmitigated explosion. In court filings this weekend, government lawyers argued that the Army does not need the now-suspended EPA permit to move forward with the detonation, because the Army has determined that the lead azide poses an “immediate threat to human health, public safety, property, or the environment.”

Last Thursday, Telle said that the delay until Monday was the “latest possible moment” for a controlled detonation before temperatures dropped dangerously low. But the military subsequently delayed the detonation again until Tuesday and then pushed it back to Wednesday.

In his social media post Monday, Trump said he thought carrying out the controlled detonation was the “proper course.” But he said he was making the decision the responsibility of the federal appeals court and Governor-elect Rob Sand, a Democrat. If the lead azide spontaneously detonates on their watch, Trump claimed, they will be fully to blame.

“If he or the Courts do not agree, we will have no choice but to not proceed with the controlled removal of these hazardous materials, and be forced to hold the incoming Governor responsible for any and all death or destruction caused by this very unfortunate, and longterm, situation,” Trump wrote.

In a social media post Sunday evening, Sand called for answers on how the situation was able to become so dangerous, specifically raising the question of why MuniRem’s contact was canceled.

“Iowans deserve transparency over how we got here, then answers and accountability to stop this from ever happening again,” he wrote.

With a Wildfire Burning Nearby, Shenandoah National Park is Cracking Down on Campfires

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

As firefighters continue to battle a nearby blaze, Shenandoah National Park is implementing rigid fire restrictions due to exceptionally dry conditions and heightened wildfire risks going into Thanksgiving week.

In a Facebook post park officials explained: “These restrictions protect public safety given the elevated risk of wildland fires throughout the park. Shenandoah National Park, Skyline Drive and other facilities and services remain open as scheduled.”

Under these new restrictions, visitors to the area will have to rethink their cooking plans since they are temporarily unable to build, attend, or maintain open fires anywhere within the park’s boundaries, including in established pits, grates, and in grills. These regulations also apply to picnic areas, shelters, and huts in the park.

These rules also impact backpackers who rely on portable stoves while recreating in the area. Liquid and wood-fuel camp stoves cannot be used within the park until further notice. At the moment, campers can still use canister stoves like Jet Boils and MSR Pocket Rockets.

This announcement came just one day after the White Oak Flat Fire erupted on U.S. Forest Service land about 50 miles southwest of Shenandoah National Park. Since Friday, the fire has grown to 1,800 acres, with 49% containment. So far, 131 wildland personnel have responded to the fire including handcrews, wildland engines, bulldozers and two helicopters. Wildland firefighters are using active suppression strategies to contain the fire. No evacuations have been reported, and a cause for the fire has yet to be released.

The NPS identifies two primary wildfire seasons in Shenandoah that run from February to May and October through December. Wildfires are relatively common in Virginia during the month of November although the frequency and impacted acreage vary by season and seasonal driving factors like low humidity and breezy weather.

Shenandoah park officials expect these restrictions to remain in place until the region receives enough precipitation to reduce fire risks.
