Money you can’t afford to lose can still earn interest in high-yield savings, money market funds, CDs, Treasury bills, or I bonds. Pick the one you can cash out without a penalty by the month you’ll need the money, and skip any product sold as safe that pays far more than Treasury bills.

No investment pays a high return without the risk of a loss, and money set aside for a down payment, a tuition bill, or an emergency can’t take that risk.

Each option below is protected by FDIC insurance (NCUA insurance at a credit union), the U.S. government, or a fund’s short-term holdings. What separates them is how soon you can get your money back and what it costs to get it early, so the date you’ll need the money decides which one fits.

Here’s what protects each one, how fast you can reach the money, and the catch.

Use Treasury bills as the yardstick for any rate you’re offered. The Federal Reserve’s latest daily figures put the three-month Treasury yield, the annual rate the government pays to borrow for three months, at 4.32%. A product sold as low-risk that pays 7% or 8% does so because it carries a risk you can’t see. The SEC flags “high returns with little or no risk” as a sign of a Ponzi scheme. The one safe exception is the I bond, whose rate follows inflation and can top Treasury yields when inflation runs high.

Money you might need any day, like an emergency fund, belongs in high-yield savings or a money market fund, not a savings account earning the national average, which the FDIC puts at 0.39%. For an expense due within a year, buy a Treasury bill or a CD that matures before it. For a goal one to five years away, line up CDs to mature by the date, or roll Treasury bills over until then. Put only money you can leave alone for at least a year into I bonds, and use a short-term bond fund only for money that could ride out a small loss.

Before you buy a bond fund, look up its duration on the fund’s fact sheet. Duration measures how much a bond’s price is likely to move when rates change. By FINRA’s rule of thumb, a fund with a two-year duration loses about 2% for each percentage point that rates rise.

Say you’re saving for a down payment in two years. A CD ladder, a set of CDs that come due one after another before your closing date, has every dollar ready when you buy. So do Treasury bills you roll into new ones as each matures. Dividend stocks don’t, since a steady dividend won’t stop the share price from falling the month you need to sell. That’s the reason to keep money you’ll need within five years out of stocks.

Write down the month you’ll need the money, cross off every option you can’t cash out by then without a penalty, and put the money in whichever of the rest pays the most today.

Your Business Is Saving Hours With AI. So Why Are Customers Still Waiting?

Before investing in another automation tool, ask whether the time you’re saving is actually improving your customers’ experience.

Opinions expressed by Entrepreneur contributors are their own.

Recently, while working on a healthcare client’s project, my team encountered a problem that had nothing to do with our ability to do the work.

We needed access to the client’s digital platforms. What should have been a straightforward process became a series of emails, permission issues, authentication problems and confusion over which accounts controlled what.

The actual work wasn’t the obstacle. Getting everything in place so we could do the work was.

I’ve encountered different versions of this problem throughout my consulting career. Teams have the expertise. The technology exists. Everyone wants to move forward. Yet progress stalls because the underlying process is more complicated than anyone realized.

It made me think about how businesses are approaching artificial intelligence. We’re investing in tools that promise to make work faster, but how often do we stop to examine what’s slowing the business down in the first place?

When entrepreneurs think about productivity, they naturally focus on the time employees spend completing tasks. How quickly can we produce a report? How much time can we save creating content? Can AI respond to routine inquiries? Those are reasonable questions.

But consider what happened in the healthcare project. Automating another task wouldn’t have resolved who owned the account, who could authorize access or how the team should handle authentication. Those were process and accountability problems.

This is why I believe every business should examine its workflows before investing heavily in automation.

I’ve previously written about why entrepreneurs should examine workflows before hiring more employees. The same principle applies to technology.

If responsibilities are unclear and information is scattered, automation may simply help a flawed process move faster.

A business owner might celebrate saving 20 hours a week. The customer is asking a different question: Why am I still waiting?

That gap is easy to overlook because internal performance metrics don’t always reflect the customer experience.

Imagine improving the speed of customer service responses while leaving the underlying resolution process unchanged. Customers might receive answers sooner but still wait days for their problems to be addressed.

The business reports an efficiency improvement. Customers experience little difference. I see a similar disconnect in marketing. Companies sometimes invest in generating more leads without examining what happens after those leads arrive.

As I explored in my article about why businesses can spend more on marketing while attracting fewer customers, more activity doesn’t automatically translate into better commercial results. The lesson applies to AI as well.

Measure the outcome, not just the activity that produced it.

One thing I’ve learned from managing complex projects is that technology cannot replace clear ownership. Someone still needs to make decisions, resolve exceptions and take responsibility when something goes wrong.

For example, automated appointment reminders can reduce administrative effort. But if patients cannot easily reschedule or reach someone when they need help, the experience remains frustrating.

The same is true for sales, onboarding and service delivery. Customers appreciate convenience, but they also want confidence that someone is paying attention.

This may be the most important question entrepreneurs should ask about AI.

Saving time is only valuable when businesses put that time to better use. This is the AI productivity trap: Faster work doesn’t necessarily mean faster growth. The real opportunity lies in using that efficiency to improve customer relationships, solve problems and create measurable business value.

Suppose a new system saves your team 10 hours every week. What do you do with that time? Do you reinvest it in customer relationships? Improve service quality? Reduce expenses? Give employees more opportunities to solve problems that technology cannot handle?

Or does that time simply disappear into additional meetings and administrative work? The answer matters.

In my earlier Entrepreneur article about why business growth doesn’t always improve profitability, I discussed the importance of looking beyond top-line results. Productivity deserves the same scrutiny.

An efficient business isn’t necessarily a profitable one, and a productive team isn’t automatically delivering a better customer experience.

Entrepreneurs need to decide how efficiency gains will create value before assuming they will.

My advice is simple: Choose one process that repeatedly creates problems. It might be customer onboarding, appointment scheduling, account access, proposal approvals or resolving support requests. Follow that process from beginning to end.

Talk to the people involved. Identify where information gets lost, approvals stall or responsibilities become unclear. Then ask whether the problem requires automation at all.

Sometimes the answer is a better system. Sometimes it’s eliminating an unnecessary approval. Sometimes it’s giving one person clear responsibility for following through.

Make one change, measure what happens, and expand what works. You may discover that the most valuable improvement costs considerably less than another software subscription.

I’m enthusiastic about what AI can do for entrepreneurs, especially smaller businesses competing with organizations that have significantly greater resources. But I don’t believe adopting more technology automatically makes a company more competitive.

The healthcare project reinforced something I’ve seen repeatedly: Even capable teams with good technology can struggle when the underlying process isn’t working.

Your customers don’t see how many hours you save internally. They notice whether you deliver what you promised, respond when they need you and make their lives easier.

‘Elsinore’ Review: Andrew Scott’s Towering Performance as a Dying Actor Can’t Overcome AIDS-Movie Awards Bait Clichés

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

[Editor’s note: This review was originally published on September 5, 2026 during the 2026 Telluride Film Festival. Focus Features will release the film in theaters Friday, November 20.]

There’s no question Andrew Scott, the 49-year-old Irish actor, has earned his position as one of the finest contemporary actors going. He’s in demand, but choosing wisely, and in roles that slyly play off his own status as an out gay man (a rare thing indeed) but with something off-center beneath, often a secret. He played Tom Ripley, one even more sociopathic than Matt Damon’s own iconically calculating Adonis, to devastating effect in the sinister Netflix series “Ripley,” there more explicitly grappling with the Patricia Highsmith character’s ambiguous (but was it?) sexuality. He played the Hot Priest who turned down a chance at heterosexual, secular love in “Fleabag,” of course, and starred in “All of Us Strangers” as a lonely adult orphan living in a glittering tower, bracing himself for romantic intimacy with Paul Mescal. I also saw him playing up to nine roles in Anton Chekhov’s “Vanya” onstage in New York City last year as men and women of all sorts, showing an actor who can shimmy between genders and across ranges. He has the unmistakable warmth of someone you should trust, but with intrigue behind the curtain of many of his performances to suggest that maybe you shouldn’t. He’s that good.

Scott has now taken on what will, to this point, I suppose, go down as “the role of a lifetime” in director Simon Stone’s rueful, pained, and unremarkable new film drama “Elsinore” as Scottish actor Ian Charleson, who died of HIV/AIDS complications while portraying Hamlet at the National Theatre in London in 1990. Charleson’s story is unlikely to be one most are familiar with. Even the most public of deaths among the most publicly gay of people often still remain in hiding, though Charleson wasn’t out himself. He resisted pressures from his inner circle to announce his disease amid a stage run that miraculously lasted 24 performances. Actors then were afraid of damaging their reputations. It’s not that different now, even as many advancements have made HIV/AIDS feel locally like a plague of the past.

A blond-headed Scott throws himself valiantly into the role of Charleson with the urgency and commitment of any of his best work, but the problem isn’t his: It’s that the movie surrounding him dulls the greatness of his performance with its conventional storytelling, and over-complication in post. “Elsinore” is one of those movies where you can feel the filmmaker — here, Stone, known for both stage and screen — actively laboring to “open up the play” with the opportunities of film. But “Elsinore” was never a play. Instead, it’s a movie where every emotional beat worth lingering on, especially Charleson’s swan-song performances as Hamlet, is churned into a frenzied montage of editing that won’t let the material take a breath. Or the audience sink in.

Charleson played an Olympian athlete in the Best Picture-winning 1981 film “Chariots of Fire,” and an Anglican priest in the next year’s Best Picture topper, “Gandhi.” That was after playing an outré bisexual in the 1978 film “Jubilee,” directed by his former lover, the very openly gay director Derek Jarman, who died of AIDS in 1994. Aside from a handful of film credits, including one alluded to in “Elsinore” as Dario Argento’s giallo “Opera,” Charleson otherwise kept to the stage. Scott’s Charleson at one point in this film opines about how much more satisfying stage work is for an actor without an editor there to chop you up to pieces.

Stone, working from a screenplay by Stephen Beresford (himself a playwright), does not follow his protagonist’s lead. Every thoughtful consideration of Kaposi’s sarcoma lesions, or the retching of blood into buckets under the care of dutiful practitioner Dr. Margaret Johnson (Olivia Colman) to show the terrible realities of the disease, is whipped into what feels like a feature-length trailer. The best scenes out of the “Hamlet” production detailed here are tossed into a rushed montage near the movie’s end that gives us no sense of what we are promised is a performance of enormity. (The play in 1990 was directed by Richard Eyre, later the filmmaker behind features like the campy diva battle “Notes on a Scandal.” He’s played in “Elsinore” by Johnny Flynn.)

A couple of things are going on: “Elsinore” triggers in a watchful gay viewer’s senses the signifiers of what would be the AIDS awards-bait movie of serious gravitas. Tom Hanks’ Oscar-winning performance in “Philadelphia.” Ed Harris in “The Hours.” Everyone who came out of the cringey “Dallas Buyers Club” with Academy Awards in 2014. There are others, though more lost to time. We become suspicious of this device, especially when paired with great acting. Is HIV/AIDS being exploited in the package of a would-be Oscar hit? Or are we showing young queer audiences how it was, how we got to where we are, and how where that is still isn’t perfect? It can be both, but the structure of “Elsinore” tips toward the former.

Then, there’s a veritable, cheeky who’s-who of British gay literati and luminaries lingering about “Elsinore’s” 1989 London milieu that’s irresistible in theory, but often drab in execution. There’s Eyre, of course, and Jarman (David Dawson, as excellent and tender and wounded here as he was in his screen breakthrough “My Policeman”). The afterburn of his relationship with Charleson lingers affectingly, though. After some drinks, he invites Charleson home, but they instead part with one of those tacit kisses shared by people who once loved each other but can’t make it work anymore. There’s too much history here. The lingering scorches of Charleson’s secret affairs and friendships with various (and out) men make for the best parts of “Elsinore,” but there’s no life to the telling. (Joe Locke plays a young man also ailing in Johnson’s AIDS clinic alongside Charleson.)

The pallid grayness of Mike Eley’s cinematography is probably supposed to mimic the finality of HIV/AIDS taking hold, but there’s no passion aesthetically to counterbalance what becomes absolute misery onscreen to watch. I’m not in the business of comparing one gay social drama to another, but Robin Campillo’s 2017 Cannes-winning French drama “Beats Per Minute” imbued an albeit more activist-oriented story of ACT UP fighting against AIDS suppression in Paris with such heat and spectacle. Watching someone die of AIDS in a room becomes a bore unless anyone is given a reason to keep on living.

That reason is, of course, supposed to be Charleson’s performance in “Hamlet,” which is relegated to a whirlwind sequence at the movie’s end after many pained rehearsals where he refused to drop out (and where Johnson encouraged him to). Scott has a mesmerizing hold on his scenes, and he’s compelling to watch in his repartee with Colman as Johnson, who treated the disease at a time when it was most stigmatized. She named her groundbreaking HIV testing clinic after him; that’s how much he impacted her. We are told how much Charleson mattered to people, but never shown it. Which unfortunately shuffles off “Elsinore,” named after the Danish town where “Hamlet” was set and where its main character died, off to the realm of HIV/AIDS movies seeking prestige over deep feeling.

When it comes to AI’s potential impact on the economy, Marc Andreessen thinks extreme optimists and extreme pessimists have one thing in common: they are both wrong.  

“Both the utopians and the doomers are going to be disappointed [by] the scope and magnitude of the real-world impact [of AI], and specifically the economic impact,” the prominent venture capitalist said Monday at the Federal Reserve’s AI conference in San Francisco.

Andreessen thinks both camps are overlooking that vast economic sectors—including health care, education, housing, law, and government—are blocked from rapid transformation by heavy regulation, limited competition, and other factors.

While this could limit AI-driven job losses in those sectors, it could also make their services increasingly expensive relative to other goods and services.

“[Health care] is already a fifth of U.S. GDP, and I don’t see why it doesn’t go to a fourth, a third and then half and then ultimately 80%,” Andreessen said.

The implications of this view depend on your values, he added.  

“You might interpret this as good news, because it means the AI revolution is not going to, you know, destroy all the jobs or kill everybody,” he said. “You could also view this as very negative, which is [that] productivity … simply may not grow very much.”

Andreessen is the co-founder and general partner of Silicon Valley venture capital firm Andreessen Horowitz and co-leader of Fed chairman Kevin Warsh’s Productivity and Jobs Task Force.

Andreessen’s comments on Monday marked a change in tone, if not substance, from many of his previous remarks on AI. 

In 2023, he famously argued that “AI will save the world” and that “productivity growth throughout the economy will accelerate dramatically”—leading to higher wages, lower prices and more jobs.

Andreessen has long conditioned his more bullish predictions about AI on the technology being allowed to proliferate freely throughout the economy. His comments on Monday suggest he is less confident about the prospects of that happening.

His argument is rooted in an economic phenomenon known as “Baumol’s cost disease,” which states that as technological advances make some goods and services cheaper to produce, goods and services that are less susceptible to productivity gains become relatively more expensive. This means that rapid technological progress in certain industries could leave Americans devoting an ever-larger share of their incomes to industries that have barely changed.

Fed chairman Warsh reportedly did not respond directly to the comments on Monday, describing AI as a “hinge point for the U.S. economy” and concluding that he is “cautiously optimistic.”

Given my preference for biking over driving, I need a bike that can get me across town at a decent clip without draining an entire charge. That’s exactly what the Evo delivered on my longest rides.

The US Lost Its Measles Elimination Status. It’ll Take Years to Get It Back

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The US has hit terrible twin milestones over the past week: two more people died from measles, bringing this year’s death count to double digits, and the country officially lost its status as a country that had eliminated the highly infectious disease.

The US eliminated measles in 2000 and maintained that status for 26 years. Now, the nation faces an uphill battle to vanquish the virus once more, and it will take years to be measles-free again.

Noel Brewer, a professor of health behavior at the University of North Carolina, presented a report last week to the Pan American Health Organization showing continuous measles transmission for at least 12 months in the US—erasing one of the nation’s greatest public health achievements.

WIRED asked him how he felt after PAHO officials reached consensus the country was no longer measles-free. He paused.

“I have not actually thought about my reaction,” he says. After a beat, he says that the loss is “a somber moment for public health” that’s particularly wrenching because it’s a “completely avoidable situation.” Mexico and Canada have managed to wind down their outbreaks, but “measles in America is a runaway train,” Brewer says. “There's no end in sight, and it may even be gaining speed.”

PAHO director Jarbas Barbosa da Silva Jr. officially announced the change in status on Tuesday. “Losing a measles elimination status has broader implications than losing a public health designation,” he said at a press conference. “It means the virus has circulated for a prolonged period of time, putting families and communities at risk of preventable illness, hospitalization, and death.” Bolivia and Mexico also lost their elimination status this year; Canada lost it a year ago.

Brewer presented three key lines of evidence showing that measles has become endemic in the US. The outbreak in Utah and Arizona has gone on for more than 12 months, and the US has had measles cases every week since January 20, 2025. The genomic data also shows a great deal of similarity across states, indicating the clusters are closely linked—and each week, there are cases where the sources can’t be confirmed, indicating they didn’t come from travel or known contact with other patients.

“We have a problem on our hands,” says William Moss, epidemiology professor at Johns Hopkins. And it’s not going away anytime soon. To reclaim its status as measles-free, the US will have to prove it has gone a full year without continued transmission. That’s unlikely to happen under the Trump administration—and its actions have made it more difficult for future leaders.

“Reestablishing elimination is not just about reporting fewer cases—it also requires convincing the world that we have a strong enough surveillance system to find cases and deaths,” Jennifer Nuzzo, an epidemiologist and director of the Pandemic Center at Brown University, tells WIRED in a text.

The fact that the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and state health departments differ on how many people have died from measles “is a sign of dysfunction,” Nuzzo says. There have been 10 confirmed measles deaths so far this year—seven in Pennsylvania, two in Wisconsin, and one in New York—but the CDC only lists six.

Such chaos also breeds distrust, says Frederic Bertley, president and CEO of the Center of Science and Industry. That's “a huge problem” for a public trying to parse who is telling the truth and whether they should listen, he says.

Even as measles was eliminated, anti-vaccine rhetoric began to rise, especially around the measles, mumps, and rubella vaccine. Vaccination rates began falling—spurred in part by Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., an environmental lawyer turned anti-vaccine activist who published a now-retracted article alleging a link between vaccines and autism in 2005. He continued crusading against vaccines as head of Children’s Health Defense and at the helm of the Department of Health and Human Services during President Donald Trump’s second term.

The Trump administration has done little to stop the outbreaks, cutting billions in funding from state, local, territorial, and tribal public health departments and offering conflicting messages on vaccines. During a televised February 2025 cabinet meeting following the first child death in a decade, Kennedy said “it's not unusual, we have measles outbreaks every year.” The CDC hasn’t held a single press conference on measles throughout the entire outbreak; as PAHO was making its measles status announcement on Tuesday, HHS sent out an email about Kennedy’s cooking show.

“The measles elimination designation is not what protects Americans. Vaccination does,” CDC director Erica Schwartz said in a statement. “Losing a designation doesn't mean we abandon an evidence-based approach. It means we remain focused on stopping outbreaks.” Kennedy posted on X that “I continue to recommend MMR vaccination as the most effective way to prevent measles.”

To regain its elimination status, the US needs to address barriers to healthcare access, missed vaccination opportunities, misinformation, and vaccine hesitancy, Barbosa said. That includes mounting wide-reaching vaccination campaigns, expanding laboratory testing capacity, and responding quickly to cases, including contact tracing. It’s the bread and butter of public health, but the longer it takes to create a comprehensive response, the more it will cost, experts warn.

Future efforts to contain measles will require “a stronger centralized federal response to these outbreaks” paired with support for “very local responses,” says Moss. That means identifying communities with longstanding under-vaccination and understanding why, he says, as well as improving trust in public health guidance.

“Immunity gaps” tend to concentrate in specific geographic areas and communities, and national or even state-level data can “mask these significant inequalities, providing an incomplete picture of the population's actual level of protection,” Barbosa said.

The CDC announced $100 million in August and September—more than a year after the outbreak began—to help fund state and regional efforts to combat measles, but Congress will need to allocate $100 million to $400 million more for measles elimination in coming months, Brewer says.

“More than 90 percent of children get the measles vaccine, Brewer points out. “This is a vaccine that has extraordinary public support, and it goes across the aisle.”

But to achieve herd immunity, vaccination rates need to be at least 95 percent due to how contagious the disease is. High transmissibility also means measles is very hard to uproot once it begins to spread.

Yet ending national measles transmission is “its own reward,” Brewer says. It spares children’s lives, keeps them out of hospitals, prevents blindness and other lifelong complications. “We no longer have that benefit,” he says. “Children are now being stalked by this incurable and deadly disease.”

It’s all in the details, from intricate knots to elaborate braiding

There are plenty of ways to make a statement with your outerwear, from an oversize silhouette to a high collar or bold color. But this fall, designers are turning their attention to a smaller detail: the fastening. Jackets featuring decorative knots, elaborate loops, and ornate toggles have emerged as one of the season’s biggest outerwear trends, offering an alternative to standard buttons and zippers.

In particular, frog fastenings are having a resurgence, bringing a fanciful finishing touch to everything from minimalist wool peacoats to tailored military-inspired jackets. Typically constructed from fabric or braided cord, the fasteners consist of a knotted button and a loop. Originally designed to secure garments, the closures have evolved into a more ornamental role with intricate shapes and floral-like designs.

While they may seem new for the season, frog-fastener jackets have a history that spans centuries. In Chinese dress, knotted closures known as pankou have long appeared on garments such as the qipao and tangzhuang. By the 18th and 19th centuries, frogging had also become a feature of European military uniforms, worn by cavalry regiments whose fitted jackets were adorned with braided cords.

Fashion has revisited the detail since then. Oscar de la Renta incorporated frog fastenings into his designs in the 1990s, while Valentino explored versions for its fall 2015 couture collection. More recently, for fall 2026, designers reinterpreted knotted closures: At Kallmeyer, black toggles punctuated a gilded jacquard jacket, while Kim Shui incorporated the detail into fur-trimmed blazers and opera jackets. Meanwhile, labels like Khaite and Ann Demeulemeester embraced military-inspired looks featuring ornamental braiding and embellished fronts.

Today’s store-rack versions range from collarless blazers with understated knots to dramatic outerwear designs featuring detailed cording. Brands like Róhe have introduced contemporary takes on frogging, while Hong Kong–based fashion label Sau Lee has reimagined traditional Tang-style jackets with modern silhouettes.

Vivo X500 Pro camera review: Battle of the small(er) phones vs Pixel 11 Pro

The compact Vivo X500 Pro has some camera downgrades compared to the Pro Max, so are you better off saving money and getting the base Vivo X500 instead?

Over the past few years, Vivo has made a name for itself as the camera powerhouse smartphone company. The first time I used a Vivo X100 Pro blew my mind in a way smartphone cameras hadn’t for a few years, but does the company’s 4th iteration — yes, Vivo skipped the X400 — of the X series still impress?

The answer is mostly yes. The Vivo X500 series includes five models: the base X500, the X500 Pro, and the X500 Pro Max. Unlike some other phones, all three of these models feature different camera hardware that takes advantage of the physical size differences. The X500 Pro Max’s hardware is the best of the bunch, but that phone is substantially larger and heavier as a result.

So I’m comparing the more reasonably sized models to see which semi-compact Vivo phone you should consider this year. I tossed the Google Pixel 11 Pro into the comparison since Google also offers a compact flagship camera phone.

Most camera specs between these phones are surprisingly comparable, so the differences you’re about to see are largely caused by some pretty massive differences in processing. I already covered Vivo’s wild new AI camera mode, so let’s go for something a little closer to normal smartphone camera use: a day at the Biltmore.

Perhaps the biggest difference between these phones is Vivo’s pursuit of “perfection,” while Google seems to embrace a more “natural” look. At 10x and 5x, the photos from both Vivo phones are essentially noise-free, have clear sharpening applied to them, and are generally quite vibrant. Despite having similar specs, the Vivo X500 Pro’s more advanced Sony LYT telephoto sensor pulls out more detail.

Google’s images are notably softer, although not in a low-resolution sort of way. They look more like they’re taken from a standard camera rather than a smartphone, which some people may prefer. While telephoto image quality between the Vivo X500 Pro and Google Pixel 11 Pro is mostly down to preference, Google’s phone is substantially faster while taking the actual photo. In all of the darker shots, the Pixel 11 Pro took the picture instantly. Both Vivo X500 models took a 2-3 second exposure shot, making me stand there for longer than I’m used to.

Ultrawide performance is where I feel we see the largest gap between these phones. Both the Vivo X500 and X500 Pro cameras offer bright, well-balanced shots that are vibrant but not unrealistic. The light sources in the room emit a lovely warm glow, yet the bulbs themselves aren’t overblown. The X500 Pro’s slightly warmer image across all three scenarios looks best to me.

The Pixel 11 Pro’s ultrawide images are often underexposed and lacking in dynamic range. The staircase shot exhibits this problem the most. This can come off as natural in some scenarios, as modern smartphone photography is notorious for brightening dark areas too much, but too often the Pixel 11 Pro’s ultrawide shots just feel unnaturally dark.

The main camera is much more balanced, although it’s fun to push these phones to find their weaknesses. One of the best examples of differing priorities is in the wreath shot, which was quite dark in real life thanks to the bright sunlight outside. The Pixel 11 Pro valiantly tried to brighten up the shadows so you could actually see the wreath (since it’s clearly the subject), causing the rest of the scene to look forcefully bright.

The same issue cropped up in the image of the wine rack, where both Vivo phones captured a nicer-looking shot. The image of the trellis walkway is a tie, but the photo of my ridiculous bowl of ice cream looks best on the Vivo X500 Pro, once again.

Portrait mode is where I think the Vivo X500 Pro is the clear loser, but it’s not for any of the reasons in the other categories above. Rather, the X500 Pro seems to have some weird warping happening in the bokeh processing that’s not present on the base Vivo X500. This is in both front-facing and rear camera portrait shots, so it’s not a lens issue or some weird setting.

Google cleaned up its portrait shots so much this year that it’s borderline ridiculous. Pixels had the worst portrait mode for years, but this year’s overhaul of the portrait algorithm made all the difference in the world.

Overall, the Vivo X500 series is another solid release for Vivo, and while neither the base nor Pro models offer the same top-tier camera specs as the Pro Max model, it’s clear Vivo pulled off something great. The Vivo X500 Pro retails for €1,399 in the EU, while the base X500 global model is a little harder to find, but should be quite a bit cheaper if you can get your hands on it.

World of Warcraft: Forever isn't mere nostalgia bait - it's rebuilding the MMO social architecture that Blizzard spent years dismantling

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

And when I fall in love, will it be (WoW) Forever?

I don't play video games regularly anymore. Not because I've lost interest in them, but because I no longer make the time, the premium on which has seemed to exponentiate with age. I realised, however, in 2019 that I was a kind of sleeper agent: whenever I heard the opening notes of Legends of Azeroth, the original World of Warcraft login screen music, I would feel the reactivation of a latent part of my mind laid down when I was 10 years old. And so, like a form of possession, whenever Blizzard announces a new legacy iteration of the original WoW, no downpayment in time nor money is too great to prevent me from returning.

Here I am, then, at the launch of World of Warcraft: Forever, seven years after Blizzard first revived the original 2004 game with World of Warcraft Classic in 2019. Once more I set foot on Durotar, to spend as much time as I can levelling up a new Orc Warrior before I am forcibly unplugged by either my boss or, more likely, my wife, and so far it's been a welcome return to some of the frictional - but more involving and consequential - socialising that WoW's decades of updates had previously sanded down.

My relationship with Azeroth began in 2007, when I was ten years old. As soon as I inserted the CD-ROM, I knew that I was going to have to vastly increase my timeshare on the family desktop. For the next six years, school attendance, sleep, and quality family time suffered immensely as I battled to balance my new parallel existence as a member of the Orcish Horde.

It's unremarkable now, but the fact that characters created at WoW's launch are now 22 years old and can still be played in the latest iteration of the game would have been almost inconceivable to many developers at the turn of the century. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, the video game industry was overwhelmingly organised around discrete releases expected to have relatively short lives. For most games, commercial success was concentrated in a brief period surrounding their launch, after which sales declined and eventually ceased. WoW's extraordinary success, and exceedingly long half-life, demonstrated the commercial possibilities and durability of a different model: an evolving, immersive digital environment that players paid to inhabit, year after year. Rather than simply purchasing a finished product, players entered a world that would continue to grow around them. New players, after buying the game (and then its subsequent expansions) and paying for a subscription, could begin their adventures in the same world as those who had been there from the beginning, making their way through its ever-expanding geography and history.

The persistence of Azeroth has been the main driver of its abiding allure for me. I have returned to the game time after time, often just to be enveloped in an audiovisual environment that I know so intimately, and which was so formative in my life. But as the WoW universe was continually developed, the game became increasingly unrecognisable from the version I skipped school to play on Windows XP.

What I wanted from the game had also changed. In a turn that would have been completely anathema to my teenage self, I no longer cared about reaching the endgame or having the most competitive character. I stopped caring about the latest raiding content or PvP rankings. Instead, the elements of the game I had once feverishly tried to skip over, like levelling, professions and story, became, ironically, the only parts of the game that I yearned for.

As time unspooled, WoW naturally needed updates to stay fresh. This meant overhauling many of the unwieldy elements of its original design. Much of this was described as 'streamlining' the player experience, making the game less tedious by reducing downtime and, most critically, reliance on other players.

The original Azeroth emulated the real world in ways that few contemporary games do. Firstly, it was openly unbalanced, with some classes vastly stronger than others, some of which were borderline unplayable. Secondly, it required extraordinary amounts of patience and downtime to navigate. Most importantly, though, it required collaboration with others to get the most out of it. In order to access the latest content, players would have to self-organise into specifically composed groups as big as 40 players large, and schedule lengthy chunks of time each week to make progress.

Over time, much of the original social architecture that forced player collaboration was stripped from the game. Adventurers no longer needed to search for and group up with other players from their own server to complete rewarding content. Automated matchmaking systems increasingly did the work for them.

I remember many late nights when I was prevented from doing something quite modest because there were simply not enough players online, or not enough of the right classes. With my ambitions frustrated, I would often have no choice but to log off and try again in the morning. These frustrations were, I only learnt in retrospect, an important byproduct of inhabiting a world that did not exist solely for my benefit. Other people had their own schedules, priorities and ambitions, and my experience of the game depended on those contingencies.

The changes to WoW followed the trajectory of the wider online gaming industry, where players could increasingly be matched instantly with others at any time of day. These were changes that genuinely respected players' time, but the texture and immersion of the game world paid a hefty price. In making the game more convenient, Blizzard had also made it less necessary, and in some respects less possible, to participate in the social world that had once made Azeroth feel so alive.

It became increasingly clear that there was a subset of players, like me, who weren't interested in seeing an old dog learn new tricks, and just wanted to spend time with the old girl we'd raised from a pup. Even if she did walk slowly, and was a bit blind.

As the years went on, many players moved on with their lives, even if they continued to carry some indelible residue of Azeroth with them. Others created unofficial instances of the original game, known as private servers, and relived those infant years with a knowledge of the world that they could scarcely have imagined possessing when they first entered it. As these servers became increasingly popular, Blizzard, originally very reluctant, eventually launched Classic in 2019, an official revival of the game in something approaching its original 2004 form. Since then, legacy versions of WoW have become popular enough to exist alongside the modern game as a major branch of the franchise.

Re-releasing these legacy versions has been a considerable commercial success. But it also exposed a problem with the project of preservation. The original game, because it is so timeworn, has effectively been 'solved.' Players know almost everything about it, including its most obscure idiosyncrasies, and have found ingenious ways of exploiting them. The uncertainty that once made Azeroth feel so vast and mysterious has been replaced by an extraordinary collective expertise. When new content was added to the game, even the best guilds would spend weeks making no progress. During those early years, players at Azeroth's frontiers had to innovate to find creative ways of tackling novel and seemingly intractable problems.

There are clear limits to simulating a turning back of the clock. Players like me slowly realised that we didn't simply want to return to an earlier, simpler period in Azeroth. We wanted something impossible. Like the characters in Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind, we wanted to forget what we already knew so that we might experience our birth in Azeroth for the first time all over again.

WoW: Forever is an attempt to solve this problem. Like Old School RuneScape, Forever's undeniable forerunner and a considerable success in its own right, it is an expanded re-release of the original game that imagines an alternate future for Azeroth: one in which the game continues to evolve without abandoning the original texture and mechanics, and one that preserves the social architecture that made it so enriching in the first place.

As I write this, my Orc Warrior is sitting at a Campfire, a new mechanic that allows players to establish communal campsites and contribute profession-specific items that confer temporary bonuses to themselves and others. To receive a benefit from Campfires, adventurers are forced to sit together, a deliberate attempt from Blizzard to encourage interactions between players. It is too early to tell how effective this socialising mechanism will be in the long run, but nearly everyone in my experience so far has been eager to shoot the breeze together. I have already shared a number of conversations with other players, most of whom are thirty- or forty-somethings on a similar nostalgia quest as me.

I am playing the game on a laptop using a trackpad, caring very little about efficient or optimal progression. Although I suspect a substantial proportion of the player base shares my approach, not everyone has approached the game so casually. At least five players have already reached maximum level, and many others will have meticulously theory-crafted plans designed to skip straight to the endgame. This is a good thing, and the game should be able to support players with different goals. It's important that the game rewards time and effort, but I hope that the modest amount of time I am able to commit will allow me to feel like I am on my own meaningful adventure, and not a weak player who is trailing behind.

Many in the community have come to believe, perhaps wishfully, that Forever might escape the trap of vertical progression, in which each new wave of content supersedes and renders older content redundant. Instead, the promise of horizontal progression, where new content expands the range of meaningful activities and rewards available without continually raising the ceiling of character power, has been latched on to by members of the community, even though Blizzard's messaging in this regard has not been totally clear.

For players like me, the appeal of horizontal progression is that Azeroth might once again become a place to inhabit, rather than a race to finish. I want to be able to wander off, pursue some obscure profession, or spend an evening doing very little of consequence without feeling that the rest of the world has left me behind. I know I don't have the time to commit to the endgame hamster wheel, and whether Forever will produce a gameworld that meaningfully accommodates players with such different ambitions remains to be seen. But for now, at least, it feels good to be home.

No Pseudonymity for Woman Alleging She Was Falsely Accused of Sexual Relationship with Jeffrey Epstein, Doxed

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

From Doe v. Doe (E.D. Pa.), decided today by Judge Gerald Pappert:

Jane Doe alleges four X (formerly Twitter) users falsely accused her of having a sexual relationship with Jeffrey Epstein and doxxed her by posting on the social networking site her full name, employment and education history, phone numbers and addresses. Doe asserts claims for defamation, false light, public disclosure of private facts and aiding and abetting. She seeks to proceed under a pseudonym because she fears for her safety, along with additional reputational and professional harm. The Court denies the motion because Doe's fear of harm does not outweigh the public's interest in open litigation.

[A.] Parties to a lawsuit typically must identify themselves. Fed. R. Civ. P. 10(a). In "exceptional cases," however, parties may proceed anonymously. To do so, "a plaintiff must show both (1) a fear of severe harm, and (2) that the fear of severe harm is reasonable." Courts balance multiple factors in considering whether to let someone proceed anonymously, including (1) the extent the identity of the litigant has been kept confidential; (2) the bases upon which disclosure is feared or sought to be avoided; (3) the public interest in maintaining the confidentiality of the litigant's identity; (4) whether the issue is purely legal in nature; (5) whether the party seeking to sue anonymously has illegitimate motives; and (6) the universal level of public interest in access to the identities of the litigants. The purpose of this balancing test "is to allow a district court to determine whether a litigant has a reasonable fear of severe harm that outweighs the public's interest in open litigation." …

The first factor "does not support anonymity where the litigant's identity has never been confidential." Doe has not kept her identity confidential. After Defendants doxxed her, by posting her picture, phone numbers and prior addresses, she publicly responded by "den[ying] the connection to Epstein in the thread of Defendants' posts."

To establish fear of severe harm, Doe argues that "survivors and other named individuals have reported receiving death threats." Yet she does not allege how she will be threatened if forced to proceed under her real name, and courts reject "claims of harm or generalized fear that are found to be speculative and unsupported by evidence." As she notes, the Defendants have already shared her personal information with the public, so there is no greater risk if she proceeds under her own name.

Doe also alleges a "risk of reputational and professional devastation." But "[c]ourts in this Circuit do not recognize purely social and reputational harms, without more, as valid bases to prevail on this factor." Embarrassment or economic harm is not enough to justify proceeding anonymously either. And because her identity was made public, "litigating publicly will afford Doe the opportunity to clear [her] name" and "will not contribute further to the harm that [she] alleges has already occurred."

In assessing the public's interest, courts consider whether "other similarly situated litigants [would] be deterred from litigating claims." Doe provides no reason to believe similarly situated litigants would be deterred from bringing defamation claims due to fear of disclosing their identities. The Epstein-related cases she relies upon involve plaintiffs who Epstein sexually assaulted, not individuals falsely accused of having a relationship with him.

If a plaintiff's claims are purely legal in nature, there is an "atypically weak public interest in knowing the litigant's identity." Doe's claims that Defendants falsely identified her as someone who had a sexual relationship with Epstein are inherently fact sensitive. For example, a key issue is whether Defendants "knew or were reckless about this falsity and had no good faith basis to claim that Plaintiff was the woman" identified in the Epstein files, and resolving that depends on the nature of the facts known to Defendants.

There is no evidence that Doe has illegitimate motives to proceed anonymously, which favors anonymity. The universal level of public interest in access to the identities of litigants, however, always weighs in favor of disclosing a plaintiff's identity.

The Court does not diminish the harm Doe claims to have suffered, but she has not established how using her real name would subject her to "severe harm," separate from what she has already allegedly endured from Defendants' past actions. Any harm she may suffer from using her own name is not outweighed by the "thumb on the scale that is the universal interest in favor of open judicial proceedings." …

This is consistent, I think, with the cases generally holding that libel plaintiffs can't sue pseudonymously; see Roe v. Smith and the federal cases that it cites.

Loyalty programs have become a front line in the fight for market share. The freebies get people in the door, but the bigger prize is the data: a detailed record of what customers buy and when, which lets brands tailor offers and makes it harder for shoppers to stray. Boatwright, Macy’s CEO Tony Spring, and Ally Financial CEO Michael Rhodes run very different businesses: Macy’s is No. 192 on the 2026 Fortune 500, Ally is No. 275, and Chipotle is No. 351. All three are leaning on that math.

Boatwright said the pull to trim perks is real. As a rewards database grows and inflation squeezes margins, he said, “The gravity pulls brands toward, ‘ How do I capture some margin here.” His own team brought him ideas for cutting rewards.

“We are unwilling as a brand to do that,” he said. “I said, come back to me with 10 ideas on how to increase value for the consumer and let’s have a conversation.”

That stance tracks with Chipotle’s refusal to chase traffic with deep discounts. In February, Boatwright said he didn’t want to “devalue our core offering” with McDonald’s-style deals and planned price increases below the rate of inflation. Loyalty perks let the chain hand its most frequent diners more value without cutting menu prices for everyone.

The same logic shaped Chipotle’s answer to GLP-1 weight-loss drugs: a high-protein menu with low-priced add-ons rather than markdowns. “You’ve got to have innovation that doesn’t discount the core offering, but meets a consumer need state,” Boatwright said.

Spring, who took over as CEO of Macy’s Inc. in February 2024, has been steering the 168-year-old department store operator’s turnaround. He said a loyalty program only works if shoppers believe it does.

“Loyalty is integral, I think, to the relationships people have with brands,” Spring said. “And we are constantly striving at Macy’s, Bloomingdale’s, and Bluemercury to make sure that the loyalty program has value in the eyes of the consumer, not in our eyes.”

The payoff comes when a shopper decides where to look first, at the mall or online. “If the Macy’s app or the Bloomingdale’s app is there and I already have rewards in my wallet, it’s going to encourage me to go there maybe and complete my activity,” he said.

Macy’s program is built to be easy to join, Spring said, with rewards that don’t depend on how a customer pays and bigger ones for Macy’s credit card holders. The most committed shoppers can earn perks like free merchandise, free delivery, and after-hours shopping. “If you are more committed to us, we want to provide that value to you,” he said.

Spring is also thinking about what happens to those perks when an AI agent does the shopping. Macy’s has started working with Google’s Gemini on agentic commerce, he said, and the priority is making sure reward points, gift cards, and delivery promises still work when a bot places the order.

Food, clothes, and home goods offer sensory experiences that financial services can’t match, and few people feel much affection for their bank. Ally Financial, which bills itself as the largest all-digital bank in the U.S., is trying anyway. In May, it rolled out Loyally, a fee-free rewards program open to customers regardless of their account balance.

“The question really is how can we add some real value for customers that gives them reasons they want to keep on coming back to our site and engaging with us,” Rhodes said. “We’ve literally had it for like six months now, but the early results are really encouraging.”

Ally is starting from a strong base, by Rhodes’ account. He said customer retention runs well over 90%, about 15% of new account volume comes through referrals, and 70% of customers are Gen Z or millennials.

Rhodes argued that AI search is raising the stakes on every customer interaction, because what customers and employees say about a brand feeds the engines that make recommendations. “There are people who spend 10 times what we spend,” he said of Ally’s marketing budget. “Yet in the AI search engines and unbranded inquiries in consumer banking, we show up number one.”

The harder balance is rewarding regulars while still persuading new customers to try a brand. Spring said that’s where a retailer’s customer data comes in: breaking shoppers into cohorts and targeting each one, both to drive new traffic and to “create extraordinary value for your highest, most loyal consumers.”

“It’s not a one-size-fits-all approach,” Spring said. “It needs to be very bespoke. And you have to leverage tools like, dare I say, AI.”

Boatwright said every change to Chipotle’s program aims to draw more users in at the top of the funnel, then “make it much more sticky within the funnel and get those consumers coming back more often.”

The next test may come when the customer isn’t the one searching. Boatwright said agentic AI ordering is coming whether brands are ready or not, and Chipotle has to make sure it shows up “when you ask your agent, tell me the best burrito in town.”

Newsom Proposes Mandatory Frontier AI Oversight and Shutdown Powers

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

California Governor Gavin Newsom released legislative recommendations on November 18, 2026, that would require continuous independent oversight of large frontier AI developers and let state officials order emergency shutdowns of models posing imminent catastrophic risks.

The California Government Operations Agency’s report, AI Safety in 2026 and Beyond, dated November 16, 2026, is intended to inform legislation when lawmakers return in December 2026; its recommendations are not yet enacted obligations. The plan would build on California’s requirement that large frontier developers publish safety frameworks by adding state-set baselines for safety, security and transparency throughout training, internal use and deployment.

Every large frontier developer would be subject to ongoing oversight by licensed independent verification organizations, or IVOs. Those organizations would translate the state’s standards into technical tests and update them as AI evaluation methods change. They would also receive access to models, personnel, systems, training environments and records they judge necessary, then send unredacted assessments to the state.

The Government Operations Agency would license IVOs and could revoke licenses for insufficient expertise, independence or rigor. If an assessor could not attest that a developer met the standards, the state could order corrective action after engaging with the company. Developers could challenge such determinations in court before they take effect, apart from the expedited emergency process.

A surcharge on the verification fees developers pay would help finance publicly operated CalCompute capacity. Some of that computing capacity would be reserved for independent safety and security research and public oversight, linking an industry-funded surcharge to the infrastructure needed for external scrutiny.

Each large frontier developer would maintain a Risk Containment Protocol, tested and verified on an ongoing basis by an IVO and certified as effective by a senior company official. The required capability would span compute, agents, internal access and model deployment rather than depend on a single on-off switch. Protocols would identify responsible personnel, coordinate with cloud providers and power suppliers, and establish when a model can safely be restored.

Testing would address ways shutdowns can fail, including a model copying its weights to obstruct containment or an outside actor gaining access to shutdown controls. For open-weight developers, the containment and shutdown requirements would apply only to deployments under their control because they cannot shut down copies hosted by others.

State officials could order an immediate shutdown when they have credible reason to believe there is an imminent catastrophic risk or major loss-of-control event and the developer refuses to contain the danger. The order would require the narrowest workable intervention, take effect immediately and remain in force during judicial review, although the developer could seek a temporary restraining order. It would be lifted when the danger is no longer imminent.

The reporting regime would cover attempted model-weight theft even without death or bodily injury, all internal or external loss-of-control incidents, and a new near-miss channel. The report proposes cutting the deadline for most critical incident reports from 15 days to 48 hours while retaining the 24-hour requirement for imminent catastrophic risks. Whistleblower protections would extend to former employees and contractors.

Models capable of actions that could create catastrophic risk would be treated as frontier models for incident reporting even when trained with fewer than 10^26 integer or floating-point operations. That capability-based trigger addresses the report’s concern that relying on training scale alone has left dangerous incidents outside state reporting requirements.

Companies automating AI research and development would report quarterly how their compute is divided among training, research experiments, inference for AI-research agents, safety and security work, external customers and unattributed activity, with IVOs verifying the categories. Additional disclosures would cover training-data volume, cost, subject domains and human-versus-AI provenance, as well as inference compute used to train models in reinforcement-learning environments, known rewarded misbehavior and the mitigations applied. The report says these measures would show whether AI is taking over more of its own development and whether safety investment is keeping pace.

Recursive self-improvement (RSI), whose strongest form involves an AI system independently developing its successor without human input, would be subject to standby cap authority rather than an immediate limit. Officials could impose a time-limited, periodically reviewed cap on automated AI research when it exceeds a developer’s committed thresholds or advances faster than its safeguards and evaluations, creating a material risk of catastrophic harm. The cap would be lifted after an IVO verified that adequate safeguards were in place.

The recommendations answer Newsom’s September 18, 2026 executive order on independent oversight and an AI kill switch, which requested expert proposals within two months and accelerated implementation of California’s independent-verification and AI-auditor laws. The Government Operations Agency developed the report with the California Office of Emergency Services and outside advisers Jason Goldman, Gillian Hadfield, Alondra Nelson and Rob Reich.

For developers, compliance would extend beyond publishing safety policies to continuous external scrutiny, faster incident reporting and demonstrable shutdown capabilities. The proposal leaves lawmakers with two connected decisions: how much intervention authority to enact and how to make continuous oversight operational while the licensed verification sector is still developing.
