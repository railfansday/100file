How Should You Spend Your Money to Be Happier?

Posted November 24, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

There are certain ways to spend money that can make us happier. But first we need to answer a more basic question. Does money buy happiness?

It was 2010. Nobel laureate Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton of Princeton University published a study that sparked a great deal of debate.

They looked at the income and happiness levels reported in more than 450,000 responses to a daily Gallup survey. They found that in the United States, day-to-day happiness rises with annual income up to $75,000. Beyond that point, earning more money doesn’t seem to make people any happier in their daily lives.

Fast-forward to 2021. Matthew Killingsworth of the Wharton School revisited the question by asking people how they felt in real time. He analyzed more than 1.7 million real-time happiness reports from more than 33,000 people and found no such cutoff at $75,000. In his study, the more people earn, the happier they tend to be.

This is what I love about science at its best. What matters is getting closer to the truth, not winning the argument.

Kahneman and Killingsworth, whose findings pointed in opposite directions, teamed up with psychologist Barbara Mellers to figure out why. In their 2023 study, they took a fresh look at Killingsworth’s original data.

They examined the incomes of 33,391 employed adults who had been pinged on their phones at random moments, typically three times a day. Each time a prompt came in, participants reported how they felt.

When the researchers analyzed the results, the contradiction cleared up, and a new picture of happiness emerged. For the least happy people, roughly the unhappiest fifth, higher earnings beyond $100,000 a year are no longer associated with greater happiness. For everyone else, however, happiness continues to rise with income, and among the happiest, it rises even faster.

In other words, for most of us, more money goes along with more happiness. But for people who are deeply unhappy, more money beyond a certain point doesn’t seem to help.

How does money do that? How can we spend it in ways that make us happier?

Whether money makes you happier depends on how you spend it.

Imagine I give you a substantial sum of money. Would you spend it on an unforgettable trip to İstanbul, or on another experience you’d love? Or would you buy yourself a Loro Piana coat? Which choice would make you happier?

That’s exactly what Amit Kumar of the University of Texas at Austin and his colleagues wanted to find out. They texted 2,635 adults at random moments during the day and asked how happy they felt right then, and whether they were using or enjoying something they had bought.

The results were clear. People in the middle of an experience they’d paid for reported feeling happier than people using a material purchase. Going to a concert, sharing a good dinner with people you love, or finally visiting a city you’ve always wanted to see tended to bring people more happiness than buying things.

Another set of studies by Jacob Lee, Deborah Hall, and Wendy Wood, published in 2018, offers an answer.

They first compared people of higher and lower socioeconomic status. They then asked participants to recall or imagine buying either an experience or something they could own, and to rate how happy each purchase made them feel.

The pattern was striking. People of higher socioeconomic status felt happier when they imagined buying experiences. For people with lower incomes, experiences didn’t give the same boost. Some were even happier with things.

When money is tight, being able to cover the basics can bring more happiness. Spending money on an experience can feel like a luxury or a waste. It makes sense, then, to cover the essentials first. Once those needs are met, spending on experiences is more likely to pay off in happiness.

So should we skip them when money is tight?

Not necessarily. Spend money on experiences that fit your budget.

I have friends who have traveled all over the world on a shoestring, staying in small hotels and eating street food. What matters is the memories you make with the money you have.

A picnic with family might be enough to boost your happiness. As a parent, spend what you can on experiences you can share with your children. When your child grows up, they probably won’t remember the expensive shoes you bought them when they were 6. But they may remember a concert, a picnic, or a trip you took together for the rest of their life.

Here’s How the Porsche 718 Boxster EV’s Fake Flat-Six and PDK Work

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

Porsche chose the 981-generation Boxster GTS model as its sound reference.

The upcoming Porsche 718 Boxster EV hops on board the growing simulated-internal-combustion-engine-and-transmission electric car trend. We just drove a prototype, and while there’s a lot to discuss about the car in general, it’s also worth honing in on the sound aspect. As you might expect, it’s rather pivotal to the experience behind the wheel.

The system that debuted in the Taycan is still called “E-Shift” here in the Boxster, and you can turn it on or off via a button on the steering wheel. Porsche chose the 3.4-liter naturally aspirated flat-six from the 981-generation Boxster as its muse. It’s a good choice, but also a bit of a surprise considering the spectacular sound out of the 4.0-liter flat-six found in the previous 718 Boxsters. But Porsche has its reasons, as Dr.-Ing. Sebastian Lefevre, team lead advanced driver assistance systems 718, tells us.

“It was a big discussion in our company,” Lefevre said. “From our point of view, the 4.0 engine is a little bit too soft in the sound. In our company, the 981 GTS sound, six-cylinder, was a little bit louder, and it would also feel more popular and familiar with our old customers.”

Once you hear the sound, there’s a good chance you’ll end up agreeing with Porsche. Lefevre mentioned the possibility of using the four-cylinder from the previous 718, but just as quickly shot the idea down referencing critique of the flat-four’s sound. How it reproduced the engine and exhaust via speakers is similar to how other OEMs with simulated internal combustion engines in EVs do it.

“A lot of microphones outside, inside,” Lefevre said. “And then we measured it all over the complete rpm range. And then we put it here, in this car, and measure it again. With the speakers, which are installed here. We have two.”

The most controversial speaker is bound to be the exterior-positioned one. More than anything, however, this exterior speaker exists as a sound maker for occupants to enjoy when the roof is off. It tries to emulate a mid-engine powertrain arrangement by way of the sound emanating from behind the driver and passenger; Porsche increases the volume inside the car with the roof removed, too. That’s a neat idea, but one that struggles to work in practice; I could hear some faint backfires on “downshifts” like Porsche’s ICE cars produce, but the experience is far from that of a real exhaust and engine echoing and reverberating off mountainsides or buildings.

Mercedes-AMG has the best ICE simulation around right now, with its AMG GT 4-Door Coupe going so far as to induce proper NVH characteristics by using exciters in the seats. The Boxster EV Prototype we drove relies entirely on the speakers to induce its hypnosis. But E-Shift brings a few theatrics to the table.

When lining up for a launch control start, Porsche pulses the torque of the electric motor to give a sense of an impending launch. It also lets you run the car into “redline,” tossing you forward with brake torque, just as it would in a manually-shifted gas-powered car. That said, Porsche took some liberties with the 8000-rpm redline depicted in this prototype; the 981 Boxster GTS instrument cluster shows a redline of 7600 rpm, so you have an extra 400 fake revs to play with in the EV.

Not as though this extra headroom matters, however, as Porsche chose to not simulate the torque curve of the flat-six. Instead, peak performance from the single electric motor is always available, and the car shuffles through its fake seven gears as speeds rise and fall. How it shifts is dependent on drive mode per usual with Porsche PDKs, and you can choose between manual or automatic shifting. You’ll even hear a tone difference in the engine noise over the speakers depending on whether you’re on- or off-throttle, just as you would were the real 3.4-liter be gulping in air behind your head.

Google will make AI agents use new gateways to reach Gmail and Drive

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Agents must switch from Workspace's regular APIs by February, and need a person's approval before sending email.

Google has made its Workspace MCP servers and CLI generally available and says AI agents must use them instead of existing REST APIs from February 1, 2027. Every call is checked against company policies, sensitive actions need user confirmation, and a new addendum bars AI tools from training on Workspace data.

Google will require AI agents to reach Gmail, Drive and other Workspace apps through new, dedicated gateways, it said on Tuesday. Regular apps can keep using Workspace’s existing APIs, but agents must switch by February 1, 2027.

The gateways are Workspace MCP servers, which use a common standard for linking AI tools to apps, and a command-line tool. Both are now generally available. Google put an early CLI version on GitHub in March.

Google says its APIs were built for apps that work at human speed, where a valid login meant the user wanted the action. Agents act on their own at machine speed. So every request now gets checked against company permissions and data-loss rules.

Agents can draft emails and summarise documents on their own. But they need a person’s sign-off before sending email, changing Drive sharing or making bulk edits.

Each agent also needs its own sign-in ID with tight limits and usage quotas. Google already gives its Gemini coworker agents their own accounts. It will begin billing for that usage on April 1, 2027.

A new AI Tools Addendum adds rules on data. AI tools cannot use Workspace data to train models or build rival services, and cannot scrape it. They must also delete it when a user disconnects.

Google also tells developers to screen for prompt injection, hidden commands in emails or files that can hijack AI agents. Some controls are still to come, including letting users see and revoke agents’ access themselves.

Other firms are drawing lines too. In October, Charles Schwab said it blocks outside agents from moving client money.

Hackers are trying to get GTA 6 running on PC, Mac, and Linux

Grand Theft Auto 6's official release seemed like the end for apparent hacker Cyberleek, who raised millions of dollars in cryptocurrency by sharing unauthorized video of the open-world game ahead of launch. Rather than fading into obscurity, Cyberleek upped the ante by releasing an apparent build of GTA 6 onto the internet. And now, hackers, vibe coders, and programming enthusiasts are rushing to try and make GTA 6 playable on PC before Rockstar does.

Those following along might recall Cyberleek as the hacker who spent weeks provoking Rockstar Games. In addition to supplying the internet with leaked footage of the unfinished version of the game, Cyberleek also critiqued Rockstar and parent company Take-Two Interactive's business practices. The leaker claimed they objected to GTA 6's digital-only release as well as its pre-order bonuses. Cyberleek encouraged Rockstar and Take-Two to change course by dangling the possibility of sharing the files they were using to play the game to the public.

This suggestion was particularly shocking since Rockstar has not made the game available outside of console platforms. The game also doesn't exist on a disc, meaning that players don't have access to the game files. Without such data, players can't peek under the hood of the game — and they definitely could not tinker with it in unintended ways, like forcing the characters to appear without clothes in the overworld.

Cyberleek's motives seemed questionable, especially since the hacker gated the release of the alleged files behind monetary milestones. One of those early milestones included nude gameplay featuring the game's co-protagonist Lucia which successfully rallied fans to donate money. Once Cyberleek shared the footage, however, the hacker lost their leverage and the public moved on.

More recently, the hacker reappeared with a more appealing proposal: they would drop the apparent files free of charge. "The torch passes to the open source recomp and emulation projects," Cyberleek said on their website.

Polygon cannot confirm the veracity of these files; Rockstar Games and Xbox did not immediately respond to a request for comment. Yet even as some hoaxes related to Cyberleek seem likely, the larger claim about the game files appears to be legitimate. Since Cyberleek dropped the alleged executable, savvy GTA 6 fans gathering on Discord have shared steady progress on making the game accessible on Linux, Mac, and Windows PC. These initial attempts don't seem impressive, especially when the game barely functions, stutters, or displays rudimentary graphics without textures​​​​​​​ and lighting.

It would be a mistake to dismiss the reverse engineering projects though. The first big hurdle — getting the game to run at all — has already been overcome​​​​​​​. A few hours after entering the game for the first time, hackers managed​​​​​​​ to load some of GTA 6's shaders, which help the game get closer to its full visual fidelity.

Under normal circumstances, before artificial-intelligence coding assistance became readily available,​​​​​​​ incremental progress like this could have taken years for a game as complex as GTA 6. To be sure, it may still be weeks, if not months, before​​​​​​​ an unauthorized PC port of GTA 6 seems plausible. Given the enormous interest in the game — and the PC gaming community feeling slighted​​​​​​​ for being left out — a port may just be a matter of time. If it comes to pass, fans may also be able to access the game without paying for it.​​​​​​​

Rockstar and Take-Two, it seems, are determined to stop that from happening. At least one leading Discord server attempting to recompile the game has been banned, and the project leader has called it quits.

"I’m sorry," the former port overseer wrote on X.com. "I really want to make cool stuff but every single time shit happens that I can’t control so I’m stepping away."

About one in three young adults in a recent U.S. survey had turned to a chatbot with questions about drugs or alcohol. Most wanted to know what would happen if they used a substance.

Nearly all of the 995 young adults surveyed had used a chatbot like ChatGPT.

Of the 321 respondents who asked chatbots about substances, 155 did so at least monthly. Of those, 103 asked once a month, 39 once a week, and 13 daily or almost daily.

Mireia Triguero Roura, a postdoctoral researcher at the Rutgers Institute for Health, Health Care Policy and Aging Research, surveyed 995 Americans aged 18 to 29 in April 2026.

Pia Mauro, an associate professor at the Rutgers School of Public Health, was the co-author.

The respondents were recruited online, and the authors wrote that an online sample may make the habit look more common than it is.

The researchers said the finding concerned them, because the programs behind chatbots can easily reproduce false information.

“Using these tools can lead to misinformed or otherwise risky substance-use behavior among young people, who are the age group most affected by adverse effects of substance use,” Triguero Roura said.

A 2025 study in JAMA Network Open had found that about one in six young people used a chatbot for mental health advice. How many used one for drug and alcohol questions was unknown until this survey.

Among the 321 respondents who had asked a chatbot about substances, 92.8% wanted to know about the consequences of using one, such as the risks.

About half, 48.6%, asked for specifics about a substance, such as how much to take. Just over a third asked about addiction and related problems.

The chatbot users were also searching the old way. Of the 321, 298 had looked up substances on Google too, and across the whole survey 57.5% had done a substance-related Google search.

The chatbot was one more source, not a replacement.

The authors gave two reasons the numbers matter. Drugs or alcohol accounted for 25% of deaths among US young adults from 2018 to 2024, according to the federal mortality data they cited.

And news reports have described the overdose death of a young adult who had used a chatbot to learn about substance use.

Age made no measurable difference. Respondents aged 18 to 23 and those aged 24 to 29 were about equally likely to have asked, and so were students and non-students. Race and sex did make a difference.

Among the 139 Black respondents, 71 had asked a chatbot about substances, compared with 181 of the 607 white respondents.

Men were more likely than women to have asked about substances. Among men, 198 of 497 had asked, compared with 123 of 496 women.

Both gaps held after the researchers accounted for the other characteristics, including age and whether a respondent was a student.

Triguero Roura offered some possible reasons for the gaps, which the study didn’t test.

“The observed disparities in who chooses to use AI for drug-related inquiries could reflect differences in healthcare access, stigma, trust, and health information-seeking behaviors.”

Prolific, the platform the researchers used to recruit respondents, did not provide information on Hispanic origin, so the researchers could not report results separately for Hispanic young adults.

The 321 chatbot users also rated how helpful, reliable, and trustworthy they found chatbots on a scale of 1 to 5, once for everyday use and once for substance questions. Ratings were high for both.

Helpfulness scored higher for everyday use, 4.44 on the 5-point scale, than for substance questions, 4.12.

Trustworthiness was higher for substance questions, 3.66, than for everyday use, 3.51. Reliability was about the same for both.

So young adults found chatbots a little less helpful on drugs than on everything else, and a little more trustworthy. Anonymity may be part of the appeal, the authors wrote.

They also cited tests of chatbots on health questions that produced concerning results and noted that young adults could act on misinformation a chatbot repeats.

The researchers recruited respondents in April 2026 through Prolific, an online panel that verifies its participants and pays them for surveys. Each respondent earned 50 cents.

The sample was built to match the US young adult population, including roughly equal numbers of men and women.

The two age groups (18–23 and 24–29) were similar in size, and the racial breakdown was based on census survey figures.

Before asking about substances, the survey described what it meant by generative AI: chatbots or apps that mimic human conversation.

ChatGPT, Google Gemini, Meta AI, and Snap’s My AI were the examples given. Of the 995 people who passed the quality checks, 968 had used one at some point.

The 27 who hadn’t were counted as not having asked about substances. Five more respondents were dropped for failing the checks.

The researchers asked who used chatbots for substance questions, not what the chatbots told them.

Whether the answers were accurate, and what young adults did with them, is the open question, the authors wrote.

The sample is a limit too. People who sign up for online surveys may be more online, and more aware of AI, than young adults in general, so the one-in-three figure may run high.

Years of Being a Private Investigator Taught Me So Much About Dating Men

And it all started with me knowing something was up with “Dirty John” long before he became infamous.

Years before he became “Dirty John,” an infamous stalker who preyed on a young woman while carrying a butcher knife hidden in a Del Taco bag, I met John Meehan over dinner at the Chateau Marmont. He was dating my friend.

It was 2010, and my friend Kate had recently met a handsome, charming anesthesiologist named John Meehan on a dating website. She was smitten. He was successful, attentive, and apparently desperate to commit. He'd even asked for a key to her house. Kate was head over heels; I was spooked. Something about John didn't add up.

Over dinner, John talked endlessly about his medical career, including the technical details of surgeries with gas ratios and how much money he made. Yet when I asked which medical school he'd attended, he became strangely evasive. When the bill arrived, John meticulously calculated his share down to the penny, despite having spent the evening boasting about his seven-figure income. Maybe he was just frugal. But there was a growing collection of little things that didn't make sense.

Kate thought I was being cynical. At the time, I was an investigative journalist and former sex columnist living in L.A. who had recently enrolled in private investigator school. So, sure, maybe that wasn’t too misplaced. But I trust my instincts and genuinely thought she was in danger—when John left the restaurant, I borrowed my then-husband's Mercedes and followed him. John had told Kate he lived with his sister in Pasadena. He drove somewhere else entirely.

Over the following days, I began digging. I discovered that John wasn't the doctor he claimed to be—he was a nurse anesthetist with a long criminal history, had lost his professional license, and had been accused of stalking women.

I had mixed feelings about telling Kate the truth. I knew that I had to break the bad news, but I was super nervous when I showed up to our happy hour date carrying a printout of John’s mug shot. Fortunately, she believed me and broke things off. And although I suspected at the time that he was a con artist with a serious capacity for violence, I was left feeling helpless.

Years later, I listened in horror as the Dirty John podcast chronicled what happened when John Meehan entered his wife Debra Newell’s life. Their story mirrored his and Kate’s, except that when she tried to break up with him, things took a much darker turn. He eventually attacked her adult daughter, Terra Newell, who bravely fought back and killed him in self-defense. Hearing that, I was relieved that Kate had escaped. But I was also haunted by the knowledge that recognizing a dangerous man hadn't stopped him from finding another victim.

From sitting outside no-tell motels on matrimonial surveillance to piecing together a crime victim’s last day on earth, I’ve seen many situations where people get into dangerous situations—from confronting love rivals to going back for one last suitcase—all for love.

Here’s what detective work has taught me about dating:

Before I became a private investigator, I was a sex and dating columnist for The Independent in London. I spent years documenting my romantic adventures, writing about everything from casual sex to heartbreak. I've always been fascinated by the similarities between dating and detective work.

In both instances, you’re sitting across from a relative stranger. You’re asking questions, and you’re trying to figure out whether they are telling the truth—all the while hoping that, in the dating realm, any skeletons you unearth are metaphorical, and not decomposing in someone’s crawlspace.

But there's one enormous difference. When I'm investigating someone professionally, I'm looking for inconsistencies. When I'm falling for someone, I'm looking for reasons to believe them. And that's where things get dangerous.

It’s been scientifically proven that love causes a dopamine reaction in the brain similar to eating pounds of chocolate or snorting cocaine. Think about it: Would you make any other major life decision—like who to live with, introduce to your children or combine finances with—while high? That’s why I love the idea of a “sexual sober friend,” aka someone who will tell you straight up if you are being totally irrational. Of course, you have to actually be willing to take said friend’s advice–see below!

One of the most useful lessons I learned in PI school was to not to “give people the benefit of the doubt.” Instead, we say, “Trust, but verify.”

When you're investigating a crime, you build a timeline. You figure out what happened, when it happened, and whether the evidence supports the accounts you've been given. I think romantic relationships benefit from a much less obsessive version of the same principle.

Someone tells you they're looking for a serious relationship. Do their actions support that statement? Do they make plans? Follow through? Introduce you to people in their lives? Respect your boundaries? Or do they deliver a spectacular speech about wanting to find their soulmate and then disappear for eleven days because, apparently, their phone fell into the sea? A charming explanation is not the same thing as a credible one.

I once worked on a surveillance case involving a wife whose husband worked a boring day job as a dentist...but she was never allowed to come to his office, and he would disappear for hours in the evening. When he told her that he wanted a divorce and was leaving, she wanted to know where he was going, so she hired me and my partner.

After 18 hours of surveillance, I was able to confirm what six years of couples’ therapy had failed to deliver: confirmation that not only was he cheating on her with his receptionist, which I captured on camera while hiding in a deserted parking lot, but that he was leading a double life as a wannabee-Soprano small-time strip club drug dealer.

The next day, as I played the video evidence of his double life, writing prescriptions for several dancers, smoking pot, and shopping the aisles of Trader Joe’s for munchies, she told me that she had suspected for years that something was off, but she never felt ready to ask him the questions that would shatter her world. That’s happened to me a lot when I was doing matrimonial surveillance: People will tell me about finding incriminating photographs, suspicious messages, or underwear that clearly doesn't belong to them—and then ask me whether I think something is wrong.

Often, they're not really asking me to uncover the truth. They're asking me to give them permission to believe what they've already seen. Which brings me to my next principle:

I believe in intuition, but I don’t believe that it’s supernatural magic. Sometimes it's simply our brains recognizing patterns before we've consciously identified them.

At that first dinner with John Meehan, I couldn't immediately explain why I felt uncomfortable. But as the evening continued, I used my investigative training to spot the inconsistencies: his evasiveness, his elaborate claims, the mismatch between the person he presented and the details of his life. The important part wasn't that I had a bad feeling, it was that I paid attention to the feeling and then looked for facts.

Even after years of studying human behavior, I still sometimes find myself making the same mistakes I warn other people about.

While working as a journalist on a murder investigation in Arkansas, I became attracted to someone I was interviewing, someone who had been good friends with a possible person of interest. I knew perfectly well that personal chemistry could interfere with my judgment but found myself getting involved anyway.

Investigative work can teach you to be skeptical, but skepticism isn't a force field. You can know that someone is wrong for you and still want them. And you can be exceptionally good at uncovering other people's secrets while remaining spectacularly talented at hiding things from yourself.

A lot of people expect me to turn into Nancy Drew with every man I meet. There’s some truth to that; like most women, I do a Google deep dive before meeting someone for the first time.

But after spending years investigating cheating spouses, con artists, and eventually a cold-case murder, I've become convinced that there's a fine line between healthy curiosity and turning your love life into a full-time surveillance operation.

I don't think women should spend their evenings running background checks on every man who buys them a cocktail. Nor should they secretly follow their partners or interpret every unanswered text as evidence of a double life.

I never invade any significant others’ privacy and never would. If I don’t trust them, the relationship isn’t worth it to me. Because tracking people, online or otherwise, is exhausting. And I already have enough of that in my day job!

For years, I thought being a good investigator meant finding answers to every question. Now I think one of the most important skills—both in detective work and in dating—is knowing when you have enough information to make a decision. And when it's time to stop investigating and get on with your life.

Planes, Trains And Automobiles' Deleted Ending Was Rightfully Cut

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Planes, Trains and Automobiles was originally supposed to have a completely different ending, but thankfully it was removed before release. Few comedy movies have endings quite as emotionally satisfying as Planes, Trains and Automobiles. John Hughes' 1987 road-trip comedy spends most of its running time putting advertising executive Neal Page (Steve Martin) through an increasingly ridiculous series of travel disasters, largely thanks to his unwanted traveling companion, Del Griffith (John Candy). However, beneath the hilarious arguments, unfortunate misunderstandings and spectacular misfortunes is a surprisingly moving story about loneliness, friendship and the importance of appreciating other people.

Planes, Trains and Automobiles’ final moments bring that story together beautifully. After finally reaching Chicago, Neal initially leaves Del behind, only to realize that his traveling companion has nowhere to go. He returns to the train station, discovers the truth about Del's circumstances, and invites him home for Thanksgiving. It's a wonderfully understated conclusion that allows both characters to experience a genuine emotional breakthrough.

However, this wasn't always how Hughes intended to finish the movie. The original Planes, Trains and Automobiles screenplay featured a considerably longer ending, including a more explicit explanation of Del's tragic circumstances and an extended Thanksgiving dinner with Neal's family. Although the additional material offered some interesting character details, Hughes ultimately made the right decision by cutting it.

In the original Planes, Trains and Automobiles screenplay, Neal initially leaves Del behind after they finally reach Chicago. When he boards the train home, Del somehow turns up at his destination, prompting Neal to question why he is still following him. Del eventually explains that his wife, Marie, died eight years earlier and that he has been living out of his trunk, moving between motels and occasionally attaching himself to strangers for companionship (via Den of Geek).

The screenplay included a much longer explanation of Del's circumstances, detailing his wife's illness, their inability to have children, and his increasing difficulty coping with life alone. He also explains that Thanksgiving is particularly painful because it reminds him of the family he never had. Rather than Neal figuring out the truth, this version has Del explicitly explain his loneliness and why he attaches himself to other people.

The original ending then continued to Neal's family home, where Del joined the Thanksgiving celebrations. The screenplay included an extended dinner sequence, with Del expressing his gratitude for being welcomed into a family gathering. Audiences at test screenings reportedly did not respond well to the ending, finding Del’s monolog frustrating and unintentionally funny, prompting the change.

The change to the Planes, Trains and Automobiles ending gives Del considerably more dignity. Throughout Planes, Trains and Automobiles, his relentless friendliness is frequently presented as a source of frustration for Neal. He's loud, intrusive, and seemingly incapable of recognizing when somebody wants to be left alone. However, the final revelation reframes his behavior without suggesting that his personality is simply a disguise for his loneliness.

In the original screenplay, Del's explanation risks making his invitation to Thanksgiving feel like an act of charity. Neal discovers the truth because Del effectively has to ask for help, and their reconciliation becomes dependent on an extended confession. Although the material provides valuable background, it also removes some of the emotional complexity from their relationship. Del becomes somebody to pity rather than somebody Neal has genuinely learned to appreciate.

The theatrical version is much more thoughtful. Neal doesn't need Del to explain his situation because he has finally started paying attention to him. Audiences don't need to see Del sitting around the table, meeting every member of Neal's family or explaining how grateful he is to have somewhere to spend the holiday. The simple image of the two men walking together with Del's enormous trunk before settling on Del's earnest smile communicates everything necessary,

Planes, Trains and Automobiles is at its best when its comedy and sentiment exist alongside one another rather than competing for attention. The theatrical ending preserves that balance, allowing the audience to experience the same realization as Neal. Hughes understood that the most affecting way to conclude Del and Neal's journey throughout Planes, Trains and Automobiles wasn't to explain their newfound friendship, but to show that it had finally happened.

How brewers can protect beers from smoke-tainted hops

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Study: Brewers can still use up to 50% of smoke-tainted hops without affecting beer taste and aroma.

The 2020 Oregon wildfires burned over 1 million acres, destroyed thousands of structures, and killed 11 people. Smoke from the fires spread across the entire region; this “smoke taint” adversely affected not only local wine production, but also hops grown for beer brewing. Since the Pacific Northwest produces more than 95 percent of US hops and about 40 percent of the global supply, the potential economic impact is significant.

That’s why Oregon State University (OSU) scientists have been studying how brewers could offset the negative impacts of smoke taint while still incorporating affected hops in their brews. Most recently, they found that it all depends on how much tainted hops are included and when hops are added during the brewing process, according to a new paper published in the Journal of the American Society of Brewing Chemists.

As previously reported, the chemistry of brewing beer is a very active area of research. For instance, in 2018, OSU chemist Thomas Shellhammer—who also co-authored this latest paper—identified the growing popularity of “dry hopping” (adding hops after fermentation, common for pale ales) among craft brewers as the likely culprit of the exploding beer bottle phenomenon.

All beer contains hops, a key flavoring agent that also imparts useful antimicrobial properties. (Beer spoils quickly without them.) Brewers mash and steep grain in hot water, which converts all that starch into sugars. This is traditionally the stage where hops are added to the liquid extract (wort) and boiled to give the beer that hint of bitterness. During the boiling process, a portion of the resins (alpha acids) in hops isomerize into iso-alpha acids. That chemical rearrangement of the molecules is what produces bitterness. Yeast is then added to trigger fermentation, turning the sugars into alcohol.

Far from being the chemically inert ingredient brewers have long assumed, hops contain starch enzymes that, under certain conditions, can boost their activity to such a great extent that excessive re-fermentation occurs (“hop-creep”) after bottling. Dry-hopped beers produce significantly higher CO2 levels and packaging pressures, because there is much more re-fermentation going on than usual. And sometimes that pressure builds until the bottle explodes.

In the wake of the 2020 wildfires, Shellhammer turned his attention to smoke-tainted hops. The primary culprit is volatile phenols (VP), which are produced when the lignin in plant cells is exposed to the high heat of a wildfire. Earlier this year, the OSU team discovered that smoke exposure while the hops are drying increased VP uptake and substantially altered the hops’ aroma; those with higher levels took on meaty, smoky, and artificial BBQ notes; medicinal flavors have also been reported. Those notes were so strong that they overpowered more desirable beery qualities, like citrus notes or aroma intensity.

For this latest project, Shellhammer worked with graduate student Cade Jobe to harvest Idaho-grown Cascade hops. Then they “smoked” the hops in kilns using wood pellets injected into the air intake systems, then dried them. They also harvested and dried non-smoked hops as a control.

Credit: Ellie Lafferty/Oregon State University Credit: Ellie Lafferty/Oregon State University

Next, Shellhammer and Jobe brewed several batches of beer, blending smoke-tainted hops with untainted hops at different levels (25,  50, and 75 percent), along with beer brewed using only tainted hops and beer brewed with no tainted hops. They did this using both a dry-hopping process and one where they added hops during wort boiling. They measured the VP concentrations of each batch of beer with gas chromatography. Finally, a panel of 24 blind tasters evaluated each beer.

Shellhammer and Jobe found that dry-hopping with smoke-tainted hops resulted in increased VP concentrations; the more tainted hops were included, the higher those concentrations. The tasters reported more intense smoky aromas in those beers as well as meaty, smoky, medicinal, and artificial BBQ flavors.

By contrast, when tainted hops were added during the boil, the resulting beer was pretty much indistinguishable from beers brewed with no smoke-tainted hops, even though the former still showed the same increases in VP concentrations. There was no discernible difference when hops were added during the wort boiling stage.

The authors suggest brewers could blend smoke-tainted hops with untainted batches when dry-hopping, keeping the smoke-tainted hops to less than 50 percent. This would still allow brewers to use tainted batches of hops, reducing the economic impact of wildfires on beer production.

“Hop growers and brewers are looking for guidance on whether and how to use smoke-affected hops,” said Jobe. “This research begins to provide some practical information to help hop growers and brewers understand how smoky hops impact beer so that they can make the best decisions.”

There’s still more research to be done to confirm the precise ratios and to replicate these initial findings. Future research should also explore the impact of hops exposed to smoke under field conditions, when the plant is still metabolically active, along with consumer studies to evaluate whether beer drinkers more generally will find the flavors of beers made with blended hops (tainted and untainted) acceptable. This Classic Italian Sandwich Is Extra Crispy & Delicious

Everybody celebrates Italy for pizza, pasta, and gelato, and while they're classics for a reason, we implore you to explore Italy's unrivaled gastronomic tapestry a little deeper. Trust us, there are some serious gems hidden just beneath the surface. One of our favorite Italian street foods that you need to try is porchetta, Italy's answer to crispy pork belly. Irresistibly crunchy and bursting with indulgent flavors, it makes for a seriously delicious sandwich filler.

Porchetta is a style of pork roast, but is completely unique in its preparation. It's typically made from a whole deboned pig or a large cut of pork belly, which is laid flat and heavily rubbed on the inside with salt, garlic, and black pepper, alongside quintessential Italian seasonings like rosemary, sage, and fennel seeds. The pork is then rolled back together and slow-roasted for several hours, allowing the interior to turn soft and tender while the skin develops a beautiful crackling.

Porchetta is a meal in itself, but goes even better in a sandwich. Start out with a thick Italian white bread, something that won't turn soggy when paired with the pork's rich meat juices and sauces — ciabatta or focaccia work perfectly here. To balance the richness, add something bright with a little zing. We think salsa verde is an excellent choice, since the lemon juice and vinegar bring a sharp acidity, while fresh parsley keeps the heavy roast from feeling cloying — it pairs perfectly with a Cuban interpretation of the recipe as well. Rather than piling on thick slabs of porchetta, slice it thinly, so it stays tender and folds nearly into the bread, and make sure every sandwich gets a few pieces of the crackling skin for a satisfying crunch. As for what else to put on the sandwich, draw inspiration from Bobby Flay's favorite Roman porchetta sandwich.

You should have no problem finding pre-rolled porchetta at the butcher, but why not save your money and make it at home instead? We promise it's not as intimidating as it looks. For the juiciest cut that's also easiest to roll and inexpensive, pick up a skin-on pork belly. Its exceptionally high fat content keeps the roast moist and makes it incredibly forgiving to work with, so even the least confident cooks won't have to worry about overcooking it.

Porchetta is the star of the sandwich, and it's the crackling skin that really steals the show, so getting it earth-shatteringly crispy is essential. Here, the secret to a crunchy crust is dryness, so before you get to work on seasoning, leave the pork uncovered in the fridge overnight to let the surface dry out. Many cooks also salt the meat generously before its rest, which draws out even more moisture. And before roasting, it's good practice to pat the skin completely dry, since any lingering moisture turns to steam and keeps the skin from crisping.

To keep the sandwich from turning into a soggy mess, it's essential to let your porchetta rest for around 15 to 30 minutes after roasting. It might be tempting to carve it right away and admire your hard work, but slicing too soon sends the flavorful juices flooding into the chopping board and then straight into the bread, which even the sturdiest ciabatta can't hold back. The logic is the same as with steak because resting gives the juices time to redistribute through the muscle, leaving the meat deliciously tender and flavorful.

Stephen King's 6 Non-Horror Shows, Ranked Worst To Best

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Stephen King’s name is synonymous with horror, but he’s written in multiple genres over the decades. From sci-fi thrillers like The Dead Zone to fantasy series like The Dark Tower, King's books are incredibly varied. While it might seem like every TV adaptation of King’s novels involves horror (to be fair, most do), there have actually been some adaptations of his non-horror works over the years. However, as with most Stephen King adaptations, the quality varies across the board.

Some of these non-horror adaptations are mostly faithful to the source material, while others are so different from the original work, they have almost nothing in common. A few are well-written, incredibly compelling, and look fantastic. Then, there are the others that might be entertaining, but suffer from writing and pacing issues. A few Stephen King non-horror works still haven’t been adapted or used to their full potential, so while this handful of shows is all that’s available now, hopefully, more adaptations will be made in the future.

Golden Years is likely a show few remember, as it aired only seven episodes on CBS in 1991. For those wondering when they missed the Stephen King novel of the same name — there wasn’t one. This adaptation actually came after King had written down the idea for it in a notebook, where it sat for years. He eventually decided to write it as a “novel for television,” according to an interview in The New York Times, which resulted in this miniseries.

While Golden Years was designed as a lead-in to a regular series, CBS opted not to pick it up. As a result, the TV show ended on a cliffhanger (though the home video version changed a few minutes of the last episode to give it a more hopeful ending). The series followed Harlan Williams, an elderly janitor who is exposed to chemicals after an explosion in a secret lab.

As a result, he begins aging in reverse, which gets the attention of those in charge of the lab. Soon, Harlan and his wife have to go on the run, where they meet Terry Spann, who has decided to help protect them. Of course, Harlan’s sudden youthful tendencies aren’t the only powers to develop; as time goes on, other strange things begin to happen. Starring Keith Szarabajka, Frances Sternhagen, R.D. Call, and Felicity Huffman, Golden Years is a decent little sci-fi thriller that mostly suffers from pacing issues and underdeveloped plot elements (and a lack of conclusion).

The second-longest-running Stephen King adaptation, Haven, is based on King’s The Colorado Kid only in the loosest sense. While the novel is a crime mystery involving the mysterious death of a stranger and a pair of older journalists sharing his tale with a newer, much younger journalist, Haven basically borrows only a few names, the location, and minor elements from the novel. The show still involves a dead man nicknamed The Colorado Kid, but it’s more of a procedural with supernatural elements.

Some Stephen King TV shows have fallen under the radar, among them a five-season sci-fi thriller that all fans of the King of Horror should watch.

When FBI Agent Audrey Parker arrives in the small town of Haven on a routine case, she soon discovers more is going on beneath its surface. The people of the town are afflicted with what they call The Troubles. These supernatural afflictions range from having what one draws come true to being a human bullet magnet. She soon quits the FBI and joins the local police, helping those who are Troubled, while also trying to figure out the mystery of her past.

It’s kind of like The X-Files but with a lower budget and less charm, and eventually the lore gets amazingly convoluted. However, Emily Rose as Park is compelling, while Eric Balfour as Duke Crocker is a blast. Best of all, Edge (the wrestler who used to tag team with Christian in the early aughts) is in the show and does a surprisingly good job.

Under the Dome is another adaptation that varies considerably from its source material. However, in this case, Stephen King took to his website during its first season to reassure fans upset about the differences and to say he wholeheartedly approved of the changes thus far. He also pointed out that the novel covers eight days, while the TV show covers a far longer period, so some changes were necessary. Those changes worked, too, as the first season of the series was intriguing.

The series follows the town of Chester’s Mill, where, as one might guess, an indestructible, transparent dome appears one day, cutting the town off from the outside world. While the military and others outside try to figure out how to get rid of the dome, those inside are left to survive dwindling resources and the inevitability of people turning on one another as fear takes hold.

However, fans and critics both soured on the series when it returned for its second and third season. Even King himself eventually decided he didn't like Under the Dome's small-screen adaptation, having initially been a fan. Unfortunately, the idea of a town stuck under a dome, told over multiple seasons, was spreading the premise a bit too thin. Under the Dome was reasonably decent as standard sci-fi fare, but it also leaned into its ridiculous elements a bit too much. Add in characters making increasingly irrational decisions, and things went far off course.

The longest-running King adaptation was The Dead Zone, which ran for six seasons. A seventh season was expected, but unfortunately, the series was canceled, partially due to financial concerns. That left the series hanging on a cliffhanger that answered some questions but raised many more. While it doesn’t have a Rotten Tomatoes score, it does carry a 7.3 on IMDb.

Stephen King's underrated six-season show is now available to stream for free, promising a blend of genres to appeal to different viewers.

Like the 1979 novel and the 1983 movie, the TV series follows Johnny Smith, a teacher who falls into a coma after a car accident and wakes up five years later. When he wakes, he finds himself having visions of the future and the past when he touches people and things. Like the novel, Johnny also discovers through a vision that politician Greg Stillson will kick off the apocalypse in the future. Unlike the novel, the show is much more of a procedural as Johnny begins solving crimes with the help of his abilities.

Overall, the sci-fi drama was enjoyable, with pretty decent visual effects depicting Johnny’s visions, even if it does veer away from the novel due to its long run. However, along with lacking a proper conclusion, The Dead Zone sometimes relied on overly formulaic storylines andoccasional shallow writing.

King explored the age-old question of what a person would do if given the chance to go back in time and prevent a tragedy in his sci-fi novel 11.22.63, when teacher Jake Epping goes back in time to stop the assassination of John F. Kennedy by Lee Harvey Oswald. The miniseries adaptation does the same, though with a few changes to the original storyline. For instance, George MacKay plays the character Bill Turcotte in 11.22.63's TV iteration, someone who doesn't exist in the book.

As Epping undertakes his mission, time seemingly begins pushing back through accidents that crop up and get in the way of his goal. He also falls in love in the past and tries to prevent smaller events while he’s there. However, as with all sci-fi time travel, the smallest of changes can have fairly dire consequences in the future.

It: Welcome to Derry is easily one of the best takes on Stephen King’s works, but a handful of other TV adaptations of his books are arguably better.

This King adaptation is well-made, and the design is incredibly authentic to the era it’s set in (not surprising, given how much research King did to make the novel historically authentic). Some fans weren’t keen on the changes to the novel’s plot, but overall, it’s a thought-provoking, bingeworthy eight episodes. The pacing does tend to drag a bit in the middle, but the series' emotional depth and strong performances save it from cratering.

An adaptation of King’s as yet unbeatable Bill Hodges trilogy, which includes the books Mr. Mercedes, Finders Keepers, and End of Watch, Mr. Mercedes was one of the best TV shows based on King’s works. The series stays far more faithful to the novels than most adaptations, and focuses on Bill Hodges, a retired detective who is still haunted by one particular case where someone drove a stolen Mercedes into a group of people at a local job fair, killing 16.

David E. Kelley (The Practice, Big Little Lies) developed the series, so it was led by someone who knew what they were doing when it came to crime thrillers. The first season pits Bill Hodges against the young psychopath Brady Hartsfield, and soon the two are drawn into a serious game of cat-and-mouse. This season is instantly intriguing, full of high tension, rich in suspense, and complex characters. The performances are outstanding, particularly by Brendan Gleeson as Bill and Harry Treadaway as Brady.

This hard-boiled detective thriller can be bleak at times, and though it isn’t horror, the subject matter can still feel creepy. While the other two seasons were decent, the quality declined a bit, and some fans feel the series should have ended after the first season. Mr. Mercedes suffered from pacing issues as time went on, as well as inconsistent writing that did it no favors. Still, if one only wants to watch a single Stephen King non-horror adaptation, this is probably the best bet.

Unfortunately, neither Meet the Fockers nor Little Fockers found the success of their predecessor. It is against that backdrop that Stiller, somewhat surprisingly, decided to take another stab at it 16 years later with the brand-new fourth installment, Focker-in-Law.

Focker-in-Law sees the return of the core cast members, with Stiller's Greg and De Niro's Jack still taking center stage. However, the franchise has added some star power to the next generation, with the DCU's Skyler Gisondo playing Greg's son Henry and singer and actress Ariana Grande playing his fiancé, Olivia. Perhaps, with that beefed-up cast, Universal was hoping for a repeat of the original that side-stepped the two follow-up flops. Sadly, Focker-in-Law offers much of the same.

In truth, Focker-in-Law got several genuine laughs out of me. It's impossible to deny how naturally funny Stiller is, and his dynamic with De Niro clearly works, given the legacy of the franchise. Yet, for as much as Greg's antics occasionally find their original charm, they also raise questions about whether this concept is well and truly played out. In fact, in a moment that is played for laughs, the movie points out that Greg is now older than Jack was when they first met.

Whereas in Meet the Parents in particular, he is a well-meaning, nervous future son-in-law who accidentally creates mayhem wherever he goes, some of Greg's behavior in Focker-in-Law is outright mean, and borders on cruel.

Unfortunately, the addition of Grande's Olivia fails to help. Ahead of the movie's release, there were grumbles about Grande joining the cast; however, she had plenty of potential to shine in the role. Her appearance in projects like Wicked have proven that she can, indeed, act (and she actually started out acting), and many of her Saturday Night Live skits have been incredibly funny, suggesting that she was up to the task of a comedy.

Yet, in Focker-in-Law, Grande's charm and wit are largely absent. Hampered by a script that doesn't seem to know what it wants to do with this new character, most of Olivia's laugh lines fall flat, and while the Meet the Parents franchise has always stretched the limits of realism, Olivia's skills, talents, and apparent ability to succeed in every single area of her life border on the absurd. In fairness, this has less to do with Grande and more to do with Focker-in-Law's refusal to give her nuance – or, frankly, any realistic, humanizing flaws.

Gisondo, meanwhile, is a standout among the cast. Teri Polo's Pam and Blythe Danner's Dina are all but sidelined, with the latter in particular being almost non-existent.

Still, Focker-in-Law isn't a complete flop. Those looking for a few good laughs and a nostalgia-packed return of the franchise will no doubt enjoy the movie, and even those who are just hoping for a feel-good film for the holiday season will have plenty of fun. It's far from the most inventive movie of 2026, to be sure, but it shouldn't be counted out altogether.

One of the hardest planets to see in the solar system — the distant ice giant Uranus — reaches its annual opposition on Wednesday (Nov. 25), just a day after the Beaver Supermoon. It's the best time to see the seventh planet from the sun, but you'll need to use stargazing binoculars or a good backyard telescope to find it.

Uranus is faint, slow-moving and rarely a target for casual stargazers. However, on Nov. 25, and, for a few weeks on either side, it will be at its closest point to Earth this year and will appear at its brightest, making it possible to spot.

Opposition occurs when Earth passes between Uranus and the sun; Uranus will be "opposite" the sun, hence, "opposition." The moment of opposition will happen at 6 p.m. ET on Nov. 25, according to EarthSky. Consequently, Uranus will rise in the east around sunset, reach its highest point in the sky around midnight and set in the west around sunrise.

You can find it in the constellation Taurus, close to the Pleiades open star cluster, also known as the Seven Sisters and M45. Uranus will be a few degrees southeast of the cluster, appearing as a faint point of light, about two-fifths of the distance between the Pleiades and Aldebaran, the brightest star in Taurus.

At magnitude +5.6, Uranus is technically visible to the naked eye under very dark skies. However, binoculars or a telescope will make it much easier to find.

Uranus as seen by NASA's Hubble Space Telescope.

At opposition, Uranus will be 18.4 astronomical units (AU) from Earth — about 1.7 billion miles (2.7 billion kilometers) away. Through a telescope, it will appear as a tiny blue-green disk. Its color comes from methane in its atmosphere, which absorbs red wavelengths of sunlight.

Named after Ouranos, the ancient Greek god of the sky and the heavens, according to the Planetary Society , Uranus is one of the least understood planets in the solar system. A day on Uranus lasts for about 17 hours, and the planet rotates sideways, according to NASA. It takes 84 Earth years to orbit the sun.

The distant planet has been visited only once, by NASA's Voyager 2 spacecraft on Jan. 24, 1986. However, contrary to what the probe found, scientists now think the planet emits its own internal heat. That could increase pressure on NASA to prepare a Uranus Orbiter and Probe (UOP), a mission concept the National Academy of Sciences recommended in 2022 as a high-priority planetary science mission for the next decade.

Patsnap, the database that hosts the filing, offers an AI-generated "technical title" that sums up the idea as a way of "enhancing user engagement."

In a sample prompt, the filing tells the AI to make a video where "the user will play the protagonist." Another example takes that a step further. If a cricket fan has been watching clips about a particular player, the AI could put them in a scene with that player or show them "cheering for India in the stands." New videos could be made on request or on a schedule, such as once a week or once a month. The launch allows institutional investors to earn returns on their stablecoin holdings by investing directly in the fund, eliminating the need to convert their digital assets into traditional currency before investing.

The Hong Kong dollar, distinct from mainland China’s yuan, has been pegged to the US dollar since 1983.

“Les Lalanne have become a global phenomenon, changing the way generations of collectors think about design,” says Jodi Pollack, chairman of 20th century design and major collections for the Americas. “A sheep can be a seat, a crocodile can be a console, and both can hold their own alongside the most important works in a collection.”

How to Deal With Holiday Arguments and Guilt Trips

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Ideally, the holidays are a time of happiness, cheer, and positive relationship interactions. In practice, however, we often need to manage some bad with the good. There are usually a few “bones to pick” over the Thanksgiving turkey and “elephants in the room” along with the Christmas tree…

So, to maximize your holiday joy (and minimize your angst), I have compiled some strategies to deal with dysfunctional games, family patterns, arguments, and emotional manipulation. By the end, you should have a good idea why Aunt Ruth keeps giving you a guilt trip, how Uncle Stu keeps making you lose your cool, and what exactly you can do about all of it. Then, maybe, you can enjoy yourself, too!

To start, according to Eric Berne in his book Games People Play (1964), people generally try to manipulate us because they want something from us—but they don’t believe they can get it honestly or fairly. These negative beliefs (called existential positions) come in two forms. On one hand, they can believe that you’re not OK and try to bully you because they think you don’t deserve their fair treatment. On the other hand, they can believe that they’re not OK (i.e., I’m not okay) and try to guilt trip you into feeling that you owe them something for nothing.

Thus, people play games, argue, and manipulate when they believe they are not OK, other people are not OK, or both. This allows them to get what they want without giving back or risking being fair and trusting. Such manipulations can have secondary benefits for them, too, like being excused from standards or responsibilities (I'm not OK), or feeling morally superior (You're not OK). Unfortunately, those bad beliefs and manipulative games also cause many of our hurt feelings and dysfunctional relationship patterns in the process as well.

How these game-playing relatives and friends push your emotional buttons was explored by Niven, Totterdell, and Holman (2009) and defined as negative affect regulation strategies. Put simply, they use emotional manipulation tactics to make you feel bad, so you give them what they want. There are four general tactics they can employ to emotionally manipulate, but they all share one common theme—they reject you and hurt your feelings, while also putting their feelings first.

Beyond that, they might choose to emotionally bully you by being disrespectful and insulting or bragging and trying to make you feel bad by comparison. This would fall under the “You’re not OK” belief and allow them to feel superior at your expense. In contrast, they might decide to guilt-trip you by criticizing how you treat them or withdrawing until you feel bad and comply with their wishes. This would be part of an “I’m not OK” belief and allow them to be excused from standards and responsibilities—like doing something worthwhile for you back. In either case, unless you know how to defend yourself, you might be left trying to choke down negative emotions, while they munch on dessert!

Fortunately, there are things that you can do to defend yourself—and possibly even improve your relationship with that unruly relative, partner, or friend, too. To begin, though, you need to decide whether you want to deal with the manipulation indirectly by diverting attention and emotion away from the problem, or directly by engaging attention and emotion toward addressing the problem. In other words, can you make light of Cousin Bessie’s snotty comments and change the subject, or do you need to talk it out and come to a better arrangement?

To help you decide, consider some indirect and direct softening techniques suggested by Overall, Simpson, and Struthers (2013). They were initially devised for addressing problems with emotionally avoidant or argumentative romantic partners—and serve as a foundation for dealing with relationship problems in my book Attraction Psychology (Nicholson, 2022). Beyond that, I have found them helpful with family and friends as well, especially when they believe one of us is not “OK” and are emotionally hurtful about it.

Dealing with issues indirectly requires that you “soften” by temporarily downplaying the severity of the problem, until their bullying or guilt-tripping lessens. To do so, it helps to build positive rapport by communicating your care, acceptance, and regard—while also minimizing their emotional manipulation attempts with positive feelings and humor (i.e., kill them with kindness). From there, you can redirect them toward other positive aspects about your relationship with them and points of agreement. Essentially, this is a lot like dealing with a toddler having a tantrum—make light of the outburst and find a more pleasant topic to change their mind and improve their behavior.

Dealing with issues directly, however, requires that you “soften” by acknowledging some aspect of their point of view as a base of agreement—without surrendering to the negative emotions from their bullying or guilt trip. Pretend you’re a cool TV lawyer who says, “We can agree that there is a problem here, but it would help us both to keep working on a better solution.” From there, you can show optimism about solving the disagreement and having a good relationship with them. Where you are able, also acknowledge some of their positive efforts in the past toward connecting and compromising with you. This can help improve their emotional outlook and motivate them to find healthier ways to interact with you in the future as well.

Whatever strategy you choose, I hope you have Happy Holidays and a Good New Year, too!

As inflation remains elevated—with the latest Consumer Price Index (CPI) coming in hotter than economists’ expectations—“resilient” growth in home prices continues to be eroded.

The September S&P Cotality Case-Shiller Home Price Index was up 0.2% month-over-month, recording a 2.1% annual gain—the same as last month. This is compared to annual inflation readings of 3.4% in both the September CPI and Personal Consumption Expenditures price index.

“Nominal home prices remain resilient, but inflation is once again widening the gap between nominal and real housing returns,” said Rebecca Kaufman, associate director of Commodities at S&P Dow Jones Indices. She noted that this marks the “16th consecutive month in which U.S. home prices declined in real terms.”

Realtor.com® Senior Economist Anthony Smith also noted that this month’s data “reflects sales closing from July through September, a period when mortgage rates edged higher, with Freddie Mac’s 30-year fixed rate averaging 6.54% in July, near 6.7% in August, and 6.86% in September.”

The 10-City Composite was up 0.2% month-over-month to a 3.5% annual gain (down from 3.6% last month), while the 20-City Composite was up 0.1% month-over-month to a 2.6% annual gain (same as last month).

Kaufman also noted that this month’s report “revealed a widening gap between seasonal weakness and underlying price momentum,” as the monthly numbers pre-seasonal adjustment were declines across the board for the National, 10-City Composite and 20-City Composite Indices (-0.3%, -0.5% and -0.5%, respectively).

“That contrast suggests that some of the apparent monthly weakness reflects the normal fall slowdown rather than a broad deterioration in home prices,” she explained.

Smith agreed that this was reflective of “the normal fall slowdown,” as the “market’s momentum has weakened since September closed.”

Taking a look at the 20 cities that make up the index, Chicago once again led the pack (for the 6th consecutive month) with a 6.9% annual gain. Following behind were New York City at 5.8% and Miami at 4.6%.

Kaufman called out that Miami’s growth is “particularly notable,” as “after moving into fourth place among the 20 cities in July and August, it has now climbed into the top three, a sharp reversal from its comparatively weak performance in 2025.”

In terms of declines, Seattle was again the leader for the 4th consecutive month with an annual fall of 1.9%. Denver (-1.0%) and Dallas (-0.9%) followed behind.

For the picture ahead, Smith said that the “rate and demand backdrop has become more challenging since September closed,” as mortgage rates are now above 7% and inflation remains fairly elevated—with “expectations for a Fed rate hike in December.”

“If financing costs stay elevated into the winter, the plateau in price growth could give way to renewed softening, particularly in supply-heavy Southern and Western markets,” he continued.
