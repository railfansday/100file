Should You Invest Now or Wait Until 2027? Here's What a Century of History Says.

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Waiting until the new year to reset comes with some drawbacks.

The S&P 500 (^GSPC -0.40%) last hit a closing record high on Oct. 6. Since then, it lost 4.4% in a single day and was as much as 6% below its all-time high. Even now, the index is roughly 3% below its October peak.

Volatility has picked up, investors have been rotating into non-tech equities, and there are certainly some potential vulnerabilities in the U.S. stock market heading into 2027. It would be understandable if someone were compelled to sit on the sidelines and wait for some of the signals to clear. But history says that investors who wait tend to come out behind.

Over the past century, the S&P 500 has returned around 10% annually. That number includes every recession, bear market, and major economic event along the way. The key, however, is remaining invested throughout these periods.

Charles Schwab (SCHW +1.73%) did a study recently about hypothetical investors who invested their money regularly along with folks who invested at market tops and bottoms. It found that people who invested entirely at the beginning of every calendar or monthly throughout the year actually didn't outperform a person who had perfect market timing (which, of course, is impossible). Even the person who invested at the market top every year still produced solid returns over the long term.

The only person who really fell behind was the one who consistently sat on the sidelines.

The lesson is that even if you consistently invest at the worst possible time, being in the market is still better than being out of it over the long term.

JPMorgan Asset Management (JPM +0.98%) did a study of forward-looking returns when investing at all-time highs versus all other days. It turns out that investing at all-time highs was actually better for long-term returns than when the market was below them.

This sounds counterintuitive. A lot of people think that upside is somehow limited when the S&P 500 is at record highs. But stocks are usually at record highs for a reason. Companies and the economy are likely performing well, and that often helps drive future returns.

The biggest takeaway is that no one knows how the stock market is going to perform at any given time. So there's little point in trying to predict it by market timing.

The goal of long-term investing is to slowly accumulate wealth over time. The best path for that is to simply invest regularly and consistently. Don't worry about investing at the "right" time. Just focus on investing, period.

Sometimes you'll buy at highs. Sometimes you'll buy at lows. But you'll be invested at all times, and that ends up being the biggest key to success of them all.

What Is Science, Really? Even Scientists Can’t Agree

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Even scientists in the same specialty disagree about science’s purpose, a survey finds, raising concerns that AI could automate one narrow approach at others’ expense.

Is science a search for objective truth, an exercise in creative problem-solving, or both? A study published in Royal Society Open Science found that scientists disagree about the meaning and purpose of science more than the general public does.

Study leader Marina Dubova, a former Santa Fe Institute postdoctoral researcher, is now an assistant professor of psychology at the University of California, Berkeley. Working with Princeton University researcher Qiawen E. Liu, she surveyed 91 scientists and 99 members of the public. Participants evaluated 18 historical descriptions of science, including science as detective work, a search for truth, and a mirror reflecting reality.

Scientists and the public generally agreed that science involves following curiosity and solving mysteries. The public tended to share an idealized picture of science as a reliable way to discover how the world works, placing less emphasis on creativity or uncertainty. That outlook could shape how people judge research and help explain their frustration when scientists cannot give firm answers or revise their conclusions as new evidence emerges.

The public’s views were closer to those of physicists and biologists than to those of researchers in psychology or the social sciences. One possible explanation, Dubova suggests, is that physics and biology feature more prominently in science education. More research is needed to test that connection.

Some scientists emphasized the search for objective truth, while others gave more weight to the judgment and creativity researchers bring to their work. They also disagreed about whether research follows established methods or requires improvisation. Few of the 18 descriptions received scientists’ full support, and they often had reservations even about ideas they generally agreed with.

“What was surprising to me was that there was quite a bit of disagreement between scientists, including scientists who are in the same subfield,” says Dubova. She suspects that researchers develop their approaches partly through experience, much as chefs develop different instincts about when to follow a recipe and when to improvise. Those differing conceptions of science could influence which discoveries researchers pursue.

That diversity also raises a question for efforts to automate scientific research. As researchers develop artificial intelligence systems capable of conducting science, whose understanding of the work will those systems embody? Dubova worries that automation could reinforce one narrow conception of science at the expense of other valuable approaches.

For Dubova, the study is an early step toward understanding how these outlooks shape research itself. “Don’t take the common understanding of science for granted,” she says. “Always ask. Always be curious about what someone has in mind.”

Netflix's New 12-Part Sci-Fi Mystery Already Feels Like a Masterpiece Just 3 Episodes In

Even in a sunless world, something has to bloom

Even in a sunless world, something has to bloom

Some of my favorite science fiction stories take one seemingly impossible premise and explore all the ways it ripples through everyday life, from Gattaca to The Leftovers. Netflix's latest original anime is another perfect example of that trope.

From a surprise collaboration between two iconic anime studios, Sunrise and Shaft, Fool Night imagines a future world where the sun has long disappeared, leaving humanity with the unusual solution of turning volunteer humans into plants for oxygen. It's a bleak setup, made all the more gripping by a visual identity that constantly juxtaposes human decay with budding flora. Netflix only provided us with the first three episodes (out of 12 total) for review, and I'm entirely hooked. Between its sharp character work, unsettling world-building, and melancholic atmosphere, Fool Night is shaping up to be one of the most striking new shows of Fall.

Based on the manga by Kasumi Yasuda and directed by Atsuyuki Yukawa, Fool Night is a surprisingly intimate story set against an enormous environmental catastrophe. Rather than throwing viewers into an expansive science fiction mystery, Yukawa takes his time introducing its main characters and how their lives become intertwined by circumstances neither could have anticipated. It's a more methodical introduction than some might expect, offering plenty of room to establish the people at the center of a world literally steeped in darkness and comforted only by the discarded remains of the volunteers transformed into grotesque flora.

That’s the kind of setting Fool Night presents, a miserable environment governed by survival. With the sun gone, oxygen increasingly scarce, and most plant life dying off as a result, humanity has turned to a process called transfloration, allowing terminally ill volunteers to undergo a procedure that implants a seed inside their bodies for a payout of 10 million yen. Over the course of two years, that seed gradually grows until that person becomes what’s called a “spiritflor,” essentially a sprout that helps replenish the oxygen humans need to survive. It's an extraordinary solution to an extraordinary problem, although the implications are considerably more disturbing when you see what it actually looks like.

Spiritflor are everywhere in Fool Night. Their bodies litter the streets, fill abandoned alleyways, and even occupy the stairwells and corridors of apartment buildings. Some look almost beautiful, with sprouts and foliage emerging from what remains of their human bodies. Others are considerably more grotesque, their former appearances barely recognizable beneath the vegetation. They're like haunting reminders of the people who once lived there, transformed into something simultaneously beautiful and deeply unsettling. The worst part, of course, is that they're still alive.

The series introduces its grotesque imagery ingeniously in its opening sequence, establishing how accustomed the world’s inhabitants are to their surrounding environment. Standing outside a guitar store, a young child named Toshio Kamiya insists to his friend Yomiko Horai that he’ll be a musician one day. It’s pure wishful thinking, and Yomiko tries to ground him, even as Toshio beams at the thought of future stardom. As they’re walking down an alleyway, with Toshio making little guitar noises and pretending to play as he twirls around, the lighthearted moment is juxtaposed with the bodies of spiritflor lining the fences around them.

It's an especially eerie scene, with the children paying little mind to the grotesquery around them. They've presumably walked past these bodies countless times before, and that's perhaps the most disturbing part of the entire sequence. This is simply what life looks like now. It's an incredibly effective way of introducing the series' central conceit without stopping to explain every little detail. The world has adapted to something that should be horrifying, and the children have grown up knowing nothing else.

What makes Fool Night immediately compelling isn't just its grim premise and setting, but how sharply it uses the childhood friends Toshio and Yomiko to expose the class divide at the heart of its dying world. Now adults and reunited after years apart, the two represent opposite ends of transfloration’s bleak economy.

Toshio is trapped at the absolute bottom of society. A disillusioned factory worker crushed by poverty, systemic debt, and an abusive home life, he’s left with no viable future other than to sell his body to the state. In doing so, Toshio trades his remaining two years of humanity for a 10-million-yen payout that he barely gets to keep — only to get robbed almost immediately following the procedure. Yomiko, by contrast, sits comfortably on the clinical side of the glass as a researcher at Transflora Medical Facility 4, approving candidates and overseeing transfloration procedures, all while the morality of what she’s doing hangs over her head like a creeping vine gathering blight.

Their contrasted situations give the series its strongest dramatic friction. Early on, Yomiko is defined by a chilling professional detachment. When a colleague admits he feels like they killed someone after performing a procedure, she coolly insists they’ve "extended" a life, hiding behind the rhetoric of personal choice. It’s easy for her to rationalize transfloration when the patients are strangers, and that’s precisely why Toshio’s sudden appearance in her waiting room hits so hard. Reuniting after years apart only to find someone she genuinely cares for cornered into becoming a plant shatters her clinical distance, turning an abstract ethical debate into an immediate personal crisis.

Rather than bogging down in misery, however, the show smartly pivots their reunion into an intriguing partnership. When Yomiko discovers that the procedure has left Toshio with the bizarre ability to hear the voices of spiritflor, she pulls him into the facility's orbit. Alongside sour-faced Unsection Chief Jin Hiragishi, the two begin untangling the darker mysteries behind the program. It’s a sharp, character-first hook that grounds the show's high-concept sci-fi in genuine human stakes, one saddled against the backdrop of a looming murder investigation that could change the world forever.

If there's one thing that immediately separates Fool Night from other science fiction anime, it's the sheer confidence of its visual presentation — though, it definitely helps that two of the most legendary animation studios collaborated on the production. Sunrise, best known for its work on Gundam and Cowboy Bebop, and Shaft, the same studio behind the Monogatari series and Virgin Punk, have more than enough history behind them. Seeing them come together on a project of this caliber is mindblowing, and the results so far more than live up to the occasion.

What impresses me most isn't necessarily the scale of the animation, but how much attention the series pays to composition. There are so many shots that deliberately position characters in unexpected places, sometimes leaving enormous portions of the screen occupied by otherwise unimportant scenery. Rather than constantly following characters with the camera, Fool Night lets them exist within the environment, allowing the world around them to communicate just as much as the dialogue.

There's a particularly good example when Yomiko speaks with the front desk clerk about midway into the first episode, asking whether Toshio had gone through with the transfloration procedure. It's a relatively somber moment, with Yomiko and the desk clerk occupying just a small portion of the left side of the screen. Taking up almost the entirety of the composition is a plant just out of focus, filling the surrounding space rather than simply centering the shot on Yomiko’s back.

The same approach appears at the very start of Fool Night. As the child versions of Toshio and Yomiko walk down a fenced-off alleyway, Toshio launches into a short sprint like a musician would on a stage. Instead of directly following him, the camera tracks the fence on his left, moving along the cold steel as the jogging Toshio only takes up the right side of the frame. These are small decisions, but they help communicate the darker implications behind this captivating sci-fi dystopia and give the show a striking visual identity unlike anything I’ve seen before.

Fool Night isn't in a rush to lay all its cards on the table. While early episodes drop breadcrumbs of the wider conspiracy surrounding an investigation into bizarre murders involving plant-based lacerations and a shadowy organization attempting to stop transfloration procedures, the series largely holds its burgeoning mysteries at bay until the final moments of episode three. Rather than feeling sluggish, that restraint pays off by offering you a firm grasp on how transfloration has warped the show’s society and why these two leads are so deeply entangled within it.

Three episodes in, it's still too early to say where Fool Night will ultimately land, particularly with so much of its sunless world left unexplored, but what I've seen so far has me hooked. Its unsettling premise, wonderfully complicated characters, and gorgeous animation have already made for a compelling start. If the series can maintain this level of visual ambition while continuing to build on its central mysteries, I can't wait to see where Fool Night goes next.

The Design Fiend’s Guide to Tangier

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Moroccan city, a complex, culturally kaleidoscopic crossroads between Europe and Africa, is home to a savvy community of creatives whose stylish impact continues to grow. Word is out.

Above: The pool terrace at Villa Mabrouka, a 12-room hotel that was once the home of Yves Saint Laurent and Pierre Bergé, with gardens designed by Madison Cox.

Writing about Tangier in the 1950s, Truman Capote observed that “the days slide by less noticed than foam in a waterfall.” Decades later, this city on Morocco’s coast, across the Strait of Gibraltar from Spain, is significantly changed from Capote’s time, when it was an exotic retreat for café society’s demimonde, people such as Barbara Hutton and Malcolm Forbes. In recent years Tangier’s mosaic of villages—from the medina’s warren of whitewashed lanes to the Ville Moderne’s blocks of Art Deco towers to the hillside villas and gardens on Jebel Kebir—has been reinvigorated, in part by an enterprising mix of Tanjawis (the locals) and expats who continue to infuse the city with a fresh creative spirit.

Yet there is much that remains the same: the dreamy light that seduced artists from Eugène Delacroix to Henri Matisse to Mariano Fortuny, the overgrown gardens of casbah courtyards, and the cooling breezes coming off the confluence of Atlantic and Mediterranean waters. And there’s still plenty of the languorous lifestyle that captivated Capote.

“Tangier is all about settling in,” says Charlotte Claydon, whose whitewashed Moroccan Art Deco house has been in her family for more than a century. Known as Dar Sinclair, the residence sits in a grove of papyrus and lilies between thick palms and weeping willows. It’s one of numerous historic villas and townhouses renovated by international designers and other creatives who live here that can be rented. There’s arguably no better way to ease into the rhythms of daily life in Tangier. Pin Affleck, who arranges travel and accommodations through the Tangier Bureau, says that “going into a private house, you get the sense of being inside Tangier.”

When Claydon and her sister Jacquetta Wheeler began renting out Dar Sinclair, they received messages from guests worried about filling up a week. “We spent hours coming up with lists of activities, knowing full well they might only get to one or two,” Claydon says, noting that most guests make their own discoveries and end up on unplanned diversions.

Among the sisters’ can’t-miss activities: tea at Café de Paris, Sunday service at historic St. Andrew’s Church, and a camel ride, especially with kids, at Achakar Beach. A typical morning in Tangier might begin with breakfast under a banyan tree at El Morocco Club. Around 11 a.m. the markets buzz to life, including the legendary fish market in the Grand Socco, the Fondouk Chejra (weavers’ market), the antiques mall on Rue de Hollande, and the Casabarata flea market, where a sharp eye is required to spot the gems amid the seedy furniture and cheap fabrics.

Lunch in town at Alma Kitchen or El Dorado is a must for crispy falafel and plates of lamb chops. Later, as the sun sinks into the sea, it’s hard to beat the views from the roof of Villa Mabrouka, the onetime home of Yves Saint Laurent and Pierre Bergé that Jasper Conran transformed into hotel with gardens by Tangier resident Madison Cox. After a martini, the hotel’s L’Orangerie restaurant is a smart spot for a dinner of thistle tagine and grilled sole; down the street, the atmospheric Hamadi serves standout harissa soup and sizzling skewers.

Claydon and Wheeler, for their part, also advise guests to reserve time for relaxing and being transported at home, one of the great pleasures of staying in a design-centric private residence. Other places available to rent include the red-and-white-striped riad owned by decorator Gavin Houghton and photographer Boz Gagovski (who took most of the photos in this story), as well as antiques dealer Gordon Watson’s large ochre house. Both weave together Moorish and European details, with objects from Tangier and around the world that reflect the city’s cosmopolitan history. As Hamish Bowles noted in his foreword to the book Inside Tangier, “The magic of this place was that everyone who had chosen to make a home here brought their own fantasy with them.”

That book was produced by my husband, designer Nicolò Castellini Baldissera, and our home here, a 1929 townhouse named Casa Tosca, is filled with a worldly mix of objects and furnishings that we designed. Outside, our terraced garden was overseen by Umberto Pasti, whose own Tangier residence is a collection of 1930s bungalows set within meandering gardens of datura trees, rambling roses, and alocasia. For us, so much of the pleasure of this place is being at home and having people over. Creating the setting with local craftspeople and market finds becomes a pastime in itself.

It’s the same for Marco Scarani and Jamie Creel, whose house, Dar Zero, has had an illustrious lineage of owners who have contributed to its rich layers. The home is in the casbah, with a bushy fig tree and bougainvillea spilling from its courtyard over the front wall. Inside, iron klismos chairs from when designers Yves Vidal and Charles Sevigny lived there sit on well-worn bejmat and zellige tiles. There are also pieces of their own creation, such as brass bird-foot candlesticks they make in Tangier and sell in their New York shop, Creel & Gow. “There is a lot of transmission from one generation to another,” Scarani says. “Friends here inherit objects and even homes. We live in houses that have the spirits of the people who came before.”

For designers Frank de Biasi and Gene Meyer, it was the relationship with artisans that prompted them a few years ago to open Habibi Burton, their boutique where they offer pillows, painted tables, and a rotation of decorative objects, all sourced locally. “Making things for the shop gives me more freedom than making things for clients,” says de Biasi. “Because we aren’t designing to a deadline, I can have four artisans contribute to a single piece.” He also recommends Las Chicas for fashion, Kasbah Collective for regional crafts, and the bookshop Les Insolites.

Down the street, artist and curator Hicham Bouzid oversees Kiosk, a cultural salon and shop that hosts talks and exhibitions bringing together creatives from Africa, the Arab world, and Europe. It has a selection of books and artist prints, as well as a restaurant and live music. Bouzid is one of the native Tanjawis who are animating the city’s cultural life. Another is Yto Barrada, perhaps Morocco’s best-known contemporary artist, who founded the popular Cinéma Rif, where screenings of contemporary and classic films share the program with talks and other cultural events.

Across town Clos Nadir, the house shared by designer Jean-Louis Deniot and William Holloway, exists in a different world, perched among pines and looking out across the strait. Focusing on spaces for entertaining, Deniot placed a 30-foot dining table on a roof, while his living room is outfitted with a large fireplace, plush carpets, and a welcoming mix of furniture, mostly made locally. “Tangier is a land of artistic opportunity, a place for aesthetes, where stylistic adventures, contemplation, and the freedom to create are central to daily life,” says the ELLE Decor A-List designer, who is opening an office here.

Tangier’s close-knit community of creatives comes alive during the summer, which is when Veere Greeney, another ELLE Decor A-List designer, hosts an annual theatrical performance in the gardens of his mountaintop villa, Gazebo. Producer Rob Ashford assembles actors from Broadway and the West End (last summer it was Dames at Sea, featuring Darren Criss and Julianne Hough), and the shows are open to the public, though tickets are limited. It’s a full sensory experience colored by the scent of jasmine and gardenias, one that captures the eccentric artistic allure of Tangier.

The energy and interest in the city continue to grow. Deniot is creating a hotel with theater producer Jordan Roth, the Four Seasons recently announced a new property on Malabata Bay, and King Mohammed VI is behind a luxurious renovation of the historic Hotel Continental.

And while there is excitement around these new projects, what appeals most about Tangier is something older. Over lunch on Grenney’s terrace, looking out at Spain, we discussed the city’s past lives. “What makes Tangier unique is its varied history and strategic position,” he observed. “The strait made it a city of diplomacy, openness, and inclusiveness. That is its bedrock.”

Alpaca Engineering posted a YouTube video yesterday showcasing his self-hostable non-planar slicer. He explains that most non-planar 3D printing — printing across all three axes, rather than in 2D horizontal layers — demonstrations are usually for research purposes, for demonstration and experimentation, with no easy way for most 3D printer owners to try out non-planar 3D printing. His project, named PancakeSlicer, aims to fix this by making his non-planar slicing algorithm as easy to implement as existing slicing workflows, even improving upon it with 3D printer management features.

Alpaca Engineering explains that every few months, a non-planar 3D printing demonstration goes viral, but is rarely adopted practically by most 3D printer owners. Most non-planar experiments are for research, with half-abandoned GitHub projects. He further explains that non-planar isn’t widely adopted not because there aren’t any general-purpose non-planar slicers, but because it’s not accessible and easily implemented in a familiar workflow, with 3D printer owners spoiled by modern workflows and not needing to manually run G-code directly from an SD card.

Commercially available 3D printers are also not optimized for non-planar 3D printing. The challenge with printing at various angles is the clearance angle needed to bring a print head up and down without crashing into its own print. The slicer uses 3D models of various print heads from different 3D printer models to know how to slice the object to avoid collisions, thus having limited support; but Alpaca Engineering has listed a large number of supported 3D printers from Bambu Lab, Prusa, Creality, Voron, and many others. The varying shapes of the print heads also mean different printers will have different capabilities, with some managing less angle and curvature than others.

Alpaca Engineering also has great animations and practical demonstrations of the different slicing algorithms in the slicer, including how the algorithm slices the object for non-planar printing. We highly suggest you watch the video if you’re interested, as his animations explain non-planar slicing intuitively.

One of the best features of this slicer is its self-hosting features. Users can host the slicer from a desktop, NAS, or a home server and access it with a web UI, allowing multiple users to send print jobs from any device. It also has 3D printer management features, applying different profiles to different printer brands, unifying the experience without hopping from one slicer to another to operate multiple 3D printers — this is even available to Bambu Lab users, replacing Bambu Studio and Bambu Connect in LAN-only mode. The slicer can also manage two slice jobs at the same time in case other users happen to be slicing at the same time.

While PancakeSlicer isn’t the first to implement self-hosted 3D printer management features, it certainly levels up his initial goal of making non-planar slicing as easy to access as most modern workflows. For the past two years, stargazers have waited with bated breath for a "new" light to appear in the night sky, born of one of the most cataclysmic events known to occur in the cosmos — a nova explosion. But what is the "Blaze Star" T. Coronae Borealis, and why is it so difficult to predict when its overdue brightening will occur? We turned to the experts to find out.

T. Coronae Borealis — or T CrB for short — isn't a single star, but rather a binary system containing a dense white dwarf star and a bloated red giant. Over a period of around 80 years, the white dwarf's gravity strips material from the red giant until it hits a critical threshold. Next comes a dramatic nova explosion that causes the star to flare to 1,000 times its usual brightness before gradually settling down and repeating the cycle.

"T CrB *will* erupt sometime soon," Bradley Schaefer of the Louisiana State University Department of Physics and Astronomy told Space.com in an email. "Everyone is very confident on this. But we cannot make high-accuracy measures of the rate of gas falling onto the surface of the white dwarf, and we do not have a high accuracy measure of the trigger mass for the big hydrogen bomb." Schaefer continued.

Historical observations reveal that the last nova eruption reached Earth in 1946, which places us well inside the window for another dramatic brightening of T. CrBb. However, the unpredictability of the white dwarf's feeding makes it difficult to pin down exactly when the explosion will become visible from Earth.

"The exact date of the upcoming T CrB eruption depends primarily on how fast the gas coming from its companion star is accumulating on the surface of the white dwarf," explained Schaefer. "When some specific trigger mass has accumulated, then T CrB will erupt. For the last 80 years, we have been watching the gas pile up on the white dwarf surface, and we know that it must be near the trigger threshold."

Astronomers have tried to predict T CrB's brightening by comparing a recent fluctuation in the light of the distant star system to a similar event that occurred in the runup to the 1946 event.

"There have already been some dimming and brightening episodes that caused excitement but did not yet turn into the main event," Elizabeth Hays, lab chief at NASA's Astroparticle Physics Laboratory at the Goddard Spaceflight Center, told Space.com in an email. "It is likely that the nova will happen in the next few years but we still don't know exactly when. I can't give you a guaranteed prediction, but I will be a little surprised if it gets to 2030, or 84 years past the last eruption."

Hays notes that the intense light from the red giant dramatically outshines the dimmer white dwarf, making detailed observation challenging. There is also no way to precisely measure the temperature and pressure of the matter lurking near the surface of the smaller star. "Predicting nova explosions are a little like predicting the weather; conditions may be right but the timing of the storm can be uncertain."

There'll be no visual warning of the eruption before it reaches Earth, though according to Hays there could be an early flash of light in the X-ray spectrum.

"The world's amateur astronomers have been doing an awesome job of keeping T CrB under hair-trigger monitoring roughly every minute or two, so when T CrB has brightened by around one magnitude, then alerts will spread around the world within minutes," said Schaefer.

Once the call goes out, telescopes and observatories around the world will kick into gear, running pre-prepared observation programmes to capitalize on the rare and cataclysmic event. "When the nuclear runaway process starts, it will take off and peak very quickly, in less than a day."

The nova will see the star jump from its normal brightness of magnitude +10 — which is beyond the detection limit of the naked eye — to magnitude +1.7, making it appear as if a star brighter than the North Star Polaris has suddenly burst to life in the night sky.

"The brightness will fall below naked-eye visibility in dark places within a couple of days and become too faint for binoculars within about a week," said Hays. "Outside the visible band, for example, in radio, X-ray and gamma rays, light caused by the eruption will be visible for months or even years. "

T CrB can be found glowing close to the magnitude +4.28 star Epsilon Coronae Borealis in the constellation Corona Borealis during the early evening in November, with the constellations of Hercules and Bootes on either side.

It will dip below the horizon for viewers in the U.S. just a few hours after sunset, before re-emerging into the northeastern sky in the predawn hours.

Why your laptop fans spin up even when you're not doing anything

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It could point to many things, including aggressive power profiles, background software updates or even dust building up.

Laptops generate heat. You can't really avoid it. Even energy-efficient models are still producing some level of heat. To try and keep things cool, your laptop fans should kick into action when you're doing something that pushes it hard, like gaming. By design, most laptops will ship with adjustable fan speeds that keep temperatures cool without too much noise. You'd usually expect the fans to quiet down, although they won't necessarily stop completely.

That isn't always the case, though. You might hear your laptop fans spin up even when you've closed all your apps. Power settings can have an impact, but so can dust and debris clogging up your case. You'll need to work out whether the noise is normal, because if it isn't, you'll need to fix it.

Closing the windows on your desktop doesn't stop everything happening on your laptop. Apps run in the background, with running processes that continue performing tasks under the hood without you realizing. These all create demands on your processor, RAM and storage. More power means more calls for the fan to spin.

Dell, for instance, recommends reviewing your startup apps to keep the loads down on your CPU, which should help prevent your fans from spinning up unnecessarily. You can review these in Task Manager by pressing Ctrl + Shift + Esc, selecting Startup apps, and disabling anything non-essential.

Other essential maintenance tasks will cause demands while your PC isn't active, too. Windows Update will do it, as will search indexing, which catalogs your files so that you can easily search for them later. That initial indexing process can take a few hours, according to Microsoft. The same applies to MacBooks, and you can use Activity Monitor to see which background apps are using the most power.

Imagine a set of scales. You can tip them one way or another, or get them perfectly balanced. Your laptop power settings work the same way. On a Windows laptop, for instance, you can switch between modes that prioritize energy efficiency, performance or a balance between the two. The most aggressive option, for best performance, will prioritize speed over saving power.

This can allow for higher power consumption under load, which'll produce more heat and make the fans more likely to kick in. You can change these settings on Windows, for instance, via Settings > System > Power & battery > Power mode. These settings will change when you've plugged in your laptop compared to when it's running on battery, too.

Your laptop might come with an app that lets you set your own cooling settings, even if you've changed Windows' own settings. For example, some Dell laptops ship with an ultra performance mode that'll increase processing power and cooling fan speeds. Switching to quiet mode reverses it, although that will reduce performance and push the temperatures up when you're using your laptop.

You'll only know what settings work best, as far as cooling is concerned, by trying them out. Try each setting at a time, then push it through your usual use. You'll want performance mode if you're doing something like gaming, because you'll want maximum power and cooling. For lighter work like typing up documents, you might manage better by switching to balanced or energy efficiency modes.

The amount of work you're doing on your laptop is only really part of the explanation. Your surroundings can have an impact. Apple, for instance, suggests that higher ambient temperatures will force fans to spin up sooner and run faster. A warm room makes it harder for your laptop to stay cool, and it'll produce different fan behavior as a result than a laptop in a cooler space.

It isn't just the air, though. Where you place your laptop is important, because you need good ventilation for your fans to lower temperatures and, as a result, work properly. Working from bed isn't great for the air from your laptop fans to circulate, for instance. With restricted airflow, temperatures will rise and the fans will keep working harder to try and compensate.

A hard, flat desk is a better option for the vents on your fan, but even better is if you can raise it up a little to give more space for the air to flow. Side and rear openings matter, too. Manufacturers like Dell and Apple recommend keeping any ventilation openings unblocked for the best performance.

Then there's dust and dirt. Air flowing through your laptop isn't just bringing in the air; it's bringing in the dust that might be in the air. This builds up over time. It collects around the vents and inside the cooling fans, and around the fan blades themselves. It raises the baseline, meaning your fans have to work harder and harder, even when you're performing the same tasks. If you keep your laptop fans clean from dirt, it'll help the air flow freely so the fans don't have to work as hard.

A good can of compressed air will help. You might be able to take the bottom panel from your laptop off to give it a more thorough clean, but in most cases, you might not need to. Use the can liberally to push as much of the dust out of your fans as possible to clean your laptop fan.

Changing the settings on your laptop isn't a miracle fix. If your fans are still spinning hard, even when your laptop isn't doing much, then you'll need to investigate a little deeper.

Don't rule out a hardware issue. If the laptop maintenance has fallen behind, you might start to hear persistent grinding or rattling, which would point to a worn-out or damaged fan. Cleaning won't help if that's the case. You'll need to check whether your laptop is still under warranty, or if it isn't, switch the fan out yourself.

Check that there aren't any software issues, though. Get the BIOS updates installed and any manufacturer-issued apps, like Dell's Dell Power Manager, to give you full control over the power settings. HP recommends that you have the latest BIOS update installed, along with chipset and graphics drivers, as your GPU can impact fan speeds.

Apple users can reset their System Management Controller if they're seeing unexpected heavy fan usage on an Intel MacBook, for instance. For newer Apple silicon MacBooks, the advice is a little simpler: just close and reopen the lid, or give it a restart.

There are some manufacturer-issue tests you can run through. For example, some Dell laptops have fan tests that you can work through during preboot diagnostics, which you can access by restarting the laptop, pressing F12 when the Dell logo appears, and then selecting Diagnostics.

You don't need to worry every time your laptop fans make themselves heard, though. Background tasks can kick them into action, but unless the noise is distracting, or you're hearing grinding or rattling, then you can usually leave them to do their job, as long as your laptop isn't overheating. Changing the settings and cleaning the vents could help, but if it doesn't, give your warranty a check and see if you can get your laptop checked for damage.

Investors now have an expanding array of options for investing in the $300 billion stablecoin industry.

The $300 billion stablecoin industry continues to see an influx of new entrants. There are now 10 different stablecoins with market caps of $1 billion or higher, led by the two stablecoin giants Tether (USDT +0.02%) and USDC (USDC +0.01%).

The latest entrant -- and one of the most intriguing ones to date -- is known as Project Faro. It's backed by 21 different banks and financial institutions, and has already raised $270 million for a new stablecoin launch in 2027.

So what does Project Faro mean for investors, and will it affect how they invest in the rapidly growing stablecoin industry?

To answer that question, it's important to know some of the heavy hitters involved in Project Faro. Wall Street investment bank Goldman Sachs (GS +0.32%) is playing a key role, as is Citigroup (C -0.04%). Other financial institutions involved in the launch include Wells Fargo (WFC +0.34%), Deutsche Bank (DB -0.08%), and Fidelity.

While Project Faro is still in the preliminary launch phase, the focus appears to extend far beyond just launching a U.S. dollar stablecoin. Plans also call for launching stablecoins pegged to other G7 currencies, including a stablecoin pegged to the euro. So this new stablecoin venture has truly worldwide ambitions.

If that sounds familiar, that's because it is. In June, a consortium of 140 different financial institutions and fintech companies announced the launch of a new stablecoin called Open USD (OUSD -0.08%). This dollar-pegged stablecoin already has a market cap of $1 billion, and has the support of some big names as well, including BlackRock (BLK +0.52%), Visa (V +1.30%), and Mastercard (MA +1.32%).

From my perspective, this is setting up an epic battle for stablecoin dominance. Right now, the two leaders are Tether and USDC, and it's not even close. Together, they account for roughly 80% of the total stablecoin market. But the arrival of these massive new financial consortia -- backed by dozens of banks and mainstream financial institutions -- can't be ignored.

The company most at risk here is Circle Internet Group (CRCL -2.54%), the issuer of the USDC stablecoin. The higher the global supply of USDC is, the more money Circle makes. Thus, the arrival of new deep-pocketed rivals could put a serious dent in Circle's future growth prospects.

Companies attempting to go it alone in the stablecoin race could be left behind. For that reason, I'm also concerned about PayPal (PYPL +1.68%). The company has a rapidly growing stablecoin, PayPal USD (PYUSD -0.02%), but is not part of either financial consortium.

The biggest winners could be Mastercard and Visa. They have done the most to move their payments to blockchain rails, and are actively working to make stablecoin-backed payments a reality. Earlier this year, for example, Mastercard launched a massive new Crypto Partner Program that involves more than 100 blockchain, fintech, and crypto firms.

It remains to be seen how these new stablecoin initiatives will affect crypto-native companies. The one company that I'm watching closely now is Coinbase (COIN -2.31%). It helped to launch the USDC stablecoin in partnership with Circle in 2018, and is now one of the founding partners of the Open USD consortium.

Both banks and credit card issuers are clearly responding to the threat posed by stablecoins. If stablecoins are able to offer highly attractive yields, they might be able to siphon away traditional bank deposits. If stablecoins become a mainstream form of payment, they might pose a threat to the big credit card giants.

That's one reason why there was so much debate over the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act ("Clarity Act") earlier this year. Banks and traditional financial institutions are doing everything they can to protect themselves against the rise of new rivals. From this perspective, both Project Faro and Open USD can be seen as defensive reactions to the exploding popularity of stablecoins.

The good news, if you're a crypto investor, is that there are now new ways to play the stablecoin boom. You can now invest in banks and financial institutions, not just fintech firms and crypto-native companies. Some early winners are already starting to emerge, and could be worth a closer look.

How long will your NVIDIA GPU be supported?

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

NVIDIA can support GeForce GPUs for years, but Game Ready updates, security patches and OS support don't all end at once.

Graphics cards do not have an expiration date on the box. That would make it easier to upgrade, but much more depressing.

NVIDIA also does not guarantee a specific number of years of driver support for all GeForce GPUs. Rather, support is largely dependent on the architecture your card uses. At time of writing, Turing, Ampere, Ada and Blackwell GPUs are still on the normal Game Ready Driver track, which includes new features, game optimizations, performance improvements and bug fixes. That means that even though the RTX 2060 was released in 2019, it continues to receive regular driver updates.

Older cards have started moving down the ladder. In October 2025, Maxwell, Pascal and Volta GPUs stopped receiving Game Ready updates, but NVIDIA will continue to offer critical security updates until October 2028. Pascal and Volta had nine years of Game Ready support, whereas Maxwell lasted for 11 years.

That's a pretty respectable run, but what comes next and how could it impact your system?

NVIDIA does have a neatly defined three-phase software support lifecycle. Unfortunately, it's about NVIDIA vGPU software; not about the GeForce card in your gaming PC.

NVIDIA vGPU is a virtualization solution, mostly for enterprise use (data centers and professional deployments), that requires an active software or support subscription to receive software support. Phase I, also known as Full Support, includes new features, performance enhancements, security patches and bug fixes. Phase II is the next three years of Extended Full Support, with the same coverage, and Phase III is the final three years of Maintenance Support, where the GPU remains on its last supported software branch and receives maintenance, security fixes and bug fixes as NVIDIA sees fit.

GeForce support is far less ceremonial. NVIDIA has moved older architectures off its primary Game Ready branch, but doesn't publish a single lifespan for all generations.

That can also make model names confusing. The GTX 750 Ti is a Maxwell card and will continue to receive security updates until October 2028, whereas the more powerful GTX 780 Ti is based on the older Kepler architecture, whose published security-update window ended in September 2024. A good rule of thumb: bigger number equals older architecture. GPU naming is weird that way.

The end of Game Ready support sounds a lot more dramatic than it normally is.

Your graphics card does not suddenly refuse to boot up Windows or stop running all of the games you already have installed. Existing drivers continue to work. What goes away is the constant flow of optimizations for new games, feature additions, performance tweaks and regular bug fixes, which can become more noticeable as newer software expects newer drivers.

Security-only support is still meaningful though. NVIDIA has already issued dedicated Maxwell, Pascal and Volta security drivers during 2026, so these cards haven't simply been abandoned.

Another clock is ticking too: your operating system. The last full Game Ready and Studio driver for Windows 10 will be available in October 2026, according to NVIDIA. Windows 10 systems will get quarterly security updates and critical bug fixes until October 2029. So even a newer RTX GPU might lose the normal Game Ready update stream due to Windows and not the hardware.

For an RTX 2060 owner, driver support alone is not much of a reason to start looking for a new GPU. Performance and VRAM limits or a lack of access to newer features may become bigger issues first. NVIDIA hasn't confirmed a new GeForce generation for 2027 either, despite reports previously pointing to late-2027 mass production for its next architecture.

It wasn’t the plane’s first flight. The CE-25A had already completed 19 autonomous test flights with nobody on board since February 2, and China’s aviation regulator is now reviewing it for a type certificate.

The CE-25A is a T-tail commuter plane with 10 electric ducted fans on the wings, five per side, instead of propellers. COMAC lists a 300 km/h cruise speed and a 400 km (250-mile) range.

The CAAC accepting COMAC’s type certificate application starts the airworthiness review. It doesn’t mean paying passengers are close, and COMAC hasn’t given a date for commercial service.

The program has been quiet for a long time. COMAC showed a CE-25A model at the China International Import Expo in Shanghai in November 2023, with a placard listing 400 to 500 km of range and a 400 km/h top cruise speed. The full-size aircraft, registered B-0EFH, only became public when photos leaked on Chinese social media on October 28.

The obvious battery candidate is CATL. In July 2023, COMAC, CATL, and Shanghai Jiao Tong University formed a joint venture with CNY 600 million in registered capital to develop electric aircraft, as we reported when CATL launched its aviation unit.

CATL had just unveiled its “condensed” battery at the time, claiming up to 500 Wh/kg at the cell level, and said it was testing the cells to aviation standards. Neither company has confirmed what’s inside the CE-25A, though.

~500 Wh/kg has long been seen as the target for battery energy density to enable electric flight on a commercial scale.

COMAC isn’t alone in flying a large electric demonstrator. In August, Heart Aerospace flew its X1 in Plattsburgh, New York, for 27 minutes. It’s a 25,000-lb aircraft with a 106-foot wingspan, which Heart calls the largest battery-electric aircraft ever flown.

Eviation flew its 9-passenger Alice back in September 2022, then laid off most of its staff in early 2025.

Heart is the closest Western comparison. It dropped its 19-seat ES-19 for the 30-seat ES-30 back in 2022, and the ES-30 now relies on reserve turbogenerators to stretch its range, with BAE Systems working on the battery. Heart is targeting 2031 for service and claims “more than 40%” lower operating costs.

For years, the go-to excuse in the West for doing nothing about emissions was China. Why should we bother, the argument went, if China won’t? Now China is pulling ahead, and it’s doing it by cutting its dependence on fossil fuels.

Look at trucking. Electric trucks hit 50.3% of China’s heavy truck sales in September, and they’re already eating into the country’s diesel demand. In the US, electric models were about 0.3% of heavy-truck sales last year, according to the ICCT.

Now it’s planes, starting where batteries make sense today: short regional routes. The CE-25A’s 400 km shrinks once you keep the reserves regulators require, and a Beechcraft 1900 turboprop in the same class covers well over 1,000 km. So it’s not replacing turboprops anytime soon. But that’s how trucking started too. Short-haul first, then long-haul tractors once the batteries caught up.

Robots now run AI-written code without an AI calling the shots, succeeding 92% of the time

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Robots completed 221 of 240 trials using Claude-written programs for tasks such as uncapping bottles. Human help was available during development, not execution.

Robot programs developed by Anthropic’s Claude Opus 5.5 coding agent completed 221 of 240 physical evaluation trials, ACE-CaP-0 researchers reported in a study posted November 12. The result shows that task-specific skills can persist in code and run without the coding agent choosing each action.

The researchers behind ACE-CaP-0, a method for developing robot control programs, reported a 92.1% success rate across eight tasks. Each program was frozen before 30 separate evaluation trials, with no further updates. Human guidance and demonstrations were available during development.

The experiment separates program development from repeated execution. The agent's work produced a program that could be used repeatedly. During evaluation, that program still used perception and control systems to respond to observations, while its decision rules stayed fixed. Neither a human nor the coding agent remained in the execution loop.

During development, the coding agent wrote, ran, inspected and revised its code using physical feedback. It could ask for textual advice or demonstrations in which a person physically guided the robot arms. Those demonstrations supplied video, arm trajectories and key joint poses.

Development allowed at most ten human-assisted scene resets per task. Resets used to collect demonstrations were excluded, and the budget counted scene resets rather than all robot executions or demonstrations.

The agent could also use simulated experiments when they helped resolve a development question. In a charger-insertion comparison, on-demand simulation produced a program with 100% evaluation success, versus 86.7% for development without simulation. The comparison used one development run per condition.

That comparison also illustrates a cost trade-off. Development took 2.6 hours with on-demand simulation versus 3.7 hours without it, while output-token usage increased from 730,000 to 1,090,000. The time and token figures leave total development costs unresolved; the researchers call for accounting that includes demonstration effort.

Repeating evaluation trials measures how the resulting program performs. Establishing how consistently the development process produces an equally capable program requires separate development runs across tasks.

ACE-CaP-0 leaves behind task-specific programs that can execute repeatedly with fresh observations and fixed decision rules. The study provides evidence for that form of skill reuse after human-assisted development. Testing those routines under new conditions is the next step toward establishing how broadly they can be reused.

Wu-Tang Clan, Billy Idol, Phil Collins Enter Rock Hall at All-Star Induction Ceremony

It was difficult to imagine who could possibly top Wu-Tang Clan’s performance at the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame induction on Saturday night at Los Angeles’ Peacock Theater. Following a spirited introduction from Chris Rock, all living members of the Staten Island hip-hop group hit the stage to perform a medley of some of their biggest hits, including “C.R.E.A.M.” and “Wu-Tang Clan Ain’t Nuthing ta F’ Wit.” The audience was on its collective feet screaming “Wu-Tang! Wu-Tang!” repeatedly as the mighty crew wielded their lyrical swords.

But then came the Red Hot Chili Peppers, who were part of Rick Rubin’s induction. Cosplaying Rubin (who, after an incredibly theatrical induction speech by Neil Young, accepted his honor in shorts, sandals and rose-tinted sunglasses), Anthony Kiedis, John Frusciante, Flea and Chad Smith erupted into their Blood Sugar Sex Magik single “Give It Away” donning fake white beards. After a short technical issue with Flea’s instrument, the ever-limber bassist and Kiedis continued to somehow defy aging and rocked it like it was 1991.

The high octane performances kept coming at a dizzying pace. The speeches were long (sans Rubin) and the event as usual ran well over four hours. But as Wu-Tang Clan and Rubin joined fellow honorees Queen Latifah, New Order/Joy Division, MC Lyte, Billy Idol, Iron Maiden, Luther Vandross, Sade, Oasis, Phil Collins, Celia Cruz, Fela Kuti, Gram Parsons, Linda Creed, Arif Mardin and Jimmy Miller, the excitement of “what’s next” made time disappear.

Of course several inductees opted not to show up (Oasis, New Order’s Bernard Sumner, Gillian Gilbert and Stephen Morris, Sade and Iron Maiden) and some of them were being inducted posthumously, but the Rock Hall was still able to make the evening magical with surprise presenters and performances.

U2 opened the show with “Silencio,” the lead single from their latest album, Carnaval de Luz, and their 1984 hit “Pride (In the Name of Love).” Jack Black joined St. Vincent, Metallica’s Kirk Hammett and Robert Trujillo and Rage Against the Machine’s Brad Wilk for an Iron Maiden tribute. MC Lyte came out firing on all cylinders for “Boom! Shake the Room” before she was joined by Queen Latifah for “U.N.I.T.Y.” and “Ladies First” featuring Megan Thee Stallion and Remy Ma. It felt like déjà vu when Trent Reznor stepped up to the mic to induct Joy Division and New Order. Seven years ago, the Nine Inch Nails maestro welcomed the Cure into the esteemed Hall and admitted he’d been “ambivalent” about “certain award ceremonies.”

But there he was in his thick, black-rimmed glasses recounting the history of both bands. Moments later, Peter Hook stood alone at the podium without Sumner, Gilbert or Morris, making it painfully clear the band was unable to put their differences aside.

“Now I’m here and I realize how special it is,” Hook said. “It’s like being part of a family, which I’m delighted about. Now I’m on my own, but I’m not alone … it’s been a wild 50 years, and I’ve been happy, I’ve been mad and I’ve been heartbroken in equal measures. The Oasis segment, however, was a sore spot. Liam and Noel Gallagher already declined the invitation, so their absence was expected. But Stephen Wilson Jr., a country artist with no ties to British rock whatsoever, performing “Wonderwall” almost felt like a slight to Oasis. Wilson knew it, too. Backstage in the press room, he was reminded that the Gallagher brothers will likely see the performance and there was a chance they’d roast him for it. He said he had “thick skin” and would be “honored” if he were to become the target of a Gallagher brothers’ joke, but coupled with the awkward introduction by Better Call Saul actor Bob Odenkirk (who satirized the Gallagher brothers’ “cheeky” attitudes for the HBO comedy series Mr. Show in the 1990s), it seemed like Oasis’ absence was just too massive of a hole to fill.

That’s not to say the Rock Hall didn’t try: Billy Idol was ushered into the Hall by No Doubt’s Gwen Stefani and Tony Kanal. Stefani called Idol’s music a “time machine” and recalled listening to him in the early 1980s. Idol’s speech didn’t fail to entertain either as he sometimes struggled to read the teleprompter.

“Rock & roll says we can be heroes,” Idol said. “In my case I called myself Billy Idol so I could be my own hero.” He and longtime guitarist Steve Stevens proceeded to perform “Rebel Yell” and “Eyes Without a Face” as a duet with Stefani.

In the press room, Idol didn’t field any questions but posed for the cameras with his signature lip snarl and fist pumps, though Stevens spoke at length.

“Music is such a universal thing that I got the opportunity at some point to stay at Billy’s parents’ house and got to see his record collection, his adolescent William Broad record collection, and they were a lot of the same records that I grew up on,” Stevens said. “We’re roughly the same age, so it was David Bowie, T. Rex, Slade, Sweet Reed. We grew up [differently], but the music and things that inspired us were very much the same.”

Stevens also talked about his hopes for future generations of rock stars, saying, “There’s not that many true rock stars around to replace a Billy Idol. Hopefully, we’ll inspire people, and there’ll be another Steve Stevens … I’ve seen them on YouTube playing the solo for ‘Rebel Yell’ and sometimes playing it better than I can, so hopefully that’s the next generation. From what I understand, a lot of the record companies are looking at, you know, ‘Wow, we really have to develop artists again,’ because just having the numbers of viewers on TikTok or something is not a career.”

The crescendo of the evening came as Public Enemy’s Chuck D, with his inimitable and commanding voice, introduced Aerosmith to the stage. Steven Tyler, Joe Perry and the rest of the band started playing the first few notes to “Walk This Way” before Tyler suddenly stopped and told Perry there was “something missing.” Just as they’d done at The Hollywood Bowl two weeks ago, Run and DMC appeared from opposite sides of the stage to perform the crossover smash, elating the crowd.
