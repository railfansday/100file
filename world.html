GTA 6 Makes It Nearly Impossible to Play Without Killing Anyone

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

In my defense, I don’t know how to drive IRL or in GTA

Grand Theft Auto 6 is out now, and for my initial playthrough of Rockstar’s latest giant open-world game, I wanted to try something a bit out of left field: How long could I play without killing anyone? I’d do my best Batman impression: brutally beating my foes, crippling them with excessive medical debt, but keeping them alive.

Yeah, that didn’t last long. Turns out trying to not commit crime in the game that’s all about committing crime is a difficult task.

The first mission was easy enough. Jason has to rescue drug runner Donnie from a gang of rednecks who keep tigers in cages. (Surely that will end well for them.) After I rescued him, I got into a fist fight with a beefy boy and knocked him out. So far so good. Jason had a pistol on him and could pick up weapons around the swamp base, but, trying to avoid a shootout at all costs, I stealthily booked it away from the rednecks and toward the water, avoiding any detection (and potential for violence).

That was the opening mostly-on-the-rails mission, but things quickly went south when I was left to my own devices. During the next in-game day, players are given a few mission options. I met up with Cal, Jason’s buddy who’s not cut out for a life of crime, and we drove to a police impound lot. I needed to steal a car with a trunk full of weed, and this is where shit hit the fan.

Look, I never learned how to drive in real life, and am not the biggest fan of it in video games. Driving for more than a few feet without hitting anything or anyone is an impossible challenge in games, and driving in GTA 6 feels especially difficult; I can never figure out the right speed to go, and sharp turns are the bane of my existence.

So maybe I was always doomed to run over a cop on my way out of the impound lot.

Police officers swarmed Jason after he unhooked the red Phoenix and hotwired it. Too much was happening at once: an on-screen message about how I could open the gate, cops flinging open the driver’s side door and trying to rip Jason from the vehicle, and police radio chatter pouring out of my controller speaker. I’m not ashamed to say I restarted this mission several times just trying to figure out how to get out of the lot by carefully not running over any cops.

And then, who pulls up in front of the gate I’m about to crash through but a do-gooder cop hopping out of his vehicle? I assumed the giant pool of blood leaking out of him after our collision meant he wasn’t long for the world.

I did get caught on that attempt (again, driving is hard), and had to restart. On my next attempt, I got away blood free. Did that mean I succeeded in not killing anyone, or did the roadkill cop mean I failed? Didn’t matter, since soon after I killed someone and there was no going back.

If you need to check Snapmatic, pull over

After purchasing a longarm from an Ammu-Nation, Jason gets jumped. This was my first fistfight, meaning I had to navigate on-screen messages telling me how to fight while trying not to die. I was just pressing buttons, y’all. Instinctively I pressed whatever button flashed up on screen, which meant, after knocking out one dude, I picked up his knife and started attacking with it. Knives — much more lethal than Jason’s fists!

I killed one goon before dropping the knife and knocking out the third and final guy. I checked my playthrough stats and, yep, that guy was definitely dead. And I was barely two hours into the game. There’s a lot to do in GTA 6, and plenty of control schemes to learn just to be able to do all that stuff. Players have already accidentally kicked bodega cats (heinous) because of a fumbled button press.

Still, not to be deterred by my early-game kills, I tried to soldier on with committing as little murder as possible. I soon learned this was impossible. During the “Hey, Arnold” mission, Jason gets into a car chase with one of landlord Brian’s old buddies. Arnold’s got goons on motorcycles protecting him and, ya know, I said I wasn’t very good at driving.

I tried my damndest not to kill them, but that wasn’t going to happen. I accidentally ran into some, and they didn’t survive their facetime with Brian’s big-ass truck. But, hey, at least I got to spare Arnold at the end.

I’ve played a few hours since, and have tried to keep up my “no killing” policy. It’s especially tough in shootouts as Rockstar’s third-person shooting has never been the most fluid, and aiming for nonvital parts of people — when getting shot at myself — is a tall task. Still I’ve kept it up. I’ve enjoyed how you can incapacitate enemies by shooting them in the legs; the hit indicator will turn yellow to show that they’ve been wounded, but not killed. Now, is that guy I shot — but didn’t kill! — who then lay unmoving and face-first in knee-high water really going to get back up again? Who knows, but I did my best to not kill him.

GTA 6 is hyperrealistic, to the point that sometimes people can’t tell the difference between GTA 6 footage and real life. That realism extends to each of its hand-crafted NPCs. They look and behave so much like real people that I’m hesitant to just pull out a gun and start blasting, a sentiment that prospective players shared ahead of the game’s release.

Violence toward very-real-looking humans is something I’ve thought a lot about this year, and the restrictions on it in 007 First Light is part of what made that game really appeal to me. Pragmata and Onimusha: Way of the Sword are two of my favorite games this year, and I have to think that’s partly due to the fact that the violence in them is directed toward robots and demons, respectively. I don’t really have to think twice about slicing a people-devouring demon in half. Firing on small-time crooks in Vice City? That’s something that will give me pause.

I don’t know how much longer I’ll try to keep up my self-imposed difficulty modifier and continue on with as little violence as possible. After all, the appeal of this series is, ya know, committing crime. Hey, sometimes you gotta shoot some dudes in GTA 6 before they shoot you. But it was certainly fun while it lasted.

Research Says Cold Muscles Produce Less Power. Here’s How to Protect Your Performance on Frigid Runs.

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Experts share how to prepare, pace, and recover so you can run strong when temperatures fall below freezing.

When temperatures rise, running performance tends to drop. Both the research and anyone who’s put in miles on a hot summer afternoon can confidently tell you that much.

But what about when the mercury moves in the opposite direction? Does colder weather in the winter—ranging from just a bit chilly to downright frigid—take a toll on your running too?

A recent review published in Temperature suggests it does. According to the study’s authors, when our muscles get too cold, neuromuscular function becomes impaired. On the run, this can contribute to stiffness, less power output, and decreased endurance, all of which may cause your winter workouts to feel harder than usual.

The good news? You can still run strong when the temps drop—as long as you make a few key training adjustments before, during, and after you log your miles in the colder months of the year. Here’s how to do just that.

There’s no exact number on the thermometer at which runners can universally expect their power, speed, or endurance to decline, Stephen Cheung, PhD, the lead author of the aforementioned review, tells Runner’s World.

Temperature is just one of the many factors that contribute to how well we perform in the cold, Cheung explains. “It’s a function of the environment, as well as how much heat you’re generating, the speed that you’re moving at, and of course, the amount of clothing that you’re wearing,” he says.

Each of these factors varies significantly from runner to runner and affects how quickly their bodies lose heat. You only need to consider those in your run group who run shirtless when it’s freezing versus those who bundle up when it’s 50 degrees Fahrenheit to understand that we all thermoregulate (maintain our internal body temperature) differently. And the equation only becomes more complex when you account for wind and moisture—both in the environment and what you create yourself from air resistance and sweat.

Heather Milton, CSCS, lead exercise physiologist at Primary MD and cofounder of Master My Run, agrees. “It would be great to be able to say, when the temperature outside reaches [a certain point], your pace slows down by this many seconds per mile—but that’s not how it works,” she says.

Keeping this in mind, Milton says the cleanest reference point for runners to begin paying more attention to how they prepare for runs in the cold is when the temperature drops below freezing. Generally speaking, the colder it gets from there, the more noticeable the negative effects on performance will be, she explains.

Cold weather leads to vasoconstriction, Milton explains, which means our blood vessels constrict more, so the muscles farther away from the core don’t receive as much blood flow. If your fingers or toes have ever gone numb in the cold, for example, you’ve experienced the joy of vasoconstriction. Our bodies prioritize heating our core over our extremities because it helps our vital organs maintain a consistent temperature, which is essential for survival in dangerous conditions.

This physiological response, however, doesn’t bode especially well for running performance.

“The cold slows nerve conduction so you have to work harder for the same power output,” Milton says. “And as temperatures drop further below freezing, that slower nerve conduction and the effects of vasoconstriction are going to be even more noticeable.” As a result, your overall exercise capacity decreases, meaning you may not be able to run for as long or at the same pace you normally would.

What else might you notice when temperatures drop? “Physically, if your muscles and tendons are cold, they’re going to be tighter, less elastic, and you won’t be able to contract them as strongly,” says Cheung. “Stiff muscles can also put you at greater risk for tears or strain injuries.”

Milton says this analogy, used by an old coach of hers, provides an easy way to understand what’s happening: When your muscles and tendons get too cold, they’re like a cold rubber band that can snap when pulled.

If you’re a runner who dislikes the cold, you might wonder if the smarter choice is to just run on the treadmill this winter. Don’t worry: As long as you prepare appropriately and use caution, you can absolutely continue logging regular miles outdoors.

Running well outside in the cold starts with warming up indoors first, says Milton. “An indoor warmup increases your internal metabolism and enhances blood flow, which helps prepare your muscles for contraction,” she explains. This also produces heat so your internal body temperature is more resilient to the cold outdoor environment.

Your winter warmup does not have to be any different or longer than your usual routine, Milton says. But you do want to make sure that you’re getting in a solid five minutes of prerun drills to adequately raise your body temperature and activate your muscles.

She recommends starting with slower, more controlled movements that help activate the core and the glutes, such as planks with leg raises, lunges with rotation, and hip CARs (controlled articular rotations). Finish with quicker drills that require your nerves to fire more rapidly, such as leg swings and butt kicks.

Equally important to warming up is layering up—with an important caveat.

When dressing for winter runs, keep in mind how you personally respond to cold temperatures, says Cheung, because everyone has different tolerances and susceptibilities. “I get very cold in my hands, so I’ll wear really thick gloves, even when I may be wearing relatively little on my torso,” he adds. “Pay attention to what’s an issue for you and adapt your clothing to both the conditions and your own peculiarities.” Cheung emphasizes this because you don’t want to end up sweating too heavily, which can then leave you chilled and clammy.

If you need a starting point for your winter running wardrobe, aim for two to three layers of breathable, moisture-wicking fabrics. If possible, choose a wind- and water-resistant shell for your outermost layer, suggests Milton. For your lower body, wear shorts over your tights to help keep your glutes warm. Finally, cover any exposed skin on your head, ears, and hands, and opt for a pair of wool socks—all of which can help ensure adequate blood distribution to the extremities, Milton adds.

When you head out for runs in the cold, slowly build up to your usual pace.

Gradually easing into your workout gives your muscles more time to adjust, and it can also help your airways get used to the colder temperatures, Milton says. You don’t necessarily need to go slower for your entire run, but you should try to match your rate of perceived exertion (RPE) with your typical effort, rather than aiming for your usual pace or heart-rate zone.

“Think about it the same way as if you were running on an extremely hot day,” Milton says. “Let’s say your RPE for an easy run is about 3 out of 10. Keep that 3 out of 10, even if your pace is a little slower.”

If you find that your run feels more difficult than usual, slow down if you need to, but don’t stop completely, Cheung advises. “Don’t take long breaks, because that’s when you aren’t generating as much heat. Just keep moving so you have that constant heat generation,” he says.

If you start to get cold on a run, try speeding up a little bit to stoke that metabolic heat production, Cheung says. But make sure you do it safely: Don’t force the effort in icy conditions or when your muscles feel especially stiff. Scouting out run routes with more tree coverage to block wind on blustery days can also help you stay warmer, he adds.

As soon as you finish your workout, change out of your sweaty running gear and into warm, dry clothes, Milton says.

Even if you’ve only sweated a small amount—for example, maybe you just have some dampness under your armpits—you still want to remove any wet fabrics against your skin. “Even that small amount of wetness can start to drop your core temperature,” Milton explains. If you wait until you’re near shivering, you’ll face an uphill battle to get yourself warm again.

You can also opt for a relaxing shower and hot chocolate—extra marshmallows.

Australia: Use Vietnam Dialogue to Press for Rights Reforms

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Vietnam's President To Lam leads his delegation in a meeting with Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. at Malacanang Palace in Manila, Philippines, June 1, 2026. © 2026 Rolex Dela Pena/AP Photo

(Sydney) – Australian officials should press the Vietnamese government to release political prisoners and reverse its intensifying repression of human rights during the upcoming 21st Australia-Vietnam Human Rights Dialogue, Human Rights Watch said today. The case of an Australian national Tran Hiep, a democracy and human rights activist, is of particular concern. He has been detained in Vietnam since he was reported missing in Cambodia in September 2026. 

Human Rights Watch urged Australia, in a submission to the Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade, to seek clear, concrete, and measurable progress from Vietnam on issues including political prisoners, labor rights, transnational repression, and freedom of religion and belief.

“Since Australia’s last dialogue, Vietnam has sharply intensified human rights abuses both inside and outside the country,” said Daniela Gavshon, Australia director at Human Rights Watch. “Australia needs to make it clear to Vietnam that merely holding another meeting is not itself a measure of progress.”

Vietnamese authorities have stepped up transnational repression, reaching beyond the country’s borders to suppress dissent by targeting human rights activists, government critics, and others in violation of their human rights. In late September, the overseas political organization Viet Tan alleged that Vietnamese police had detained three of its members in Cambodia, including Tran Hiep, and transferred them to Vietnam.

The Vietnamese government currently holds more than 160 political prisoners for exercising their rights to free expression or for peacefully advocating human rights and democracy. Dozens more are detained and awaiting trial. During the first nine months of 2026, Vietnamese courts convicted and sentenced at least 31 activists to lengthy prison terms, including the prominent bloggers Hoang Thi Hong Thai and Huynh Ngoc Tuan. During this period, the police arrested at least 46 other people for criticizing the government.

The upcoming dialogue should also focus on Vietnam’s suppression of independent labor organizing. Both Australia and Vietnam are parties to the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership, which contains provisions obligating Vietnam to carry out reforms and to allow the creation of independent trade unions. However, as Human Rights Watch recently documented, the Vietnamese government has not actually reformed its policies and practices, and prevents workers from forming and operating genuinely independent unions.

The Australian government should communicate concerns about these violations. It should also outline how Vietnam’s failures to reform will affect Australia-Vietnam relations and implementation of trade and tariff provisions in the partnership. 

“The Australia-Vietnam human rights dialogue will accomplish little so long as concrete outcomes aren’t formalized,” Gavshon said. “A human rights dialogue shouldn’t be a box-ticking exercise but instead a diplomatic exchange with real-world implications.”

Spider-Man’s Dark Reboot Universe Unveils Twisted New Design For Classic Villain

Warning! This post contains SPOILERS for Midnight Spider-Man #2

Marvel's new Midnight Universe has begun, featuring dark and horrific new takes on classic heroes in an alt universe unlike anything we've seen before. However, the latest issue of Midnight Spider-Man has confirmed that villains are receiving just as terrifying redesigns as well.

As seen in the first issue of Midnight Spider-Man, this dark new continuity's Peter Parker has become a truly grotesque David Cronenberg-style body-horror version of a human-spider hybrid, the result of twisted Oscorp experiments in the pursuit of eternal life. Contrasting with the classic Spider-Man motto, "With great power comes great responsibility", the darker world and circumstances surrounding Peter have instead instilled the phrase: "power makes monsters." Now that Peter has become a monster himself, his future looks to be all kinds of horrifying.

Now, Midnight Spider-Man #2 by Phillip Kennedy Johnson and Scietronc has debuted a brand-new version of Doctor Octopus, one that's easily more disturbing than any version seen before in the pages of Marvel Comics.

The story in Midnight Spider-Man #2 is aptly titled "The Eight-Handed Man," giving readers our first look at Otto Octavius and his role as a lead researcher at Oscorp behind the Augustine Project and the company's mission to achieve eternal life via twisted experiments involving animal-human hybrids. Initially shown as a reclusive germaphobe who hates interacting with foreign contaminants and individuals outside his lab, an incredibly diminutive Otto keeps himself isolated behind glass with various metal pins sticking out of his head.

The lab itself is full of dissection tables with various genetically mutated specimens that have gruesomely been ripped apart by his "assistants". Rather than having his traditional four robotic arms, this dark new version of Doctor Octopus controls four human beings through cybernetic brain implants somewhat resembling the classic tentacles. All of their individual humanity looks to have been stripped away, leaving them as nothing more than remote-controlled extensions of Octavius. Likewise, Octavius even threatens to turn Felicia Hardy into his next assistant should she fail to recover the spider that escaped in the new series' first issue.

Having wound up in Peter's backpack, the large radioactive spider ultimately fused with Peter and embedded itself in his chest. Currently unaware of this monstrous transformation, Otto desperately wants the spider back, and for Felicia to ensure that Peter signs a contract of employment to start working at Oscorp itself.

Ultimately, this super dark intro for Midnight Doc Ock makes it clear that as monstrous as Peter Parker might have just become in this dark new universe, Oscorp is the true and much bigger monster behind it all.

Nevertheless, it's extremely unsettling to see "Spider-Man" launching himself at Felicia Hardy (perhaps a future Midnight Black Cat?) and her men when they come to Peter's house, ripping the security guards to shreds with all kinds of blood and gore, leaving the house with only Felicia alive and paralyzed with fear. Clearly, this is going to be one of the scariest and most violent Spider-Man ever seen.

With the first issue strongly implying that the spider killed Uncle Ben before fusing with Peter, the grief and horror that will come once Peter discovers this will likely be what fully turns him against Oscorp (he had been more optimistic about joining them and turning them into a force for good from within, despite Uncle Ben suing them for their involvement in the deaths of Peter's parents).

Assuming Peter will find a way to control or at least direct his monstrous impulses toward those responsible, it follows that Midnight Spider-Man will likely wage a one-man war against Oscorp in future issues. As such, his new status as a human-spider hybrid will no doubt be seen as a major breakthrough by Octavius, making a confrontation between the two more than inevitable in the issues to come.

10 Strongest Superheroes And Villains Batman Has Beaten In Official DC Canon

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Batman has built his reputation over the years through detective work, exemplary martial arts skill, and an ability to counter threats that would overwhelm any regular human. In keeping with that reputation, his victories have not been confined to Gotham’s criminals, as the Dark Knight has repeatedly confronted opponents whose powers and resources far exceed his own.

Across DC’s history, he has repeatedly outsmarted formidable supervillains, challenged fellow members of the Justice League, and survived encounters with figures whose strength borders on godlike. These triumphs rarely come through brute force alone, as he relies instead on preparation, tactical judgment, and an extensive understanding of the people standing against him.

The Hyperclan first appeared as a seemingly benevolent group of alien champions, claiming it could protect Earth more effectively than the Justice League could possibly manage. Its members reshaped the Sahara and executed supervillains, while telepathically manipulating public opinion around the world to weaken the League before their invasion finally began.

Batman suffered an early defeat when A-Mortal destroyed his Batplane, but the Hyperclan wrongly assumed that the Dark Knight had died in the crash during their initial assault. He would later infiltrate their Antarctic citadel, identify the invaders as White Martians, and use fire to incapacitate several members, helping the League defeat the rest.

Aquaman has long served as both Atlantis's monarch and a prominent member of the Justice League, fighting beside Batman against threats to humanity. His superhuman strength, resilience, and command of marine life complement his exceptional swimming ability, while his experience as a warrior makes him dangerous outside his kingdom as well.

Batman’s more decisive encounter with Aquaman came in Batman: Endgame, where his Justice Buster armor coated the Atlantean in dehydrating foam, subduing him during the Joker’s toxin-driven assault. Arthur had also challenged Batman in “Reign of the Joker,” after Batman declined direct intervention against the Joker-backed takeover of Atlantis, but the Dark Knight subdued him through pure hand-to-hand skill.

Ra's al Ghul is the formidable commander of the League of Assassins, directing its operations through an international network built to achieve his radical goals. His centuries of experience, considerable wealth, fighting expertise, and repeated rejuvenation through the Lazarus Pit have sustained his rivalry with Batman throughout their many encounters.

One of their most notable confrontations occurred in Detective Comics #840, where he defeated Ra’s and his assassins in Gotham, then secretly committed him to Arkham under the false identity of Terry Gene Kase. Their other confrontation in Batman #244 also ended in a victory for the Dark Knight, as he knocked Ra’s unconscious after recovering from a deadly venom.

Vandal Savage has spent millennia influencing human history, using his long life to gather immense resources and pursue political power. His immortality followed exposure to a radioactive meteorite in prehistoric times, an event that also increased his strength, speed, and intelligence and enabled his repeated clashes with generations of superheroes.

When Savage gunned him down in Crime Alley, Batman was only able to survive thanks to the stolen Fabergé egg he had been pursuing. The object contained a White Lantern ring, whose power revived Bruce and enabled him to overpower Savage in Batman: Universe #5, before its energy signature summoned the Green Lantern Corps.

Barry Allen is the best-known bearer of the Flash mantle, serving as Central City’s protector and a longtime member of the Justice League. His bond with the Speed Force grants extraordinary speed, accelerated perception, rapid healing, and molecular vibration, while also letting him travel through time and occasionally cross dimensions.

During the Joker's assault in Batman #35, Batman's Justice Buster mapped Barry's movements and sprayed frictionless fluid across his path, sending the speedster crashing into a building. A separate encounter unfolded in Batman: The Red Death #1, where Earth-52's Bruce used stolen Rogues' weapons and Mr. Freeze's formula to capture Barry and absorb his Speed Force.

The Batman Who Laughs is a version of Bruce Wayne from Earth-22, a Dark Multiverse reality where killing the Joker exposed him to a deadly toxin. The infection warped his mind, and he slaughtered his world's Bat-Family and Justice League before joining Barbatos's Dark Knights during their assault on the main DC Universe.

Batman's final clash with his counterpart culminated in The Batman Who Laughs #7, after the villain's serum infected Bruce and threatened to drive him insane as well. Alfred, however, secured a young alternate Bruce and used his blood to purge the infection, while Batman hatched a plan that left the villain imprisoned beneath the Hall of Justice.

The Joker is Batman's most infamous adversary, a dangerous criminal mastermind whose theatrical crimes repeatedly combine elaborate murder plots, chemical weapons, and psychological torment. His origins vary across retellings, though a familiar account follows a struggling comedian who falls into chemicals during a robbery, leaving him disfigured and driven by bitterness ever since.

One of their earliest decisive encounters unfolded in Batman: The Man Who Laughs, where Batman foiled the Joker's plan to poison Gotham's drinking supply and delivered him to Arkham. Their later clash in Batman #251 ended after Batman saved the Joker's final hostage from a shark tank and caught the escaping clown on an oil-slick beach.

Diana is the Amazon princess of Themyscira and one of the Justice League's most powerful founding members, representing her people in humanity's affairs. Her strength, speed, endurance, and flight are complemented by exceptional battlefield experience, enchanted bracelets, and a Lasso that compels anyone it binds to speak truthfully in battle.

She also clashed with Batman during Batman: Endgame, where she joined the infected heroes in pursuing Bruce through Gotham after the Joker’s toxin overwhelmed her. Using the Justice Buster armor, Batman was able to drive her into a corner and employed the Bind of Veils to convince her she had won and end her pursuit of him.

As ruler of Apokolips, Darkseid commands armies, advanced weapons, and lieutenants devoted to enforcing his rule across the worlds he seeks to dominate. The New God ultimately pursues the Anti-Life Equation, hoping to erase free will itself. This ambition frequently puts him at odds with the Justice League, often leading to some devastating confrontations.

Batman faced him in Final Crisis #6, firing the Radion round Darkseid had used to kill Orion and striking him in the shoulder. This contributed greatly to weakening the ruthless despot as the rest of the Justice League eventually finished him off. An earlier Superman/Batman #12 standoff saw Batman successfully corner Darkseid and force the safe release of Supergirl.

Though their methods often differ, Batman and Superman share one of the Justice League's strongest friendships, built through decades of working side by side. Clark recognizes Bruce's resolve and strategic prowess, while Batman relies on Superman's integrity, compassion, strength, and willingness to stand between danger and those unable to defend themselves.

Superman was one of the more difficult opponents to beat in the Endgame storyline, but Batman managed it nonetheless. Bruce’s Fenrir armor served him well for a while, but Clark ripped through the machinery and carried Bruce high above Gotham. Thankfully, Batman kept some Kryptonite-laced gum hidden in his mouth and spat it into Clark's eye, definitively stopping the Kryptonian juggernaut.

Distributed LLM Training Across Metros: Streaming DiLoCo in MaxText

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Traditionally, training state-of-the-art Large Language Models (LLMs) relies on a monolithic cluster of accelerators, where all devices are collocated and tightly coupled via high-bandwidth to constantly synchronize gradients. However, physical and cost constraints make scaling a single slice increasingly difficult.

To scale further, we can connect multiple compute slices across geographically distributed datacenters. However, when training across multiple datacenters over wide-area networks (WAN), standard data-parallel training suffers from severe network stalls because slow WAN transfers cannot be fully overlapped with computation.

To address this, the Google DeepMind and Google Research teams developed DiLoCo (Distributed Low-Communication), a distributed optimization algorithm designed to make training resilient to these low-bandwidth, high-latency connections. In this blog post, we discuss the core concepts of DiLoCo, how it is implemented in MaxText (Google's open-source JAX-based LLM training framework), and the performance benefits it brings to large-scale LLM training on Google Cloud.

DiLoCo was introduced by Google DeepMind in the paper DiLoCo: Distributed Low-Communication Training of Language Models. Instead of synchronizing gradients across all devices at every single training step (as in standard Data Parallelism), DiLoCo partitions training across M independent compute groups, referred to as learners, and allows each learner to perform many local optimization steps independently before communicating across groups.

Despite communicating significantly less frequently (by a factor of H, e.g., 1-2 orders of magnitude less) than standard synchronous training, DiLoCo has been shown to achieve comparable convergence and model quality.

The Vanilla DiLoCo described above reduces total communication volume by a factor of H, however, all learners still transmit their weight deltas for the entire model all at once at every H-th step. On a thin network pipe, that full-model transfer creates a large periodic stall every H steps. This issue is alleviated by Streaming DiLoCo, which is described in the next section.

In multi-slice training, communication within a single slice is handled by high-speed Inter-Chip Interconnects (ICI). When scaling to multiple slices, synchronization across slices must traverse the Data Center Network (DCN) or, if the slices are geographically distributed, wide-area networks (WAN).

Within a single datacenter, DCN bandwidth is high enough that JAX and XLA can often hide standard Data Parallel all-reduce collectives by overlapping gradient communication with backward-pass computation. Across datacenters or congested links, however, bandwidth drops and latency rises sharply. Standard DP stalls at every step waiting for global gradient synchronization, collapsing Model FLOPS Utilization (MFU).

Streaming DiLoCo eliminates this synchronization stall by partitioning the model's parameters into P disjoint fragments. Over the course of H steps, every single fragment in the model is synchronized exactly once, achieving the exact same outer-loop cadence as vanilla DiLoCo without spiky bandwidth utilization.

Using a roofline model (similar to the JAX Scaling Book), we can calculate the network bandwidth required to prevent communication stalls for a model with ∣Θ∣ parameters (at b bytes per parameter, e.g., b=2 for bfloat16) and per-step compute time tstep=tfwd+tbwd. For example, the standard Data Parallel algorithm transfers 2∣Θ∣b bytes per sync via an all-reduce collective, which is a distributed reduce-scatter where each node reduces a different part, followed by an all-gather so all participants receive all parts. To achieve zero communication stalls, we overlap the reduce-scatter of the gradients with the backward pass, which produces them and takes tbwd time. Therefore, the peak required bandwidth is Bdp ≈ 2∣Θ∣b / tbwd.

Thankfully, females have not yet turned their back on the printed page, but ominous trends in reading habits for girls and boys suggest that at least the level of enthusiasm for reading among both genders is in decline. And as motivation to read slips, so does reading skill.

Where this relationship is most pronounced, however, is with boys, who have significantly lower levels of reading engagement than their female counterparts and fall well short of girls in reading achievement on global, national, and state-level reading assessments. When it comes to enjoyment of reading, time spent reading for pleasure, and diversity of text read (i.e., newspapers, magazines, fiction, nonfiction) girls in the U.S. and in most countries of the world have significantly and consistently higher levels.

I’ve often discussed in my books on the topic of boys and reading, as well as in speeches and lectures at conferences, that for those of us with sophisticated literacy skills, we hope the same for the guys within our care. This is because we desire boys, and indeed all youth, to experience the joy and benefits of the type of highly developed literacy abilities that we value and that have in very real ways contributed to our personal enrichment and professional accomplishments. However, there’s no shortcut to getting there. We can’t expect boys who rarely read to ever possess the level of literacy we wish for them. Parents who accommodate their sons’ non-reading habits are in some ways enabling stunted literacy growth.

Research tends to do a good job of documenting the decline in reading engagement and achievement without always specifying the causes. Thus, it’s helpful to look at the literature related to motivating students to read and then by inference appreciate the reasons for the lack of motivation. For example, researchers have discovered factors that positively influence motivation to read, such as (a) creating contexts for free self-selected reading, (b) giving students more text choices, and (c) structuring collaboration for reading and learning. Looked at from the opposite perspective, then, one can infer that students are disengaged when time is not set aside for free reading, when students’ choices about what to read are restricted or non-existent, and when students only read in isolation without opportunities to discuss and share texts.

These same lessons from research can help guide parents who are eager to create supportive environments for reading for their sons.

I’m fond of saying at the conclusion of my talks on this subject that “A boy’s love of reading doesn’t begin with scholarship, it begins with discovery." The more we do to help boys discover or rediscover the joy and pleasure of reading, the greater the chances they’ll become engaged and skillful readers for life.

The Stedelijk Museum in Amsterdam has announced that the Dutchman Krist Gruijthuijsen will take over as its new director from July 2027.

Moving from Finland, where he has since 2025 been director of the Espoo Museum of Modern Art (EMMA), he said he wants to bring an outsider’s perspective and a focus on how the modern city connects with the world.

“The Stedelijk museum’s strength is in bringing large and Modernist exhibitions to the Netherlands,” he tells The Art Newspaper. “People go to the Rijksmuseum for Rembrandt and to the city [Stedelijk] museum to understand how Amsterdam has developed, to understand Modernism through the lens of art and design.”

The Stedelijk Museum enjoyed success last year with an Anselm Kiefer show—organised with the neighbouring Van Gogh Museum—and an exhibition of work by the Dutch photographer Erwin Olaf, increasing annual ticket revenue compared to 2024 by almost a quarter, to €10.8 million. Its current Yayoi Kusama show has been extended to the end of January due to its popularity.

This success stands out against a backdrop of Dutch museums reporting financial pressure and a fall in visits compared to pre-pandemic levels. The Rijksmuseum’s most recent annual report describes internal discussions that took place about the fact visitor numbers fell 4% in 2025 to 2.3 million, while the Rembrandt House Museum recently warned that rising tourist taxes will damage cultural tourism.

But Gruijthuijsen said he is more interested in variety than visitor records. “I’m not a visitor numbers director,” he says. “It is quality rather than quantity: what we need economically, and the rest is a plus. It is very difficult these days because people only choose what they know. My goal is first and foremost to support artists and art.”

Gruijthuijsen takes over from Rein Wolfs, who has been the director since 2019.

From one of the first exhibitions to establish Van Gogh’s work in 1905 to experimental shows of the 1960s, the Stedelijk Museum created an international reputation for contemporary art, which Gruijthuijsen wants to make relevant today. “The DNA of the city museum is a modern day and global perspective—trying to find connections,” he says.

From the Finns, he will also bring an important lesson in terms of improving diversity and tackling under-represented subjects, he says: “Less talking, more doing.”

A few months back, I wrote a piece here about the viral conspiracy that Earth would lose gravity for 7 seconds on August 12th. I discussed how, despite not actually believing that the event would occur—because of a slew of reasons—the open-minded part of me (albeit still skeptical) nevertheless suggested developing a quick “just in case” protocol for the unlikely event. I received some positive feedback on how I exemplified the importance of cautious thinking throughout the piece and how, in context, we can engage two important dispositions towards thinking (Dwyer et al., 2016): open-mindedness and skepticism. On the other hand, though, some implied a level of foolishness in my thinking.

The latter cohort, I believe, missed my point, and so I aim to make it clearer in this follow-up. In that previous piece, I stated on multiple occasions that I didn’t actually believe the event would occur. In fact, I highlighted why it was nearly impossible.

But I used this event prediction as an example to make the point that cautious thinkers are typically happy to make minor mistakes in efforts to avoid risk, even if the risk is extremely low. That is, through cost-benefit analysis (consistent with many existing cost-benefit models we commonly see in psychology; e.g. Lave and March 1975; Thibaut and Kelley 1959; Uehara 1990), good thinkers may often choose a 99 percent likely mistake made out of caution, if the mistake yields minimal negative impact (e.g., looking foolish in front of one’s spouse). This will be selected over doing nothing potentially “foolish” (e.g., in light of being 99 percent “sure”), despite the disastrous impact that 1 percent chance would have because they failed to act (e.g., if the event occurred, death would be a near certainty if we were outdoors).

Simply, good thinkers don’t care about labels of wrong or right; they care about the outcomes of their decisions. It’s cautious thinking… and critical thinkers are cautious thinkers.

It’s much like hope for the best, plan for the worst. It’s why we develop contingency plans; it’s why we buy insurance. It’s largely why I don’t go outside in a thunderstorm, despite the odds against getting hit by lightning. It’s why I buy a lotto ticket once every few weeks, when the thought occurs to me on the day of a draw (i.e., I will not notice the loss of a couple of euros [thankfully], whereas winning the lotto would be life-changing). You never know.

With that, context (as well as moderation) is key. It might be foolish to buy too much insurance, to spend €200 weekly on the lotto, or to waste your time hiding in a cellar for three hours during your run-of-the-mill thunderstorm. Again, caution is considered in terms of cost (be it time, money, or status) relative to benefit or risk avoidance.

From an epistemological perspective, an established truth is that we cannot see into the future (as of yet, anyway [there’s some doozy of a meta-joke in there]). We do not know what’s going to happen. Objectively, we can place likelihoods on certain events happening, but that’s it. It’s our human interpretation that labels these probabilities as impossible, unlikely, possible, likely, highly probable, definite, etc. We might say definite, but that’s not exactly a guarantee. Consider the late Daniel Kahneman’s work on weather and stock forecasting (see also Kahneman, 2011). If we lived in a world of certainty (which we don’t), you’d never hear the phrase, “Well, that was unexpected.”

In moments of excitement and uncertainty, good thinkers are able to quickly evaluate probabilities, not just in terms of odds, but in terms of interpretation of outcomes (e.g., consider naturalistic decision-making [Klein, 2008]). The ability to do this quickly is a result of previous consideration and reflective judgment on priorities and values—things for which good thinkers would be concerned and cautious. They apply these considerations efficiently, if and when they need to. For example, despite less than a half-percent chance of your school burning down during your tenure in that school, how many fire drills were you a part of as a student? A lot, right? But you knew what to do in case it was for real.

Bizarrely, what’s your plan for a zombie apocalypse? Stop for a moment and think about it... Did your answer come to you fairly quickly? Even in the extreme unlikelihood of such a thing happening, it’s likely you thought about it or something similar before, at least once.

There’s nothing wrong with considering such things. Planning in such a way is a good cognitive exercise. It’s also good for creativity!

Cautious people often think in similar ways. Such cautious thinking might get accused of being “overthinking.” But that’s not necessarily a bad thing, unless it becomes a common or costly focus. Indeed, catastrophizing can be problematic. Moderation is important. But so too is caution, especially when it comes to ensuring good outcome-focused thinking. So, don’t sweat making small mistakes in life, especially if it’s done out of calculated caution. There’s nothing foolish about it.

The flight marked the official arrival of the T-19A Kestrel light trainer at Davis Airfield in Colorado Springs. Instead of wading through decades of defense bureaucracy and drawn-out congressional appropriations, USAFA secured its next-generation aerobatic aircraft in just 10 months, cutting through red tape with the help of a private donor group led by Andrew McKenna.

The T-19A is a derivative of the commercial GB1 GameBird. By skipping the traditional procurement, leadership bypassed the budget overruns and timeline slips that have paralyzed programs like the Boeing T-7A Red Hawk. The lightweight plane is purpose-built for agility thanks to its designer, German aerobatic champion Philipp Steinbach. He is a co-founder of Game Aerospace Composites in Bentonville, Arkansas, which produces the off-the-shelf civilian variant certified to pull a staggering load of ten G-forces.

The Kestrel went from concept to delivery in 10 months using $3 million in private donations, whereas the Red Hawk has spent over a decade in development using billions in taxpayer funds and still faces multi-year delays. The T-19A will fill a major gap in modern USAFA flight training by giving cadets early exposure to high-G, unlimited aerobatics and complex energy management before they ever step into Undergraduate Pilot Training.

The T-19A, or GB1 GameBird, is the only American-built two-seat aircraft approved for Unlimited-category competition aerobatics. Its all-carbon monocoque airframe lacks traditional mechanical wing-attach joints, relying on a continuous composite spar cured inside an autoclave. This gives the plane an operating empty weight of roughly 1,290 pounds (585 kg) while enabling symmetric flight loads of up to 10-G at its aerobatic maximum limit.

Under the hood, a 583-cubic-inch Lycoming AEIO-580-B1A flat-six piston engine delivers 303 horsepower (226 kW) to a four-blade composite MT-Propeller system. An inverted oil and fuel system keeps the engine running under sustained negative-g flight and zero-g push-overs. Cadets command the airframe from tandem seats equipped with Garmin G3X Touch glass displays, combining edge-of-envelope stick-and-rudder forces with modern electronic flight screens before stepping into standard turboprops.

To make the transition, USAFA pulled six active-duty instructor pilots from the 306th Flying Training Group to stand up the first training cadre. These officers will shape a new syllabus to train cadets and establish a demonstration and competition team. The initiative comes as the wider military struggles with a persistent shortfall of roughly 2,000 operational pilots, forcing leadership to seek shortcuts to build stick-and-rudder skills earlier in pilot development.

The Academy's existing fleet jumps from unpowered TG-16A gliders and basic T-53A/T-41D piston trainers straight to the UPT's T-6A Texan II turboprops after graduation. USAF Chief of Staff General Ken Wilsbach and Academy Superintendent Lt. Gen. Paul Moga secured the four airframes thanks to a $3 million donation coordinated by philanthropist Andrew McKenna. Meanwhile, the USAFA paid $400,000 in operational funds for initial tooling, spares, and flight gear, along with a projected annual operating budget of $1 million to maintain the fleet.

The Kestrel will act as an intermediate high-performance step, teaching cadets how to manage high power-to-weight ratios, rapid throttle response, and aggressive energy management. Standard trainers are built for stability and prohibit extreme maneuvers. Because the Kestrel is certified for unlimited aerobatics, instructors can deliberately put cadets into inverted spins, vertical stalls, and disorienting attitudes, teaching them instinctive recovery skills that directly prevent real-world loss-of-control accidents.

The Air Force is facing a persistent pilot deficit and needs to push students through UPT faster without lowering standards. By giving top cadets advanced stick-and-rudder proficiency during their junior and senior years at the Academy, they arrive at UPT needing fewer remedial rides and moving through the syllabus faster. Additionally, building an aerobatic competition team around the Kestrel will push the selected cadets to fly under public scrutiny with the precision and discipline they will further refine in operational flying squadrons.

Operating the Kestrel at the USAFA in Colorado Springs comes with real performance trade-offs. The Academy's Davis Airfield sits at an elevation of 6,572 feet (2,003 meters), and summer heat often makes the air feel like it is over 9,000 feet (2,743 meters). That means the T-19A's non-turbocharged engine loses roughly 22% to 26% of its power. That thinner mountain air requires longer takeoff runs and leaves pilots with less excess engine power during demanding maneuvers.

Weight limits create another hurdle for daily operations, as the Kestrel can weigh up to 2,200 pounds (998 kg) for standard point-to-point flights. Its maximum takeoff rating for extreme 10-G aerobatics only applies at 1,940 pounds (880 kg) or less. Subtracting the plane's 1,290-pound (585 kg) empty weight leaves just 650 pounds (295 kg) for people, gear, and fuel. Once an instructor and a cadet strap in, only enough weight allowance remains for 25 to 30 gallons (95 to 114 liters) of fuel.

That limits intense flights to roughly 45 minutes, requiring ground crews to refuel and turn the planes around quickly between sorties. Moving cadets from unpowered gliders into a 200-knot (370 km/h) airplane is also a major operational adjustment. Because rapid spins and heavy G-forces can easily cause motion sickness or make new pilots lose track of which way is up, instructors must design a step-by-step program that gradually introduces cadets to the plane's capabilities.

Built entirely from carbon fiber, the T-19A brings modern composite design to a general aviation field long dominated by traditional steel-tube frames. Even compared to the newer carbon-fiber Extra NG, the Kestrel weighs about 110 pounds (50 kg) less at an empty weight of 1,290 pounds (585 kg). Its wing and fuselage tanks also hold 81 gallons (307 liters) of fuel, giving it a cross-country range of over 1,000 nautical miles (1,852 kilometers).

In the air, the Kestrel reacts far faster than almost all other military trainers. The USAF's primary turboprop trainer, the Beechcraft T-6A Texan II, rolls at 180 degrees per second and handles forces between positive 7.0-G and negative 3.5-G. The Kestrel more than doubles that agility, rolling at 400 degrees per second while withstanding up to positive and negative 10-G.

Unlike older aerobatic planes, the Kestrel avoids the sluggish handling caused by a heavy nose-mounted radial engine. Its horizontally opposed Lycoming responds instantly, without the twisting-torque side effects of a radial engine during aggressive maneuvers. That instant throttle response also translates into blistering acceleration and vertical climb performance. Its power-to-weight ratio allows the Kestrel to take off in just 680 feet (207 meters) and climb at 4,200 feet per minute (1,280 meters per minute).

This sharp handling marks a major departure from the Academy’s existing T-53As, which are militarized Cirrus SR20s built to train for instrument flying rather than agility and dogfighting. The Kestrel bridges that gap, letting cadets practice inverted flight, vertical spins, and high-G recovery before they arrive at active-duty pilot training bases to begin the pipeline to the cockpit of a warplane.

The T-19A's performance derives from its lightweight carbon-fiber fuselage built around a single, continuous spar that runs straight through the cabin floor. By dropping heavy metal wing joints, Game Aerospace saved weight while also making the 25.26-foot (7.70-meter) span wings strong enough to withstand extreme loads up to 15-G.

Up front, the engine spins a 74.8-inch (1,900 mm) German-made MT-Propeller built from a compressed wood core wrapped in a tough composite shell with stainless-steel leading edges. Because standard engines lose oil pressure when flying upside down, the Kestrel uses a specialized inverted oil system that keeps oil flowing smoothly between 55 and 95 psi (3.8 and 6.5 bar) at temperatures up to 245°F (118°C), even during violent tumbling maneuvers.

Instead of flexible steel cables that can stretch under heavy loads, the pilot's control stick connects to the wings and tail via rigid metal pushrods mounted on sealed bearings for instant, slop-free response. Small aerodynamic paddles beneath the wings help balance the large carbon ailerons so the stick stays light at speeds up to 175 knots (324 km/h), while tiny fins along the wing surface keep airflow smooth and prevent sudden wing stalls down to 60 knots (111 km/h).

5 Legendary Rock Bands With Only One Perfect Album, Ranked

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

It can take years for a rock band to finally get radio play, but for a lucky few exceptionally talented acts, all it takes is one album to make a lasting impression. It's not every day that a band comes together, puts out a single project, and then leaves it all behind. Whether it's a side project, an inability to maintain the same chemistry beyond the debut, or simply a matter of unfortunate circumstances, there's something fascinating about bands with only one album to their name.

Arguably, a one-album discography can represent some of the most honest work in a band's career, seizing its artistry at its rawest. There's also the pressure of making that one record count, leaving listeners with something truly memorable. Without further ado, here are five of the greatest rock bands with only one perfect album, ranked.

Following the massive MTV success of Enema of the State and Take Off Your Pants and Jacket, it was only a matter of time before fans (and their record label) expected Blink-182 to follow up with another hit-making album built around their tried-and-true three-power-chord formula. But for members Tom DeLonge and Travis Barker, they needed an outlet to explore heavier sounds and darker subject matter—something that contrasted with Blink-182's rambunctious energy, immature antics, and crass humor. Enter their side project, Box Car Racer. Originally conceived as a creative outlet during a break from touring, the short-lived project gave DeLonge and Barker room to branch out musically and experiment with a different sound, something they would continue exploring in later Blink-182 releases and with DeLonge’s future band, Angels & Airwaves.

Inspired by DeLonge's interest in post-hardcore bands like Fugazi, Refused, and Quicksand, Box Car Racer ditches the fast, chord-driven riffs he was accustomed to playing in favor of heavier guitar textures, distorted riffs, and drop-D tuning—all channeled into their self-titled album. While traces of his pop-punk roots remain unmistakable, the band isn't chasing catchy hooks designed to dominate radio airplay. Instead, explores darker themes ranging from apocalyptic destruction to Freemasonry—definitely a far cry from singing about teenage break ups and personal insecurities.

The Los Angeles hardcore punk scene owes a lot to The Germs. Their original run may have lasted only four years in the late '70s, but the band helped lay the groundwork for the scene, leaving an influence that would carry on for decades. The brainchild of lead vocalist Darby Crash and guitarist Pat Smear (who would later play with Nirvana and the Foo Fighters), The Germs drew inspiration from a surprisingly wide range of artists, including Iggy Pop, The Ramones, and The Sex Pistols. Their only studio album, GI, is regarded as one of the earliest full-length hardcore punk records and is notably produced by Joan Jett of The Runaways.

GI proves that hardcore's unrestrained chaos doesn't have to come at the expense of structure or artistic expression. Rather than reducing the genre to theatrical antics and one-minute bursts of noise with barely intelligible lyrics, The Germs showed that hardcore could be just as expressive and musically thoughtful as any other genre. Hardcore punk may have been at the heart of their sound, but it was their frenetic aggression, both on record and during notoriously chaotic live performances that sometimes got them kicked offstage, that cemented their status as legends of the first generation of Los Angeles punk bands.

Their time in the industry might be short-lived, but The La's got the world singing in karaoke thanks to their hit track "There She Goes," which has become an anthem for many romantic comedies. For a bunch of Brit lads making jolly, Merseybeat sing-along tunes à la The Kinks, The Who, and The Rolling Stones, The La's were surprisingly perfectionistic. This not only became a huge obstacle during the production of their debut album but also put a strain on the band's relationships, eventually contributing to their breakup. It's quite a contrast for a band whose repertoire sounds so raw and organic to have gone through a recording process filled with rejected takes, endless re-recordings, and rotating producers.

While The La's weren't happy with their self-titled debut album (which took a total of three years to record), its sound became a blueprint for Britpop, thanks to its emphasis on guitar-driven music and 1960s British rock influences. The album's legacy can still be heard in the music of Oasis, Arctic Monkeys, and Fontaines D.C. While it's a shame The La's never released another studio album, they at least had the chance to create something that would stand the test of time. The recording process may have been a pain, but the fruits of their labor are unmistakable.

Them Crooked Vultures takes the term "supergroup" to another level, bringing together a trio that's a match made in rock heaven: Josh Homme of Queens of the Stone Age, John Paul Jones of Led Zeppelin, and Dave Grohl of Nirvana and the Foo Fighters. Their sound feels like a more laid-back take on Homme's work with his own band, but with a catchier, more modern rock edge. The project took years to come together, with the trio making its debut in 2009. All three musicians bring their own playing styles to the table, but somehow, everything clicks.

Their self-titled album is a real treat for rock fans, especially when you consider the heavyweight talent packed into this tight trio. There's no shortage of heavy guitar riffs, but the album isn't all about loud guitars and flashy musicianship. It also throws in funk influences, piano arrangements, and plenty of catchy vocal hooks from Homme. There's a little bit of everything here, and that's what makes the record such a familiar yet fresh listen. Rather than sounding like three rock legends simply showing off what they can do, Them Crooked Vultures brings their individual strengths together without the need to overpower each other.

Britain's biggest troublemakers have long sparked debate over whether they're true punk pioneers or just cynical posers who were more interested in the lifestyle than the music itself. It doesn't help that bassist Sid Vicious reportedly could barely play his own instrument, with Steve Jones having to step in and re-record the bass tracks. Still, whether you buy into their authenticity or not, there's no denying that the Sex Pistols left a lasting mark on anti-establishment culture. Their rebellious attitude was a sharp contrast to Britain's prim and proper image, particularly the kind of regal etiquette expected in a society still shaped by its monarchy.

Their only studio album, Never Mind the Bollocks, Here's the Sex Pistols, is pretty much the epitome of their bad-boy attitude. Dropped by record labels, plastered across tabloids, and met with concert cancellations, the album attracted plenty of public scrutiny for its outrageous content. Few songs were as controversial as "God Save the Queen" and "Anarchy in the U.K.," both of which captured the spirit of punk at its most rebellious. The album was packed with political undertones, and at a time when censorship was still rife in an uptight British society, the Sex Pistols came in swinging with a sound that refused to follow the rules and lyrics that could give the royal family a headache.

Ayumu Imazu Talks ‘musubi’ & The Belief that Music ‘Brings People Together’: Billboard Japan Monthly Interview

He chatted with Billboard Japan about the making of the song & what he envisions for the Budokan and beyond.

Singer/songwriter Ayumu Imazu spoke to Billboard Japan for its Monthly Feature interview series spotlighting artists and releases on its radar.

Imazu’s latest single “musubi,” released digitally on Oct. 2, has become the biggest hit of his career. “musubi” is a love song portraying the bonds between people through the motif of the “red string of fate.” A demo posted to TikTok in late August, when only a single verse and chorus existed, spread widely and led to an “emergency release.” The track swept the charts across streaming services immediately after it dropped and earned the ​26-year-old artist his first-ever No. 1 on Billboard Japan’s Streaming Songs chart (dated Oct. 14, 2026), marking the biggest breakthrough of his career to date.

2026 has also been a milestone year for him — it marks the fifth anniversary of his major-label debut, he announced a partnership with SKY-HI-led BMSG in February, and in May he released CLASSIC, his first album in four years. After pouring all of himself into CLASSIC, “musubi” emerged as Imazu circled back to the foundation of his music, which is about “bringing people together.” He chatted with Billboard Japan about the making of the song, the inspiration he’s drawn from his new environment at BMSG, and what he envisions for the Budokan and beyond.

“musubi” has been generating a huge response. When did you start working on it?

Around two months ago, in mid-August. I finished the first verse and chorus at the end of August and posted it on TikTok maybe two days after that.

The title is symbolic. “musubi” is a love song about the bonds that tie people together. Where did the idea come from?

In May​,​ I released CLASSIC, my first album in four years, and most of it was new songs I co-wrote with overseas producers. I put so much into making it that I felt pretty empty in a way afterward. I was gradually taking things in and building my energy back up while touring, and as I considered what to make next, I vaguely thought it’d be nice to do something about bringing people together, because I feel that’s the biggest purpose of making music. I started making it with that as the big goal.

Bringing people together through music — is that one of the motivations you’ve always held onto?

Maybe not always, but with the way the times are going… right now I feel like everyone’s own worlds of the things they like are scattered all over the place. It’s becoming harder for everyone to get excited about a single piece of music or to share the same feelings. That’s why I wanted to make a song like “musubi.”

You announced your partnership with BMSG this February, and in April you also shared that you’d gotten married. Do you feel those changes in your working environment and life stage have influenced you?

I wasn’t thinking about those things in particular. More than anything, I really wanted to make something that carried on from the album. I put out CLASSIC after forming the partnership with BMSG, wanting to show the fullest extent of myself all over again, like, “This is Ayumu Imazu.” So doing the same thing didn’t feel right. My next focus naturally turned to what’s at the core of my music, the theme of connecting people.

CLASSIC was an album built around powerful, polished dance numbers that were quintessential Ayumu Imazu. At the same time, vocal-driven songs that showcase the beauty of the Japanese language, like “Anata To Itai” and “musubi,” are essential to any discussion of your discography. How do you think about balancing or blending the two?

When I was first starting out and trying to show people who Ayumu Imazu is as an artist, I was really conflicted between going with dance tracks and the fact that I also love ballads. But my strongest feeling was that I didn’t want to choose (just one), so after putting out all kinds of songs, I now feel the dance tracks are very Ayumu Imazu, and I also really feel that slow-tempo songs like “musubi” are me, too. The range of musical styles is broad, but I don’t really feel like I’m moving away from the foundation of my own feelings and personality.

Does your actual production process change depending on the style of the song?

It does. With dance tracks​,​ I usually start with the beat, and with ballad-type songs like this one, I usually work out the chords on acoustic guitar or piano, write the melody to a certain point, and then program it.

What did you focus on most in terms of your vocals?

The demo was already out there and everyone had gotten used to hearing it, so when I recorded the final take, I had to either make it sound similar or surpass it. I’d gotten used to hearing it myself, especially from just before the chorus through the first half of the chorus, so that was where I struggled most, and probably the part I was most particular about.

Where do you feel you managed to “surpass” it?

If anything, I think it has a different kind of appeal. The demo had its own appeal as a demo, that relaxed quality of casually singing at home. The released version has more feeling put into it, I guess… it really comes across as emotional. Personally, I really feel that difference.

When you shared the demo, you posted on TikTok that you were “still deciding on a title.”

I read through lots of comments and ideas. A lot of people said they “want to use it at their wedding,” so when I was finishing the full version, I thought, “Maybe I could make it a bit more like a wedding song.” It was a great reference and really helped me out.

Tell us about the changes in your environment, too. BMSG, the company you partnered with, brings together artists brimming with individuality under the slogan “​​Never extinguish talent.” What kind of inspiration and influence have you gotten in that environment?

At first​,​ I was completely wrapped up in my own stuff, but I recently took part in BMSG FES for the first time and got to see the other BMSG artists perform, and I was really inspired. Personally, it was my first time riding a trolley float, so that was a great experience too. [Laughs] Also, it reminded me that it truly is a music-first company, and since what everyone expresses is pretty different, I thought it was really great that every group and every artist puts their own colors front and center without being bound by any set format.

BMSG also has a vision called “TYOISM,” about reaching the world from Tokyo. With you joining — someone who moved to the U.S. at 14 and is still based there and in Japan — it seems likely to spark some new chemistry.

I think I have a pretty good feel for how things are done abroad, so when it comes to opening doors to global challenges, I definitely want to do that together with BMSG. Mr. Hidaka (SKY-HI) and I had talked about that kind of thing from the start.

Now that five years have passed since your 2021 major-label debut, how do you see the phase you’re in as an artist?

Right now might be a phase where I just make music really freely, without overthinking it. Last year or so, I was thinking a lot about things like, “I want to release this kind of song and become this kind of artist,” but lately I feel like I’ve thought it all through, so it’s more like I’m going with the flow.

What artists have you been listening to a lot lately?

I’ve had Remi Wolf’s album (Mud) on heavy rotation lately. I really love indie rock-type stuff, too.

You have a headlining show set at Nippon Budokan in January 2027. Tell us how you’re feeling going in.

The Budokan is truly a stage I’ve set as a goal, so I think it’s going to be a really flashy show. [Laughs] More people than ever are listening to my music, and I’ve got a concert booked at my biggest venue yet, so I’m super happy and feel like all I have to do is keep doing what I’ve been doing. I want to keep creating spaces where everyone can come together as one and enjoy music at bigger and bigger venues.

Plants mix different breeding strategies to survive in a world with changing climates and fewer bees

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Simulations show plants that reject their own pollen and plants that self-fertilize can coexist for ages, swapping dominance without settling.

Apple trees have a strict biological rule for reproduction. They reject their own pollen. That means they need a completely different type of apple tree nearby to produce fruit.

While many wild plants rely on this exact same partner system, plenty of other plant species have evolved to drop the rule entirely, allowing them to successfully fertilize themselves.

A population holding both kinds was supposed to be passing through on its way to choosing one rule or the other.

In computer simulations of evolving plants, the mixed populations didn’t get there. With nothing in the environment changing, the two kinds kept trading dominance for as long as the simulations ran. Neither kind took over.

The researchers call it a volatile mixed mode. A mixed population in the wild that looks unstable, they wrote, may be in a lasting state of its own.

Tamar Friedlander, a senior lecturer at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem’s Robert H. Smith Institute of Plant Sciences and Genetics in Agriculture, led the work with Amit Jangid.

Ohad Noy Feldheim of the University’s Einstein Institute of Mathematics is the third author. Everything here happened inside a model.

In an interview with Earth.com, Friedlander said the result broke a habit of thought. Biologists expect that when a population has several options, some are better than others, she said.

Over the long run, the population is supposed to reach the better one and stay there. Earlier models had the mix fading away or holding steady at fixed proportions.

“Thus, it was very surprising that we didn’t get either of these under a large parameter regime,” she said.

The populations, in her words, “not only did not settle to either of the ‘pure’ strategies (only self-fertilization or only outcrossing) but also did not settle at fixed proportions of these strategies.”

The rejection system works like a lock and a set of keys. The female side of a flower makes a toxin that stops a pollen tube from growing. Each pollen grain carries proteins that disarm the toxins of other mating types but not its own.

About 40 percent of plant families use some version of this, including wild tomatoes, potatoes, apples, pears, citrus, and coffee. Washington State University‘s tree fruit site has a primer on why orchards need two varieties.

The system can break. A mutation that silences the toxin, or adds a key that fits the plant’s own lock, produces a plant that can fertilize itself.

That plant gains a sure route to seeds when pollen is scarce, but it passes on two sets of genes to each seed instead of one.

It pays for that with inbreeding. Seeds from self-fertilization carry more harmful mutations, and more of them fail.

The plants that reject their own pollen pay a different price. Their pollen is useless on every plant of their own mating type, so a type that grows common finds fewer partners.

Friedlander’s team simulated populations of 500 plants for 100,000 generations, discarding the first 50,000 as a warm-up.

Each plant carried two copies of the gene set, and each copy held one toxin and nine keys. Every protein was written as a string of 18 amino acids that could mutate.

Two dials set the outcome. One was how badly self-fertilized seeds suffer from inbreeding. The other was how loosely the keys fit the locks, which decides how many mating types a population can hold.

With heavy inbreeding costs and loose-fitting keys, every plant ended up rejecting its own pollen. With light costs, every plant ended up self-fertilizing.

In between lay the third state, where the share of self-rejecting plants rose and fell for the rest of the run and the number of mating types swung with it.

Types tended to vanish several at a time, in what the authors describe as avalanches. What triggers those collapses, they wrote, is still obscure.

A shortage of pollen was expected to push populations toward self-fertilization, since a plant that can pollinate itself doesn’t need a visitor. The model found the opposite.

When cross-pollen was scarce, the range of settings in which self-rejection won grew at the expense of the mixed state. Friedlander said the explanation lies in how the two sexes compete.

When pollen is plentiful, every female is sure of a partner and only the males compete, so female proteins evolve mainly to avoid matching their own pollen.

When pollen is scarce, females must compete too, which makes them easier to match and raises the number of mating types a population can support.

Researchers often treat self-fertilization as a plant’s backup plan for when cross-pollination fails, she told Earth.com. Self-fertilizing plants do turn up at the edges of a species’ range, where partners are few.

The move from rejecting self-pollen to accepting it has long been treated as a one-way street. A plant that gives up the rule usually sheds the flower traits that attract insects and purges some of its harmful mutations.

Across much of the model’s settings, though, the end state didn’t depend on the start, and a population that began self-fertilizing could evolve the rejection system from scratch.

The exception was the loosest-fitting keys, where the mutation needed for the reverse trip was so rare that the wait became impossibly long.

The model also held the cost of inbreeding fixed, so it describes the short term, before prolonged self-fertilization purges enough mutations to change the odds.

Mixed populations exist in the wild. Long-term studies that track one population’s proportions over time are lacking, the authors wrote, and those are what would test the prediction.

The model also predicts that mixed populations carry fewer and shorter-lived mating types than purely self-rejecting ones, and more inbreeding written into their genomes. Both could be checked in plant genomes.

Asked which population she would most like to watch, Friedlander pointed to wild tomato species growing in South America.

She also said the model doesn’t apply directly to orchards, where trees are genetically identical and do not evolve. Self-rejecting crops do depend on pollinators, she added, and a decline in them could cut yields.

A warming climate and fewer pollinating insects both change the pressures on how a plant reproduces, the authors wrote.

Whether real populations respond by picking a side, or by swinging between the two for generations, is what a long field study of those wild tomatoes could begin to answer.

The full study was published in the journal PRX Life.

Photos reveal scale of Trump’s White House ballroom, drawing criticism

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Earlier this month, Trump’s White House ballroom and military bunker laid its final structural steel beam, with politicians and critics weighing in on the scale of the project.

Recently taken images of the extension, which show the structure with massive concrete shear walls, steel beams, and columns, along with a partially enclosed roof structure, have drawn attention to the scale of the building.

No sign of its planned neoclassical ornamentation has yet been implemented.

Officially called the East Wing Modernization Project, the structure dwarfs the Executive Residence and West Wing.

Trump announced the laying of the last steel beam in the superstructure on 4 November, the day after the national midterm elections.

“This is a special structure,” Trump said on a tour on 4 November, according to NBC News. “It’s going to be not only one of the biggest, but it’ll be the most beautiful ballroom, I think, anywhere in the world, and they’ve been trying to get it for 150 years.”

It says everything about Donald Trump that his gilded ballroom dwarfs the size of the People's House—and it says everything about the American people that they repudiated him loudly and clearly last Tuesday. pic.twitter.com/ou23LpAes8

Since then, there has been a renewed wave of criticism online over the project, ranging from outrage to humour.

Former senator and presidential candidate Hillary Clinton wrote on X (formerly Twitter), commenting on the size of the structure and noting the midterm election’s “repudiation” of Trump.

“It says everything about Donald Trump that his gilded ballroom dwarfs the size of the People’s House—and it says everything about the American people that they repudiated him loudly and clearly last Tuesday,” wrote Clinton.

Congressman Jim Clyburn took a similar stance in a statement on Facebook, noting that the ballroom was “much bigger” than the rest of the White House.

Architecture critic Kate Wagner took a more humorous approach, noting that the ballroom is not neoclassical in construction. Wagner compared it to a Ramada Inn, a chain of hotels in the US, that they will “glue pediments to”.

“[It’s] an outcome designed in a lab to make everyone angry. possibly the most trump building ever built,” wrote Wagner on X.com.

The ballroom has been controversial since Trump demolished the White House’s West Wing in October 2025. The National Capital Planning Commission approved the project in April.

It has faced legal challenges, with US District Judge Leon issuing a stay in response to a lawsuit led by the National Trust for Historic Preservation.

the fact that trump's ballroom is clearly not neoclassical in any way shape or form is possibly the funniest moment in architectural history

In August, the Supreme Court allowed the project to continue, though the dissenting opinion said it was “likely unlawful”.

Despite the legal challenges, construction has continued, with Trump’s team leaning on the national security aspects of the underground bunker.

The administration has also floated plans to add a screening facility at Sherman Park – a small park to the southwest of the White House. Trump also installed a paved helipad on the White House lawn.

How did England cling on against Spain and what did Tuchel do differently?

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

James Trafford (right) kept Spain out in the latter stages

England held on to beat Spain 3-2 with a backs-to-the-wall finale in the Nations League to finish top of their group.

But many observers would have been feeling a sense of deja vu from the World Cup semi-final when England sat back and were beaten by Argentina.

So what was different this time? BBC Sport has a look.

First of all the context of the two games was entirely different.

England were 1-0 up against Argentina in the 55th minute on neutral territory in Atlanta - and still had all their players.

Anthony Gordon had put them ahead but Lionel Messi set up Enzo Fernandez and then Lautaro Martinez late on.

But against Spain the best performance of Thomas Tuchel's reign had them 3-0 up through goals from Harry Kane, Ezri Konsa and Ben White.

Elliot Anderson then fouled Lamine Yamal for a penalty, which Mikel Oyarzabal scored - and that began the siege at a partisan Riyadh Air Metropolitano.

Nico Williams made it 3-2 but Spain could not find an equaliser, thanks in part to England keeper James Trafford.

"Half an hour feels like two hours against a team like this [with 10 men]," Tuchel told ITV afterwards. "That's the way it is. Without the red card we were clear winners."

60 minutes of perfection, 30 minutes of chaos - an England win for the ages

England cling on to stun world champions Spain in thriller

Making defensive subs is fine when it works - but when it does not, boy will a manager hear about it.

In the World Cup last 16, England had been 2-1 up against Mexico in the Azteca when Jarell Quansah was sent off.

Tuchel brought on three defenders for a defender, a midfielder and a forward.

But England won 3-2 and Dan Burn's cameo was the stuff of legend.

However, nine days later with England 1-0 up against Argentina, Tuchel did the same again - three defenders on for a defender, midfielder and forward.

The first of those subs, with Konsa replacing Anthony Gordon, was in the 72nd minute.

And England just sat back until Argentina broke their resistance.

However, against Spain this time, despite England being down to 10 men and facing incessant pressure, Tuchel was not so quick with defensive subs.

Myles Lewis-Skelly, replacing Harry Kane, was the first on after 78 minutes - 15 minutes after Anderson's sending off. And then five minutes later he brought on two more players - but one of them was a striker in Dominic Calvert-Lewin.

Burn replaced fellow defender White in the 89th minute.

"Harry played 78 minutes, Jude [Bellingham] 83 minutes and then just defending and defending, it's tiring," explained Tuchel.

"So we needed fresh energy. I was asked yesterday what happens if there needs to be a critical or crucial substitution. And I said, I feel free enough to take any substitution if I think it is necessary to help the team and to influence the game.

"I feel free enough to do that and I think there was a lot of defending needed. So we just went for some fresh legs."

Dan Burn usually comes on when England are trying to hang on - but he was only introduced for the last few minutes

England actually faced more of a siege this time than against the Argentines, the stats confirm.

In the final 30 minutes they faced 19 shots from Spain, with seven on target and an expected goals (xG) total of 2.53.

Argentina, in that time, had 10 shots, with four on target and an xG of 1.41.

In terms of passes from England the numbers were similar with 65 against Argentina and 61 against Spain - both times with an accuracy rate of about 64%.

In Thursday's 2-1 win against Croatia, as a comparison, they tried 557 passes with accuracy of 89%.

"For half an hour it was Mexico reloaded and it felt like we were back in the Azteca. We had to overcome 30 minutes which, against Spain, felt like three hours," Tuchel told BBC Radio 5 Live.

"The difficult thing is it's not only a red card, it's a red card, and a goal so that makes a completely new game. Thirty minutes against the world champion feels like three hours. I said it before in the stadium and wave after wave after wave."

England were criticised for blowing a 2-1 half-time lead at Wembley in their first Nations League game with Spain.

But on that day their final 30-minute figurers were much better - with only five shots faced and 176 attempted passes with a 90% accuracy.

So what was different this time? Perhaps the team showed more resilience and unity?

After Spain had pulled one back England's players had a huddle.

"I love the fact the team went into a huddle and if anything the manager's stepped back because he wants to see the player reaction," said ex-England defender Matt Upson on BBC Radio 5 Live.

"I almost think that the trauma from that sending off of Elliot Anderson has almost been a positive thing for England because it's given the team a chance to get together and cope in what is an incredibly difficult situation and come through the other side.

"There was so much going on in this game. England had to suffer and came through the other side."

Defender and goalscorer White was asked about the huddle after the game.

"Just stay calm, when you're one down it becomes more difficult and things change so it was all about managing that," he said.

Another factor was the performance of England keeper James Trafford, who made six saves - with four of them coming in the final 12 minutes of the game with the score at 3-2.

He made an incredible double save to deny Fermin Lopez and Lamine Yamal in injury time.

Upson said: "I think that double save was the moment for Spain and Trafford had the answers."
