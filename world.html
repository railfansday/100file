Destination Spotlight: Crystal Mountain

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Skiing at Crystal Mountain, Michigan. (Photo: Crystal Mountain)

As a proud Michigander, I spent many childhood summers “Up North,” a region of the state with beautiful lakes, cozy cabins, extensive hiking and biking trails, and every other manner of outdoor summer fun. But “Up North” isn’t strictly a summer destination; it’s a four-season adventure hub that offers everything from spectacular fall leaf-peeping to world-class winter skiing.

Among the best of these Michigan winter playgrounds is Crystal Mountain. Just a short drive from Traverse City and a stone’s throw from Sleeping Bear Dunes National Lakeshore, Crystal Mountain got its start 70 years ago with a single rope tow, an old car engine, and a dream. From these humble beginnings, it has blossomed into one of the best and most visited ski hills in the state, with 59 downhill runs, six chairlifts, two magic carpets, and more than 100 acres of skiable terrain.

The mission at the heart of the operation has always remained the same: to be a place where families come together to have fun, make memories, and experience all the best the outdoors have to offer. This philosophy has immortalized Crystal Mountain in the hearts of midwesterners like me, encouraging generation after generation of skiers to fall in love with the sport and the outdoors. It’s where I learned to ski—and where you can still take lessons from PSIA/AASI-trained instructors.

Beyond the ski hill, there’s no shortage of things to do at Crystal. Glide through this winter wonderland on cross-country skis—the resort’s 25 kilometers of trails were named best in the state by Midwest Living Magazine. Or let a team of Belgian horses do the gliding for you as you enjoy the views of the forests and hills from a horse-drawn carriage. Lace up your ice skates in the heart of the resort’s village at Barr Park, or explore the Michigan Legacy Art Park, a 30-acre reserve located on the grounds of Crystal Mountain where you can snowshoe, hike, and take in the sculptures and scenery.

When you’ve had enough of the cold, unwind at the outdoor hot tub, the indoor pool at the Peak Fitness Center, or the award-winning Crystal Spa. Consistently recognized as one of the best in the country, Crystal Spa features infrared saunas, eucalyptus steam rooms, and a relaxation lounge with a cozy fireplace, making it the perfect oasis after a day spent out in the winter weather.

There are endless ways to enjoy Crystal Mountain, but the true magic of this place can’t be credited to any one thing—it’s the feeling you get when you’re here. It’s the memories you’ll cherish for a lifetime.

Established in 1956, Crystal Mountain is a family-owned, four-season resort located 28 miles southwest of Traverse City, Michigan, and a short drive from Sleeping Bear Dunes National Lakeshore. The 1,500-acre resort features lodging, dining, downhill and cross-country skiing, two championship golf courses, shopping, the award-winning Crystal Spa, Michigan’s only alpine slide, Peak Fitness, the Park at Water’s Edge, Michigan Legacy Art Park, weddings, a conference center, and real estate options.

Willie Mays and Pat Summitt to be honored with U.S. Postal Service stamps next year

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

WASHINGTON (AP) — Baseball Hall of Famer Willie Mays and longtime Tennessee women's basketball coach Pat Summitt will be honored with stamps next year, the U.S. Postal Service announced Monday.

Mays, who died in 2024 at age 93, was a 24-time All-Star and two-time National League Most Valuable Player.

His stamp, designed to look like a baseball card, will feature a portrait of Mays with the New York Giants and an inset image of the outfielder making his famous, over-the-shoulder catch of a drive by Cleveland's Vic Wertz in the 1954 World Series opener. Mays batted .301 with 660 homers and 1,909 RBIs from 1951-73.

The orange background refers to a color of Mays' teams, the New York and San Francisco Giants and the New York Mets. It will be the 50th stamp of the USPS's Black Heritage series and was designed by Antonio Alcalá, an art director of the USPS.

Summitt, who died in 2016 at age 64, led Tennessee to eight NCAA championships and compiled a career record of 1,098-208 from 1974-2012. Her stamp features an oil-on-primed-linen portrait created by Roberto Parada. It was designed by Ethel Kessler, an art director for USPS.

Summitt is in the Naismith Basketball Hall of Fame and the Women's Basketball Hall of Fame.

Posted November 9, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills

Finding the right therapist can feel a lot like dating. You browse through profiles, ask around, and then schedule an initial meeting, hoping there’s chemistry. Sometimes you click immediately, but other times, you have to wait for your therapist to grow on you.

Just like any other relationship, the therapeutic relationship is about finding the right match. Not every therapist is going to be a good fit for you. Just because someone has a degree, a cute office, and a comfy couch doesn’t necessarily mean they have the capacity to support your needs.

Therapy is an investment of your time, money, and emotional energy. You deserve to feel that the person sitting across from you is genuinely invested in understanding you and helping you grow.

Of course, good therapy isn’t always comfortable. In fact, the most meaningful sessions are often the ones that leave you a little confused and a little raw. Questioning your assumptions, confronting your beliefs, and recognizing patterns are non-negotiable aspects of successful therapy.

But there’s a difference between being challenged in therapy and feeling that your therapist isn’t meeting your needs.

Here are seven signs your therapist might not be the right fit.

If your therapist thinks you’re always right, all your exes are narcissists, and everyone in your family is toxic, you may have hired a very expensive cheerleader.

A good therapist will validate your feelings, but they’ll also, in the nicest possible way, confront you and challenge your assumptions.

Ask yourself if your therapist invites you to consider another perspective and helps you confront how your own fears, expectations, and behaviors contribute to your experiences rather than just validating you.

You’ve spent three sessions talking about how your sister wasn’t invited to your wedding because she had an affair with your fiancé. And the next week, your therapist asks, “So, how are things with your sister? Have you two been spending time together?”

Therapists don’t need photographic memories. We’re human, and we talk to a lot of people. But it’s our responsibility to find ways to remember important patterns, relationships, and events in someone’s life so we can help them build awareness and make connections.

When a therapist repeatedly forgets significant details, you may find yourself feeling betrayed and spending session time reiterating the same stories. Also, paying attention to your story is a part of helping you feel seen. It’s challenging to develop trust when you feel dismissed, which is inevitable when someone who is paid to pay attention to you doesn't.

You chat, you laugh, you catch up, but the conversations don’t lead anywhere.

You discuss your weekend, complain about your boss, recap your latest dating disaster, and suddenly your 50 minutes are up. See you next Tuesday!

Therapy isn’t always about measurable progress, and chitchat is part of relationship building, but there should be some sense of direction, insight, and exploration that your sessions build on.

Seemingly ordinary conversations can allow us to make significant discoveries. A conversation about a disagreement with a roommate might open the door to exploring your discomfort with conflict or your tendency to prioritize everyone else’s needs.

You don’t need a life-changing revelation every week, but over time, therapy should offer more than an opportunity to vent.

Your mother is histrionic. Your dad is a workaholic. Your boyfriend is avoidant. Apparently, you’re the only psychologically healthy person in your world.

A good therapist helps you understand relational dynamics, identify your own role in them, and empowers you to make changes, rather than handing out diagnoses to people they’ve never met.

It’s tempting to explain difficult relationships by attaching psychological labels to the people involved. However, your therapist’s job is to recognize problematic behaviors, not assign a clinical diagnosis based on someone else’s account.

The goal of therapy should be to guide you to understand your relationships, empower you, and help you develop healthier ways of navigating them.

You’re reaching for tissues as you tell your therapist about a painful breakup, and suddenly you’re hearing a 15-minute story about their divorce.

Appropriate self-disclosure can be useful, but if you’re learning more about your therapist than yourself, something isn’t right.

Therapists are people, too. We have families, hobbies, favorite books, and experiences that sometimes overlap with those of our clients. Sharing a personal anecdote can help normalize an experience, strengthen connection, or reduce isolation. However, if you regularly find yourself listening to your therapist’s problems, comforting them, or wondering how to redirect the conversation back to yourself, the boundaries may have become blurred.

You tell them something they said didn’t sit right with you, and suddenly they’re more defensive than your ex.

A good therapist can acknowledge mistakes, welcome feedback, and work through ruptures in the therapeutic relationship.

Your therapist, who is also human, may make inaccurate assumptions, forget a session, or approach a sensitive topic a little too abruptly. Human error is inevitable in any meaningful relationship, including therapy. Misunderstandings in therapy may serve as an opportunity for you to voice your needs and boundaries. If your therapist is able to respond with accountability, that might serve as a corrective experience and may even strengthen the therapeutic alliance.

In contrast, a therapist who becomes dismissive, defensive, or makes you feel guilty for expressing your concerns may unintentionally reinforce the very relational patterns you’re trying to change.

A good therapist isn’t there to make your decisions for you. They should help you understand what’s driving your choices, explore your options, and develop the confidence to make decisions that are right for you.

Of course, therapists can offer guidance, practical suggestions, and recommendations when appropriate. Therapy is about helping someone evaluate their choices, not telling them how to live.

For example, you might already know that your boyfriend is letting you down. What you may not understand is why leaving him feels impossible or why being alone feels more frightening than being with someone who is not all that nice to you. Good therapy helps you develop the insight and emotional capacity to make your own decisions, even when those decisions are difficult.

Your therapist doesn’t need to be your best friend, your personal cheerleader, or your life coach. They should be someone who helps you see what you might not want to see while creating an environment where you feel safe enough to explore it.

And remember, a therapist can be perfectly competent and still not be the right fit for you.

Different therapists have different personalities, approaches, and areas of expertise. Some are more structured and directive, while others are more exploratory. Some employ humor and use themselves as a part of the process, while others are a little more distant and leave more room for reflection. What works beautifully for one person may feel unhelpful to another.

Many therapists offer complimentary consultation sessions for you to ask them questions and assess fit. Don’t feel like you have to commit to the first therapist you meet.

Therapy asks you to be vulnerable, honest, and willing to examine parts of yourself you may have spent years avoiding. Finding someone you can trust with that process is an important piece.

Netflix's 4-Season Dark Fantasy Series Was This Close To Greatness

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Netflix really had something great on its hands with its four-season dark fantasy series, but it fell short in the very last moments. The world of television is already pretty unforgiving, and the streaming landscape has made it even harder for projects to stand out amid a sea of content. The fantasy genre doesn't make things any easier, since it's all too common for these shows to flop after only a single season if they don't check all the right boxes. It's for all these reasons that it's rather remarkable when anything sticks.

One of these remarkable TV shows is Netflix's The Umbrella Academy. The show premiered in 2019 and pretty quickly proved itself to be unlike any other fantasy series on screens. The Umbrella Academy is deliciously dark, but this is layered up with a quirky, character-driven narrative that aims to subvert the superhero genre at every turn. This isn't an ordinary Marvel or MCU show. Instead, Umbrella Academy is all about complicated families.

The story of The Umbrella Academy revolves around a group of seven adopted children with miraculous abilities who are raised as a superhero team, known, of course, as the Umbrella Academy. This family ultimately fractured and dissolved, but the estranged siblings find their reunion as adults following their adoptive father's death. The show is all time travel and apocalypse-level events, layered with family dysfunction and character quirks. For its first three seasons, The Umbrella Academy was on top of the world. Unfortunately, season 4 is when things collapsed.

It's important to note that, otherwise, The Umbrella Academy is an excellent series that is still entirely worth watching. Unfortunately, there is really no denying that the fourth and final season really missed the mark. While seasons 2 and 3 both earned impressive 91% scores on Rotten Tomatoes, season 4 came in shockingly low at a "rotten" 55%. Something went wrong, and critics had no trouble pointing out exactly what.

First things first, The Umbrella Academy season 4 suffered because of its rushed narrative. While previous installments were made up of 10 episodes, season 4 had only six. It simply wasn't enough time to meaningfully wrap up the established story, but things got even worse when the episodes seemed to focus on all the wrong things. Beloved characters were misused or abandoned, tired plot devices were repeated, and the final big twist of Umbrella Academy felt, to many, like a slap to the face. Overall, it was just unfortunate.

The fact that, despite all of this, The Umbrella Academy is still an easy show to recommend is really a testament to the excellence of those first few seasons, especially seasons 2 and 3. It is perhaps better to know ahead of time that the ending won't satisfy the way many would have hoped. However, if you hope to give this fantasy series a try, keep in mind that the ending may still be revised.

Like many great superhero TV shows, The Umbrella Academy is based on a comic book series. Like many other great fantasy TV shows, The Umbrella Academy got ahead of its source material. This meant that Netflix's writers were on their own for season 4 and its ending, on top of the disadvantage of having to wrap things up in six episodes. The good news is that future installments of The Umbrella Academy are on the way, with original creators Gerard Way and Gabriel Bá continuing to put out new content.

Since The Umbrella Academy came to its unfortunate close in 2024, the comics have proceeded with several new issues, the most recent having been released on October 28, 2026. The series finale, issue #6, is expected to hit shelves near the beginning of 2027. This will definitively wrap up the story as it was intended to be, and there will be absolutely no need to rush through in the way Netflix did. So, if you get hooked on The Umbrella Academy, take comfort in the knowledge that there is still light at the end of the tunnel.

6 X-Men Who Were Better Leaders Than Cyclops

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Although Cyclops remains the X-Men's best-known field commander, his leadership has never represented the only approach capable of holding the team together during difficult periods. Different eras have elevated mutants whose judgment, compassion, resolve, or ability to negotiate with outsiders proved better suited to the crises confronting their people and allies.

Effective leadership can take many forms, from calming a divided team to challenging decisions made by those in authority. The following X-Men have demonstrated abilities Cyclops could not always match, guiding their teammates through conflicts that tested loyalty, judgment, and the meaning of their cause during moments of profound uncertainty.

Cyclops is often remembered for directing the X-Men in battle, but Emma Frost developed a different record through education, political maneuvering, and the management of powerful institutions. Her career began as the White Queen of the Hellfire Club, where she controlled its Inner Circle and established the Massachusetts Academy for gifted young mutants.

At first, Emma trained her students as the Hellions while pursuing the Inner Circle’s ambitions, but Trevor Fitzroy’s attack killed many of them. The tragedy drove her away from the Hellfire Club, and after helping Banshee rescue young mutants from the Phalanx, she became co-headmistress of Generation X at the Massachusetts Academy.

Emma eventually joined the X-Men and shared control of Xavier’s school with Cyclops, overseeing students while Scott continued directing the team’s field missions. Her direct manner could create friction, yet her experience with young mutants and her ability to read people helped her address problems that tactical planning alone could not resolve.

Krakoa offered Emma a larger arena for those abilities, particularly when her intervention helped secure a United Nations vote on mutant sovereignty. As a Quiet Council member, she also helped direct the Hellfire Trading Company, coordinating trade and transport routes that supported the nation and brought endangered mutants out of hostile territories.

Jean Grey joined Xavier’s first class of X-Men alongside Scott Summers, whose tactical ability soon established him as their field commander. She developed another kind of authority through empathy and telepathic insight, often helping teammates understand one another while confronting threats built around fear and manipulation within their ranks and beyond.

After Genosha’s destruction and a crisis at the Xavier Institute, Professor X considered stepping aside and naming Jean as his successor. His choice reflected confidence in her judgement and standing among the X-Men, even though events prevented her from formally assuming responsibility for the school during an especially unsettled period.

After years away, Jean returned to a world facing renewed anti-mutant hostility and assembled a new squad featuring Nightcrawler, Namor, and Laura Kinney. Her team confronted a campaign threatening Xavier’s dream, undertaking dangerous missions while gathering allies and responding to growing attacks against mutants across the globe and protecting vulnerable communities.

Jean’s responsibilities widened on Krakoa, where she served on the Quiet Council as part of its Summer faction, associated with more empathetic and measured voices. She later resigned to join Scott in rescuing Cable from Otherworld, then helped establish a new X-Men team whose members were chosen through a mutant-wide election.

When Professor X formed the New Mutants, Dani Moonstar emerged as a central member, eventually sharing command with Cannonball after Karma disappeared. She handled much of the field direction, while Sam offered steadiness to younger teammates, establishing an early partnership that drew on different strengths without leaving Dani’s decisions secondary.

That investment in younger mutants continued when Xavier’s school expanded, and Dani recruited Sofia Mantega, David Alleyne, Kevin Ford, Josh Foley, and Laurie Collins. The students selected her as their adviser, while her bond with Josh deepened into legal guardianship after his parents rejected him, extending her care well past classroom instruction.

The Fifty-State Initiative later recruited Dani to train Trauma, whose ability to embody another person’s deepest fear echoed her own former power to create fear-based illusions. She encouraged him to use that gift therapeutically, but Henry Gyrich dismissed her after she opposed his plan to employ Trauma as an Initiative weapon.

On Utopia, Cyclops eventually entrusted Dani with the New Mutants after Cannonball stepped down, assigning them rescue work for isolated mutants. During the War of the Realms, she again commanded an X-Men squad while Scott defended their base, dividing their efforts between the immediate battle and the safety of their home.

Charles Xavier founded the X-Men around a class of gifted teenagers, recruiting Cyclops, Jean Grey, Beast, Angel, and Iceman to study at his school. He taught them to control their mutations and use their abilities in defense of people who feared them, establishing the mission that subsequent generations continued for decades.

When the original X-Men vanished on Krakoa, Xavier recruited seven mutants from different countries to mount a rescue, assembling the second-generation roster. Storm, Wolverine, Nightcrawler, Colossus, Banshee, Sunfire, and Thunderbird joined Cyclops on the mission, demonstrating Xavier’s ability to recognize talent across backgrounds and select members suited to a particularly dangerous challenge.

After the Brood crisis left the X-Men presumed dead, Xavier initially resisted training another class, troubled by the losses already suffered among his students. Moira MacTaggert persuaded him to reconsider, and he assembled the New Mutants, renewing his commitment to educating adolescents with emerging abilities at a difficult point in his career.

Xavier’s influence also rested on the institution he built, which remained a refuge and training ground for mutant students as new generations arrived. Scott Summers became the regular field commander, while Xavier continued shaping the X-Men’s wider purpose through recruitment, teaching, and his commitment to coexistence despite entrenched public prejudice.

Rogue’s acceptance into the X-Men followed a brief period with Mystique’s Brotherhood, where absorbing Carol Danvers’s powers and memories left her struggling with the other woman’s persona. After she helped save Mariko Yashida and Colossus, the others gradually trusted her, and Wolverine’s Danger Room test ultimately led them to choose her as their field leader.

Cyclops later asked Rogue to assemble an emergency-response squad against escalating mutant threats, giving her considerable say over its composition and mission priorities. She combined experienced allies with former enemies, directing their campaign on several fronts against the Children of the Vault, Pandemic, and Hecatomb while managing competing loyalties within its ranks.

At the Jean Grey School, Rogue led a squad of veteran X-Men responsible for teaching students and protecting the campus from recurring attacks. The assignment brought instruction and security together, as threats such as the N’Garai and Weapon Omega tested the adults’ ability to keep young mutants safe while maintaining their education.

Following Krakoa’s collapse, Rogue became the head of the Louisiana-based X-Men, operating from Haven House with Gambit, Wolverine, Nightcrawler, and Jubilee. They also cared for four young Outliers whose powers were difficult to control, requiring Rogue to balance active operations with the responsibility of helping a new generation find its footing.

Storm first acquired a separate following among the Morlocks after Callisto captured Angel and threatened the X-Men who entered their underground settlement. She challenged their leader in a knife fight, won control of the community, and instructed its members to stop attacking people living above ground in New York City.

When Cyclops considered returning to the X-Men, Ororo had already proved herself through years of difficult decisions and a period without her weather powers. In Uncanny X-Men #201, she defeated Scott in a Danger Room contest and claimed command, relying on combat skill instead of elemental control or weather manipulation.

After Wolverine’s death, Storm took charge of the X-Men while also overseeing the Jean Grey School, whose students faced danger from a spreading M-Pox outbreak. She arranged for the school to be moved into Limbo with help from Doctor Strange, establishing X-Haven as shelter for mutants endangered by Terrigen Mist exposure.

Arakko later presented Storm with a new political arena, where she won the right to serve as Regent after defeating Nameless in a formal contest. As the Voice of Sol, she represented the planet before major galactic civilizations and empires and challenged efforts to subordinate Arakko to outside military interests.

The 500e Isn't Fiat's Smallest Car, But It Is The Only One You Can Drive On US Highways

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

It may be true that the smallest production cars ever built make modern compacts look huge, but everything's relative. The tiniest new car you can get for 2026 in the U.S. — the Fiat 500e — is still quite small when compared to most other rides today. Oh, and speaking of tiny, that word applies to the current Fiat lineup as well. It only includes the 500e and the adorable Fiat Topolino low-speed vehicle that was designed for stunting on your suburban neighbors – not traveling on U.S. highways.

The all-electric city car is a mere 142.95 inches long and 74.12 inches wide (including its mirrors), and its height is 66.3 inches. Note that the 2026 Mini Cooper is even more vertically challenged, as it checks in at 57.6 inches in height, yet it's nearly 16 inches longer and roughly 3.5 inches wider than the 500e. Meanwhile, for comparison's sake, one of the biggest new passenger vehicles available right now is another EV, the Cadillac Escalade IQL — a 750-horsepower, 19-foot-long American luxury beast. To be exact, the IQL stretches a tick over 19 feet, at 228.5 inches, and it's a whopping 94.1 inches wide.

The 500e's sticker price is also on the small side, at least compared to the iQL. The Fiat starts at $35,700 before a $1,995 destination charge, while the king-sized Cadillac starts at $130,405 and requires a destination charge of $2,895. Unfortunately for Fiat, the cost of the 500e doesn't look so good when you look at other small EVs like the Nissan Leaf (MSRP: $29,990) or even the Chevrolet Equinox (MSRP: $34,995).

All that said, you've got to hand it to Fiat engineers. They managed to carve out a surprising amount of interior space in the 500e's cabin in their efforts to help win America's heart. You'll find a total of 101.2 cubic feet of interior volume waiting there. True, only about 7.5 cubic feet is dedicated for rear storage behind the back seats. But the 93.7 cubic feet leftover for passengers is more than in some noticeably larger cars. For example, the 2026 Toyota Corolla only has 88.6 cubic feet of passenger space despite being almost 40 inches longer than the Fiat. The 2026 Hyundai Venue SUV checks in with 91.9 cubic feet of passenger space, even though it's roughly 16 inches longer than the 500e.

The Fiat's specs also translate into a real-world advantage. When Jalopnik's own Collin Woodard drove the 500e in 2024, he was still able to fit a 6-footer in the rear seats. Also noting the car's relatively meager 149-mile range, the bottom line was that the Fiat proved why city cars are best as EVs.

The flipside to the roomy interior is a standard cabin-equipment list that's a bit lacking, especially in a car that sets you back close to $38,000 as a starting point. Remember, the 2026 Nissan Leaf opens at $29,990, before $1,575 in destination charges, and it comes standard with blind-spot monitoring/intervention, rear cross-traffic alert, a driver-attention monitor, a surround-view system, a 12.3-inch touchscreen, and even Nissan's ProPILOT Assist driver-assist system — none of which is standard for the base 500e.

‘Rivals’ Season 2's Return Teases Its Most Ambitious Shakeup Yet | Review

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

As the saying goes, good things come to those who wait. Well, as Rivals fans, we've done our fair share of waiting. After its first season premiered in October 2024, the sleeper hit series quickly became a favorite, both for longtime fans of Jilly Cooper and new viewers discovering her world for the first time. Now, after a five-month hiatus between Part 1 and Part 2, the raunchy, soapy series is finally paying off the promise it made back in May of this year. Despite some issues with pacing — which can be almost entirely blamed on the bizarre split-season release — Rivals Season 2 proves it can not only embrace the glitz and glamor of Cooper's world, but also forge its own path and create something new.

Following the shocking death of Monica Baddingham (Claire Rushbrook), all of Rutshire is in shambles. Tony (David Tennant) is spiraling deeper into the darkness; Rupert (Alex Hassell) has been thoroughly shaken after it's revealed that Monica spent her dying moments with him, and the O'Haras are on the brink of collapse with the secret of Maud (Victoria Smurfit) and Tony's affair hanging over them like the Sword of Damocles. Although Part 2 has its share of celebratory moments — there's a bonfire night, a debauched toga party, and a wedding celebration for the ages — the ghost of Monica and the fallout from Episode 6 haunt this half of Season 2.

For much of Episodes 7 to 10, the show loses some of that bubbliness that has become signature to the series. There's still big hair and tons of sex with some globe-trotting tossed in, but there's a sense that some light has left Rutshire. Your mileage may vary with how successful this tonal shift is, especially for those who enjoyed the show for its levity; Part 2 is full of fights, tears, threats, and broken hearts. If you're familiar with Cooper's books, this is probably less of a surprise, but even those who know the 1988 novel from back to front will be hit with shocking twists they never expected.

Rivalry, sex, and deceit are the best themes for an addictive TV show.

The person who shines the most in this darkness is Tennant, whose Tony Baddingham is absolutely riveting to watch and tops even his book counterpart. He's darker than he's ever been, but he's not twirling his mustache and cackling maniacally; he's a man who has been utterly torn apart and is at his most dangerous. Dogged and slightly deranged, Tennant gives a masterful performance, showing a man who has nothing left to lose.

Alongside Tennant is Hassell, who is the embodiment of a tortured soul. Part 2 humbles Rupert even more, and Hassell is brilliant at trading in the suave and charismatic bad boy for an emotionally mature reformed rake. As he becomes the platonic safe haven to the women of Rutshire while trying to remain honorable for Taggie (Bella Maclean), his narrative arc is one of the most satisfying in the show.

For those ready for your favorite couples to finally get together, don't expect it to happen immediately. For Freddie (Danny Dyer) and Lizzie (Katherine Parkinson), the two don't waste any time in starting their affair up again. However, the stakes are finally raised as their effervescent love affair is finally weighed down with real-world consequences. With two other spouses and four kids between them, Season 2 properly shows the collateral damage of their affair. Lisa McGrillis' Valerie, who is so often played as comedic relief, is given some meaty scenes, and you might find yourself torn about whether Freddie should abandon his family in pursuit of true love.

Meanwhile, Bas Baddingham (Luca Pasqualino) has firmly wedged himself between Rupert and Taggie. Maclean gives Taggie some teeth in Part 2, which is a delightful development considering how sweet and pure she has been played up until now. When adapting a narrative, it's important to either try to stick as closely to the source material as possible, or to veer away from the foundations in meaningful ways while also keeping the spirit and heart of the story and characters.

If an adaptation attempts to adapt a story faithfully but makes a handful of changes, it will often be torn apart by the fanbase as they pick apart how it lost sight of what made the series great in the first place. Alternatively, if the story feels so drastically different that it fails to even evoke a sense of what the original story inspired, it begs the question of why it was considered an adaptation in the first place.

So, when Prime Video's The Boys franchise took its lead from the comics as imagined by Garth Ennis and Darick Robertson, it was exciting and refreshing to see where the show changed course to make a story that would be engaging and surprising for longstanding fans of the franchise and newcomers to the series.

While The Boys comics serve as the foundation for the TV show and its spin-offs, Gen V and soon the prequel series Vought Rising, they are fundamentally different to the Prime Video series. Just on the surface level, the show makes a major narrative arc around how members of The Boys are people without powers, and they generally despise supes, but in the comics, all of the members of the team take Compound V to gain permanent powers and fight against the corrupt supes.

Additionally, there is a pivotal difference in the fact that the show positions the story as this group of underdogs fighting against a massive corporation, Vought International, as they expose corporate greed, manipulation, and coercion. Evidently, the show, and showrunner Eric Kripke, had a lot of love for the comics and the ideas they introduced in a landscape dominated by more traditional superhero stories, but they were able to make it their own and create something that ultimately feels much more prescient and relevant for modern audiences.

But, if The Boys had truly departed from the source material completely, it would lead to a subpar series that failed to capture what the original creators set out to create. Instead, The Boys took the core of the story, an examination of what flawed people would be like if given incredible and veritable powers, and the core cast, and made something that felt fresh while honoring the source material.

Many of the main characters are carried over from the comics to the screen, and in many ways, they thoroughly resemble their drawn counterparts. From the abrasive and gruff Billy Butcher, to the arrogant supe with a god complex, Homelander, these characters feel largely the same.

As a result, the series benefits massively as it draws in the established fanbase alongside fresh eyes, and delivers something that genuinely feels like it belongs in the world of the comics, but does not remain constrained by what came before. With that, The Boys and its wider franchise have become one of Prime Video's biggest success stories.

Hong Kong: Joshua Wong Gets Nearly 10 More Years in Prison

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Joshua Wong speaks to protesters near the Legislative Council in Hong Kong, June 17, 2019.  © 2019 Kin Cheung/AP Photo 

(Tokyo) – The Hong Kong High Court on November 5, 2026, sentenced Joshua Wong, a well-known democracy activist, to 10 years in prison on baseless national security charges, Human Rights Watch said today. Two months of the sentence will run concurrently with his current national security sentence. Wong has been in custody since November 2020, when he was 24, and faces imprisonment until late 2036, when he will be 40.

The Hong Kong government should immediately and unconditionally release Wong. Foreign governments should denounce the sentences against him and other wrongfully detained pro-democracy activists and impose targeted sanctions on officials responsible for Hong Kong’s growing repression.

“The harsh sentence on baseless charges against Joshua Wong is cruel and unjust,” said Elaine Pearson, Asia director at Human Rights Watch. “Young people promoting social change are an asset for any vibrant society, but the Hong Kong government puts them behind bars.”

To keep Hong Kong society subdued, and to reduce the chance of an international outcry, the Hong Kong government appears to have adopted the tactic of stacking charges against influential pro-democracy leaders to ensure their prolonged detention, Human Rights Watch said. Other notable Hong Kong figures who have faced similar tactics include Jimmy Lai, founder of Apple Daily, and veteran democracy activists Lee Cheuk-yan and Albert Ho.

The court sentenced Wong, 30, under the National Security Law for “conspiring to collude with foreign forces.” The authorities accused him of “conspiring” with the activist Nathan Law and other “unknown” individuals between 2016 and November 2020 to urge foreign governments to sanction Hong Kong and Chinese government officials as they advocated for Hong Kong’s “democratic self-determination.” 

The prosecution relied heavily on the international lobbying and media advocacy that Wong and Demosisto, the political party Wong co-founded in 2016, carried out before the National Security Law took effect. Wong initiated online petitions, spoke to international media, and met with foreign officials to draw attention to human rights abuses in Hong Kong. He urged governments to sanction Chinese and Hong Kong officials responsible for abuses and to stop selling weapons to Hong Kong police that they use to carry out rights violations.

Wong had mostly stopped these activities before the National Security Law became effective. Demosisto disbanded on June 30, 2020, and Wong resigned from the group. However, the government quoted social media posts by Wong as ostensible evidence that his alleged “conspiracy” with Law to commit “foreign collusion” continued after the law took effect. In these posts, Wong said he “would not change his views” and that he would “continue to seek support from the international community” in his personal capacity, while Law and others abroad would continue their international advocacy.

The government also cited Wong’s subsequent opinion pieces and media interviews, though none directly called for sanctions, which the National Security Law specifically prohibits. It also cited his reposts of Law’s posts broadly calling for sanctions against Chinese officials and Hong Kong police.

The court ruled that Wong’s offense was of a “grave nature.” The National Security Law sets prison terms of 3 to 10 years for “foreign collusion,” or 10 years to life for offenses deemed to be of a “grave nature.” Wong formally pleaded guilty on September 2. A guilty plea normally results in a one-third sentence reduction. But a sentence reduction cannot bring the term below the statutory minimum.

Because of reporting restrictions on pretrial committal proceedings, when a magistrate determines whether to send a case to a higher court for trial, the public did not know the details of the accusations against Wong until the formal plea. The government enacted a law in 2024 to enable the court, on national security grounds, to deny the defendant’s right to ask the court to lift restrictions on reporting about the case.

By restricting media reporting on the pretrial proceedings in national security cases, the government can control the narrative and undermine the fundamental legal principle of open justice.

Since Beijing imposed the National Security Law on Hong Kong in June 2020, Hong Kong authorities have used the new legal regime—along with existing laws—to rapidly dismantle civil society and detain, imprison, or force into exile hundreds of activists and government critics.

Wong has been in custody since November 2020, when he pleaded guilty to two charges of organizing and inciting an unauthorized assembly linked to the 2019 citywide protest movement. After that, he pleaded guilty to five more charges on separate occasions, including for violating an anti-mask law, participating in an unauthorized vigil for the 1989 Tiananmen Massacre, and breaching a court ban to disclose a police officer’s personal information.

While serving these sentences, Wong was charged with two national security offenses, which carry far harsher penalties. In January 2021, Wong was charged with “conspiring to commit subversion” for participating in an unofficial democratic primary in 2020 and sentenced to another four years and eight months in prison. 

Before Wong could complete these sentences, the authorities brought the “foreign collusion” charge against him in June 2025, ensuring that he would remain in prison.

Wong became one of Hong Kong’s best-known democracy activists as a teenager, after leading a massive 2012 protest against a government educational curriculum widely seen as political indoctrination. He became the global face of Hong Kong’s democracy movement during the 2014 Umbrella Movement and co-founded Demosisto with Law and others in 2016. The party disbanded on June 30, 2020, hours before the National Security Law came into effect.

The National Security Law’s sweeping provisions have been used to punish peaceful protest and expression. Its use against Wong and others violates the right to freedom of expression under the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, which is incorporated into Hong Kong law through the city’s de facto constitution, the Basic Law, and the Bill of Rights Ordinance.

“Beijing has paid no real price for its increasing repression in Hong Kong,” Pearson said. “Concerned governments should press for the release of Wong and other activists, impose targeted sanctions against implicated officials, and review their relationship with China more broadly in light of the deepening abuses.”

Beginner Tips And Tricks In Loftia

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The world of Loftia is all about adorable sustainability, but it can be a little confusing when you first start out. Here are some tips and tricks you might need to get yourself fully immersed in this lighthearted, uplifting world.

Keep in mind that, as of the time of writing, Loftia is in its Early Access and as such does not contain its final features.

When you're just starting out, you don't need to worry too much about the aesthetics of your house. You'll want quick and easy access to your Project and Cooking Stations so that you can make what you need without having to run all the way to some obscure area first. I have my machines right inside my front door, sitting on opposite sides as you come in. This makes it easy to grab and go as I'm playing.

Learn how to get energy, upgrade your battery, and expand your solar farm in this complete energy Loftia guide.

Loftia City is the central hub of the game, full of resources and helpful NPCs you can talk to and get things from. It does, however, require you to jump in a balloon and move to a brand new area, which means waiting on loading screens.

Rather than having to hop back and forth between the city and your neighborhood constantly, try to plan ahead a little bit. Get all of your shopping and resource gathering done in one go as often as you can so that, when you do go back home, you've got everything you need to expand, plant, craft, and decorate for whatever project you're working on at the time.

You can expand your little island in Loftia to match your needs, whether that's giving yourself more room to craft, garden, or decorate. You do this by getting permits from Amani in Loftia City. It's a good idea to start with practical upgrades like a second energy station or garden bed before you focus on the upgrades that will give you more room to play with aesthetics. This lets you progress the game a bit more quickly and efficiently so you're not waiting around forever for things to grow or batteries to recharge.

Navigating Loftia City can be a little confusing at first; it's large, round, and pretty easy to run through blindly during the tutorial when you're looking for a specific NPC or location, meaning you don't get a great sense for the layout until you're exploring on your own later on.

While most of the major locations are marked, a couple of places (like the construction sight) aren't, which can make getting to them to go resource hunting a little more challenging. If you open your map (press M) and then right click, you can place a marker that will help you navigate toward your destination.

Markers will show up separately to your main quest arrows, so you don't have to worry about confusing the two.

As with any resource management game, you'll want to sort out your system for storing your resources and other materials early on. I like to have all of my raw materials in one chest, seeds and/or cooked items in another, crafted items in a different one, etc. You should also keep your chests relatively close to your machines if possible as well — again, having them by the front door is a good move, though you might also store them in front of the door as a part of your decorations.

I keep storage chests for food and seeds next to my Cooking Station, which is near where I laid out my garden beds for maximum convenience. Having a workflow makes life easier, even in gaming.

Cooking in Loftia is pretty easy, but it does involve a little clicker mini-game. You'll need to keep your mixing spoon in an ideal zone on a circle by clicking, not too quickly but not too slowly. If you can do that, you'll cook higher-quality food worth more Dew. If, however, you can't keep up with clicker games for whatever reason, Loftia has included an accessibility feature that turns off the cooking mini-game. I really appreciate this, as some folks may have limited dexterity and still want to enjoy the game; some folks might also just want to play the game in as low-stakes a mode as possible, and that's perfectly valid, too.

You can find the toggle for this by hitting Escape, then the gear for main settings, then tabbing to the Accessibility menu.

If you ever find yourself stuck or unsure what to do next, take a little time to chat with the NPCs around Loftia! Speaking to different characters can unlock additional quests, which gives you more activities to work toward when you're running low on things to do. Many of these quests also come with their own rewards, including new items, cosmetics, and other fun goodies to collect.

Even if you're more interested in decorating your home or dressing up your character than completing every task on your list, it's worth making the rounds and getting to know the locals. You never know what cute reward might be waiting behind a conversation!

Before buying a bunch of seeds, stop by Hazel's Seed Shop and check how much each crop sells for in the top-left corner of the page! The shop provides useful information about the selling prices of different crops, which makes it easier to plan your garden and decide which seeds are worth investing in.

If you're trying to make money efficiently, this is a great way to compare your options before spending your hard-earned resources. A bit of planning now can save you from filling your entire garden with crops that aren't particularly profitable!

‘The Greatest’ Review: Jaalen Best Gives a Star-Making Performance in Amazon’s Uneven but Impressive Muhammad Ali Series

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

With Ben Watkins ('Cross') as creator, the eight-episode season follows the then-Cassius Clay from Olympic gold to his first shot at the heavyweight title.

There’s something amusing about Amazon and creator Ben Watkins casting relatively unknown Jaalen Best as the lead in the synonymously eponymous new series The Greatest, as if Jon Hamm were cast in a series about a 16th-century English philosopher titled simply Bacon.

It’s also appropriate because Best turns out to be the, well, best reason to watch The Greatest, taking on the mammoth challenge of embodying burgeoning boxing legend Cassius Clay on the brink of his transformation into Muhammad Ali. Clay/Ali remains one of the most indelible personalities of the 20th century, a man whose every public utterance and movement have become the subject of adoration and imitation. That Best captures Clay’s spirit in a way that’s instantly recognizable but rarely just mimicry is a remarkable achievement, especially for an actor this young faced with a task this daunting. Now He's Playing 'The Greatest' Boxer Onscreen TV 'American Hostage' Review: Jon Hamm and Giovanni Ribisi in an MGM+ Kidnapping Thriller With Lots of Potential but Little Payoff The Greatest The Bottom Line Packs a punch. Airdate: Wednesday, November 4 (Amazon)Cast: Jaalen Best, Omari Hardwick, Dana Gourrier, Kai Parham, Amin Joseph, Michael Ealy, Giovanni RibisiCreator: Ben Watkins

Best is not, however, the only reason to watch The Greatest. Watkins, showrunner of Amazon’s Cross, has gazed at Ali’s sprawling life with intimate focus, using the first season’s eight-episode duration as a vehicle for mixing moments that will be universally familiar, moments that are well-known among appreciators of old school pugilism, and smaller, slightly more obscure incidents.

Yes, this authorized biographical series is hagiography. But it’s hagiography that understands that at least some of the “imperfections” in Ali’s character were what caused much of America to relate to him and admire him. The Greatest never shies away from the reality that for all that love, Ali was, because of his race, his religion and his outspoken nature, an existential threat to the entrenched establishment in both his sport of choice and his nation of birth. The Greatest loves the man who became Muhammad Ali for all the reasons that hegemonic, primarily white, forces hated him.

Watkins approaches The Greatest as almost a cross between NatGeo’s Genius franchise, putting the lives of great men (and Aretha Franklin) in their historical context, and FX’s Feud franchise, exploring the parallel squabbles of catty women (and Truman Capote). It results in a series with wide-ranging ambition that generally hits its marks.

Watkins, who directed the season’s first two episodes, begins his take on the story with Cassius Clay in Rome at the 1960 Olympics, first visiting the Coliseum and getting a rather pointed lecture on the ancient Romans and their love of bloodsport and then winning the gold medal.

Clay returns to Louisville with big choices. With the devoted assistance of his brother Rudy (Kai Parham), Cassius dreams of becoming the youngest heavyweight champion ever, which requires he go pro immediately. He and his father (Omari Hardwick’s Cassius Senior), a philandering but devoted painter, have different thoughts on who should train and manage him. His mother (Dana Gourrier’s Odessa), a domestic worker and the family’s glue, is convinced he’s too physically immature to enter the ring with grown men, urging caution and possibly years of additional maturation.

It’s spoiling little to say that Cassius’ ambitions win out, but he struggles to build the proper team around him. Cassius’ largely improvisational style runs counter to generations of conventional boxing wisdom, so we know it’s a matter of waiting for the right man to let Clay be Clay. Angelo Dundee (Giovanni Ribisi, gruffly affectionate), a trainer known for working with smaller fighters, is down in Miami already obsessing over his skills.

At the same time, we meet Sonny Liston (Amin Joseph) in the penitentiary dominating bare-knuckle fights. He’s released and swooped up by mafia-controlled boxing interests, which puts a target on his back with the government and boxing authorities. Liston is lethal in the ring, but he’s the very opposite of what the white boxing establishment is prepared to embrace, no matter how much Liston’s new romantic interest (Erica Tazel’s Geraldine) attempts to polish his image.

Actually, neither Liston nor Cassius, refining a performative confidence inspired by wrestler Gorgeous George (Chris Jericho in a single great scene), is what the establishment wants. The establishment is content with current champ Floyd Patterson (Brandon McKnight), the fighter both Liston and Cassius are eyeing.

Not every life would benefit from an approach this deliberate, but slow-playing this particular life is the correct choice. The first season is a build-up to Cassius Clay’s first shot at the heavyweight crown and to his transformation into Ali, a process that wouldn’t have been possible without his growing devotion to the Nation of Islam and the mentorship of Malcolm X (Michael Ealy).

Most of the incidents in the series are verifiable, though when there’s ambiguity, Watkins tends to lean into the legend — as with the famous incident involving Cassius and the casting of his Olympic gold medal into the Ohio River. It’s generally accepted that that story was embellished or fabricated by his autobiography’s ghost writer to illustrate Cassius’ response to the prejudice of early 1960s Kentucky, and darned if Watkins doesn’t engage in the same embellishment/fabrication. The series has points it wants to make and it makes them with very little subtlety, but Muhammad Ali was a figure so big that he can withstand some heavy-handedness.

Watkins is intrigued by the things that make Clay special — and equally by the things that make him dangerous and, in turn, endanger his opportunities to claim a title belt. He juxtaposes Clay’s rise with the developing civil rights movement, monitoring the pace of progress, which means something different to each character. What feels like progress to Sonny Liston — who’s treated like an animal in the fields of Arkansas and then by the media and even Clay as he becomes a title contender — isn’t the same as what feels like progress to Clay Senior, who had aspirations to be a legitimate artist and instead paints billboards. And it isn’t the same as what feels like progress to Malcolm X and Elijah Muhammad (Hill Harper), who see in Cassius Clay the most visible spokesman the Nation would ever have.

The eight-episode length of the series gives every character motivations and complications, whether it’s Cassius’ determination spawned by the murder of Emmett Till or his mother’s concern from having seen boxer Sam Baroudi killed in the ring in 1948. There are gaps in some of the characterizations, with the women feeling especially archetypal and Rudy reduced to a devoted sidekick, but it’s generally a benefit to build out a world around Ali.

Still, it’s Best’s show, and he nails the two biggest challenges in this mammoth role. He builds up Cassius’ voice from soft-spoken Kentucky lilt to a poetry-spewing trumpet, embodying the braggadocious icon viewers think they know, while showing the man who existed at the dining room table or in a pew at a house of worship. This is a Cassius Clay who lived in the spotlight and a man who existed in private as well.

Best’s physicality is perhaps even more impressive, whether it’s reproducing expressions and posture from famous Ali snapshots or navigating the ring convincingly in the series’ fight sequences. There have been decent boxing shows in the past, like FX’s Lights Out, but Watkins and subsequent directors deliver more and better fight scenes than I’ve ever seen in the medium. Unlike Hulu’s uninspired Mike Tyson scripted series, the fight scenes here stay fairly close to the historical record, so if you watch The Greatest and then watch YouTube videos of the notorious London showdown with Henry Cooper or the first fight against Liston, the reproduction and the truth line up well. Much of that is due to Best’s emulation of his character’s fancy footwork, pawing jab and viciously timed attacks.

Some of the supporting casting is initially off-putting, with several actors who are, and come across as, significantly older than the characters they’re playing. Oddly, if you stick with it, those choices work.

Amin Joseph is 46, playing Sonny Liston, who was in his early-to-mid 30s, but Liston was an old 33, having spent years in prison after his rough and impoverished beginnings. Joseph makes Liston into a flawed but tragic figure, ferocious in the ring but eager to find acceptance outside of it. A scene with Liston flailing in an effort to humanize himself for the pubic by telling a joke during a talk show appearance is one of the series’ highlights.

Michael Ealy is 55, playing a man who was killed at 39, but the Malcolm X we meet here is a Malcolm X at a crossroads, deservedly worn beyond his years. The extra gap in age between Ealy and Best helps to illustrate the gap in maturity between the two men, one just starting his public life and one soon to have it ended.

Hardwick makes Cassius Senior volatile but wounded, with Gourrier centering Odessa’s fierce devotion and pride. Parham has a strong physical presence, but the show doesn’t quite get Rudy, even denying him the chapter of his own boxing career that started at the same time. Perhaps that will get worked into future seasons.

And I hope there will be future seasons of The Greatest, because this was only one chapter in a life filled with even more potent journeys.

Based on the confidence of this round of episodes, I’d say Watkins might be able to get two seasons out of Ali’s Vietnam protest, his exile from boxing and his first loss (with Joe Frazier taking on the secondary parallel role); another full season leading up to and including the Rumble in the Jungle (with George Foreman as the parallel foil); and then a final season on the closing chapters of his career, from the Thrilla in Manila on (perhaps making more use out of Regen Wilson’s Howard Cosell).

In an ideal world, Watkins would be able to take a few between seasons, just to let Best age up a little and continue what should be a well-deserved breakout.

“I think I’m a good gift giver,” Jost tells ELLE. “Giving gifts is stressful because I do want it to be a meaningful gift. I hate the idea of just buying something because you have to give someone something.”

For Jost, the secret to getting it right has less to do with how much you spend and more to do with how much thought goes into it. “At every kind of price point for any gift, there [are] thoughtful and unthoughtful versions,” he says. “Something can be really expensive but not thoughtful.”

It’s a sentiment that aligns with Kay’s latest campaign, which encourages shoppers to go “all in” when expressing their love. How to choose the right gaming monitor for you

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Refresh rates, size and screen technology all play a part.

Choosing the right gaming monitor is a lot like choosing the right outfit. You wouldn't wear the same thing to the gym as you would to the opera, while lifting weights in a tuxedo would inconvenience you and damage the tux. Similarly, a monitor that works great for one PC or console isn't necessarily the best fit for another and gamers who enjoy competitive shooters should look for different options than those who enjoy visually rich single-person narratives.

Only you can figure out exactly which monitor you should buy. To do that, you'll first need to understand which specs best suit your needs. Modern monitors have a lot more to them than resolution and frame rate. Different display panel types suit different uses and picture quality can vary between models. A quick crash course on the basics will get you ready to shop for the monitor you'll be happiest with in the long run.

Before we dive into specs, the first thing to consider when picking a gaming monitor is the existing hardware you'll pair it with. If your PC is decked out with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 or 5080, you can run games on the bleeding edge of performance and have real reasons to consider a top-shelf monitor with a 4K panel and an ultra-high refresh rate. On the other hand, if you're plugging the monitor into an older or lower-powered GPU, consider the performance you'll actually get in the games you play. If you aren't routinely seeing frame rates above, say, 90 fps, there's no reason to invest in a 240Hz monitor. Resolution and refresh rate move in opposite directions, so if you play competitive shooters and esports titles like Valorant, it's best to sacrifice pixels for higher frames, whereas players of visually rich first-person titles like Control: Resonant can make the opposite trade-off or find a balance in the middle.

Once you know the spec range you're looking for, you can move on to other concerns such as panel type, HDR and brightness support, port selection and VRR technologies like G-Sync. Size and resolution are personal preferences. Resolution is tied to graphical performance, but there are reasons other than gaming to consider a 1440p or 4K display. If you do a lot of photo or video editing, or if you watch a lot of video, a higher resolution can be worthwhile. Size is related to resolution, in the sense that the smaller of two monitors with the same resolution will have a higher pixel density  — a finer level of image detail, which can be measured in pixels per inch (PPI).

Esports players often prefer smaller monitors around 24 inches in order to see everything at once, while single-player gamers who value immersion may gravitate toward 27 inches or higher. Larger displays also support multitasking better, but work best when you sit farther back. You'll also need to consider your ideal aspect ratio. A squatter shape is better for productivity tasks, but 16:9 widescreen is the de facto aspect ratio for gaming. Some gamers enjoy ultra-widescreen monitors to fill their peripheral vision, but in games where HUD information is crucial, ultrawide displays can force you to look away from the action.

Refresh rate is often considered the most essential specification when choosing a monitor, and should be the first thing you settle on. As noted above, your GPU should dictate this. Monitors are now shipping with up to 1,000 Hz in lower-resolution modes, at which point you'd question whether you can see the difference even if you could push your hardware that far.

The average PC gamer in September 2026 was running an RTX 5070, 5060, 4060 or even a 3060, according to the Steam Hardware Survey. Accordingly, the sweet spot for the average gamer is likely to land somewhere from 144Hz to 240Hz on a 1440p or 1080p panel. If you have a similar configuration but want to play competitive, lightweight games, you may want to look for a 1080p panel with a significantly higher frame rate. Players of these titles with top-end hardware (such as an RTX 5080 or 5090) can look for 1440p or 4K panels at any refresh rate.

That said, only you can determine the real-world frame rates you're getting on your hardware. One way to do that is to turn on the Steam, NVIDIA, or Xbox in-game overlays while playing and enable the small heads-up frame rate counter they offer. You can also use a benchmarking tool like 3DMark and some games, including Cyberpunk 2077, have built-in benchmark tests. Even if your current monitor can't reach high frame rates, you'll still see a readout showing how many frames per second your graphics card is producing.

After playing multiple games that are representative of your general interests, note where you saw the highest frame rates. Unless you plan to upgrade your GPU in the next several years, there's no need to overspend on a monitor with an unachievably high refresh rate for your system.

If you're looking for a monitor to use with a console, it's worth noting that even the best current-generation consoles like the PlayStation 5 Pro or Xbox Series X don't exceed refresh rates of 120 Hz. That means you're better off putting your money toward a larger or higher-resolution monitor instead of buying a panel with blistering speeds you'll never utilize.

After determining the refresh rate, resolution and aspect ratio ideal to your setup, the biggest choice you'll make is the type of panel your monitor is fitted with. For most gamers, the two best choices are OLED and Fast IPS, in that order.

OLED is well known for its technically infinite contrast ratios, accurate colors and near-instant response times. Because each pixel is self-emissive, it produces its own light rather than relying on a backlight; OLED pixels simply do not illuminate where an image is truly black, meaning they stay black rather than turning dark grey in the way LCD panels do. That makes them ideal for gaming, especially if you value HDR performance.

However, OLED displays have their drawbacks. They require more care to prevent OLED burn-in, a condition in which leaving a static image on screen for too long leaves a permanent ghost of that image on the screen. Newer OLEDs address this with pixel-shifting that slightly moves the picture around and pixel-cleaning cycles that help keep wear and tear even across the display. If you're the sort of person who leaves your monitor on for hours when you're not using it, an OLED will force you to change that behavior.

OLEDs are also dimmer than LCDs and are consequently worse in well-lit environments. Some manufacturers offer matte coatings on their OLED models, which can help, but these displays look best in a dim or dark room. Text fringing is another issue, as is motion flicker and while the latest generation of OLED has come a long way in minimizing them, gamers who also work with documents and spreadsheets should take those issues into consideration before paying the higher cost for an OLED. Thatcost is perhaps the biggest downside, as even entry-level OLEDs start around $400 in late 2026. If you do choose to go OLED, scout for sales.

LCD panels lack the sheer wow factor of OLED, but they are more versatile. There are a range of LCD types, but Fast IPS is generally the best for gaming. IPS panels deliver excellent colors, great viewing angles and they have very consistent performance. However, regular IPS can introduce significant motion blur, which is reduced in a Fast IPS panel. For users who game in bright environments, plan to do productivity work on their gaming PC, or are on a budget, Fast IPS is an easy recommendation.

Gaming on a PC can be uneven, and one of the most frustrating issues you'll commonly encounter is screen tearing. This occurs when the GPU cranks out frames at a different rate than the monitor shows them, so the display shows parts of two frames at the same time. Substantial tearing can completely ruin a gaming session, but there's a surefire fix for screen tearing: variable refresh rate (VRR) technology. NVIDIA G-Sync, AMD FreeSync and the generic Adaptive Sync are all examples of VRR.

For a deeper dive, check out our article on screen tearing and how to fix it, but the bottom line is that you'll want G-Sync or FreeSync on your monitor. By now, it's an industry standard and you won't need to spend much extra money, if any, to get a monitor that has one of those two certifications.

G-Sync can cost extra because it uses a hardware module inside the monitor. However, NVIDIA has certified plenty of monitors as G-Sync Compatible. This means that a monitor doesn't need that module to work with G-Sync. Many FreeSync monitors are G-Sync Compatible.

Console gamers can completely ignore G-Sync, as no console uses an NVIDIA GPU. The Xbox Series X and Series S support FreeSync because of their AMD hardware, while the PlayStation 5 and PS5 Pro support only HDMI 2.1 VRR.

Regardless of your hardware, VRR only works with the right cable. Make sure you're using a cable with at least DisplayPort 1.2a. If you're using HDMI, some HDMI 1.4 and 2.0 connections will support VRR, but the most robust support comes with HDMI 2.1 onward.

Thinking rationally is often presented as a magic answer to life’s problems. If we can just think more rationally and understand the reality of our world, then we will feel much better.

At least that’s what people say. But the truth is, just recognizing the reality of what is happening to us and around us often is not enough. We can understand clearly what is happening and still feel lousy about it.

Take the following example. A person recognizes that their job is no longer working for them. They go into work every day, put in the hours and feel absolutely terrible. After thinking about it, they realize that they hate their job. It may be that the work is no longer meaningful, they don’t feel like they are going anywhere, their boss is a tyrant, or a combination of all three. Coming to the conclusion, rationally, that the source of their distress is the meaninglessness or difficulty of their job can be useful.

But notice how recognizing reality does not guide the person on what to do here. It does not tell the person whether to leave their job, talk with their boss, or find some way to manage the problems the job presents. It also offers no guidance on how to handle the stress, as the person remains at the job even if they decide to look for a new one.

We often expect that if we just understand the reality of a situation, we will be able to deal with it or know what to do with it. But that is not the way reality actually works. Recognizing that something “is the way it is”, to quote an overused cliche, does not mean that we will know how to handle that reality.

Our experiences are ones where we all have to deal with the reality of our social world. But reality itself does not present information about what meaning we associate with it. Things happen, situations evolve, and people act as they do without these situations providing any clear guidance on how we are supposed to respond.

I once had a conversation with an expert in biological evolution who used a phrase that I still use today: “Evolution isn't good or bad, it just is.” I think about that phrase whenever I reflect on the fact that the world itself does not provide any clear indication of what things mean when they occur. All we know about the world is that things happen, and that things are the way they are, but knowing that rationally does not tell us what it means.

Microsoft has been doing a lot of work to improve memory usage and increase performance on Windows 11 PCs over the past year. Last month the company revealed Windows 11 is now “more responsive” and runs better on PCs with 8GB of RAM, and now the company is releasing a new update today that includes further memory improvements.

“On 8 GB PCs, Windows is now up to 20 percent more responsive, and multitasking is up to 40 percent faster in our lab test,” says Windows chief Pavan Davuluri. One of the key ways Microsoft has achieved better performance on PCs with 8GB of RAM is through compression.

“As a result, Windows can now keep up to 2 GB more of your active data in memory instead of moving it to slower storage, so an app or tab you left open is less likely to reload when you return,” says Davuluri. This compression helps Windows avoid writing data to a virtual memory paging file on a disk, slowing down apps and performance.

Microsoft is also making improvements across Windows, Edge, and Microsoft 365 apps that reduce “committed-memory growth by up to 900 MB.” Memory improvements in Word, PowerPoint, Excel, and Outlook alone add up to 360MB of RAM savings. Windows 11 no longer loads widgets on a PC with 8GB of RAM until you open the widgets panel, saving around 200MB of RAM. Inactive tabs in Edge now sleep after 15 minutes, reducing memory consumption further.

While Microsoft is shipping an update to Windows 11 with these memory improvements, the company is still working on improving RAM usage in Windows in the coming months. Memory compression will get “smarter and more efficient” over the next few months, and Windows features and apps will share more common parts like widgets and search sharing a single web engine. Microsoft is also working with PC makers and chipmakers to optimize how much memory a PC needs to reserve for its hardware, enabling more for Windows.
