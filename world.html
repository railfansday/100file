Twenty years after Civil War and ten years after Civil War II, Marvel teases a new conflict starring Iron Man. Iron Man’s psychological default is to mitigate risk, even at the cost of personal liberty. In the first Civil War, Tony Stark's guilt over disasters like Stamford drove him to become the bureaucratic enforcer of the Superhuman Registration Act, with a genuine belief that government oversight is the only way to keep the superhero community in check. In Civil War II, Iron Man clashed with Captain Marvel over predictive justice, as Stark’s mathematical mindset recognized that profiling the timeline based on unverified visions would be an attack on free will.

The conspicuous absence of another full-scale superhuman war since the catastrophic fallout of the second Civil War reflects collective maturity across a superhero community that realized these ideological purges only benefit their worst enemies. The tragedies of losing Captain America in the aftermath of the first Civil War and seeing Rhodey die in Civil War II taught Earth's protectors that internal warfare has fatal costs. The Avengers, Fantastic Four, and standalone vigilantes now proactively prioritize diplomatic channels to resolve internal disputes to prevent their pride from tearing their alliances apart.

Considering Iron Man is at the center of both conflicts, the most likely hero to launch a third rift among the Marvel Universe's superheroes is Tony Stark, especially when he's pushed to the limit by villains and allies alike.

Although Tony Stark is at the height of his superhero career's success following his mostly victorious encounters with villains like Madame Masque, the Whiplashes, and his former pupil Adam Ware, there's a growing concern about his future. Stark has been acting strangely, shifting his focus away from his own inventions and instead putting all his attention into the technology of others. Pepper Potts asks Reed Richards and Sue Storm to speak with Tony, and while they start on amicable terms, a particularly hotheaded Stark teases Reed Richards into a tense discussion about who could defeat whom.

One of the schemes Tony Stark mentions is the creation of a new Iron Man suit using Galactus' armor, which Mister Fantastic immediately shuts down.

After presenting the strategies they would use to defeat each other, Tony's girlfriend Luna Lucia leaves, leading Reed to apologize for the argument and admit he would never fight Iron Man. However, Tony Stark doubles down and tells Mister Fantastic that he would definitely win in a battle. With Happy Hogan's unexpected return, AIM's constant attacks, the events of The Infernal Hulk and Armageddon, and his time as the Incarcerated Iron Man adding more pressure, Tony Stark comes closer and closer to starting a new Civil War-like internal battle within the superhero community.

Although the Marvel Universe's heroes have spent several years proactively maintaining peace to avoid repeating past tragedies, the constant friction between mutant nation-states, superhero teams, and unauthorized vigilante groups creates an unstable foundation. Even the tightest alliances eventually break when an unpredictable tragedy forces heroes to choose between security and freedom, or between prevention and action. Earth's Mightiest Heroes continue to be caught off guard by villains like Varnae, Doctor Doom, and Red Hulk, and the world continues to be shaken by tragedies like the vampire takeover, Doctor Doom's dictatorship, and Red Hulk's attempt to launch World War III.

The Avengers have clashed and split up multiple times in Marvel Comics, and their inner conflicts have left permanent scars on the team.

Iron Man's past rivalries with Captain America and Captain Marvel pitted logic against pure morality and predictive military justice. Should a new conflict ignite, a clash between Mister Fantastic and Iron Man would be a war of competing hyper-intelligences, as teased by Reed Richards and Tony Stark's argument in Iron Man #11. Both super-geniuses are motivated by their compulsive need to solve the world's problems with the ambitious ideas they devise, but their methodologies are fundamentally opposed. Iron Man is focused on attacking threats head-on, saving as many people as possible with state-of-the-art engineering, whereas Mister Fantastic prefers less offensive strategies that exchange immediate results in favor of long-term safety.

Hong Kong: Joshua Wong Gets Nearly 10 More Years in Prison

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Joshua Wong speaks to protesters near the Legislative Council in Hong Kong, June 17, 2019.  © 2019 Kin Cheung/AP Photo 

(Tokyo) – The Hong Kong High Court on November 5, 2026, sentenced Joshua Wong, a well-known democracy activist, to 10 years in prison on baseless national security charges, Human Rights Watch said today. Two months of the sentence will run concurrently with his current national security sentence. Wong has been in custody since November 2020, when he was 24, and faces imprisonment until late 2036, when he will be 40.

The Hong Kong government should immediately and unconditionally release Wong. Foreign governments should denounce the sentences against him and other wrongfully detained pro-democracy activists and impose targeted sanctions on officials responsible for Hong Kong’s growing repression.

“The harsh sentence on baseless charges against Joshua Wong is cruel and unjust,” said Elaine Pearson, Asia director at Human Rights Watch. “Young people promoting social change are an asset for any vibrant society, but the Hong Kong government puts them behind bars.”

To keep Hong Kong society subdued, and to reduce the chance of an international outcry, the Hong Kong government appears to have adopted the tactic of stacking charges against influential pro-democracy leaders to ensure their prolonged detention, Human Rights Watch said. Other notable Hong Kong figures who have faced similar tactics include Jimmy Lai, founder of Apple Daily, and veteran democracy activists Lee Cheuk-yan and Albert Ho.

The court sentenced Wong, 30, under the National Security Law for “conspiring to collude with foreign forces.” The authorities accused him of “conspiring” with the activist Nathan Law and other “unknown” individuals between 2016 and November 2020 to urge foreign governments to sanction Hong Kong and Chinese government officials as they advocated for Hong Kong’s “democratic self-determination.” 

The prosecution relied heavily on the international lobbying and media advocacy that Wong and Demosisto, the political party Wong co-founded in 2016, carried out before the National Security Law took effect. Wong initiated online petitions, spoke to international media, and met with foreign officials to draw attention to human rights abuses in Hong Kong. He urged governments to sanction Chinese and Hong Kong officials responsible for abuses and to stop selling weapons to Hong Kong police that they use to carry out rights violations.

Wong had mostly stopped these activities before the National Security Law became effective. Demosisto disbanded on June 30, 2020, and Wong resigned from the group. However, the government quoted social media posts by Wong as ostensible evidence that his alleged “conspiracy” with Law to commit “foreign collusion” continued after the law took effect. In these posts, Wong said he “would not change his views” and that he would “continue to seek support from the international community” in his personal capacity, while Law and others abroad would continue their international advocacy.

The government also cited Wong’s subsequent opinion pieces and media interviews, though none directly called for sanctions, which the National Security Law specifically prohibits. It also cited his reposts of Law’s posts broadly calling for sanctions against Chinese officials and Hong Kong police.

The court ruled that Wong’s offense was of a “grave nature.” The National Security Law sets prison terms of 3 to 10 years for “foreign collusion,” or 10 years to life for offenses deemed to be of a “grave nature.” Wong formally pleaded guilty on September 2. A guilty plea normally results in a one-third sentence reduction. But a sentence reduction cannot bring the term below the statutory minimum.

Because of reporting restrictions on pretrial committal proceedings, when a magistrate determines whether to send a case to a higher court for trial, the public did not know the details of the accusations against Wong until the formal plea. The government enacted a law in 2024 to enable the court, on national security grounds, to deny the defendant’s right to ask the court to lift restrictions on reporting about the case.

By restricting media reporting on the pretrial proceedings in national security cases, the government can control the narrative and undermine the fundamental legal principle of open justice.

Since Beijing imposed the National Security Law on Hong Kong in June 2020, Hong Kong authorities have used the new legal regime—along with existing laws—to rapidly dismantle civil society and detain, imprison, or force into exile hundreds of activists and government critics.

Wong has been in custody since November 2020, when he pleaded guilty to two charges of organizing and inciting an unauthorized assembly linked to the 2019 citywide protest movement. After that, he pleaded guilty to five more charges on separate occasions, including for violating an anti-mask law, participating in an unauthorized vigil for the 1989 Tiananmen Massacre, and breaching a court ban to disclose a police officer’s personal information.

While serving these sentences, Wong was charged with two national security offenses, which carry far harsher penalties. In January 2021, Wong was charged with “conspiring to commit subversion” for participating in an unofficial democratic primary in 2020 and sentenced to another four years and eight months in prison. 

Before Wong could complete these sentences, the authorities brought the “foreign collusion” charge against him in June 2025, ensuring that he would remain in prison.

Wong became one of Hong Kong’s best-known democracy activists as a teenager, after leading a massive 2012 protest against a government educational curriculum widely seen as political indoctrination. He became the global face of Hong Kong’s democracy movement during the 2014 Umbrella Movement and co-founded Demosisto with Law and others in 2016. The party disbanded on June 30, 2020, hours before the National Security Law came into effect.

The National Security Law’s sweeping provisions have been used to punish peaceful protest and expression. Its use against Wong and others violates the right to freedom of expression under the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, which is incorporated into Hong Kong law through the city’s de facto constitution, the Basic Law, and the Bill of Rights Ordinance.

“Beijing has paid no real price for its increasing repression in Hong Kong,” Pearson said. “Concerned governments should press for the release of Wong and other activists, impose targeted sanctions against implicated officials, and review their relationship with China more broadly in light of the deepening abuses.”

“My coaching experience [helps], we speak English and Spanish because we don’t speak German in this World Squad with the staff. In the beginning people thought it was just a social project because the kids have to apply by sending a video.

“He got his debut against Gladbach. It was 3-0 [to Bayern] with a red card. The camera turned to the side and he was coming on [as a sub]. I was proud, but prouder for the people who invented [the World Squad] six years ago.

‘The Greatest’ Review: Jaalen Best Gives a Star-Making Performance in Amazon’s Uneven but Impressive Muhammad Ali Series

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

With Ben Watkins ('Cross') as creator, the eight-episode season follows the then-Cassius Clay from Olympic gold to his first shot at the heavyweight title.

There’s something amusing about Amazon and creator Ben Watkins casting relatively unknown Jaalen Best as the lead in the synonymously eponymous new series The Greatest, as if Jon Hamm were cast in a series about a 16th-century English philosopher titled simply Bacon.

It’s also appropriate because Best turns out to be the, well, best reason to watch The Greatest, taking on the mammoth challenge of embodying burgeoning boxing legend Cassius Clay on the brink of his transformation into Muhammad Ali. Clay/Ali remains one of the most indelible personalities of the 20th century, a man whose every public utterance and movement have become the subject of adoration and imitation. That Best captures Clay’s spirit in a way that’s instantly recognizable but rarely just mimicry is a remarkable achievement, especially for an actor this young faced with a task this daunting. Now He's Playing 'The Greatest' Boxer Onscreen TV 'American Hostage' Review: Jon Hamm and Giovanni Ribisi in an MGM+ Kidnapping Thriller With Lots of Potential but Little Payoff The Greatest The Bottom Line Packs a punch. Airdate: Wednesday, November 4 (Amazon)Cast: Jaalen Best, Omari Hardwick, Dana Gourrier, Kai Parham, Amin Joseph, Michael Ealy, Giovanni RibisiCreator: Ben Watkins

Best is not, however, the only reason to watch The Greatest. Watkins, showrunner of Amazon’s Cross, has gazed at Ali’s sprawling life with intimate focus, using the first season’s eight-episode duration as a vehicle for mixing moments that will be universally familiar, moments that are well-known among appreciators of old school pugilism, and smaller, slightly more obscure incidents.

Yes, this authorized biographical series is hagiography. But it’s hagiography that understands that at least some of the “imperfections” in Ali’s character were what caused much of America to relate to him and admire him. The Greatest never shies away from the reality that for all that love, Ali was, because of his race, his religion and his outspoken nature, an existential threat to the entrenched establishment in both his sport of choice and his nation of birth. The Greatest loves the man who became Muhammad Ali for all the reasons that hegemonic, primarily white, forces hated him.

Watkins approaches The Greatest as almost a cross between NatGeo’s Genius franchise, putting the lives of great men (and Aretha Franklin) in their historical context, and FX’s Feud franchise, exploring the parallel squabbles of catty women (and Truman Capote). It results in a series with wide-ranging ambition that generally hits its marks.

Watkins, who directed the season’s first two episodes, begins his take on the story with Cassius Clay in Rome at the 1960 Olympics, first visiting the Coliseum and getting a rather pointed lecture on the ancient Romans and their love of bloodsport and then winning the gold medal.

Clay returns to Louisville with big choices. With the devoted assistance of his brother Rudy (Kai Parham), Cassius dreams of becoming the youngest heavyweight champion ever, which requires he go pro immediately. He and his father (Omari Hardwick’s Cassius Senior), a philandering but devoted painter, have different thoughts on who should train and manage him. His mother (Dana Gourrier’s Odessa), a domestic worker and the family’s glue, is convinced he’s too physically immature to enter the ring with grown men, urging caution and possibly years of additional maturation.

It’s spoiling little to say that Cassius’ ambitions win out, but he struggles to build the proper team around him. Cassius’ largely improvisational style runs counter to generations of conventional boxing wisdom, so we know it’s a matter of waiting for the right man to let Clay be Clay. Angelo Dundee (Giovanni Ribisi, gruffly affectionate), a trainer known for working with smaller fighters, is down in Miami already obsessing over his skills.

At the same time, we meet Sonny Liston (Amin Joseph) in the penitentiary dominating bare-knuckle fights. He’s released and swooped up by mafia-controlled boxing interests, which puts a target on his back with the government and boxing authorities. Liston is lethal in the ring, but he’s the very opposite of what the white boxing establishment is prepared to embrace, no matter how much Liston’s new romantic interest (Erica Tazel’s Geraldine) attempts to polish his image.

Actually, neither Liston nor Cassius, refining a performative confidence inspired by wrestler Gorgeous George (Chris Jericho in a single great scene), is what the establishment wants. The establishment is content with current champ Floyd Patterson (Brandon McKnight), the fighter both Liston and Cassius are eyeing.

Not every life would benefit from an approach this deliberate, but slow-playing this particular life is the correct choice. The first season is a build-up to Cassius Clay’s first shot at the heavyweight crown and to his transformation into Ali, a process that wouldn’t have been possible without his growing devotion to the Nation of Islam and the mentorship of Malcolm X (Michael Ealy).

Most of the incidents in the series are verifiable, though when there’s ambiguity, Watkins tends to lean into the legend — as with the famous incident involving Cassius and the casting of his Olympic gold medal into the Ohio River. It’s generally accepted that that story was embellished or fabricated by his autobiography’s ghost writer to illustrate Cassius’ response to the prejudice of early 1960s Kentucky, and darned if Watkins doesn’t engage in the same embellishment/fabrication. The series has points it wants to make and it makes them with very little subtlety, but Muhammad Ali was a figure so big that he can withstand some heavy-handedness.

Watkins is intrigued by the things that make Clay special — and equally by the things that make him dangerous and, in turn, endanger his opportunities to claim a title belt. He juxtaposes Clay’s rise with the developing civil rights movement, monitoring the pace of progress, which means something different to each character. What feels like progress to Sonny Liston — who’s treated like an animal in the fields of Arkansas and then by the media and even Clay as he becomes a title contender — isn’t the same as what feels like progress to Clay Senior, who had aspirations to be a legitimate artist and instead paints billboards. And it isn’t the same as what feels like progress to Malcolm X and Elijah Muhammad (Hill Harper), who see in Cassius Clay the most visible spokesman the Nation would ever have.

The eight-episode length of the series gives every character motivations and complications, whether it’s Cassius’ determination spawned by the murder of Emmett Till or his mother’s concern from having seen boxer Sam Baroudi killed in the ring in 1948. There are gaps in some of the characterizations, with the women feeling especially archetypal and Rudy reduced to a devoted sidekick, but it’s generally a benefit to build out a world around Ali.

Still, it’s Best’s show, and he nails the two biggest challenges in this mammoth role. He builds up Cassius’ voice from soft-spoken Kentucky lilt to a poetry-spewing trumpet, embodying the braggadocious icon viewers think they know, while showing the man who existed at the dining room table or in a pew at a house of worship. This is a Cassius Clay who lived in the spotlight and a man who existed in private as well.

Best’s physicality is perhaps even more impressive, whether it’s reproducing expressions and posture from famous Ali snapshots or navigating the ring convincingly in the series’ fight sequences. There have been decent boxing shows in the past, like FX’s Lights Out, but Watkins and subsequent directors deliver more and better fight scenes than I’ve ever seen in the medium. Unlike Hulu’s uninspired Mike Tyson scripted series, the fight scenes here stay fairly close to the historical record, so if you watch The Greatest and then watch YouTube videos of the notorious London showdown with Henry Cooper or the first fight against Liston, the reproduction and the truth line up well. Much of that is due to Best’s emulation of his character’s fancy footwork, pawing jab and viciously timed attacks.

Some of the supporting casting is initially off-putting, with several actors who are, and come across as, significantly older than the characters they’re playing. Oddly, if you stick with it, those choices work.

Amin Joseph is 46, playing Sonny Liston, who was in his early-to-mid 30s, but Liston was an old 33, having spent years in prison after his rough and impoverished beginnings. Joseph makes Liston into a flawed but tragic figure, ferocious in the ring but eager to find acceptance outside of it. A scene with Liston flailing in an effort to humanize himself for the pubic by telling a joke during a talk show appearance is one of the series’ highlights.

Michael Ealy is 55, playing a man who was killed at 39, but the Malcolm X we meet here is a Malcolm X at a crossroads, deservedly worn beyond his years. The extra gap in age between Ealy and Best helps to illustrate the gap in maturity between the two men, one just starting his public life and one soon to have it ended.

Hardwick makes Cassius Senior volatile but wounded, with Gourrier centering Odessa’s fierce devotion and pride. Parham has a strong physical presence, but the show doesn’t quite get Rudy, even denying him the chapter of his own boxing career that started at the same time. Perhaps that will get worked into future seasons.

And I hope there will be future seasons of The Greatest, because this was only one chapter in a life filled with even more potent journeys.

Based on the confidence of this round of episodes, I’d say Watkins might be able to get two seasons out of Ali’s Vietnam protest, his exile from boxing and his first loss (with Joe Frazier taking on the secondary parallel role); another full season leading up to and including the Rumble in the Jungle (with George Foreman as the parallel foil); and then a final season on the closing chapters of his career, from the Thrilla in Manila on (perhaps making more use out of Regen Wilson’s Howard Cosell).

In an ideal world, Watkins would be able to take a few between seasons, just to let Best age up a little and continue what should be a well-deserved breakout.

How to choose the right gaming monitor for you

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Refresh rates, size and screen technology all play a part.

Choosing the right gaming monitor is a lot like choosing the right outfit. You wouldn't wear the same thing to the gym as you would to the opera, while lifting weights in a tuxedo would inconvenience you and damage the tux. Similarly, a monitor that works great for one PC or console isn't necessarily the best fit for another and gamers who enjoy competitive shooters should look for different options than those who enjoy visually rich single-person narratives.

Only you can figure out exactly which monitor you should buy. To do that, you'll first need to understand which specs best suit your needs. Modern monitors have a lot more to them than resolution and frame rate. Different display panel types suit different uses and picture quality can vary between models. A quick crash course on the basics will get you ready to shop for the monitor you'll be happiest with in the long run.

Before we dive into specs, the first thing to consider when picking a gaming monitor is the existing hardware you'll pair it with. If your PC is decked out with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 or 5080, you can run games on the bleeding edge of performance and have real reasons to consider a top-shelf monitor with a 4K panel and an ultra-high refresh rate. On the other hand, if you're plugging the monitor into an older or lower-powered GPU, consider the performance you'll actually get in the games you play. If you aren't routinely seeing frame rates above, say, 90 fps, there's no reason to invest in a 240Hz monitor. Resolution and refresh rate move in opposite directions, so if you play competitive shooters and esports titles like Valorant, it's best to sacrifice pixels for higher frames, whereas players of visually rich first-person titles like Control: Resonant can make the opposite trade-off or find a balance in the middle.

Once you know the spec range you're looking for, you can move on to other concerns such as panel type, HDR and brightness support, port selection and VRR technologies like G-Sync. Size and resolution are personal preferences. Resolution is tied to graphical performance, but there are reasons other than gaming to consider a 1440p or 4K display. If you do a lot of photo or video editing, or if you watch a lot of video, a higher resolution can be worthwhile. Size is related to resolution, in the sense that the smaller of two monitors with the same resolution will have a higher pixel density  — a finer level of image detail, which can be measured in pixels per inch (PPI).

Esports players often prefer smaller monitors around 24 inches in order to see everything at once, while single-player gamers who value immersion may gravitate toward 27 inches or higher. Larger displays also support multitasking better, but work best when you sit farther back. You'll also need to consider your ideal aspect ratio. A squatter shape is better for productivity tasks, but 16:9 widescreen is the de facto aspect ratio for gaming. Some gamers enjoy ultra-widescreen monitors to fill their peripheral vision, but in games where HUD information is crucial, ultrawide displays can force you to look away from the action.

Refresh rate is often considered the most essential specification when choosing a monitor, and should be the first thing you settle on. As noted above, your GPU should dictate this. Monitors are now shipping with up to 1,000 Hz in lower-resolution modes, at which point you'd question whether you can see the difference even if you could push your hardware that far.

The average PC gamer in September 2026 was running an RTX 5070, 5060, 4060 or even a 3060, according to the Steam Hardware Survey. Accordingly, the sweet spot for the average gamer is likely to land somewhere from 144Hz to 240Hz on a 1440p or 1080p panel. If you have a similar configuration but want to play competitive, lightweight games, you may want to look for a 1080p panel with a significantly higher frame rate. Players of these titles with top-end hardware (such as an RTX 5080 or 5090) can look for 1440p or 4K panels at any refresh rate.

That said, only you can determine the real-world frame rates you're getting on your hardware. One way to do that is to turn on the Steam, NVIDIA, or Xbox in-game overlays while playing and enable the small heads-up frame rate counter they offer. You can also use a benchmarking tool like 3DMark and some games, including Cyberpunk 2077, have built-in benchmark tests. Even if your current monitor can't reach high frame rates, you'll still see a readout showing how many frames per second your graphics card is producing.

After playing multiple games that are representative of your general interests, note where you saw the highest frame rates. Unless you plan to upgrade your GPU in the next several years, there's no need to overspend on a monitor with an unachievably high refresh rate for your system.

If you're looking for a monitor to use with a console, it's worth noting that even the best current-generation consoles like the PlayStation 5 Pro or Xbox Series X don't exceed refresh rates of 120 Hz. That means you're better off putting your money toward a larger or higher-resolution monitor instead of buying a panel with blistering speeds you'll never utilize.

After determining the refresh rate, resolution and aspect ratio ideal to your setup, the biggest choice you'll make is the type of panel your monitor is fitted with. For most gamers, the two best choices are OLED and Fast IPS, in that order.

OLED is well known for its technically infinite contrast ratios, accurate colors and near-instant response times. Because each pixel is self-emissive, it produces its own light rather than relying on a backlight; OLED pixels simply do not illuminate where an image is truly black, meaning they stay black rather than turning dark grey in the way LCD panels do. That makes them ideal for gaming, especially if you value HDR performance.

However, OLED displays have their drawbacks. They require more care to prevent OLED burn-in, a condition in which leaving a static image on screen for too long leaves a permanent ghost of that image on the screen. Newer OLEDs address this with pixel-shifting that slightly moves the picture around and pixel-cleaning cycles that help keep wear and tear even across the display. If you're the sort of person who leaves your monitor on for hours when you're not using it, an OLED will force you to change that behavior.

OLEDs are also dimmer than LCDs and are consequently worse in well-lit environments. Some manufacturers offer matte coatings on their OLED models, which can help, but these displays look best in a dim or dark room. Text fringing is another issue, as is motion flicker and while the latest generation of OLED has come a long way in minimizing them, gamers who also work with documents and spreadsheets should take those issues into consideration before paying the higher cost for an OLED. Thatcost is perhaps the biggest downside, as even entry-level OLEDs start around $400 in late 2026. If you do choose to go OLED, scout for sales.

LCD panels lack the sheer wow factor of OLED, but they are more versatile. There are a range of LCD types, but Fast IPS is generally the best for gaming. IPS panels deliver excellent colors, great viewing angles and they have very consistent performance. However, regular IPS can introduce significant motion blur, which is reduced in a Fast IPS panel. For users who game in bright environments, plan to do productivity work on their gaming PC, or are on a budget, Fast IPS is an easy recommendation.

Gaming on a PC can be uneven, and one of the most frustrating issues you'll commonly encounter is screen tearing. This occurs when the GPU cranks out frames at a different rate than the monitor shows them, so the display shows parts of two frames at the same time. Substantial tearing can completely ruin a gaming session, but there's a surefire fix for screen tearing: variable refresh rate (VRR) technology. NVIDIA G-Sync, AMD FreeSync and the generic Adaptive Sync are all examples of VRR.

For a deeper dive, check out our article on screen tearing and how to fix it, but the bottom line is that you'll want G-Sync or FreeSync on your monitor. By now, it's an industry standard and you won't need to spend much extra money, if any, to get a monitor that has one of those two certifications.

G-Sync can cost extra because it uses a hardware module inside the monitor. However, NVIDIA has certified plenty of monitors as G-Sync Compatible. This means that a monitor doesn't need that module to work with G-Sync. Many FreeSync monitors are G-Sync Compatible.

Console gamers can completely ignore G-Sync, as no console uses an NVIDIA GPU. The Xbox Series X and Series S support FreeSync because of their AMD hardware, while the PlayStation 5 and PS5 Pro support only HDMI 2.1 VRR.

Regardless of your hardware, VRR only works with the right cable. Make sure you're using a cable with at least DisplayPort 1.2a. If you're using HDMI, some HDMI 1.4 and 2.0 connections will support VRR, but the most robust support comes with HDMI 2.1 onward.

Thinking rationally is often presented as a magic answer to life’s problems. If we can just think more rationally and understand the reality of our world, then we will feel much better.

At least that’s what people say. But the truth is, just recognizing the reality of what is happening to us and around us often is not enough. We can understand clearly what is happening and still feel lousy about it.

Take the following example. A person recognizes that their job is no longer working for them. They go into work every day, put in the hours and feel absolutely terrible. After thinking about it, they realize that they hate their job. It may be that the work is no longer meaningful, they don’t feel like they are going anywhere, their boss is a tyrant, or a combination of all three. Coming to the conclusion, rationally, that the source of their distress is the meaninglessness or difficulty of their job can be useful.

But notice how recognizing reality does not guide the person on what to do here. It does not tell the person whether to leave their job, talk with their boss, or find some way to manage the problems the job presents. It also offers no guidance on how to handle the stress, as the person remains at the job even if they decide to look for a new one.

We often expect that if we just understand the reality of a situation, we will be able to deal with it or know what to do with it. But that is not the way reality actually works. Recognizing that something “is the way it is”, to quote an overused cliche, does not mean that we will know how to handle that reality.

Our experiences are ones where we all have to deal with the reality of our social world. But reality itself does not present information about what meaning we associate with it. Things happen, situations evolve, and people act as they do without these situations providing any clear guidance on how we are supposed to respond.

I once had a conversation with an expert in biological evolution who used a phrase that I still use today: “Evolution isn't good or bad, it just is.” I think about that phrase whenever I reflect on the fact that the world itself does not provide any clear indication of what things mean when they occur. All we know about the world is that things happen, and that things are the way they are, but knowing that rationally does not tell us what it means.

Rational thinking is a good starting place for handling reality effectively. But it is not enough. In order to effectively use rational information, you need to incorporate your own thinking about values and meaning.

Even though I knew Microsoft’s new Xbox Nano controller was tiny, I still gasped when I unboxed it a few days ago. The compact controller is small enough to slip into a pocket unnoticed, and it has been designed specifically for younger people, or for anyone with smaller hands.

I’m not young, but I do have small hands, and I’ve been surprised at how comfortable the Nano is during gaming sessions. At just over 5 inches long, almost everything on this controller has been shrunk down somehow. The ABXY buttons are ever so slightly smaller than a regular Xbox controller, and the thumbsticks feel like miniature versions. Microsoft hasn’t changed the D-pad, though, or the size of the glowing Xbox button at the center of the controller.

The big difference I noticed with the Nano over a regular Xbox controller was the size of the triggers. While they are fully analog and pressure sensitive, they have more of a click when you bottom them out compared with the thud on a bigger Xbox controller. They’re noticeably smaller, as you’d expect, and the bumper buttons above the triggers feel less clicky than the regular ones.

I thought the Nano controller would feel awkward after a few hours of gaming, but the grips really help with comfort levels — a lot of smaller controllers sacrifice grips to keep the controller thin and lightweight. It does mean the Nano doesn’t sit flush on a table, but at 101 grams (0.22 pounds) it’s still really lightweight.

I can’t speak for those with bigger hands, but I’d expect the Nano controller won’t be well-suited to everyone. The size makes it ideal for young children, though. I tried to get my 1-year-old son to try the Nano, and he responded with a smile, so I guess he likes it.

I haven’t had enough time to get a full idea of battery life yet, but it seems to drain around 10 percent in around 30 to 40 minutes. That suggests a lot less battery life than the 30 hours you’d get on a rechargeable pack for the larger Xbox wireless controller. Thankfully, the Nano charges quickly, and I found I could get from 30 percent to 60 percent charge in less than 10 minutes. You can plug in a USB-C charger to top up the battery and carry on playing while you charge.

The only thing missing on the Nano controller that I would have loved is vibration support. I assume it’s not there because of an impact on battery life, because even the smaller Nintendo Joy-Con controllers have rumble support.

Microsoft has been doing a lot of work to improve memory usage and increase performance on Windows 11 PCs over the past year. Last month the company revealed Windows 11 is now “more responsive” and runs better on PCs with 8GB of RAM, and now the company is releasing a new update today that includes further memory improvements.

“On 8 GB PCs, Windows is now up to 20 percent more responsive, and multitasking is up to 40 percent faster in our lab test,” says Windows chief Pavan Davuluri. One of the key ways Microsoft has achieved better performance on PCs with 8GB of RAM is through compression.

“As a result, Windows can now keep up to 2 GB more of your active data in memory instead of moving it to slower storage, so an app or tab you left open is less likely to reload when you return,” says Davuluri. This compression helps Windows avoid writing data to a virtual memory paging file on a disk, slowing down apps and performance.

Microsoft is also making improvements across Windows, Edge, and Microsoft 365 apps that reduce “committed-memory growth by up to 900 MB.” Memory improvements in Word, PowerPoint, Excel, and Outlook alone add up to 360MB of RAM savings. Windows 11 no longer loads widgets on a PC with 8GB of RAM until you open the widgets panel, saving around 200MB of RAM. Inactive tabs in Edge now sleep after 15 minutes, reducing memory consumption further.

While Microsoft is shipping an update to Windows 11 with these memory improvements, the company is still working on improving RAM usage in Windows in the coming months. Memory compression will get “smarter and more efficient” over the next few months, and Windows features and apps will share more common parts like widgets and search sharing a single web engine. Microsoft is also working with PC makers and chipmakers to optimize how much memory a PC needs to reserve for its hardware, enabling more for Windows.
