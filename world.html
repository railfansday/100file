Hands-on: Sony’s Walkman-revival USB-C headphones are back in stock and worth a look

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Sony’s iconic Walkman headphones are back in the USB-C MDR-3R and, after quickly selling out on launch day, they’ve now been restocked. And, after using them, I can see why they sold out so quickly.

The MDR-3R are Sony’s revival of the wired over-ear headphones that many used with a classic Walkman player. The retro style still looks great in 2026, but using an original pair is a bit of a headache in today’s age of headphone jack-less smartphones. So it was nice to see Sony give this a revival with USB-C.

Update 11/24: After we first published our hands-on with MDR-3R last week, Sony’s USB-C headphones sold out pretty quickly again. As of today, though, they’re back in stock and still shipping in early December – don’t miss out!

While the style won’t be for everyone, I think these work brilliantly. The sound quality is excellent and having a pair of headphones that doesn’t fully enclose my ears is pretty refreshing. There’s no noise cancellation, but with open-ear earbuds becoming more popular, I actually really like these as a similar solution (though high volumes will definitely be heard by those around you). I’m not fully cut off from the world around me, but I’m also able to get good sound quality. Lows are the weakness here, with unimpressive bass drops, but the sound quality is crisp and clear and pretty neutral, and the open-ish style makes it feel like the room around me is filled with music.

The physical hardware is great. While a plastic and metal build is objectively not as comfy as a premium pair of ANC headphones, the extremely light weight helps MDR-3R become an afterthought. You simply forget you’re wearing them after a few minutes.

The earpads are swappable, with orange and blue options included in the box.

As for the USB-C cable, it’s fully plug-and-play. No app needed and it worked across every device I tested (including Pixel, which doesn’t work with all USB-C audio output). I do wish the cable was perhaps a few inches longer, but it’s long enough to wear the headphones with my phone in my front pocket. There’s a remote along the cable for play/pause and volume adjustments, though I think it’s a little too high on the cable.

For $59.99, these are a fun pair of USB-C headphones to have on hand. They sound good, work great, and don’t cost an arm and a leg. I can see why they sold out so quickly on launch day – in just 10 hours, Sony told me.

Sony MDR-3R are back in stock now and shipping in early December. If you want a pair, I say don’t wait. They’re well worth at least trying if you can get them for retail price – please don’t give in to the scalpers selling these at double, they’re not worth that.

EVA International Announces Theme for 50th Anniversary Edition in 2027

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The EVA International biennial in Limerick, Ireland, will celebrate its 50th anniversary next year. Running from August 27 to October 27, it will be titled “Forms of Inheritance.”

The biennial also announced that Matt Packer will step down after 10 years as director in spring 2028.

EVA’s organizers said in a release that the upcoming theme will question “the values between the past and the future” during a time when “promises carried forward from the late twentieth century—integration, openness and shared progress—can no longer be taken as given.” The works on view will pull from EVA International’s history with a series of new artist commissions, as well as other commissions that focus on the wider historical narratives between Ireland and Europe. Years of Being a Private Investigator Taught Me So Much About Dating Men

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

And it all started with me knowing something was up with “Dirty John” long before he became infamous.

Years before he became “Dirty John,” an infamous stalker who preyed on a young woman while carrying a butcher knife hidden in a Del Taco bag, I met John Meehan over dinner at the Chateau Marmont. He was dating my friend.

It was 2010, and my friend Kate had recently met a handsome, charming anesthesiologist named John Meehan on a dating website. She was smitten. He was successful, attentive, and apparently desperate to commit. He'd even asked for a key to her house. Kate was head over heels; I was spooked. Something about John didn't add up.

Over dinner, John talked endlessly about his medical career, including the technical details of surgeries with gas ratios and how much money he made. Yet when I asked which medical school he'd attended, he became strangely evasive. When the bill arrived, John meticulously calculated his share down to the penny, despite having spent the evening boasting about his seven-figure income. Maybe he was just frugal. But there was a growing collection of little things that didn't make sense.

Kate thought I was being cynical. At the time, I was an investigative journalist and former sex columnist living in L.A. who had recently enrolled in private investigator school. So, sure, maybe that wasn’t too misplaced. But I trust my instincts and genuinely thought she was in danger—when John left the restaurant, I borrowed my then-husband's Mercedes and followed him. John had told Kate he lived with his sister in Pasadena. He drove somewhere else entirely.

Over the following days, I began digging. I discovered that John wasn't the doctor he claimed to be—he was a nurse anesthetist with a long criminal history, had lost his professional license, and had been accused of stalking women.

I had mixed feelings about telling Kate the truth. I knew that I had to break the bad news, but I was super nervous when I showed up to our happy hour date carrying a printout of John’s mug shot. Fortunately, she believed me and broke things off. And although I suspected at the time that he was a con artist with a serious capacity for violence, I was left feeling helpless.

Years later, I listened in horror as the Dirty John podcast chronicled what happened when John Meehan entered his wife Debra Newell’s life. Their story mirrored his and Kate’s, except that when she tried to break up with him, things took a much darker turn. He eventually attacked her adult daughter, Terra Newell, who bravely fought back and killed him in self-defense. Hearing that, I was relieved that Kate had escaped. But I was also haunted by the knowledge that recognizing a dangerous man hadn't stopped him from finding another victim.

From sitting outside no-tell motels on matrimonial surveillance to piecing together a crime victim’s last day on earth, I’ve seen many situations where people get into dangerous situations—from confronting love rivals to going back for one last suitcase—all for love.

Here’s what detective work has taught me about dating:

Before I became a private investigator, I was a sex and dating columnist for The Independent in London. I spent years documenting my romantic adventures, writing about everything from casual sex to heartbreak. I've always been fascinated by the similarities between dating and detective work.

In both instances, you’re sitting across from a relative stranger. You’re asking questions, and you’re trying to figure out whether they are telling the truth—all the while hoping that, in the dating realm, any skeletons you unearth are metaphorical, and not decomposing in someone’s crawlspace.

But there's one enormous difference. When I'm investigating someone professionally, I'm looking for inconsistencies. When I'm falling for someone, I'm looking for reasons to believe them. And that's where things get dangerous.

It’s been scientifically proven that love causes a dopamine reaction in the brain similar to eating pounds of chocolate or snorting cocaine. Think about it: Would you make any other major life decision—like who to live with, introduce to your children or combine finances with—while high? That’s why I love the idea of a “sexual sober friend,” aka someone who will tell you straight up if you are being totally irrational. Of course, you have to actually be willing to take said friend’s advice–see below!

One of the most useful lessons I learned in PI school was to not to “give people the benefit of the doubt.” Instead, we say, “Trust, but verify.”

When you're investigating a crime, you build a timeline. You figure out what happened, when it happened, and whether the evidence supports the accounts you've been given. I think romantic relationships benefit from a much less obsessive version of the same principle.

Someone tells you they're looking for a serious relationship. Do their actions support that statement? Do they make plans? Follow through? Introduce you to people in their lives? Respect your boundaries? Or do they deliver a spectacular speech about wanting to find their soulmate and then disappear for eleven days because, apparently, their phone fell into the sea? A charming explanation is not the same thing as a credible one.

I once worked on a surveillance case involving a wife whose husband worked a boring day job as a dentist...but she was never allowed to come to his office, and he would disappear for hours in the evening. When he told her that he wanted a divorce and was leaving, she wanted to know where he was going, so she hired me and my partner.

After 18 hours of surveillance, I was able to confirm what six years of couples’ therapy had failed to deliver: confirmation that not only was he cheating on her with his receptionist, which I captured on camera while hiding in a deserted parking lot, but that he was leading a double life as a wannabee-Soprano small-time strip club drug dealer.

The next day, as I played the video evidence of his double life, writing prescriptions for several dancers, smoking pot, and shopping the aisles of Trader Joe’s for munchies, she told me that she had suspected for years that something was off, but she never felt ready to ask him the questions that would shatter her world. That’s happened to me a lot when I was doing matrimonial surveillance: People will tell me about finding incriminating photographs, suspicious messages, or underwear that clearly doesn't belong to them—and then ask me whether I think something is wrong.

Often, they're not really asking me to uncover the truth. They're asking me to give them permission to believe what they've already seen. Which brings me to my next principle:

I believe in intuition, but I don’t believe that it’s supernatural magic. Sometimes it's simply our brains recognizing patterns before we've consciously identified them.

At that first dinner with John Meehan, I couldn't immediately explain why I felt uncomfortable. But as the evening continued, I used my investigative training to spot the inconsistencies: his evasiveness, his elaborate claims, the mismatch between the person he presented and the details of his life. The important part wasn't that I had a bad feeling, it was that I paid attention to the feeling and then looked for facts.

Even after years of studying human behavior, I still sometimes find myself making the same mistakes I warn other people about.

While working as a journalist on a murder investigation in Arkansas, I became attracted to someone I was interviewing, someone who had been good friends with a possible person of interest. I knew perfectly well that personal chemistry could interfere with my judgment but found myself getting involved anyway.

Investigative work can teach you to be skeptical, but skepticism isn't a force field. You can know that someone is wrong for you and still want them. And you can be exceptionally good at uncovering other people's secrets while remaining spectacularly talented at hiding things from yourself.

A lot of people expect me to turn into Nancy Drew with every man I meet. There’s some truth to that; like most women, I do a Google deep dive before meeting someone for the first time.

But after spending years investigating cheating spouses, con artists, and eventually a cold-case murder, I've become convinced that there's a fine line between healthy curiosity and turning your love life into a full-time surveillance operation.

I don't think women should spend their evenings running background checks on every man who buys them a cocktail. Nor should they secretly follow their partners or interpret every unanswered text as evidence of a double life.

I never invade any significant others’ privacy and never would. If I don’t trust them, the relationship isn’t worth it to me. Because tracking people, online or otherwise, is exhausting. And I already have enough of that in my day job!

For years, I thought being a good investigator meant finding answers to every question. Now I think one of the most important skills—both in detective work and in dating—is knowing when you have enough information to make a decision. And when it's time to stop investigating and get on with your life.

How brewers can protect beers from smoke-tainted hops

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Study: Brewers can still use up to 50% of smoke-tainted hops without affecting beer taste and aroma.

The 2020 Oregon wildfires burned over 1 million acres, destroyed thousands of structures, and killed 11 people. Smoke from the fires spread across the entire region; this “smoke taint” adversely affected not only local wine production, but also hops grown for beer brewing. Since the Pacific Northwest produces more than 95 percent of US hops and about 40 percent of the global supply, the potential economic impact is significant.

That’s why Oregon State University (OSU) scientists have been studying how brewers could offset the negative impacts of smoke taint while still incorporating affected hops in their brews. Most recently, they found that it all depends on how much tainted hops are included and when hops are added during the brewing process, according to a new paper published in the Journal of the American Society of Brewing Chemists.

As previously reported, the chemistry of brewing beer is a very active area of research. For instance, in 2018, OSU chemist Thomas Shellhammer—who also co-authored this latest paper—identified the growing popularity of “dry hopping” (adding hops after fermentation, common for pale ales) among craft brewers as the likely culprit of the exploding beer bottle phenomenon.

All beer contains hops, a key flavoring agent that also imparts useful antimicrobial properties. (Beer spoils quickly without them.) Brewers mash and steep grain in hot water, which converts all that starch into sugars. This is traditionally the stage where hops are added to the liquid extract (wort) and boiled to give the beer that hint of bitterness. During the boiling process, a portion of the resins (alpha acids) in hops isomerize into iso-alpha acids. That chemical rearrangement of the molecules is what produces bitterness. Yeast is then added to trigger fermentation, turning the sugars into alcohol.

Far from being the chemically inert ingredient brewers have long assumed, hops contain starch enzymes that, under certain conditions, can boost their activity to such a great extent that excessive re-fermentation occurs (“hop-creep”) after bottling. Dry-hopped beers produce significantly higher CO2 levels and packaging pressures, because there is much more re-fermentation going on than usual. And sometimes that pressure builds until the bottle explodes.

In the wake of the 2020 wildfires, Shellhammer turned his attention to smoke-tainted hops. The primary culprit is volatile phenols (VP), which are produced when the lignin in plant cells is exposed to the high heat of a wildfire. Earlier this year, the OSU team discovered that smoke exposure while the hops are drying increased VP uptake and substantially altered the hops’ aroma; those with higher levels took on meaty, smoky, and artificial BBQ notes; medicinal flavors have also been reported. Those notes were so strong that they overpowered more desirable beery qualities, like citrus notes or aroma intensity.

For this latest project, Shellhammer worked with graduate student Cade Jobe to harvest Idaho-grown Cascade hops. Then they “smoked” the hops in kilns using wood pellets injected into the air intake systems, then dried them. They also harvested and dried non-smoked hops as a control.

Credit: Ellie Lafferty/Oregon State University Credit: Ellie Lafferty/Oregon State University

Next, Shellhammer and Jobe brewed several batches of beer, blending smoke-tainted hops with untainted hops at different levels (25,  50, and 75 percent), along with beer brewed using only tainted hops and beer brewed with no tainted hops. They did this using both a dry-hopping process and one where they added hops during wort boiling. They measured the VP concentrations of each batch of beer with gas chromatography. Finally, a panel of 24 blind tasters evaluated each beer.

Shellhammer and Jobe found that dry-hopping with smoke-tainted hops resulted in increased VP concentrations; the more tainted hops were included, the higher those concentrations. The tasters reported more intense smoky aromas in those beers as well as meaty, smoky, medicinal, and artificial BBQ flavors.

By contrast, when tainted hops were added during the boil, the resulting beer was pretty much indistinguishable from beers brewed with no smoke-tainted hops, even though the former still showed the same increases in VP concentrations. There was no discernible difference when hops were added during the wort boiling stage.

The authors suggest brewers could blend smoke-tainted hops with untainted batches when dry-hopping, keeping the smoke-tainted hops to less than 50 percent. This would still allow brewers to use tainted batches of hops, reducing the economic impact of wildfires on beer production.

“Hop growers and brewers are looking for guidance on whether and how to use smoke-affected hops,” said Jobe. “This research begins to provide some practical information to help hop growers and brewers understand how smoky hops impact beer so that they can make the best decisions.”

There’s still more research to be done to confirm the precise ratios and to replicate these initial findings. Future research should also explore the impact of hops exposed to smoke under field conditions, when the plant is still metabolically active, along with consumer studies to evaluate whether beer drinkers more generally will find the flavors of beers made with blended hops (tainted and untainted) acceptable. The findings may not extend to wine production, however, since the fermentation processes are so different, per the authors. And grapes comprise almost all the raw materials in wine, while hops are a smaller fraction of beer brewing ingredients.

The US Air Force Bolted Jet Engines Onto Its Largest-Ever Bomber, But It Still Wasn't Enough To Save It

The Convair B-36 Peacemaker strategic bomber is one of the most ambitious bombers ever built. While the unusual pusher configuration of its six Pratt & Whitney R-4360 Wasp Major piston engines is perhaps its most distinctive feature, later variants also carried four General Electric J47 turbojets. These jets were added to improve take-off performance, climb rate, and high-speed dash capability, helping keep the enormous bomber operationally relevant.

This remained the case until newer all-jet bombers, particularly the B-52 Stratofortress, assumed the primary nuclear deterrence role. The United States Air Force procured 384 B-36s, each capable of carrying up to 86,000 lb (39,000 kg) of bombs. Although none remain airworthy today, four complete aircraft have been preserved in museums, where they provide a reminder of the largest piston-engined combat aircraft ever placed into operational service.

The Convair B-36 Peacemaker was the largest bomber built by the US, and it can be thought of as the most important transitional bomber ever built. The US Air Force Museum states that "responding to the U.S. Army Air Forces' requirement for a strategic bomber with intercontinental range, Consolidated Vultee (later Convair) designed the B-36 during World War II. The airplane made its maiden flight in August 1946, and in June 1948 the Strategic Air Command received its first operational B-36."

The museum also explains that "some B-36s served as photographic reconnaissance aircraft, and others were adapted to launch and retrieve specially modified RF-84F/K reconnaissance planes." Although conceived during the Second World War as an ultra-long-range conventional bomber, the B-36 entered service as the United States shifted towards a strategy centred on delivering nuclear weapons across intercontinental distances without relying on overseas bases.

This remains a central mission of the world's current strategic bombers (B-52, B-1B, B-2, B-21, Tu-160, and Tu-95MS). The Peacemaker's origins date to 1941, which is before the US even entered the Second World War. There was a fear in the US that the UK could fall to Germany, and if that happened, the US would lack a forward base for its bombers to raid Germany. As such, the requirements for the bomber included that it could fly intercontinentally from North America to Europe.

Designing and developing the bomber took time, and by the time it entered service in 1948, the Second World War had been over for three years. As things had panned out, the US had been able to use Britain against Germany, and had island-hopped to bring its B-29s within range of Japan.

However, the delays in designing the bomber also allowed engineers to update the design to carry the heavy nuclear bombs that weighed around 8,800 to 11,000 lbs (4,000 to 5,000 kg) and even the extremely heavy hydrogen bombs. The first experimental thermonuclear devices weighed tens of tonnes, while the first operational hydrogen bombs introduced in the early 1950s typically weighed around 30,000–42,000 lb (14,000–19,000 kg).

The Peacemaker was designed with the gigantic bomb bay doors needed to carry these bombs that no other aircraft could. When it entered service, it was able to carry around 86,000 lbs (39,000 kg) of payload, vastly more than any other bomber. With the establishment of the Strategic Air Command in 1946, the B-36 became the first true intercontinental nuclear bomber.

The world's geostrategic picture changed in 1949 as the Soviet Union exploded its first atomic bomb. Then, the US pivoted to requiring a bomber able to strike targets deep inside the USSR while using bases in the continental United States and be able to carry heavy first-generation nuclear bombs. This is something the Boeing B-29 Superfortress couldn't do, but it was very similar to what the B-36 was originally designed for.

Germany developed flying prototypes of longer range bombers that would be able to fly over and bomb New York City.

With the benefit of hindsight, the B-36 was always likely to be overtaken by the rapid pace of jet-powered aviation. It was conceived during the Second World War, but was ultimately built for a very different strategic environment. At first (late 1940s to early 1950s), the B-36 was unmatched.

No fighter could reliably intercept the Peacemaker at its high operating altitude (around 40,000 feet or 12,000 meters, later extending to 45,000 feet or 13,700 meters). This was before the USSR developed an integrated air defense network and a time when early Soviet jet fighters struggled to reach and engage such high-flying targets.

The Peacemaker's enormous range also allowed it to approach the USSR from multiple directions, further complicating interceptions. But the B-36 was becoming a relic of a bygone era. It was designed before jet-powered strategic bombers were practical. By the mid-1950s, new Soviet jet interceptors like the MiG-17 and then MiG-19 made it increasingly vulnerable.

This worsened with the development of increasingly capable radar-guided air defense systems and surface-to-air missile technology. The arrival of the all-jet B-52 Stratofortress further hastened the B-36's retirement by offering greater speed, improved survivability, and comparable intercontinental range. Perhaps somewhat similar, the arrival of the B-21 will inevitably increase pressure to retire the older, expensive, and less survivable B-2 Spirits.

By the time it entered service, the B-52 was in development, but was still years away. The Peacemaker was primarily powered by six enormous 28-cylinder Pratt & Whitney R-4360 Wasp Major piston engines arranged in a distinctive pusher configuration. However, these were too slow in the emerging jet age. In an effort to bridge the gap, the US Air Force invested heavily in modernizing the Peacemaker with four General Electric J47 turbojets.

The J47 turbojet engines were mounted in two twin-engine pods beneath the outwings and earned it the famous slogan "six turning and four burning." They were not intended for continuous cruise as they were incredibly fuel inefficient. Instead, they were used for heavy-weight takeoffs, improved climbing, increased dash speed in hostile territory, and reduced time spent over defended targets.

The USAF looked for other ways to improve the performance of the Peacemaker. It introduced the Featherweight modification programs, which removed defensive armament, ammunition, armor, and other equipment to improve altitude, range, and speed. The B-36 was given improved electronics, while the RB-36 strategic reconnaissance variant was also developed.

Ultimately, the improvements addressed symptoms, but were unable to fix the underlying problem, which was that the B-36 just didn't belong in the jet age.

Relying on piston engines, the B-36 was too slow, typically cruising at 200 knots (378 knots at maximum speed). The B-47 Stratojet could cruise at 434 knots, and the B-52 at 456 knots. By the mid-1950s, increasingly capable Soviet jet interceptors such as the MiG-17 and MiG-19, together with radar-guided surface-to-air missile systems including the S-25 Berkut and later the S-75 Dvina, steadily eroded the B-36's survivability.

The Peacemaker faced other pressures. For example, the complex push-configuration radial engines frequently overheated, caught fire, and required changing a staggering 336 spark plugs per service. The massive size of the aircraft also demanded immense resources to maintain and operate.

The final B-36 Peacemaker was retired in 1959, just one year before a Soviet S-75 Dvina (SA-2) downed a Lockheed U-2 spy plane at an altitude of around 70,000 feet. The development contributed to the program that would give rise to the SR-71 as well as contributed to the abandoning of the XB-70 Valkyrie bomber.

Today, the Peacemaker is largely forgotten in popular memory. It is overshadowed by the earlier B-29 Superfortress, which entered service too late to make a difference in Germany but in time to play a pivotal role in ending the War in the Pacific, including dropping the nuclear bombs on Japan. The B-36 arrived too late for the Second World War, but it was also ill-prepared for the realities of the Cold War and the jet age.

It was quickly overshadowed by the early Cold War Boeing B-52 Stratofortress that entered service in the mid 1950s and continues in service today. These two successful bombers have had the effect of pushing the Peacemaker out of popular imagination. Unlike the B-29 and B-52, relatively few B-36s were preserved after retirement, with only four surviving in museums. None remain airworthy.

Here’s How To Make Your Holiday EV Road Trip Easier

It’s a short workweek for most of us here in the United States. In just a day or two, many of us will load up our cars and trek around the country to our loved ones. If you’re reading this, there’s a decent chance you’ll be taking on of those trips in an electric vehicle. EVs have come a long way, so in theory, range anxiety shouldn’t be much of an issue for anyone these days. Still, with so many drivers on the roads, there are bound to be annoyances that come with long EV road trips.

Here are a few tips that can take the edge off a long trip.

DC fast charging times are getting better and better, but charging still takes longer than stopping for gas. Thus, bottlenecks can occur on well-traveled routes with limited DC fast charging stops. It’s just kind of the nature of the beast when most people are stopping for 20-30 minutes. Multiply that by the plethora of drivers also trying to charge up and get home, and well, it’s not hard to see why EV charging queues can balloon, adding time to your drive home.

But you can head some of that off if you prep the night before. For those with home charging, that means simply plugging the car in and ensuring it’s fully charged for the next day’s trip. For those who don’t have access to home charging, using a DC fast charger the night before, when everyone isn’t rushing to leave, could ease some of the frustration of charging up before getting on the roads.

Also, some DC fast charging companies give a discount for charging during off-peak hours. Prepping the night before won’t just save you time, it might save you money.

We may have a set route in mind for our trip, and that could be all fine and dandy, but is it really the best route for our cars? Electric vehicles tend to consume more energy at higher speeds and when traveling up hills. Thus, it could be advantageous to maximize range by picking a flatter route, or one with lower speeds.

In most cases this won’t be necessary, but if you’re pushing the limits of your car and the infrastructure around you, it’s good to know. Most route planning apps—like A Better Route Planner, Plugshare, or even Google Maps—take into account which route is friendlier to your car’s range calculator, and may suggest detours if you get a big range boost from a route that’s nearly as quick.

These route planners will tell you exactly when and where to stop, depending on your car’s efficiency, your destination, your desired state of charge at arrival, and road conditions along the way. They can be adjusted to fit your real-world efficiency data or your need for breaks. Rather than just winging, using a route planner ensures you’re never in an uncomfortable situation without the range or charging ability to get where you need to go.

This goes hand in hand with route planning, but I think it is relevant here. The Sunday after Thanksgiving is typically the single busiest travel day of the year. You’re not the only one on the road who will need charging. Prepping the night before can help with the initial charging wait times at the start of your trip. If you’re on a long journey, chances are you’ll find yourself in a big queue at a strange charger.

When you’re planning your route with charging stops, pay attention to what brands they are and what ports they use. An increasing number of highway stops often have several competing charging stations. A Tesla Supercharging station could have an Ionna Rechargery nearby. Or maybe it’s an EV Go station. Or maybe it’s a charging station by Circle K or Shell. In any case, they tend to cluster together near high-power infrastructure. Rather than fumble with apps and payments at the station, I think it’s best if you research the charging companies on your route and pre-emptively install their apps and set up payment.

Similarly, bring an adapter. NACS is quickly making its way across the country, but not every station has NACS ports, and not every one has CCS. Some of the stations with your desired plug will likely be busy. An adapter that allows your car to access both NACS and CCS ports will save a lot of headaches.

Once again, you’re not the only one trying to get home for the holidays. Let’s keep the queue moving, and try not to charge more than 80%, if possible. Charging speeds slow substantially when EV batteries surpass 80%. It may have only taken 15-20 minutes to get to 80%, but it’ll take another 20 or so to reach 100%, or much longer in some cars. Chances are, there’s someone waiting. Not only is it courteous to unplug and move on, but it’s also probably quicker to charge again (if you need it) closer to your destination.

I know this list has mostly been about minimizing time lost due to charging, but I think it’s important to remember that it’s okay to take a break. Charging stops are inherently longer than refuelling a gas car, but I think that 15-30 minute break is helpful for safe driving. Taking a rest, using the restroom, or getting a snack can take the edge off a trip. Use an EV’s characteristics to your advantage.

I’ve been doing EV road trips since 2021, and it’s amazing to see just how far both vehicle range and charging infrastructure have come. Long trips are possible for most drivers these days, whereas years ago, it was a huge hardship or even impossible. With better apps, better infrastructure, and better cars, it’s only gotten easier. But it still pays to know what you’re doing. Drive safely out there, folks.

It also advocated for multilateral approaches to resolving conflicts around the world. All of this enjoyed broad public support. But all of this has also changed since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine and the wars in the Middle East. Now, Germany's foreign policy has become increasingly contentious and is fueling growing public anxiety.

Long-held assumptions are beginning to crumble, including the belief that the US would ultimately remain firmly on Germany's side. This is one of the central findings of a new survey by the Hamburg-based Körber Foundation.

In the foundation's latest poll on public attitudes on German foreign policy, 65% of respondents said they fear further military conflicts around the world. Meanwhile, 48% believe that Germany should adopt a more restrained approach to international affairs — a five percentage point increase from last year.

Since 2017, the non-profit Körber Foundation, established in the northern German city of Hamburg in 1959 by industrialist Kurt Körber, has conducted the annual surveys titled "the Berlin Pulse." For this year's study, the polling institute Forsa surveyed around 1,500 eligible voters in September.

The findings make one thing clear: The current government's approach under Chancellor Friedrich Merz — seeking to have a say in virtually every international conflict and drawing on Germany's position as the largest EU economy — is increasingly met with skepticism. Only 40% of respondents now favor greater international engagement, down from 48% in 2025.

Florian Bigge, who helped compile the study for the Körber Foundation, commented on the findings on Monday: "This is a significant decline, especially since we have been asking this question for years, with changes in the range of just two to three percentage points."

Meanwhile, 77% of respondents want Germany to focus primarily on diplomacy rather than military action.

The most dramatic shift in public opinion concerns the war in Ukraine. Germany's military support for the Eastern European country, including the delivery of billions of euros' worth of weapons, no longer has the backing of a majority of the population. In September 2026, only 46% of respondents supported continued military assistance, compared to 59% in the previous year's survey. Currently, a narrow majority of 53% want to halt weapons deliveries altogether.

As Jonathan Lehrer of "the Berlin Pulse" explains, opposition is particularly strong among supporters of the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD).

"Eighty-eight precent of AfD supporters oppose military assistance to Ukraine," Lehrer said. "This is an interesting finding, especially considering political developments in Germany, including state elections in which the AfD gained support by campaigning against aid to Ukraine."

The AfD has recently overtaken the chancellor's conservative Christian Democratic Union (CDU) in nationwide polls.

Support for Ukraine was also the subject of a recent poll conducted by the opinion research institute Infratest Dimap. This study also found that public support for Ukraine is continuing to decline, with a majority now opposed to providing assistance (see graphic).

One finding from the Berlin Pulse study is particularly alarming for politicians and experts committed to transatlantic relations. As the US and China are increasingly seen as rivals on the global stage, 83% of respondents believe that Germany should remain neutral. Only 15% would side with the US.

This may stem from the policies of US President Donald Trump. Florian Bigge of the Körber Foundation said of the widespread preference for neutrality: "This sentiment is strong among Germans across all age groups and political parties. In eastern Germany, support for neutrality is at 92%, compared to 81% in the west. We interpret this as a desire for security but also reluctant commitment: Germans do not want to take sides in this conflict between the great powers."

The picture becomes clearer when respondents are asked about Russia. Under President Vladimir Putin, 82% of those surveyed see Russia as a military threat. However, public opinion is more divided when Germany responds firmly to such threats, including hybrid attacks. For instance, after the attempted drone attack on Leipzig Airport in early May, Germany responded by closing the Russian consulate in Bonn. However, only 51% support such measures, while 46% favor a more restrained approach.

One of the world’s rarest fish lives in a single cave in Death Valley National Park, and earthquakes have left it on the verge of extinction

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Devils Hole pupfish live in just one cavern of Death Valley National Park. Earthquakes are rapidly pushing this fish towards extinction.

One of the rarest fish on Earth lives in just one place: a water-filled cavern in the Nevada desert called Devils Hole, part of Death Valley National Park. In the spring of 2025, after two earthquakes shook its tiny home, biologists counted only 38 of them.

The National Park Service announced the new figure in November. Divers and surface counters found the 137 Devils Hole pupfish during a survey on October 10, nearly double the 77 seen in the spring of 2026 and about double the 69 recorded in the fall of 2025.

Devils Hole is a cave about 12 feet wide and more than 500 feet deep. The bottom has never been mapped. The water stays at a constant 92 degrees Fahrenheit (33 degrees Celsius).

Pupfish have been found as deep as 66 feet, but they feed and spawn only on a shallow rock shelf near the surface, where algae grows.

The species has been isolated there for 10,000 to 20,000 years, longer than any other pupfish in the Death Valley system. The region’s caves keep other long records too, like a Nevada cave that reveals 580,000 years of climate swings.

It is the only naturally occurring population of the species, which was listed as endangered in 1967. Biologists from the National Park Service and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service have counted the fish almost every spring and fall since 1972, with scuba divers in the cavern and counters at the surface.

One of the fish’s main threats can come from thousands of miles away. Large earthquakes as far away as Japan, Indonesia and Chile have made the water slosh in Devils Hole like water in a bathtub, according to the park.

Waves can splash as high as two meters (about 6.5 feet) up the walls and sweep the shelf clean. The park calls it a tsunami in the desert, although it is tiny next to the ocean tsunamis that big earthquakes can unleash.

That is what happened in the winter of 2024 to 2025. Two earthquakes, one in December and one in February, set off waves that moved algae, organic material and fish eggs off the shelf.

The spring count in 2025 found 38 fish. One year earlier, the official spring count had recorded 191.

On July 29, 2025, just 21 minutes after a magnitude 8.8 earthquake struck Russia’s Kamchatka Peninsula, waves rippled through Devils Hole, nearly 4,000 miles away. Those waves were only about 10 inches high and removed just some material from the shelf.

Then, after an earthquake in Panama on October 9, 2026, nearly 3,000 miles away, biologists at first saw signs of stress in the fish. On a later visit, they found them in healthier condition.

The team had prepared for a crash like this for years. Pupfish eggs from Devils Hole had been collected regularly to build a backup population at the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service’s Ash Meadows Fish Conservation Facility.

In 2025, biologists added captive-raised fish to the wild population for the first time. Between March 2025 and February 2026, scientists released 72 of them into Devils Hole. They also gave the fish extra food while the algae grew back.

The fish live an average of only 10 to 14 months. Because of that, biologists believe most of the 137 fish counted this fall hatched and grew up in Devils Hole itself.

“It’s exciting to see Devils Hole pupfish numbers increase so significantly over the past year,” said Death Valley National Park Superintendent Mike Reynolds.

“These results reflect the dedication of our scientists and conservation partners working to protect this extraordinary species. While challenges remain, we’re encouraged by this progress and committed to its long-term survival.”

The pupfish has survived human threats before. In the late 1960s, a farming operation drilled large wells in the Ash Meadows area, and the water level in Devils Hole began to drop toward the shelf the fish need.

Biologists even hung a floating, artificially lit shelf in the cave as a substitute. The fish never used it.

A federal court halted further pumping in 1971, and the fight reached the U.S. Supreme Court. Its 1976 decision in Cappaert v. United States recognized the water right of Devils Hole as part of a national monument and limited pumping so the natural shelf would stay underwater.

Until the mid-1990s, spring surveys counted more than 200 fish. In the early 2000s, spring counts averaged about 90, and in 2013 the number fell to 35. The cause of that long decline has not been determined.

The new count is an encouraging step, but the park says the population naturally fluctuates and remains highly vulnerable.

It calls continued monitoring, research and habitat management essential if one of the world’s rarest fish is to keep its foothold in a single desert cave.

Research Says Cold Muscles Produce Less Power. Here’s How to Protect Your Performance on Frigid Runs.

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Experts share how to prepare, pace, and recover so you can run strong when temperatures fall below freezing.

When temperatures rise, running performance tends to drop. Both the research and anyone who’s put in miles on a hot summer afternoon can confidently tell you that much.

But what about when the mercury moves in the opposite direction? Does colder weather in the winter—ranging from just a bit chilly to downright frigid—take a toll on your running too?

A recent review published in Temperature suggests it does. According to the study’s authors, when our muscles get too cold, neuromuscular function becomes impaired. On the run, this can contribute to stiffness, less power output, and decreased endurance, all of which may cause your winter workouts to feel harder than usual.

The good news? You can still run strong when the temps drop—as long as you make a few key training adjustments before, during, and after you log your miles in the colder months of the year. Here’s how to do just that.

There’s no exact number on the thermometer at which runners can universally expect their power, speed, or endurance to decline, Stephen Cheung, PhD, the lead author of the aforementioned review, tells Runner’s World.

Temperature is just one of the many factors that contribute to how well we perform in the cold, Cheung explains. “It’s a function of the environment, as well as how much heat you’re generating, the speed that you’re moving at, and of course, the amount of clothing that you’re wearing,” he says.

Each of these factors varies significantly from runner to runner and affects how quickly their bodies lose heat. You only need to consider those in your run group who run shirtless when it’s freezing versus those who bundle up when it’s 50 degrees Fahrenheit to understand that we all thermoregulate (maintain our internal body temperature) differently. And the equation only becomes more complex when you account for wind and moisture—both in the environment and what you create yourself from air resistance and sweat.

Heather Milton, CSCS, lead exercise physiologist at Primary MD and cofounder of Master My Run, agrees. “It would be great to be able to say, when the temperature outside reaches [a certain point], your pace slows down by this many seconds per mile—but that’s not how it works,” she says.

Keeping this in mind, Milton says the cleanest reference point for runners to begin paying more attention to how they prepare for runs in the cold is when the temperature drops below freezing. Generally speaking, the colder it gets from there, the more noticeable the negative effects on performance will be, she explains.

Cold weather leads to vasoconstriction, Milton explains, which means our blood vessels constrict more, so the muscles farther away from the core don’t receive as much blood flow. If your fingers or toes have ever gone numb in the cold, for example, you’ve experienced the joy of vasoconstriction. Our bodies prioritize heating our core over our extremities because it helps our vital organs maintain a consistent temperature, which is essential for survival in dangerous conditions.

This physiological response, however, doesn’t bode especially well for running performance.

“The cold slows nerve conduction so you have to work harder for the same power output,” Milton says. “And as temperatures drop further below freezing, that slower nerve conduction and the effects of vasoconstriction are going to be even more noticeable.” As a result, your overall exercise capacity decreases, meaning you may not be able to run for as long or at the same pace you normally would.

What else might you notice when temperatures drop? “Physically, if your muscles and tendons are cold, they’re going to be tighter, less elastic, and you won’t be able to contract them as strongly,” says Cheung. “Stiff muscles can also put you at greater risk for tears or strain injuries.”

Milton says this analogy, used by an old coach of hers, provides an easy way to understand what’s happening: When your muscles and tendons get too cold, they’re like a cold rubber band that can snap when pulled.

If you’re a runner who dislikes the cold, you might wonder if the smarter choice is to just run on the treadmill this winter. Don’t worry: As long as you prepare appropriately and use caution, you can absolutely continue logging regular miles outdoors.

Running well outside in the cold starts with warming up indoors first, says Milton. “An indoor warmup increases your internal metabolism and enhances blood flow, which helps prepare your muscles for contraction,” she explains. This also produces heat so your internal body temperature is more resilient to the cold outdoor environment.

Your winter warmup does not have to be any different or longer than your usual routine, Milton says. But you do want to make sure that you’re getting in a solid five minutes of prerun drills to adequately raise your body temperature and activate your muscles.

She recommends starting with slower, more controlled movements that help activate the core and the glutes, such as planks with leg raises, lunges with rotation, and hip CARs (controlled articular rotations). Finish with quicker drills that require your nerves to fire more rapidly, such as leg swings and butt kicks.

Equally important to warming up is layering up—with an important caveat.

When dressing for winter runs, keep in mind how you personally respond to cold temperatures, says Cheung, because everyone has different tolerances and susceptibilities. “I get very cold in my hands, so I’ll wear really thick gloves, even when I may be wearing relatively little on my torso,” he adds. “Pay attention to what’s an issue for you and adapt your clothing to both the conditions and your own peculiarities.” Cheung emphasizes this because you don’t want to end up sweating too heavily, which can then leave you chilled and clammy.

If you need a starting point for your winter running wardrobe, aim for two to three layers of breathable, moisture-wicking fabrics. If possible, choose a wind- and water-resistant shell for your outermost layer, suggests Milton. For your lower body, wear shorts over your tights to help keep your glutes warm. Finally, cover any exposed skin on your head, ears, and hands, and opt for a pair of wool socks—all of which can help ensure adequate blood distribution to the extremities, Milton adds.

When you head out for runs in the cold, slowly build up to your usual pace.

Gradually easing into your workout gives your muscles more time to adjust, and it can also help your airways get used to the colder temperatures, Milton says. You don’t necessarily need to go slower for your entire run, but you should try to match your rate of perceived exertion (RPE) with your typical effort, rather than aiming for your usual pace or heart-rate zone.

“Think about it the same way as if you were running on an extremely hot day,” Milton says. “Let’s say your RPE for an easy run is about 3 out of 10. Keep that 3 out of 10, even if your pace is a little slower.”

If you find that your run feels more difficult than usual, slow down if you need to, but don’t stop completely, Cheung advises. “Don’t take long breaks, because that’s when you aren’t generating as much heat. Just keep moving so you have that constant heat generation,” he says.

If you start to get cold on a run, try speeding up a little bit to stoke that metabolic heat production, Cheung says. But make sure you do it safely: Don’t force the effort in icy conditions or when your muscles feel especially stiff. Scouting out run routes with more tree coverage to block wind on blustery days can also help you stay warmer, he adds.

As soon as you finish your workout, change out of your sweaty running gear and into warm, dry clothes, Milton says.

Even if you’ve only sweated a small amount—for example, maybe you just have some dampness under your armpits—you still want to remove any wet fabrics against your skin. “Even that small amount of wetness can start to drop your core temperature,” Milton explains. If you wait until you’re near shivering, you’ll face an uphill battle to get yourself warm again.

You can also opt for a relaxing shower and hot chocolate—extra marshmallows.

Distributed LLM Training Across Metros: Streaming DiLoCo in MaxText

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Traditionally, training state-of-the-art Large Language Models (LLMs) relies on a monolithic cluster of accelerators, where all devices are collocated and tightly coupled via high-bandwidth to constantly synchronize gradients. However, physical and cost constraints make scaling a single slice increasingly difficult.

To scale further, we can connect multiple compute slices across geographically distributed datacenters. However, when training across multiple datacenters over wide-area networks (WAN), standard data-parallel training suffers from severe network stalls because slow WAN transfers cannot be fully overlapped with computation.

To address this, the Google DeepMind and Google Research teams developed DiLoCo (Distributed Low-Communication), a distributed optimization algorithm designed to make training resilient to these low-bandwidth, high-latency connections. In this blog post, we discuss the core concepts of DiLoCo, how it is implemented in MaxText (Google's open-source JAX-based LLM training framework), and the performance benefits it brings to large-scale LLM training on Google Cloud.

DiLoCo was introduced by Google DeepMind in the paper DiLoCo: Distributed Low-Communication Training of Language Models. Instead of synchronizing gradients across all devices at every single training step (as in standard Data Parallelism), DiLoCo partitions training across M independent compute groups, referred to as learners, and allows each learner to perform many local optimization steps independently before communicating across groups.

Despite communicating significantly less frequently (by a factor of H, e.g., 1-2 orders of magnitude less) than standard synchronous training, DiLoCo has been shown to achieve comparable convergence and model quality.

The Vanilla DiLoCo described above reduces total communication volume by a factor of H, however, all learners still transmit their weight deltas for the entire model all at once at every H-th step. On a thin network pipe, that full-model transfer creates a large periodic stall every H steps. This issue is alleviated by Streaming DiLoCo, which is described in the next section.

In multi-slice training, communication within a single slice is handled by high-speed Inter-Chip Interconnects (ICI). When scaling to multiple slices, synchronization across slices must traverse the Data Center Network (DCN) or, if the slices are geographically distributed, wide-area networks (WAN).

Within a single datacenter, DCN bandwidth is high enough that JAX and XLA can often hide standard Data Parallel all-reduce collectives by overlapping gradient communication with backward-pass computation. Across datacenters or congested links, however, bandwidth drops and latency rises sharply. Standard DP stalls at every step waiting for global gradient synchronization, collapsing Model FLOPS Utilization (MFU).

Streaming DiLoCo eliminates this synchronization stall by partitioning the model's parameters into P disjoint fragments. Over the course of H steps, every single fragment in the model is synchronized exactly once, achieving the exact same outer-loop cadence as vanilla DiLoCo without spiky bandwidth utilization.

Using a roofline model (similar to the JAX Scaling Book), we can calculate the network bandwidth required to prevent communication stalls for a model with ∣Θ∣ parameters (at b bytes per parameter, e.g., b=2 for bfloat16) and per-step compute time tstep=tfwd+tbwd. For example, the standard Data Parallel algorithm transfers 2∣Θ∣b bytes per sync via an all-reduce collective, which is a distributed reduce-scatter where each node reduces a different part, followed by an all-gather so all participants receive all parts. To achieve zero communication stalls, we overlap the reduce-scatter of the gradients with the backward pass, which produces them and takes tbwd time. Therefore, the peak required bandwidth is Bdp ≈ 2∣Θ∣b / tbwd.

Thankfully, females have not yet turned their back on the printed page, but ominous trends in reading habits for girls and boys suggest that at least the level of enthusiasm for reading among both genders is in decline. And as motivation to read slips, so does reading skill.

Where this relationship is most pronounced, however, is with boys, who have significantly lower levels of reading engagement than their female counterparts and fall well short of girls in reading achievement on global, national, and state-level reading assessments. When it comes to enjoyment of reading, time spent reading for pleasure, and diversity of text read (i.e., newspapers, magazines, fiction, nonfiction) girls in the U.S. and in most countries of the world have significantly and consistently higher levels.

However, there’s no shortcut to getting there. We can’t expect boys who rarely read to ever possess the level of literacy we wish for them. Parents who accommodate their sons’ non-reading habits are in some ways enabling stunted literacy growth.

Research tends to do a good job of documenting the decline in reading engagement and achievement without always specifying the causes. Thus, it’s helpful to look at the literature related to motivating students to read and then by inference appreciate the reasons for the lack of motivation. For example, researchers have discovered factors that positively influence motivation to read, such as (a) creating contexts for free self-selected reading, (b) giving students more text choices, and (c) structuring collaboration for reading and learning. Looked at from the opposite perspective, then, one can infer that students are disengaged when time is not set aside for free reading, when students’ choices about what to read are restricted or non-existent, and when students only read in isolation without opportunities to discuss and share texts.

These same lessons from research can help guide parents who are eager to create supportive environments for reading for their sons.

I’m fond of saying at the conclusion of my talks on this subject that “A boy’s love of reading doesn’t begin with scholarship, it begins with discovery." The more we do to help boys discover or rediscover the joy and pleasure of reading, the greater the chances they’ll become engaged and skillful readers for life.

The Stedelijk Museum in Amsterdam has announced that the Dutchman Krist Gruijthuijsen will take over as its new director from July 2027.

Moving from Finland, where he has since 2025 been director of the Espoo Museum of Modern Art (EMMA), he said he wants to bring an outsider’s perspective and a focus on how the modern city connects with the world.

“The Stedelijk museum’s strength is in bringing large and Modernist exhibitions to the Netherlands,” he tells The Art Newspaper. “People go to the Rijksmuseum for Rembrandt and to the city [Stedelijk] museum to understand how Amsterdam has developed, to understand Modernism through the lens of art and design.”

The Stedelijk Museum enjoyed success last year with an Anselm Kiefer show—organised with the neighbouring Van Gogh Museum—and an exhibition of work by the Dutch photographer Erwin Olaf, increasing annual ticket revenue compared to 2024 by almost a quarter, to €10.8 million. Its current Yayoi Kusama show has been extended to the end of January due to its popularity.

This success stands out against a backdrop of Dutch museums reporting financial pressure and a fall in visits compared to pre-pandemic levels. The Rijksmuseum’s most recent annual report describes internal discussions that took place about the fact visitor numbers fell 4% in 2025 to 2.3 million, while the Rembrandt House Museum recently warned that rising tourist taxes will damage cultural tourism.

But Gruijthuijsen said he is more interested in variety than visitor records. “I’m not a visitor numbers director,” he says. “It is quality rather than quantity: what we need economically, and the rest is a plus. It is very difficult these days because people only choose what they know. My goal is first and foremost to support artists and art.”

Gruijthuijsen takes over from Rein Wolfs, who has been the director since 2019.

From one of the first exhibitions to establish Van Gogh’s work in 1905 to experimental shows of the 1960s, the Stedelijk Museum created an international reputation for contemporary art, which Gruijthuijsen wants to make relevant today. “The DNA of the city museum is a modern day and global perspective—trying to find connections,” he says.

From the Finns, he will also bring an important lesson in terms of improving diversity and tackling under-represented subjects, he says: “Less talking, more doing.”

How Thinking Outcomes Can Be More Important Than Being Right

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted November 18, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills

A few months back, I wrote a piece here about the viral conspiracy that Earth would lose gravity for 7 seconds on August 12th. I discussed how, despite not actually believing that the event would occur—because of a slew of reasons—the open-minded part of me (albeit still skeptical) nevertheless suggested developing a quick “just in case” protocol for the unlikely event. I received some positive feedback on how I exemplified the importance of cautious thinking throughout the piece and how, in context, we can engage two important dispositions towards thinking (Dwyer et al., 2016): open-mindedness and skepticism. On the other hand, though, some implied a level of foolishness in my thinking.

The latter cohort, I believe, missed my point, and so I aim to make it clearer in this follow-up. In that previous piece, I stated on multiple occasions that I didn’t actually believe the event would occur. In fact, I highlighted why it was nearly impossible.

But I used this event prediction as an example to make the point that cautious thinkers are typically happy to make minor mistakes in efforts to avoid risk, even if the risk is extremely low. That is, through cost-benefit analysis (consistent with many existing cost-benefit models we commonly see in psychology; e.g. Lave and March 1975; Thibaut and Kelley 1959; Uehara 1990), good thinkers may often choose a 99 percent likely mistake made out of caution, if the mistake yields minimal negative impact (e.g., looking foolish in front of one’s spouse). This will be selected over doing nothing potentially “foolish” (e.g., in light of being 99 percent “sure”), despite the disastrous impact that 1 percent chance would have because they failed to act (e.g., if the event occurred, death would be a near certainty if we were outdoors).

Simply, good thinkers don’t care about labels of wrong or right; they care about the outcomes of their decisions. It’s cautious thinking… and critical thinkers are cautious thinkers.

It’s much like hope for the best, plan for the worst. It’s why we develop contingency plans; it’s why we buy insurance. It’s largely why I don’t go outside in a thunderstorm, despite the odds against getting hit by lightning. It’s why I buy a lotto ticket once every few weeks, when the thought occurs to me on the day of a draw (i.e., I will not notice the loss of a couple of euros [thankfully], whereas winning the lotto would be life-changing). You never know.

With that, context (as well as moderation) is key. It might be foolish to buy too much insurance, to spend €200 weekly on the lotto, or to waste your time hiding in a cellar for three hours during your run-of-the-mill thunderstorm. Again, caution is considered in terms of cost (be it time, money, or status) relative to benefit or risk avoidance.

From an epistemological perspective, an established truth is that we cannot see into the future (as of yet, anyway [there’s some doozy of a meta-joke in there]). We do not know what’s going to happen. Objectively, we can place likelihoods on certain events happening, but that’s it. It’s our human interpretation that labels these probabilities as impossible, unlikely, possible, likely, highly probable, definite, etc. We might say definite, but that’s not exactly a guarantee. Consider the late Daniel Kahneman’s work on weather and stock forecasting (see also Kahneman, 2011). If we lived in a world of certainty (which we don’t), you’d never hear the phrase, “Well, that was unexpected.”

In moments of excitement and uncertainty, good thinkers are able to quickly evaluate probabilities, not just in terms of odds, but in terms of interpretation of outcomes (e.g., consider naturalistic decision-making [Klein, 2008]). The ability to do this quickly is a result of previous consideration and reflective judgment on priorities and values—things for which good thinkers would be concerned and cautious. They apply these considerations efficiently, if and when they need to. For example, despite less than a half-percent chance of your school burning down during your tenure in that school, how many fire drills were you a part of as a student? A lot, right? But you knew what to do in case it was for real.

Bizarrely, what’s your plan for a zombie apocalypse? Stop for a moment and think about it... Did your answer come to you fairly quickly? Even in the extreme unlikelihood of such a thing happening, it’s likely you thought about it or something similar before, at least once.

There’s nothing wrong with considering such things. Planning in such a way is a good cognitive exercise. It’s also good for creativity!

Cautious people often think in similar ways. Such cautious thinking might get accused of being “overthinking.” But that’s not necessarily a bad thing, unless it becomes a common or costly focus. Indeed, catastrophizing can be problematic. Moderation is important. But so too is caution, especially when it comes to ensuring good outcome-focused thinking. So, don’t sweat making small mistakes in life, especially if it’s done out of calculated caution. There’s nothing foolish about it.

Why Doesn't GTA 6 Have Licensed Vehicles Despite Rockstar's Immense Wealth?

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Grand Theft Auto, as you might imagine, has plenty of extremely cool cars. Grand Theft Auto VI brings this focus on its titular Autos even further, with a genuine race track and all the associated automotive accoutrements, but one series trend remains stalwart: No matter how close GTA's cars come to their real-life inspirations, Rockstar Games still won't place real logos or brand names on them. Why not?

Rockstar cofounder Jamie King has two reasons: Cost and creativity. For the former, GTA VI isn't even out yet and per GTABase, fans have already catalogued 317 unique vehicles — Rockstar has plenty of money, sure, but individually licensing each of those makes and models would still take serious cash that could be better used on other aspects of development. For the latter, not slavishly matching existing vehicles allows Rockstar freedom in designing and engineering the cars to better fit a game world. Cars don't need to match their real-world suspension design, drivetrains, or numbers of doors, and can instead tweak those to be better suited for what players need in-game.

There's another reason, though, that King didn't mention: Automakers are picky about how their cars are used in games. Grand Theft Auto is as much about the Grand Theft as the Autos, and carmakers often limit how much their cars can be damaged in-game — "GTA" players would be disappointed if they shot up a car and found it immune to bullet holes, explosions, and fire. By avoiding real-world branded vehicles, and the associated rules automakers place on their intellectual property, Rockstar can build more verisimilitude within its games.

Rockstar owns up to cost and creative control as reasons it doesn't license cars for Grand Theft Auto, but freedom to do what it wants with the vehicles in its games is likely just as big a reason. T-19A Kestrel: Meet The US Air Force Academy's New Unlimited Aerobatic Aircraft For Cadets

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

On November 12, four new composite planes swept low over the Cadet Wing assembled at the US Air Force Academy in Colorado. The flight marked the official arrival of the T-19A Kestrel light trainer at Davis Airfield in Colorado Springs. Instead of wading through decades of defense bureaucracy and drawn-out congressional appropriations, USAFA secured its next-generation aerobatic aircraft in just 10 months, cutting through red tape with the help of a private donor group led by Andrew McKenna.

The T-19A is a derivative of the commercial GB1 GameBird. By skipping the traditional procurement, leadership bypassed the budget overruns and timeline slips that have paralyzed programs like the Boeing T-7A Red Hawk. The lightweight plane is purpose-built for agility thanks to its designer, German aerobatic champion Philipp Steinbach. He is a co-founder of Game Aerospace Composites in Bentonville, Arkansas, which produces the off-the-shelf civilian variant certified to pull a staggering load of ten G-forces.

The Kestrel went from concept to delivery in 10 months using $3 million in private donations, whereas the Red Hawk has spent over a decade in development using billions in taxpayer funds and still faces multi-year delays. The T-19A will fill a major gap in modern USAFA flight training by giving cadets early exposure to high-G, unlimited aerobatics and complex energy management before they ever step into Undergraduate Pilot Training.

The T-19A, or GB1 GameBird, is the only American-built two-seat aircraft approved for Unlimited-category competition aerobatics. Its all-carbon monocoque airframe lacks traditional mechanical wing-attach joints, relying on a continuous composite spar cured inside an autoclave. This gives the plane an operating empty weight of roughly 1,290 pounds (585 kg) while enabling symmetric flight loads of up to 10-G at its aerobatic maximum limit.

Under the hood, a 583-cubic-inch Lycoming AEIO-580-B1A flat-six piston engine delivers 303 horsepower (226 kW) to a four-blade composite MT-Propeller system. An inverted oil and fuel system keeps the engine running under sustained negative-g flight and zero-g push-overs. Cadets command the airframe from tandem seats equipped with Garmin G3X Touch glass displays, combining edge-of-envelope stick-and-rudder forces with modern electronic flight screens before stepping into standard turboprops.

To make the transition, USAFA pulled six active-duty instructor pilots from the 306th Flying Training Group to stand up the first training cadre. These officers will shape a new syllabus to train cadets and establish a demonstration and competition team. The initiative comes as the wider military struggles with a persistent shortfall of roughly 2,000 operational pilots, forcing leadership to seek shortcuts to build stick-and-rudder skills earlier in pilot development.

The Academy's existing fleet jumps from unpowered TG-16A gliders and basic T-53A/T-41D piston trainers straight to the UPT's T-6A Texan II turboprops after graduation. USAF Chief of Staff General Ken Wilsbach and Academy Superintendent Lt. Gen. Paul Moga secured the four airframes thanks to a $3 million donation coordinated by philanthropist Andrew McKenna. Meanwhile, the USAFA paid $400,000 in operational funds for initial tooling, spares, and flight gear, along with a projected annual operating budget of $1 million to maintain the fleet.

The Kestrel will act as an intermediate high-performance step, teaching cadets how to manage high power-to-weight ratios, rapid throttle response, and aggressive energy management. Standard trainers are built for stability and prohibit extreme maneuvers. Because the Kestrel is certified for unlimited aerobatics, instructors can deliberately put cadets into inverted spins, vertical stalls, and disorienting attitudes, teaching them instinctive recovery skills that directly prevent real-world loss-of-control accidents.

The Air Force is facing a persistent pilot deficit and needs to push students through UPT faster without lowering standards. By giving top cadets advanced stick-and-rudder proficiency during their junior and senior years at the Academy, they arrive at UPT needing fewer remedial rides and moving through the syllabus faster. Additionally, building an aerobatic competition team around the Kestrel will push the selected cadets to fly under public scrutiny with the precision and discipline they will further refine in operational flying squadrons.

Operating the Kestrel at the USAFA in Colorado Springs comes with real performance trade-offs. The Academy's Davis Airfield sits at an elevation of 6,572 feet (2,003 meters), and summer heat often makes the air feel like it is over 9,000 feet (2,743 meters). That means the T-19A's non-turbocharged engine loses roughly 22% to 26% of its power. That thinner mountain air requires longer takeoff runs and leaves pilots with less excess engine power during demanding maneuvers.

Weight limits create another hurdle for daily operations, as the Kestrel can weigh up to 2,200 pounds (998 kg) for standard point-to-point flights. Its maximum takeoff rating for extreme 10-G aerobatics only applies at 1,940 pounds (880 kg) or less. Subtracting the plane's 1,290-pound (585 kg) empty weight leaves just 650 pounds (295 kg) for people, gear, and fuel. Once an instructor and a cadet strap in, only enough weight allowance remains for 25 to 30 gallons (95 to 114 liters) of fuel.

That limits intense flights to roughly 45 minutes, requiring ground crews to refuel and turn the planes around quickly between sorties. Moving cadets from unpowered gliders into a 200-knot (370 km/h) airplane is also a major operational adjustment. Because rapid spins and heavy G-forces can easily cause motion sickness or make new pilots lose track of which way is up, instructors must design a step-by-step program that gradually introduces cadets to the plane's capabilities.

Built entirely from carbon fiber, the T-19A brings modern composite design to a general aviation field long dominated by traditional steel-tube frames. Even compared to the newer carbon-fiber Extra NG, the Kestrel weighs about 110 pounds (50 kg) less at an empty weight of 1,290 pounds (585 kg). Its wing and fuselage tanks also hold 81 gallons (307 liters) of fuel, giving it a cross-country range of over 1,000 nautical miles (1,852 kilometers).

In the air, the Kestrel reacts far faster than almost all other military trainers. The USAF's primary turboprop trainer, the Beechcraft T-6A Texan II, rolls at 180 degrees per second and handles forces between positive 7.0-G and negative 3.5-G. The Kestrel more than doubles that agility, rolling at 400 degrees per second while withstanding up to positive and negative 10-G.

Unlike older aerobatic planes, the Kestrel avoids the sluggish handling caused by a heavy nose-mounted radial engine. Its horizontally opposed Lycoming responds instantly, without the twisting-torque side effects of a radial engine during aggressive maneuvers. That instant throttle response also translates into blistering acceleration and vertical climb performance. Its power-to-weight ratio allows the Kestrel to take off in just 680 feet (207 meters) and climb at 4,200 feet per minute (1,280 meters per minute).

This sharp handling marks a major departure from the Academy’s existing T-53As, which are militarized Cirrus SR20s built to train for instrument flying rather than agility and dogfighting. The Kestrel bridges that gap, letting cadets practice inverted flight, vertical spins, and high-G recovery before they arrive at active-duty pilot training bases to begin the pipeline to the cockpit of a warplane.

The T-19A's performance derives from its lightweight carbon-fiber fuselage built around a single, continuous spar that runs straight through the cabin floor. By dropping heavy metal wing joints, Game Aerospace saved weight while also making the 25.26-foot (7.70-meter) span wings strong enough to withstand extreme loads up to 15-G.

Up front, the engine spins a 74.8-inch (1,900 mm) German-made MT-Propeller built from a compressed wood core wrapped in a tough composite shell with stainless-steel leading edges. Because standard engines lose oil pressure when flying upside down, the Kestrel uses a specialized inverted oil system that keeps oil flowing smoothly between 55 and 95 psi (3.8 and 6.5 bar) at temperatures up to 245°F (118°C), even during violent tumbling maneuvers.

Instead of flexible steel cables that can stretch under heavy loads, the pilot's control stick connects to the wings and tail via rigid metal pushrods mounted on sealed bearings for instant, slop-free response. Small aerodynamic paddles beneath the wings help balance the large carbon ailerons so the stick stays light at speeds up to 175 knots (324 km/h), while tiny fins along the wing surface keep airflow smooth and prevent sudden wing stalls down to 60 knots (111 km/h).

5 Legendary Rock Bands With Only One Perfect Album, Ranked

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

It can take years for a rock band to finally get radio play, but for a lucky few exceptionally talented acts, all it takes is one album to make a lasting impression. It's not every day that a band comes together, puts out a single project, and then leaves it all behind. Whether it's a side project, an inability to maintain the same chemistry beyond the debut, or simply a matter of unfortunate circumstances, there's something fascinating about bands with only one album to their name.

Arguably, a one-album discography can represent some of the most honest work in a band's career, seizing its artistry at its rawest. There's also the pressure of making that one record count, leaving listeners with something truly memorable. Without further ado, here are five of the greatest rock bands with only one perfect album, ranked.

Following the massive MTV success of Enema of the State and Take Off Your Pants and Jacket, it was only a matter of time before fans (and their record label) expected Blink-182 to follow up with another hit-making album built around their tried-and-true three-power-chord formula. But for members Tom DeLonge and Travis Barker, they needed an outlet to explore heavier sounds and darker subject matter—something that contrasted with Blink-182's rambunctious energy, immature antics, and crass humor. Enter their side project, Box Car Racer. Originally conceived as a creative outlet during a break from touring, the short-lived project gave DeLonge and Barker room to branch out musically and experiment with a different sound, something they would continue exploring in later Blink-182 releases and with DeLonge’s future band, Angels & Airwaves.

Inspired by DeLonge's interest in post-hardcore bands like Fugazi, Refused, and Quicksand, Box Car Racer ditches the fast, chord-driven riffs he was accustomed to playing in favor of heavier guitar textures, distorted riffs, and drop-D tuning—all channeled into their self-titled album. While traces of his pop-punk roots remain unmistakable, the band isn't chasing catchy hooks designed to dominate radio airplay. Instead, explores darker themes ranging from apocalyptic destruction to Freemasonry—definitely a far cry from singing about teenage break ups and personal insecurities.

The Los Angeles hardcore punk scene owes a lot to The Germs. Their original run may have lasted only four years in the late '70s, but the band helped lay the groundwork for the scene, leaving an influence that would carry on for decades. The brainchild of lead vocalist Darby Crash and guitarist Pat Smear (who would later play with Nirvana and the Foo Fighters), The Germs drew inspiration from a surprisingly wide range of artists, including Iggy Pop, The Ramones, and The Sex Pistols. Their only studio album, GI, is regarded as one of the earliest full-length hardcore punk records and is notably produced by Joan Jett of The Runaways.

GI proves that hardcore's unrestrained chaos doesn't have to come at the expense of structure or artistic expression. Rather than reducing the genre to theatrical antics and one-minute bursts of noise with barely intelligible lyrics, The Germs showed that hardcore could be just as expressive and musically thoughtful as any other genre. Hardcore punk may have been at the heart of their sound, but it was their frenetic aggression, both on record and during notoriously chaotic live performances that sometimes got them kicked offstage, that cemented their status as legends of the first generation of Los Angeles punk bands.

Their time in the industry might be short-lived, but The La's got the world singing in karaoke thanks to their hit track "There She Goes," which has become an anthem for many romantic comedies. For a bunch of Brit lads making jolly, Merseybeat sing-along tunes à la The Kinks, The Who, and The Rolling Stones, The La's were surprisingly perfectionistic. This not only became a huge obstacle during the production of their debut album but also put a strain on the band's relationships, eventually contributing to their breakup. It's quite a contrast for a band whose repertoire sounds so raw and organic to have gone through a recording process filled with rejected takes, endless re-recordings, and rotating producers.

While The La's weren't happy with their self-titled debut album (which took a total of three years to record), its sound became a blueprint for Britpop, thanks to its emphasis on guitar-driven music and 1960s British rock influences. The album's legacy can still be heard in the music of Oasis, Arctic Monkeys, and Fontaines D.C. While it's a shame The La's never released another studio album, they at least had the chance to create something that would stand the test of time. The recording process may have been a pain, but the fruits of their labor are unmistakable.

Them Crooked Vultures takes the term "supergroup" to another level, bringing together a trio that's a match made in rock heaven: Josh Homme of Queens of the Stone Age, John Paul Jones of Led Zeppelin, and Dave Grohl of Nirvana and the Foo Fighters. Their sound feels like a more laid-back take on Homme's work with his own band, but with a catchier, more modern rock edge. The project took years to come together, with the trio making its debut in 2009. All three musicians bring their own playing styles to the table, but somehow, everything clicks.

Their self-titled album is a real treat for rock fans, especially when you consider the heavyweight talent packed into this tight trio. There's no shortage of heavy guitar riffs, but the album isn't all about loud guitars and flashy musicianship. It also throws in funk influences, piano arrangements, and plenty of catchy vocal hooks from Homme. There's a little bit of everything here, and that's what makes the record such a familiar yet fresh listen. Rather than sounding like three rock legends simply showing off what they can do, Them Crooked Vultures brings their individual strengths together without the need to overpower each other.

Britain's biggest troublemakers have long sparked debate over whether they're true punk pioneers or just cynical posers who were more interested in the lifestyle than the music itself. It doesn't help that bassist Sid Vicious reportedly could barely play his own instrument, with Steve Jones having to step in and re-record the bass tracks. Still, whether you buy into their authenticity or not, there's no denying that the Sex Pistols left a lasting mark on anti-establishment culture. Their rebellious attitude was a sharp contrast to Britain's prim and proper image, particularly the kind of regal etiquette expected in a society still shaped by its monarchy.

Their only studio album, Never Mind the Bollocks, Here's the Sex Pistols, is pretty much the epitome of their bad-boy attitude. Dropped by record labels, plastered across tabloids, and met with concert cancellations, the album attracted plenty of public scrutiny for its outrageous content. Few songs were as controversial as "God Save the Queen" and "Anarchy in the U.K.," both of which captured the spirit of punk at its most rebellious. The album was packed with political undertones, and at a time when censorship was still rife in an uptight British society, the Sex Pistols came in swinging with a sound that refused to follow the rules and lyrics that could give the royal family a headache.

Ayumu Imazu Talks ‘musubi’ & The Belief that Music ‘Brings People Together’: Billboard Japan Monthly Interview

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

He chatted with Billboard Japan about the making of the song & what he envisions for the Budokan and beyond.

Singer/songwriter Ayumu Imazu spoke to Billboard Japan for its Monthly Feature interview series spotlighting artists and releases on its radar.

Imazu’s latest single “musubi,” released digitally on Oct. 2, has become the biggest hit of his career. “musubi” is a love song portraying the bonds between people through the motif of the “red string of fate.” A demo posted to TikTok in late August, when only a single verse and chorus existed, spread widely and led to an “emergency release.” The track swept the charts across streaming services immediately after it dropped and earned the ​26-year-old artist his first-ever No. 1 on Billboard Japan’s Streaming Songs chart (dated Oct. 14, 2026), marking the biggest breakthrough of his career to date.

2026 has also been a milestone year for him — it marks the fifth anniversary of his major-label debut, he announced a partnership with SKY-HI-led BMSG in February, and in May he released CLASSIC, his first album in four years. Next up, on Jan. 31, 2027, he’ll play his first-ever headlining show at Nippon Budokan, a venue he has long named as a goal.  Related Wale on Triumphant Pair of 2027 Grammy Nominations for 'Everything Is a Lot': 'I Hope It Teaches a Lot of People About Resilience' Miley Cyrus Reacts to Shared Grammy Nod With Dolly Parton: 'A Beautiful Way to Honor Her Enormous Heart' Rosalía Reacts to Her 9 Grammy Nominations: See How She Celebrated Explore Billboard See latest videos, charts and news

After pouring all of himself into CLASSIC, “musubi” emerged as Imazu circled back to the foundation of his music, which is about “bringing people together.” He chatted with Billboard Japan about the making of the song, the inspiration he’s drawn from his new environment at BMSG, and what he envisions for the Budokan and beyond.

“musubi” has been generating a huge response. When did you start working on it?

Around two months ago, in mid-August. I finished the first verse and chorus at the end of August and posted it on TikTok maybe two days after that.

The title is symbolic. “musubi” is a love song about the bonds that tie people together. Where did the idea come from?

In May​,​ I released CLASSIC, my first album in four years, and most of it was new songs I co-wrote with overseas producers. I put so much into making it that I felt pretty empty in a way afterward. I was gradually taking things in and building my energy back up while touring, and as I considered what to make next, I vaguely thought it’d be nice to do something about bringing people together, because I feel that’s the biggest purpose of making music. I started making it with that as the big goal.

Bringing people together through music — is that one of the motivations you’ve always held onto?

Maybe not always, but with the way the times are going… right now I feel like everyone’s own worlds of the things they like are scattered all over the place. It’s becoming harder for everyone to get excited about a single piece of music or to share the same feelings. That’s why I wanted to make a song like “musubi.”

You announced your partnership with BMSG this February, and in April you also shared that you’d gotten married. Do you feel those changes in your working environment and life stage have influenced you?

I wasn’t thinking about those things in particular. More than anything, I really wanted to make something that carried on from the album. I put out CLASSIC after forming the partnership with BMSG, wanting to show the fullest extent of myself all over again, like, “This is Ayumu Imazu.” So doing the same thing didn’t feel right. My next focus naturally turned to what’s at the core of my music, the theme of connecting people.

CLASSIC was an album built around powerful, polished dance numbers that were quintessential Ayumu Imazu. At the same time, vocal-driven songs that showcase the beauty of the Japanese language, like “Anata To Itai” and “musubi,” are essential to any discussion of your discography. How do you think about balancing or blending the two?

When I was first starting out and trying to show people who Ayumu Imazu is as an artist, I was really conflicted between going with dance tracks and the fact that I also love ballads. But my strongest feeling was that I didn’t want to choose (just one), so after putting out all kinds of songs, I now feel the dance tracks are very Ayumu Imazu, and I also really feel that slow-tempo songs like “musubi” are me, too. The range of musical styles is broad, but I don’t really feel like I’m moving away from the foundation of my own feelings and personality.

Does your actual production process change depending on the style of the song?

It does. With dance tracks​,​ I usually start with the beat, and with ballad-type songs like this one, I usually work out the chords on acoustic guitar or piano, write the melody to a certain point, and then program it.

What did you focus on most in terms of your vocals?

The demo was already out there and everyone had gotten used to hearing it, so when I recorded the final take, I had to either make it sound similar or surpass it. I’d gotten used to hearing it myself, especially from just before the chorus through the first half of the chorus, so that was where I struggled most, and probably the part I was most particular about.

Where do you feel you managed to “surpass” it?

If anything, I think it has a different kind of appeal. The demo had its own appeal as a demo, that relaxed quality of casually singing at home. The released version has more feeling put into it, I guess… it really comes across as emotional. Personally, I really feel that difference.

When you shared the demo, you posted on TikTok that you were “still deciding on a title.”

I read through lots of comments and ideas. A lot of people said they “want to use it at their wedding,” so when I was finishing the full version, I thought, “Maybe I could make it a bit more like a wedding song.” It was a great reference and really helped me out.

Tell us about the changes in your environment, too. BMSG, the company you partnered with, brings together artists brimming with individuality under the slogan “​​Never extinguish talent.” What kind of inspiration and influence have you gotten in that environment?

At first​,​ I was completely wrapped up in my own stuff, but I recently took part in BMSG FES for the first time and got to see the other BMSG artists perform, and I was really inspired. Personally, it was my first time riding a trolley float, so that was a great experience too. [Laughs] Also, it reminded me that it truly is a music-first company, and since what everyone expresses is pretty different, I thought it was really great that every group and every artist puts their own colors front and center without being bound by any set format.

BMSG also has a vision called “TYOISM,” about reaching the world from Tokyo. With you joining — someone who moved to the U.S. at 14 and is still based there and in Japan — it seems likely to spark some new chemistry.

I think I have a pretty good feel for how things are done abroad, so when it comes to opening doors to global challenges, I definitely want to do that together with BMSG. Mr. Hidaka (SKY-HI) and I had talked about that kind of thing from the start.

Now that five years have passed since your 2021 major-label debut, how do you see the phase you’re in as an artist?

Right now might be a phase where I just make music really freely, without overthinking it. Last year or so, I was thinking a lot about things like, “I want to release this kind of song and become this kind of artist,” but lately I feel like I’ve thought it all through, so it’s more like I’m going with the flow.

What artists have you been listening to a lot lately?

I’ve had Remi Wolf’s album (Mud) on heavy rotation lately. I really love indie rock-type stuff, too.

You have a headlining show set at Nippon Budokan in January 2027. Tell us how you’re feeling going in.

The Budokan is truly a stage I’ve set as a goal, so I think it’s going to be a really flashy show. [Laughs] More people than ever are listening to my music, and I’ve got a concert booked at my biggest venue yet, so I’m super happy and feel like all I have to do is keep doing what I’ve been doing. I want to keep creating spaces where everyone can come together as one and enjoy music at bigger and bigger venues.

—This interview by Takuto Ueda first appeared on Billboard Japan

Humanity braces for an alien invasion in 3 Body Problem S2 teaser

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

“Wake up! They haven’t come 24 trillion miles to negotiate. They are coming to exterminate us.”

You might be excused for forgetting about the existence of 3 Body Problem, Netflix’s heady sci-fi series adapted from the award-winning novel The Three-Body Problem by Liu Cixin, the first book in his Remembrance of Earth’s Past trilogy. After all, it debuted in March 2024. But it was an exceptionally well-done, thematically rich series, and we’re finally getting the second of a planned three seasons in January. Netflix just dropped the first teaser.

Created by David Benioff, D.B. Weiss (Game of Thrones), and Alexander Woo (True Blood), the first season followed two primary timelines. In the earlier one, a young Chinese astrophysicist, Ye Wenjie (Rosalind Chao), witnessed her father being beaten to death by Red Guards during the Cultural Revolution. Ye figured out a means of sending an interstellar message to possible extraterrestrial civilizations, receiving a response from a planet called Trisolaris.

The planet had three suns, which wreaked havoc on Trisolaris via unpredictable “chaotic periods”—hence the novel’s title, which refers to a classic problem in celestial mechanics. Despite being warned that the aliens intended to invade and conquer Earth, Ye responded to the message and invited them to do so, bitter and disillusioned by the state of the world. She eventually became an established professor (Zine Tseng played the older version).

In the present day, the consequences of Ye’s fateful decision began to play out, beginning with a series of mysterious suicides by scientists, many of whom had possessed a highly advanced VR gaming headset. Among the dead: a particle physicist named Vera Ye (daughter of Ye Wenjie). Five of her former students (known as the Oxford Five) joined forces to figure out what was going on, eventually finding themselves caught in a global fight to save Earth from the invading Trisolarians (or San-Ti). Another human cult faction was just as intent on enabling the San-Ti invasion, believing they would be spared as a reward, while Ye Wenjie still believed the entire human race deserved to be annihilated.

The second season will adapt the second novel in the series, The Dark Forest, with the third book, Death’s End, serving as source material for the third season. Per the official S2 synopsis: “As the alien invasion nears, humanity prepares to meet them—on Earth and beyond. While a space fleet embarks on a mission that spans centuries, a secret locked inside of one man’s mind might be humanity’s only hope for survival.”

We lost two members of the Oxford Five in S1: Jack Rooney (John Bradley), killed by cult member Tatiana (Marlo Kelly); and physics teacher Will Downing (Alex Sharp), who was dying of cancer and chose to euthanize himself and donate his cryogenically frozen brain to a bold effort to stave off the invasion. (While the probe carrying Will’s brain veered off-course in the season finale, the original plan was for the San-Ti to use the brain to reconstruct his body, so some version of Will could show up again.)

Kelly’s Tatiana is returning for S2, along with surviving Oxford Five members Jin Cheng (Jess Hong), Saul Durand (Jovan Adepo), and Auggie Salazar (Eiza Gonzalez), whose pioneering nanofibers were memorably used in S1 to slice through a ship in the Panama Canal, killing all the pro-alien cult members on board in gruesome fashion. Also returning are Liam Cunningham as Thomas Wade; Benedict Wong as MI6 agent Clarence “Da” Shi; Sea Shimooka as Sophon, an avatar in the show’s mysterious VR game; and Saamer Usmani as Jin’s Royal Navy officer boyfriend Prithviraj Varma.

New cast members include Claudia Doumit as Captain Van Rijn and Ellie de Lange as Ayla. In addition, Alfie Allen, David Yip, and Jordan Sunshine will appear in as-yet-undisclosed guest roles.

The second season of 3 Body Problem drops on Netflix on January 14, 2027.
