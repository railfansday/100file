8 Smartest Characters In The Lord Of The Rings Movies, Ranked By Intelligence

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

In The Lord of the Rings, J.R.R. Tolkien presents what we would call "intelligence" in quite a nuanced way. It's important to realize Tolkien was no fan of the modern idea of "intelligence." As he wrote in one letter, "I have never yet heard of an 'intelligence' test that was not specially suited to select nitwits (bright perky little nitwits possibly, but not always even so). But what would you: they are invented by nitwits (or some by impostors)."

Tolkien prized carefulness of thought, not quick-thinking. He believed in the value of knowledge, but that knowledge had to be digested and interpreted. In truth, I don't think Tolkien actually believed in the pursuit of intelligence at all; rather, as a Catholic, he honored something that his faith would probably call "wisdom." All that makes it fascinating to look at The Lord of the Rings through the lens of intelligence, though, because it helps us to see the world as Tolkien did—and to learn from him.

Grima Wormtongue is one of Tolkien's most underestimated characters, in large part because Peter Jackson's movies diminish Wormtongue quite significantly. Jackson presents Wormtongue more as a channel of Saruman's power than a mastermind in his own right. When Gandalf frees Théoden from Wormtongue's influence, there's a magical backlash that knocks Saruman off his feet. But that idea is entirely absent in the books themselves.

In the movies, you see hints of Wormtongue's true nature. He's ruthless, cunning, a manipulative mastermind who can figure out any way to spin a crisis to his advantage. Grima Wormtongue is the consummate modern politician, the spin doctor if you like. It's not a coincidence that Tolkien portrays him as the villain whose intelligence—deployed so selfishly—almost brings down an entire kingdom. With Wormtongue, we immediately see that intelligence is a very dangerous thing if you're lacking in character.

It's easy to criticize Denethor. A ruler of Gondor, he is slow to respond to Gandalf's appeals, simply because his intelligence is stronger than the strength of his convictions. He's right in his estimation of Mordor's forces, and he correctly understands the strategic importance of Minas Tirith. He's something of a political mastermind in his own right, understanding the relationships between different countries. I like to think of the Bismarck of Lord of the Rings.

But again, notice Denethor's weaknesses. It's possible he was a little too intelligent; he believes he could filter the facts, and thus discounted the possibility he was being manipulated. Emotions increasingly compromised his judgment—and, again, he's unaware of it. Tolkien's characters are moral ones, and Denethor is a warning to the world that intelligence without morality and conviction can and will lead you astray. Especially if others are there to manipulate you.

It would be so easy to miss Smaug on this list. We tend to focus on Smaug as one of The Lord of the Rings' most powerful villains, but the Great Dragon displays a great deal of what we would call intelligence. He's surprisingly strong when it comes to understanding and interpreting others, as seen in the riddles he sets. Those demonstrate a remarkable depth of strategic thinking, deductive reasoning, and perception.

Tolkien reveals Smaug's intelligence in so many subtle ways. His manner of speaking is more complex than other characters, showing an intuitive grasp of metaphor and demonstrating quite sophisticated rhetorical techniques. He swiftly identifies Bilbo's psychological weaknesses, manipulating them with skill. But again, note that this interpersonal intelligence is flawed because of Smaug's character. The Great Dragon isn't necessarily a warning against the excess of power, but rather of trickery and cunning.

As physically powerful as the elves may be in The Lord of the Rings, they are also notable for their intelligence and wisdom (although the latter is often presented as flawed, compromised by excessive nostalgia). Elrond is thousands of years old, and he's been part of so many critical events on Middle-earth; you can get a sense of how important he is in The Rings of Power, where a younger Elrond is a key character in a story loosely based on Tolkien's notes.

Importantly, though, Elrond's historical knowledge informs his analytical reasoning. He's able to place the present danger in its historical context, and understand both the risks and the best strategies. Remarkably, he is willing to listen to different perspectives; he even sets aside the traditional conflict between Dwarves and Elves, allowing every member of the Fellowship a voice. But again, note that he's a little too conservative, a little too cautious. He has to be heeded, but sometimes overruled.

I suspect some readers will disagree with my placing Galadriel above Elrond. But the comparison between the two is striking; like Elrond, she has accumulated a vast amount of knowledge and experience, beyond anything mortals can ever imagine. What matters, though, is that Galadriel demonstrates what we would now call emotional intelligence. She sees into the souls of others, understanding their characters and motivations.

She is also strikingly humble. Notice Galadriel's reaction when Frodo offers her the One Ring; she knows exactly what it would do to her, and she wants nothing to do with it. Galadriel's perception is relatively untouched by ego, meaning she moves beyond mere intelligence. She's the first character on this list to truly demonstrate wisdom.

In pure intellectual ability, Saruman is almost unparalleled in The Lord of the Rings. It helps that, like the Elves, he draws on a vast reservoir of experience; but his experience is even less limited than theirs because wizards are essentially angels in Tolkien's mythology. Saruman is a scholar and a gifted researcher, an academic who asks the questions no one else dares ask. He notices the sweeping patterns of history, the decline of magic, and the potential of industrialization.

But, of course, Saruman is limited because of his own ego. He sees patterns, and believes he can master them. He looks on others with disdain, viewing them as a means to the end he seeks to accomplish. What's more, because the pattern of progress is visible, he believes he should master it and determine it. Saruman's intelligence is second to his ambition, demonstrating once again that character comes first in The Lord of the Rings.

Think of Gandalf and Saruman as two sides of the same coin. Gandalf's natural intelligence is roughly equal to Saruman's, who perhaps has the edge on him in terms of overall analytical ability. But with Gandalf, intelligence is tempered both by conviction and humility. Gandalf recognizes the patterns of history, but refuses to ally with things he believes are wrong. His morality gives him a different starting point, if you like, meaning the outcome is very different.

Time and again, Gandalf demonstrates that his intelligence is subordinate to his humility. According to Tolkien, Gandalf was always more powerful than Saruman; he just didn't desire fear or reverence, and thus never capitalized on it. The Lord of the Rings carefully tests Gandalf's humility. By passing these tests, ultimately setting aside the egotistical desire to control and risking everything by sacrificing his own life, Gandalf proved worthy to become the White. Gandalf's story is the triumph of character over intelligence.

And that, of course, brings me to the final entry in this list: Sauron himself, easily the most intelligent being on Middle-earth, and a cautionary tale from Tolkien's perspective. The Rings of Power has helped demonstrate this, revealing the range of Sauron's accomplishments; he is a craftsman and artisan as well as a military strategist, a would-be creator whose intelligence is turned towards refashioning the entire world into his own image.

If Gandalf's story is the triumph of character over intelligence, then Sauron's evil is the inverse. He is far more intelligent than anyone else in this story, a skilled strategist who is capable of weaponizing the most sophisticated abstract principles and turning them to his own advantage. But always to his own advantage, because Sauron is an egotist at heart. He failed the tests Gandalf passed, and became the greatest monster of The Lord of the Rings.

5 Thanksgiving Hosting Tips That’ll Make Your Dinner Parties Feel More Special Any Time of Year

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Experts say the secret is using what you love—and skipping what you don’t.

For many households, Thanksgiving is the main hosting event they look forward to all year. But what is it about this holiday that makes it such an annual highlight, and how can you emulate that same essence more than just once a year?

We reached out to entertaining experts to learn which Thanksgiving hosting tips you can apply to year-round gatherings. These are the lessons that will make even your most laid-back get-togethers all the more memorable.

According to Whitney Ray, owner of Wyeth Ray Interiors, one reason that hosting Thanksgiving feels so special is that it's the one time of year most hosts actually break out their nice tableware. However, she believes you should be using these beautiful and sentimental pieces more often.

"So many people wait to use their best serving and dinnerware pieces at holidays like Thanksgiving, but I say those beautiful dishes should be used all year long," she says. "A Tuesday night dinner with a few friends doesn’t need to be formal, but mixing your nice china with your everyday pieces elevates a normal night into something more."

Hovik Harutyunyan is an event designer, etiquette specialist, and the founder of Harutyunyan Events. He believes that one key logistical trick a host can apply to any dinner or party they host is curating a menu full of familiar foods that are easy to prepare ahead.

"Thanksgiving menus work so well because the recipes are familiar as well as tried-and-true," he says. "For example, let's talk about the pies. Thanksgiving dessert is so easy because most of us opt for pumpkin and pecan pie. We know the dessert, and it's extremely easy to make in advance."

He also notes that it's best not to try out complicated, brand-new recipes for a big hosting event. You should opt for an elevated menu that is easy to scale and distribute across a large number of guests.

Thanksgiving is one meal where guests love contributing by bringing a side dish or dessert. While not every gathering needs to have a potluck element, there are other ways you can lighten your cooking load.

Christina Samatas and Renee DiSanto are principals of Park & Oak, and DiSanto is also the owner of Marcel’s Culinary Experience, a culinary community and cooking school. Both of them fully endorse taking a few shortcuts when it comes to hosting any big dinner.

"Not every detail needs to be homemade to feel special," DiSanto says. "There are many ways to save you a few steps, such as using pre-cut veggies or incorporating a few prepared appetizers or sides. Spend more time on the details that matter the most to you, rather than the little things."

For any event you hold, it's pretty much inevitable that guests always end up in the kitchen. "Whether you invite guests in or not, Thanksgiving shows us the kitchen is where people naturally congregate before a meal, whether they are wanting to help or simply catch up before dinner is served," Ray says.

For this reason, she says you should always plan to entertain in the kitchen at any gathering. Consider adding small bites and a drinks station on your kitchen counter or island, and try to clean as you go while making the meal.

Thanksgiving is a celebration of gratitude, and Harutyunyan believes you should apply the same spirit to any party. Anytime you gather with loved ones is an opportunity to give them a special experience.

"Thanksgiving, and the holidays in general, is one of the most magical times of the year," he says. "As stressful and chaotic as they may be, we love to gather with our loved ones to enjoy each other's company. We share stories, memories, and gratitude. The holiday spirit takes over all of us, which is what drives us to create such fabulous dinner spreads, decorate the home, and create an intentional experience for those on the guest list."

Emirati curator Hoor Al Qasimi to lead Venice Biennale in 2028

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Hoor Al Qasimi, one of the preeminent figures of contemporary art in the Middle East, has been named curator of the Venice Biennale's next art exhibition, the Biennale has announced.

Following this year's edition, which was curated by the late Cameroon-born curator Koyo Kouoh, the appointment of Al-Qasimi, from the United Arab Emirates (UAE), marks the second consecutive edition in which a woman from outside the Western art world has been chosen for the role.

Al Qasimi was born into the royal family of the port city of Sharjah and is the daughter of Sultan bin Muhammad Al Qasimi, the current leader of the emirate of Sharjah.

She has been involved in the Sharjah Biennial since 2003, when she curated the exhibition's sixth edition after completing her undergraduate studies at the Slade School of Fine Art in London. In 2009, she founded the Sharjah Art Foundation, a public art organisation that promotes and funds contemporary art collaborations in the emirate and internationally, including organising the Sharjah Biennial.

She holds board positions for numerous other arts organisations, including the International Biennial Association, Sharjah Museums Authority, Sharjah Architecture Triennial and University of the Arts Sharjah. She has also curated major exhibitions including the Lahore Biennale in 2020, the Aichi Triennale in Japan in 2025, and this year's edition of the Biennale of Sydney.

Pietrangelo Buttafuoco, president of the Venice Biennale, says in a statement that Al Qasimi's work gives “voice to stories and sensibilities shaped by research and the unexpected”.

He adds: “Her work brings together a profound attentiveness to deep-rooted memory and its legacies, an open perspective on the intricate complexities of the present, and a rare ability to listen to places and their people...These are qualities that we feel are particularly close to La Biennale’s vision”.

Buttafuoco, a right-leaning journalist and intellectual who was born and raised in Catania in Sicily, converted to Islam in 2015, adopting the name Giafar al-Siqilli (“Jafar the Sicilian”) in a reference to the Arab-Sicilian general associated with the Fatimid conquest of North Africa.

Since becoming president of the Biennale in 2024, he has sometimes championed art from the Middle East. Under his watch, Qatar opened a national pavilion at the Biennale last year, making it the first permanent addition to the Giardini, one of the Biennale's two main venues, in thirty years.

In a statement, Al Qasimi says that the Biennale “continues to be a critical space to engage with the work of influential artists and curators”, adding that she was “honoured” to be appointed as curator.

Al Qasimi could not immediately be reached for comment.

Lisa Marei Schmidt appointed to lead Kunstmuseum Bern and Zentrum Paul Klee

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Lisa Marei Schmidt has worked at other institutions including Kunstsammlung Nordrhein-Westfalen in Düsseldorf and Berlin's Hamburger Bahnhof

Photo David von Becker/Kunstmuseum Bern – Zentrum Paul Klee

Lisa Marei Schmidt has been appointed as the new director of two major museums in Bern: Kunstmuseum Bern and Zentrum Paul Klee. The board of trustees of the foundation that oversees both institutions selected Schmidt from 97 applicants following an international search, according to a release. She will lead on artistic direction and also chair the museums' management board.

Schmidt has directed the Brücke-Museum in Berlin, home to the world’s largest collection of works by artists from the German Expressionist group Die Brücke, since 2017. During that time she has co-curated exhibitions exploring complex and sometimes dark aspects of the history of German Expressionism, such as Escape into Art? The Brücke Painters in the Nazi Period (2019) and Whose Expression? The Brücke Artists and Colonialism (2021–22). Under her direction, the museum has also strengthened its work in provenance research, restoration, art education and digitisation. The German Association of Art Critics (AICA) named the Brücke-Museum as its museum of the year for 2023.

Born in 1978, Schmidt studied art history in Marburg, Amsterdam and Berlin, and curating contemporary art at the Royal College of Art in London. Before joining the Brücke-Museum, she worked as a research trainee at Kunstsammlung Nordrhein-Westfalen and served as a curator at Berlin's Hamburger Bahnhof from 2012 to 2017. In 2020, she curated Bonzo's Dream by Swiss artist Vivian Suter, which received AICA's Special Exhibition award.

“To me, museums are places where art and people meet,” Schmidt said in a statement. "They provide space for encounters, new perspectives and dialogue across society." She said she looked forward to getting to know the teams in Bern and building on the distinctive qualities of the two museums, which have shared strategic leadership under the umbrella foundation Kunstmuseum Bern – Zentrum Paul Klee since 2015.

Schmidt will also inherit a pressing challenge at Kunstmuseum Bern. In June, voters in the canton rejected a CHF15.7m (£14.4m) planning credit, or budget allocation, for a project to renovate the museum's historic building and replace its 1983 extension, with 51.8% voting against it. The museum says its ageing buildings still urgently need repairs.

Schmidt succeeds Nina Zimmer, who led the institutions for a decade and will become director-general of Vienna's Belvedere in January 2027. The foundation’s commercial director Thomas Soraperra will serve as interim director until Schmidt arrives.

Am I Bringing My Partner to Too Many Things?

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Should you really have a permanent plus-one?

Ah, the holiday season: A time of good food, holiday decorations, friends and family, and—if you’re in a relationship—adding a plus-one to all of your holiday party invitations.

This year is the first time in a long time that I have a serious partner to bring along to “friendsgivings,” white elephant parties, and New Year’s Eve festivities. And while I’m so excited to bring the person I love around the other people I love (and be able to take cute holiday pictures), I’m a little weary of being that person that has to have their significant other by their side at all times.

Of course, the holiday season is unique: the idea is to bring everyone together and to get to have new experiences with my partner. But by adding my boyfriend as a plus-one to all these events, am I setting a precedent that will translate into the rest of the new year?

“Just because you're in a relationship doesn't mean you're automatically a package deal everywhere you go,” professional matchmaker and dating expert Jill Dunn tells Cosmopolitan.

So how do I find the right balance between including my partner in experiences I want us to have with each other... and maintaining my own independence when it comes to spending time with my friends?

I need to preface this by saying I love hanging out with my boyfriend—I wouldn’t be dating him if I didn’t. That being said, I also love hanging out with my friends. And while my partner gets along with all of my friends, I don’t think that means he automatically gets an invite whenever there’s an event or gathering.

Dunn agrees and says that it’s important to read the room—it really just depends on the situation.

“If your girlfriend invites you out with a few friends, that doesn't necessarily mean your boyfriend should be tagging along,” she says. “But if it's a big birthday celebration or a couples' dinner, that's a different story.”

And while you may fall into the trap of being afraid of being too intertwined with your partner, it’s okay to want to include them in things. “There's a difference between having your own life and keeping someone separate from your life,” Dunn says.

She explains that if you’ve been with your partner for a year, for example, and they have never met any close friends, hasn’t been invited to family celebration, and is consistently left out of important events, you may be sending the signal that they’re not actually a part of your life. And a partner should be part of your life.

When you’re in a relationship, you essentially are gradually becoming part of each other’s lives. And even though you want to have your own separate friends and relationships (which is completely healthy, by the way!) it shouldn’t feel like they’re part of totally different universes.

“You can have amazing chemistry, incredible dates, and a wonderful time together, but if you're not actually integrating into each other's worlds, what are you building toward?” Dunn asks. “You want to feel like you're part of someone's life, not just part of their weekend plans.”

Weddings, holiday parties, or situations where other people’s partners will be present are the perfect time to bring your partner along (if you’ve been given a plus-one, of course).

Now, integrating your partner into your life in a real way doesn’t mean you two have to be attached at the hip no matter the circumstances.

Listen, I get it. Love is so much fun. And when you’re really into someone, you want to spend as much time as possible with them. But that relationship shouldn’t overtake every other aspect of your life.

“I don't think it's healthy for your entire world to revolve around one person,” Dunn says.

If you’re seeing some friends after some time apart, going to an event where no other S.O. is present, or just need a GNO, it is totally fine (and preferable) to let your partner know that you’re going to just be with your friends. In fact, your partner should be hanging out with their friends too! This is especially important to maintain the friendships you already have.

“The dynamic changes when someone's significant other is always there,” Dunn says. “Maybe your girlfriend wants to talk about something personal. Maybe the group wants to reminisce or just have a girls' night. It's not that they don't like your partner. They just want time with you!”

The reason we even have other friendships and relationships is because we need to be able to water different parts of ourselves. And more than anything, it actually benefits your relationship to have a life outside of each other. You know how they say, “distance makes your heart grow fonder”? Well, they’re not wrong. By spending some time apart and living your own life, you become a more interesting, independent person.

“You don't have to share every single experience,” Dunn says. “Sometimes it's nice to go out, have your own conversations, enjoy your friends, and come home with something new to talk about.”

Archaeologists in Cerveteri, Italy, uncovered Etruscan tombs with stone thrones, red-painted walls and a decorated jar overlooked by looters.

Emergency work at the Monte Abatone necropolis in Cerveteri, the Etruscan city of Caere, has turned up stone thrones, walls painted red, and a nearly intact decorated jar.

The work was carried out by Italy’s heritage authority for the province of Viterbo and southern Etruria, the Soprintendenza Archeologia, Belle Arti e Paesaggio. Some of the tombs had collapsed or become unstable.

Clandestine diggers had entered these tombs before the archaeologists arrived. They left the jar behind.

The collapses and instability meant the tombs had to be investigated, documented and secured. The team cleared the entrances and, as a precaution, removed the fill that had built up inside.

The chambers were dug into tuff, a soft volcanic rock. Excavators have also found places on the hill where blocks of tuff were quarried in several stages.

During a 2019 excavation, a team including the University of Bonn documented a Monte Abatone tomb whose ceiling had partly fallen in along a crack.

The researchers linked such cracks to movements of the tuff layer over a less stable layer of clay. The Soprintendenza has not said what caused the latest collapses, or when they occurred.

One of the chambers still keeps much of its original vaulted ceiling.

In another Etruscan chamber tomb, the funerary furniture was carved directly out of the tuff, including thrones.

A third chamber has extensive red painted decoration on its walls.

Similar furnishings are known from earlier work at Monte Abatone. In 2019, years before the emergency repairs, excavators recorded kline-type death beds, shaped like couches, and narrower benches lining the walls of another chamber.

Cerveteri’s larger Banditaccia necropolis and the Monterozzi necropolis at Tarquinia together form a UNESCO World Heritage property. At Banditaccia, thousands of tombs are laid out in a city-like plan, with streets and small squares.

The most complete find is an olla stamnoide, a jar shaped like a stamnos. It is almost intact. Its surface carries incised figures of animals, with some details picked out in red paint.

The jar will be cleaned and restored, then put on public display. No date or venue for the display has been announced.

The earlier clandestine digging compromised the original context of the burials. Some materials survived it and were recovered.

Looters have missed objects at Monte Abatone before. In the 2019 excavation, Tomb 642 showed signs of their digging, yet it still held a large pithos, a bucchero drinking set, Ionian bowls and Corinthian ointment jars.

Monte Abatone lies immediately south of the ancient city. It flourished in the seventh and sixth centuries BCE, according to a 2022 report on Monte Abatone.

In the 1950s and 1960s, surveys and excavations explored 641 tombs there. That work was carried out on behalf of the heritage authority in response to heavy looting.

Burial types at the site include simple trench graves, chamber tombs and tombs under burial mounds, among them the Campana Tumulus.

That great mound dates to the middle of the Orientalizing period and had not been surveyed since the first half of the 19th century.

Older finds show that burials there continued from the late eighth century BCE into the Hellenistic period and the early Roman Empire.

Researchers also examined the area west and north of the Campana Tumulus. It held tombs of the sixth to third centuries BCE, excavated in the past and partly backfilled, and funerary terraces facing the city.

A research project on Monte Abatone began in the early 1990s. After a new project was set up in 2017, a group of Italian and German universities ran excavation campaigns at the site from 2018 to 2021.

Ground-penetrating radar was used across an area of about 7.4 acres (3 hectares) east of the Campana Tumulus. An earlier geophysical test had revealed a densely packed cluster of tombs.

Excavations on the south side also uncovered what the team describes as a “possible ‘family’ cluster of tombs,” used from the early Orientalizing period into the Archaic period.

“This suggests the existence of a planning by some form of ‘urban’ authority,” the authors of the 2022 report wrote about the rows of mounds.

Laser scanners recorded the site in fine detail, producing a combined model of about 4.3 billion points, roughly 1.2 billion of them for the Campana Tumulus alone. A drone survey added a photogrammetric model of the plateau.

the search for a home without a fixed address, from prefabricated houses to floating dwellings

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

from postwar demountable houses to floating neighborhoods, architects have repeatedly questioned the assumption that buildings must remain tied to one site. the answers reveal different ideas about permanence, ownership, and the future of domestic space.

For decades, architects have imagined homes that can be packed up, transported, and assembled somewhere new, designing alternatives to the idea that housing must be tied to a permanent address. From prefabricated dwellings designed for mass production to experimental houses conceived for remote landscapes, these projects explore how architecture might exist beyond the limits of a fixed site.

One early expression of this ambition came from American architect and inventor Buckminster Fuller. In 1928, he developed the concept for the Dymaxion House, a prefabricated dwelling conceived for mass production and delivery as a single unit by aircraft. Its lightweight aluminum structure reflected Fuller’s interest in applying industrial manufacturing to housing. Although the project never developed into a commercially viable housing system, it proposed a home manufactured away from its eventual site and transported there for assembly.

The idea took a different form in the late 1960s with Finnish architect Matti Suuronen’s Futuro House. Designed as a portable ski cabin, the fiberglass dwelling could be dismantled and transported to remote locations, including by helicopter. Its rounded silhouette and synthetic materials reflected the period’s fascination with space exploration and technological progress.

Mobility, however, does not necessarily mean moving an entire building. It can also involve designing structures that can be taken apart, reassembled, extended, or adapted as needs change. This distinction became particularly important in the work of French architect and designer Jean Prouvé, who explored industrialized construction as a way to make buildings faster and easier to assemble. In the final years of the Second World War, Prouvé developed demountable houses for people whose homes had been destroyed in eastern France. His 8×8 house used a structural system based on a central steel portal frame, with components designed for assembly and disassembly. The project responded to an urgent need for housing, but it also demonstrated how prefabrication could make construction more flexible.

By the 1960s, the question of impermanence had expanded from individual dwellings to the scale of the city. British architectural group Archigram imagined urban environments in which buildings and infrastructure could be continually replaced rather than treated as finished, unchanging compositions. Peter Cook’s 1964 Plug-In City proposed a large structural framework into which housing, offices, shops, and other units could be inserted or removed according to changing needs.

Mobile and temporary architecture is not always driven by a desire for a different lifestyle. In many cases, it responds to an immediate need for shelter. Following the 1994 Rwandan genocide, Japanese architect Shigeru Ban began developing paper-tube structures for refugee accommodation, experimenting with inexpensive, readily available materials that could be used to build temporary homes.

The shelters were designed to be assembled with simple construction methods, making them suitable for situations where building resources were limited. Ban’s work brought architectural thinking into a humanitarian context, showing that even temporary structures require careful consideration of how people live, rather than simply where they sleep.

Another reason to reconsider fixed construction is the instability of the environments in which buildings are placed. Rising water levels, flooding, and pressure on urban land have encouraged architects and communities to explore ways of living on or alongside water. Floating architecture changes the relationship between a structure and its site, allowing the building to respond to water. In Amsterdam, Schoonschip offers an example at the scale of a neighborhood. Developed by its residents with urban planning support from architecture studio Space&Matter, the floating community comprises 30 water plots and 46 homes. Shared infrastructure connects the dwellings, while solar panels, batteries, and a smart grid allow residents to generate and exchange electricity.

Other projects approach the same question at a smaller scale. NOA’s DeKruif House, for example, is a prefabricated timber residence conceived for a family that does not want to commit to one location. Designed to adapt to different waterfront settings, it considers how a house might respond to changing views, orientation, and surroundings. David Rule’s TinyTanic takes mobility onto the water itself, compressing basic domestic functions into a miniature houseboat designed for short journeys. While their scale and ambitions differ, both projects treat the relationship between home and location as something that can be reconsidered.

It is easy to see the appeal of a house that can move. It opens up the possibility of taking a home somewhere new, adapting it to a different setting, or using it for a limited period of time. But moving a building does not automatically make it cheaper, greener, or easier to live in. It still needs materials, energy, water, electricity, and waste systems. A house may be able to travel, but it cannot escape the practical needs of everyday life. These projects also raise a question about why homes are expected to stay in one place in the first place.

From prefabricated houses and temporary shelters to floating neighborhoods, architects have explored different ways of building for changing needs and surroundings. Not every structure needs to be mobile, but more buildings could be designed to adapt, come apart, or be used again rather than remain fixed forever. The Futuro House is a good example of how this idea can come full circle. Designed to be transported and assembled in different locations, it is now a rare piece of architecture that people want to preserve. A house once imagined for life on the move has become something that must stay put. Perhaps the question is not whether we will all live in houses that travel, but whether our homes can change along with the way we live and the places we inhabit.

Before I became a private investigator, I was a sex and dating columnist for The Independent in London. I spent years documenting my romantic adventures, writing about everything from casual sex to heartbreak. I've always been fascinated by the similarities between dating and detective work.

Someone tells you they're looking for a serious relationship. Do their actions support that statement? Do they make plans? Follow through? Introduce you to people in their lives? I believe in intuition, but I don’t believe that it’s supernatural magic. Sometimes it's simply our brains recognizing patterns before we've consciously identified them.

At that first dinner with John Meehan, I couldn't immediately explain why I felt uncomfortable. But as the evening continued, I used my investigative training to spot the inconsistencies: his evasiveness, his elaborate claims, the mismatch between the person he presented and the details of his life. The important part wasn't that I had a bad feeling, it was that I paid attention to the feeling and then looked for facts.

I knew perfectly well that personal chemistry could interfere with my judgment but found myself getting involved anyway.

For years, I thought being a good investigator meant finding answers to every question. Now I think one of the most important skills—both in detective work and in dating—is knowing when you have enough information to make a decision. And when it's time to stop investigating and get on with your life.

How brewers can protect beers from smoke-tainted hops

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Study: Brewers can still use up to 50% of smoke-tainted hops without affecting beer taste and aroma.

The 2020 Oregon wildfires burned over 1 million acres, destroyed thousands of structures, and killed 11 people. Smoke from the fires spread across the entire region; this “smoke taint” adversely affected not only local wine production, but also hops grown for beer brewing. Since the Pacific Northwest produces more than 95 percent of US hops and about 40 percent of the global supply, the potential economic impact is significant.

That’s why Oregon State University (OSU) scientists have been studying how brewers could offset the negative impacts of smoke taint while still incorporating affected hops in their brews. Most recently, they found that it all depends on how much tainted hops are included and when hops are added during the brewing process, according to a new paper published in the Journal of the American Society of Brewing Chemists.

As previously reported, the chemistry of brewing beer is a very active area of research. For instance, in 2018, OSU chemist Thomas Shellhammer—who also co-authored this latest paper—identified the growing popularity of “dry hopping” (adding hops after fermentation, common for pale ales) among craft brewers as the likely culprit of the exploding beer bottle phenomenon.

All beer contains hops, a key flavoring agent that also imparts useful antimicrobial properties. (Beer spoils quickly without them.) Brewers mash and steep grain in hot water, which converts all that starch into sugars. This is traditionally the stage where hops are added to the liquid extract (wort) and boiled to give the beer that hint of bitterness. During the boiling process, a portion of the resins (alpha acids) in hops isomerize into iso-alpha acids. That chemical rearrangement of the molecules is what produces bitterness. Yeast is then added to trigger fermentation, turning the sugars into alcohol.

Far from being the chemically inert ingredient brewers have long assumed, hops contain starch enzymes that, under certain conditions, can boost their activity to such a great extent that excessive re-fermentation occurs (“hop-creep”) after bottling. Dry-hopped beers produce significantly higher CO2 levels and packaging pressures, because there is much more re-fermentation going on than usual. And sometimes that pressure builds until the bottle explodes.

In the wake of the 2020 wildfires, Shellhammer turned his attention to smoke-tainted hops. The primary culprit is volatile phenols (VP), which are produced when the lignin in plant cells is exposed to the high heat of a wildfire. Earlier this year, the OSU team discovered that smoke exposure while the hops are drying increased VP uptake and substantially altered the hops’ aroma; those with higher levels took on meaty, smoky, and artificial BBQ notes; medicinal flavors have also been reported. Those notes were so strong that they overpowered more desirable beery qualities, like citrus notes or aroma intensity.

For this latest project, Shellhammer worked with graduate student Cade Jobe to harvest Idaho-grown Cascade hops. Then they “smoked” the hops in kilns using wood pellets injected into the air intake systems, then dried them. They also harvested and dried non-smoked hops as a control.

Credit: Ellie Lafferty/Oregon State University Credit: Ellie Lafferty/Oregon State University

Next, Shellhammer and Jobe brewed several batches of beer, blending smoke-tainted hops with untainted hops at different levels (25,  50, and 75 percent), along with beer brewed using only tainted hops and beer brewed with no tainted hops. They did this using both a dry-hopping process and one where they added hops during wort boiling. They measured the VP concentrations of each batch of beer with gas chromatography. Finally, a panel of 24 blind tasters evaluated each beer.

Shellhammer and Jobe found that dry-hopping with smoke-tainted hops resulted in increased VP concentrations; the more tainted hops were included, the higher those concentrations. The tasters reported more intense smoky aromas in those beers as well as meaty, smoky, medicinal, and artificial BBQ flavors.

By contrast, when tainted hops were added during the boil, the resulting beer was pretty much indistinguishable from beers brewed with no smoke-tainted hops, even though the former still showed the same increases in VP concentrations. There was no discernible difference when hops were added during the wort boiling stage.

The authors suggest brewers could blend smoke-tainted hops with untainted batches when dry-hopping, keeping the smoke-tainted hops to less than 50 percent. This would still allow brewers to use tainted batches of hops, reducing the economic impact of wildfires on beer production.

“Hop growers and brewers are looking for guidance on whether and how to use smoke-affected hops,” said Jobe. “This research begins to provide some practical information to help hop growers and brewers understand how smoky hops impact beer so that they can make the best decisions.”

There’s still more research to be done to confirm the precise ratios and to replicate these initial findings. Future research should also explore the impact of hops exposed to smoke under field conditions, when the plant is still metabolically active, along with consumer studies to evaluate whether beer drinkers more generally will find the flavors of beers made with blended hops (tainted and untainted) acceptable. The findings may not extend to wine production, however, since the fermentation processes are so different, per the authors. And grapes comprise almost all the raw materials in wine, while hops are a smaller fraction of beer brewing ingredients.

The US Air Force Bolted Jet Engines Onto Its Largest-Ever Bomber, But It Still Wasn't Enough To Save It

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

The Convair B-36 Peacemaker strategic bomber is one of the most ambitious bombers ever built. While the unusual pusher configuration of its six Pratt & Whitney R-4360 Wasp Major piston engines is perhaps its most distinctive feature, later variants also carried four General Electric J47 turbojets. These jets were added to improve take-off performance, climb rate, and high-speed dash capability, helping keep the enormous bomber operationally relevant.

This remained the case until newer all-jet bombers, particularly the B-52 Stratofortress, assumed the primary nuclear deterrence role. The United States Air Force procured 384 B-36s, each capable of carrying up to 86,000 lb (39,000 kg) of bombs. Although none remain airworthy today, four complete aircraft have been preserved in museums, where they provide a reminder of the largest piston-engined combat aircraft ever placed into operational service.

The Convair B-36 Peacemaker was the largest bomber built by the US, and it can be thought of as the most important transitional bomber ever built. The US Air Force Museum states that "responding to the U.S. Army Air Forces' requirement for a strategic bomber with intercontinental range, Consolidated Vultee (later Convair) designed the B-36 during World War II. The airplane made its maiden flight in August 1946, and in June 1948 the Strategic Air Command received its first operational B-36."

The museum also explains that "some B-36s served as photographic reconnaissance aircraft, and others were adapted to launch and retrieve specially modified RF-84F/K reconnaissance planes." Although conceived during the Second World War as an ultra-long-range conventional bomber, the B-36 entered service as the United States shifted towards a strategy centred on delivering nuclear weapons across intercontinental distances without relying on overseas bases.

This remains a central mission of the world's current strategic bombers (B-52, B-1B, B-2, B-21, Tu-160, and Tu-95MS). The Peacemaker's origins date to 1941, which is before the US even entered the Second World War. There was a fear in the US that the UK could fall to Germany, and if that happened, the US would lack a forward base for its bombers to raid Germany. As such, the requirements for the bomber included that it could fly intercontinentally from North America to Europe.

Designing and developing the bomber took time, and by the time it entered service in 1948, the Second World War had been over for three years. As things had panned out, the US had been able to use Britain against Germany, and had island-hopped to bring its B-29s within range of Japan.

However, the delays in designing the bomber also allowed engineers to update the design to carry the heavy nuclear bombs that weighed around 8,800 to 11,000 lbs (4,000 to 5,000 kg) and even the extremely heavy hydrogen bombs. The first experimental thermonuclear devices weighed tens of tonnes, while the first operational hydrogen bombs introduced in the early 1950s typically weighed around 30,000–42,000 lb (14,000–19,000 kg).

The Peacemaker was designed with the gigantic bomb bay doors needed to carry these bombs that no other aircraft could. When it entered service, it was able to carry around 86,000 lbs (39,000 kg) of payload, vastly more than any other bomber. With the establishment of the Strategic Air Command in 1946, the B-36 became the first true intercontinental nuclear bomber.

The world's geostrategic picture changed in 1949 as the Soviet Union exploded its first atomic bomb. Then, the US pivoted to requiring a bomber able to strike targets deep inside the USSR while using bases in the continental United States and be able to carry heavy first-generation nuclear bombs. This is something the Boeing B-29 Superfortress couldn't do, but it was very similar to what the B-36 was originally designed for.

Germany developed flying prototypes of longer range bombers that would be able to fly over and bomb New York City.

With the benefit of hindsight, the B-36 was always likely to be overtaken by the rapid pace of jet-powered aviation. It was conceived during the Second World War, but was ultimately built for a very different strategic environment. At first (late 1940s to early 1950s), the B-36 was unmatched.

No fighter could reliably intercept the Peacemaker at its high operating altitude (around 40,000 feet or 12,000 meters, later extending to 45,000 feet or 13,700 meters). This was before the USSR developed an integrated air defense network and a time when early Soviet jet fighters struggled to reach and engage such high-flying targets.

The Peacemaker's enormous range also allowed it to approach the USSR from multiple directions, further complicating interceptions. But the B-36 was becoming a relic of a bygone era. It was designed before jet-powered strategic bombers were practical. By the mid-1950s, new Soviet jet interceptors like the MiG-17 and then MiG-19 made it increasingly vulnerable.

This worsened with the development of increasingly capable radar-guided air defense systems and surface-to-air missile technology. The arrival of the all-jet B-52 Stratofortress further hastened the B-36's retirement by offering greater speed, improved survivability, and comparable intercontinental range. Perhaps somewhat similar, the arrival of the B-21 will inevitably increase pressure to retire the older, expensive, and less survivable B-2 Spirits.

By the time it entered service, the B-52 was in development, but was still years away. The Peacemaker was primarily powered by six enormous 28-cylinder Pratt & Whitney R-4360 Wasp Major piston engines arranged in a distinctive pusher configuration. However, these were too slow in the emerging jet age. In an effort to bridge the gap, the US Air Force invested heavily in modernizing the Peacemaker with four General Electric J47 turbojets.

The J47 turbojet engines were mounted in two twin-engine pods beneath the outwings and earned it the famous slogan "six turning and four burning." They were not intended for continuous cruise as they were incredibly fuel inefficient. Instead, they were used for heavy-weight takeoffs, improved climbing, increased dash speed in hostile territory, and reduced time spent over defended targets.

The USAF looked for other ways to improve the performance of the Peacemaker. It introduced the Featherweight modification programs, which removed defensive armament, ammunition, armor, and other equipment to improve altitude, range, and speed. The B-36 was given improved electronics, while the RB-36 strategic reconnaissance variant was also developed.

The Air Force Museum states that "powered by six Pratt & Whitney R-4360 engines, the B-36J cruised at 230 mph [200 knots], but for additional bursts of speed its four General Electric J47s increased the maximum speed to 435 mph [378 knots]." Ultimately, the improvements addressed symptoms, but were unable to fix the underlying problem, which was that the B-36 just didn't belong in the jet age.

Relying on piston engines, the B-36 was too slow, typically cruising at 200 knots (378 knots at maximum speed). The B-47 Stratojet could cruise at 434 knots, and the B-52 at 456 knots. By the mid-1950s, increasingly capable Soviet jet interceptors such as the MiG-17 and MiG-19, together with radar-guided surface-to-air missile systems including the S-25 Berkut and later the S-75 Dvina, steadily eroded the B-36's survivability.

The Peacemaker faced other pressures. For example, the complex push-configuration radial engines frequently overheated, caught fire, and required changing a staggering 336 spark plugs per service. The massive size of the aircraft also demanded immense resources to maintain and operate.

The final B-36 Peacemaker was retired in 1959, just one year before a Soviet S-75 Dvina (SA-2) downed a Lockheed U-2 spy plane at an altitude of around 70,000 feet. The development contributed to the program that would give rise to the SR-71 as well as contributed to the abandoning of the XB-70 Valkyrie bomber.

Today, the Peacemaker is largely forgotten in popular memory. It is overshadowed by the earlier B-29 Superfortress, which entered service too late to make a difference in Germany but in time to play a pivotal role in ending the War in the Pacific, including dropping the nuclear bombs on Japan. The B-36 arrived too late for the Second World War, but it was also ill-prepared for the realities of the Cold War and the jet age.

It was quickly overshadowed by the early Cold War Boeing B-52 Stratofortress that entered service in the mid 1950s and continues in service today. These two successful bombers have had the effect of pushing the Peacemaker out of popular imagination. Here’s How To Make Your Holiday EV Road Trip Easier

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

It’s a short workweek for most of us here in the United States. In just a day or two, many of us will load up our cars and trek around the country to our loved ones. If you’re reading this, there’s a decent chance you’ll be taking on of those trips in an electric vehicle. EVs have come a long way, so in theory, range anxiety shouldn’t be much of an issue for anyone these days. Still, with so many drivers on the roads, there are bound to be annoyances that come with long EV road trips.

Here are a few tips that can take the edge off a long trip.

DC fast charging times are getting better and better, but charging still takes longer than stopping for gas. Thus, bottlenecks can occur on well-traveled routes with limited DC fast charging stops. It’s just kind of the nature of the beast when most people are stopping for 20-30 minutes. Multiply that by the plethora of drivers also trying to charge up and get home, and well, it’s not hard to see why EV charging queues can balloon, adding time to your drive home.

But you can head some of that off if you prep the night before. For those with home charging, that means simply plugging the car in and ensuring it’s fully charged for the next day’s trip. For those who don’t have access to home charging, using a DC fast charger the night before, when everyone isn’t rushing to leave, could ease some of the frustration of charging up before getting on the roads.

Also, some DC fast charging companies give a discount for charging during off-peak hours. Prepping the night before won’t just save you time, it might save you money.

We may have a set route in mind for our trip, and that could be all fine and dandy, but is it really the best route for our cars? Electric vehicles tend to consume more energy at higher speeds and when traveling up hills. Thus, it could be advantageous to maximize range by picking a flatter route, or one with lower speeds.

In most cases this won’t be necessary, but if you’re pushing the limits of your car and the infrastructure around you, it’s good to know. Most route planning apps—like A Better Route Planner, Plugshare, or even Google Maps—take into account which route is friendlier to your car’s range calculator, and may suggest detours if you get a big range boost from a route that’s nearly as quick.

These route planners will tell you exactly when and where to stop, depending on your car’s efficiency, your destination, your desired state of charge at arrival, and road conditions along the way. They can be adjusted to fit your real-world efficiency data or your need for breaks. Rather than just winging, using a route planner ensures you’re never in an uncomfortable situation without the range or charging ability to get where you need to go.

This goes hand in hand with route planning, but I think it is relevant here. The Sunday after Thanksgiving is typically the single busiest travel day of the year. You’re not the only one on the road who will need charging. Prepping the night before can help with the initial charging wait times at the start of your trip. If you’re on a long journey, chances are you’ll find yourself in a big queue at a strange charger.

When you’re planning your route with charging stops, pay attention to what brands they are and what ports they use. An increasing number of highway stops often have several competing charging stations. A Tesla Supercharging station could have an Ionna Rechargery nearby. Or maybe it’s an EV Go station. Or maybe it’s a charging station by Circle K or Shell. In any case, they tend to cluster together near high-power infrastructure. Rather than fumble with apps and payments at the station, I think it’s best if you research the charging companies on your route and pre-emptively install their apps and set up payment.

Similarly, bring an adapter. NACS is quickly making its way across the country, but not every station has NACS ports, and not every one has CCS. Some of the stations with your desired plug will likely be busy. An adapter that allows your car to access both NACS and CCS ports will save a lot of headaches.

Once again, you’re not the only one trying to get home for the holidays. Let’s keep the queue moving, and try not to charge more than 80%, if possible. Charging speeds slow substantially when EV batteries surpass 80%. It may have only taken 15-20 minutes to get to 80%, but it’ll take another 20 or so to reach 100%, or much longer in some cars. Chances are, there’s someone waiting. Not only is it courteous to unplug and move on, but it’s also probably quicker to charge again (if you need it) closer to your destination.

I know this list has mostly been about minimizing time lost due to charging, but I think it’s important to remember that it’s okay to take a break. Charging stops are inherently longer than refuelling a gas car, but I think that 15-30 minute break is helpful for safe driving. Taking a rest, using the restroom, or getting a snack can take the edge off a trip. Use an EV’s characteristics to your advantage.

I’ve been doing EV road trips since 2021, and it’s amazing to see just how far both vehicle range and charging infrastructure have come. Long trips are possible for most drivers these days, whereas years ago, it was a huge hardship or even impossible. With better apps, better infrastructure, and better cars, it’s only gotten easier. But it still pays to know what you’re doing. Drive safely out there, folks.

United Airlines Remembers Your Bad Flight And May Upgrade You Next Time

United Airlines appears to be quietly remembering when passengers suffer through a bad trip and then surprising some of them with a complimentary Economy Plus seat on a future flight. It costs United very little when the seat would otherwise remain empty, but it communicates something valuable to the passenger: we remember what happened to you.

A passenger recently shared an interesting message from United Airlines with aviation insider JonNYC.

The traveler had missed a connection about a month earlier and then received the following message before another United flight:

“We know your previous travel experience may have faced some challenges, and we want to make your journey more comfortable.”

United moved the passenger to Economy Plus, offering additional legroom at no charge.

What makes the gesture especially interesting is that the passenger had no MileagePlus elite status. The upcoming flight was lightly booked, which presumably made it easier for United to offer the seat without giving up revenue.

JonNYC described it as United’s technology performing more “dark magic,” and I think that is a fair description. The system appears to have connected an earlier operational disruption with a future reservation, then identified an inexpensive opportunity to improve the next journey.

Reports of similar gestures stretch back at least to 2025, so this does not appear to be entirely new. One United Silver member reported receiving an Economy Plus assignment after an eight-hour delay, even after United had already provided a $75 travel credit.

But United has not publicly announced this as a formal benefit, and we do not know precisely how passengers are selected (not that I would expect otherwise with such a “Surprise and Delight” program, lest it become an entitlement).

With advancing technology, airlines already know when they have inconvenienced you.

They know when your flight was delayed, when you missed a connection, and when you were rebooked. They also know whether you have another reservation and which seats remain available on that flight.

Yet customers are often forced to explain the entire situation again in a complaint, including information the airline already has in its own systems. That creates more work for the passenger after the airline has already disrupted the trip, though I’m sadly not expecting carriers to offer proactive compensation, even when delays are within its control.

United appears to be using its data more intelligently, like with its ConnectionSaver technology, that can help some passengers without inconveniencing others.

I have written before about United introducing technology that saves callers from repeating information when they are transferred from an automated system to a human agent. I also welcomed the addition of an app feature that watches earlier flights for disrupted passengers while preserving their confirmed reservations.

This Economy Plus gesture reflects a similar approach: use the information already available to reduce friction and improve the passenger experience.

An Economy Plus seat is not a first class upgrade. The passenger receives more legroom, but the onboard service remains the same as regular economy class.

Even so, it has value. United charges for these seats, sometimes quite a bit depending upon the route and seat location. Receiving one unexpectedly can make a future flight noticeably more comfortable.

The economics also make sense for United. If the flight is lightly booked and the Economy Plus seat would otherwise remain empty, the marginal cost of assigning it to an inconvenienced passenger is essentially zero.

The value to the passenger can be much greater than the cost to the airline.

I also appreciate that the message explicitly connects the better seat to the previous experience. A random seat reassignment might go unnoticed, while a clearly explained gesture tells the passenger that United remembers what happened and wants another chance to deliver a better trip.

That is smart service recovery…I wonder if the system is smart enough to do this when passengers have to be moved anyway due to weight and balance reasons? (why let a good opportunity go to waste?)

This is not a published benefit, and passengers should not expect a complimentary Economy Plus assignment every time something goes wrong. We do not know what level of disruption qualifies, how much empty space must be available, or whether United considers the passenger’s fare or travel history…and that’s the point, lest it become an expectation.

And of course United must be careful when selecting a seat to avoid a familiar complaint on Delta Air Lines. Moving someone from a regular economy aisle seat to an Economy Plus middle seat will not strike every passenger as an improvement. Ideally, the system should preserve the passenger’s aisle or window preference whenever possible or offer the upgrade rather than automatically moving the traveler.

Furthermore, if a MileagePlus Premier is expecting an entire row and suddenly has a seatmate, I can understand there will be annoyance (though that’s a sort of entitlement that is not helpful either)…in the past I’ve passed on upgrades to domestic first class in order to stretch across multiple seats in economy class.

Still, those limitations do not diminish the appeal of the idea. This is a discretionary gesture, not something passengers can demand, and that element of surprise is part of what makes it effective.

When airlines discuss service recovery, the conversation often centers on travel credits or miles. Those can be appropriate, particularly after a serious operational failure. But compensation need not be limited to those forms.

A better seat, lounge invitation, meal voucher, or another small benefit can communicate recognition when it is offered thoughtfully. The key is identifying something the passenger will appreciate that the airline can provide at a modest cost.

United has become particularly skilled at using technology to address operational problems. ConnectionSaver is the best example, intelligently holding certain flights when connecting passengers can reach the gate without causing a significant delay.

United appears to be quietly offering complimentary Economy Plus seats to some passengers whose previous trips went poorly, including travelers without elite status.

The gesture will not erase a missed connection or make every passenger forget a miserable journey. But it shows that United remembers the disruption and recognizes an opportunity to do something helpful. An empty Economy Plus seat costs United almost nothing. Used this way, it can generate far more goodwill than another automated apology email…it’s a positive step.

Weapons, war and wavering trust: Germans want to see more restraint in foreign policy

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

According to a study by the Körber Foundation, a growing number of Germans now oppose providing military aid to Ukraine. The majority also believes that Germany should take a neutral position between the US and China.

For decades, German foreign policy was never seen as particularly controversial at home. The country was firmly integrated into NATO and supported EU expansion and cooperation across Europe. It also advocated for multilateral approaches to resolving conflicts around the world. All of this enjoyed broad public support. But all of this has also changed since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine and the wars in the Middle East. Now, Germany's foreign policy has become increasingly contentious and is fueling growing public anxiety.

Long-held assumptions are beginning to crumble, including the belief that the US would ultimately remain firmly on Germany's side. This is one of the central findings of a new survey by the Hamburg-based Körber Foundation.

In the foundation's latest poll on public attitudes on German foreign policy, 65% of respondents said they fear further military conflicts around the world. Meanwhile, 48% believe that Germany should adopt a more restrained approach to international affairs — a five percentage point increase from last year.

Since 2017, the non-profit Körber Foundation, established in the northern German city of Hamburg in 1959 by industrialist Kurt Körber, has conducted the annual surveys titled "the Berlin Pulse." For this year's study, the polling institute Forsa surveyed around 1,500 eligible voters in September.

The findings make one thing clear: The current government's approach under Chancellor Friedrich Merz — seeking to have a say in virtually every international conflict and drawing on Germany's position as the largest EU economy — is increasingly met with skepticism. Only 40% of respondents now favor greater international engagement, down from 48% in 2025.

Florian Bigge, who helped compile the study for the Körber Foundation, commented on the findings on Monday: "This is a significant decline, especially since we have been asking this question for years, with changes in the range of just two to three percentage points."

Meanwhile, 77% of respondents want Germany to focus primarily on diplomacy rather than military action.

The most dramatic shift in public opinion concerns the war in Ukraine. Germany's military support for the Eastern European country, including the delivery of billions of euros' worth of weapons, no longer has the backing of a majority of the population. In September 2026, only 46% of respondents supported continued military assistance, compared to 59% in the previous year's survey. Currently, a narrow majority of 53% want to halt weapons deliveries altogether.

As Jonathan Lehrer of "the Berlin Pulse" explains, opposition is particularly strong among supporters of the far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD).

"Eighty-eight precent of AfD supporters oppose military assistance to Ukraine," Lehrer said. "This is an interesting finding, especially considering political developments in Germany, including state elections in which the AfD gained support by campaigning against aid to Ukraine."

The AfD has recently overtaken the chancellor's conservative Christian Democratic Union (CDU) in nationwide polls.

Support for Ukraine was also the subject of a recent poll conducted by the opinion research institute Infratest Dimap. This study also found that public support for Ukraine is continuing to decline, with a majority now opposed to providing assistance (see graphic).

One finding from the Berlin Pulse study is particularly alarming for politicians and experts committed to transatlantic relations. As the US and China are increasingly seen as rivals on the global stage, 83% of respondents believe that Germany should remain neutral. Only 15% would side with the US.

This may stem from the policies of US President Donald Trump. Florian Bigge of the Körber Foundation said of the widespread preference for neutrality: "This sentiment is strong among Germans across all age groups and political parties. In eastern Germany, support for neutrality is at 92%, compared to 81% in the west. We interpret this as a desire for security but also reluctant commitment: Germans do not want to take sides in this conflict between the great powers."

The picture becomes clearer when respondents are asked about Russia. Under President Vladimir Putin, 82% of those surveyed see Russia as a military threat. However, public opinion is more divided when Germany responds firmly to such threats, including hybrid attacks. For instance, after the attempted drone attack on Leipzig Airport in early May, Germany responded by closing the Russian consulate in Bonn. However, only 51% support such measures, while 46% favor a more restrained approach.

"The gap between external expectations and domestic support is the central challenge facing German foreign policy," said Sarah Pagung of the Körber Foundation.

One of the world’s rarest fish lives in a single cave in Death Valley National Park, and earthquakes have left it on the verge of extinction

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Devils Hole pupfish live in just one cavern of Death Valley National Park. Earthquakes are rapidly pushing this fish towards extinction.

One of the rarest fish on Earth lives in just one place: a water-filled cavern in the Nevada desert called Devils Hole, part of Death Valley National Park. In the spring of 2025, after two earthquakes shook its tiny home, biologists counted only 38 of them.

The National Park Service announced the new figure in November. Divers and surface counters found the 137 Devils Hole pupfish during a survey on October 10, nearly double the 77 seen in the spring of 2026 and about double the 69 recorded in the fall of 2025.

Devils Hole is a cave about 12 feet wide and more than 500 feet deep. The bottom has never been mapped. The water stays at a constant 92 degrees Fahrenheit (33 degrees Celsius).

Pupfish have been found as deep as 66 feet, but they feed and spawn only on a shallow rock shelf near the surface, where algae grows.

The species has been isolated there for 10,000 to 20,000 years, longer than any other pupfish in the Death Valley system. The region’s caves keep other long records too, like a Nevada cave that reveals 580,000 years of climate swings.

It is the only naturally occurring population of the species, which was listed as endangered in 1967. Biologists from the National Park Service and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service have counted the fish almost every spring and fall since 1972, with scuba divers in the cavern and counters at the surface.

One of the fish’s main threats can come from thousands of miles away. Large earthquakes as far away as Japan, Indonesia and Chile have made the water slosh in Devils Hole like water in a bathtub, according to the park.

Waves can splash as high as two meters (about 6.5 feet) up the walls and sweep the shelf clean. The park calls it a tsunami in the desert, although it is tiny next to the ocean tsunamis that big earthquakes can unleash.

That is what happened in the winter of 2024 to 2025. Two earthquakes, one in December and one in February, set off waves that moved algae, organic material and fish eggs off the shelf.

The spring count in 2025 found 38 fish. One year earlier, the official spring count had recorded 191.

On July 29, 2025, just 21 minutes after a magnitude 8.8 earthquake struck Russia’s Kamchatka Peninsula, waves rippled through Devils Hole, nearly 4,000 miles away. Those waves were only about 10 inches high and removed just some material from the shelf.

Then, after an earthquake in Panama on October 9, 2026, nearly 3,000 miles away, biologists at first saw signs of stress in the fish. On a later visit, they found them in healthier condition.

The team had prepared for a crash like this for years. Pupfish eggs from Devils Hole had been collected regularly to build a backup population at the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service’s Ash Meadows Fish Conservation Facility.

In 2025, biologists added captive-raised fish to the wild population for the first time. Between March 2025 and February 2026, scientists released 72 of them into Devils Hole. They also gave the fish extra food while the algae grew back.

The fish live an average of only 10 to 14 months. Because of that, biologists believe most of the 137 fish counted this fall hatched and grew up in Devils Hole itself.

“It’s exciting to see Devils Hole pupfish numbers increase so significantly over the past year,” said Death Valley National Park Superintendent Mike Reynolds.

“These results reflect the dedication of our scientists and conservation partners working to protect this extraordinary species. While challenges remain, we’re encouraged by this progress and committed to its long-term survival.”

The pupfish has survived human threats before. In the late 1960s, a farming operation drilled large wells in the Ash Meadows area, and the water level in Devils Hole began to drop toward the shelf the fish need.

Biologists even hung a floating, artificially lit shelf in the cave as a substitute. The fish never used it.

A federal court halted further pumping in 1971, and the fight reached the U.S. Supreme Court. Its 1976 decision in Cappaert v. United States recognized the water right of Devils Hole as part of a national monument and limited pumping so the natural shelf would stay underwater.

Until the mid-1990s, spring surveys counted more than 200 fish. In the early 2000s, spring counts averaged about 90, and in 2013 the number fell to 35. The cause of that long decline has not been determined.

The new count is an encouraging step, but the park says the population naturally fluctuates and remains highly vulnerable.

It calls continued monitoring, research and habitat management essential if one of the world’s rarest fish is to keep its foothold in a single desert cave.

Research Says Cold Muscles Produce Less Power. Here’s How to Protect Your Performance on Frigid Runs.

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Experts share how to prepare, pace, and recover so you can run strong when temperatures fall below freezing.

When temperatures rise, running performance tends to drop. Both the research and anyone who’s put in miles on a hot summer afternoon can confidently tell you that much.

But what about when the mercury moves in the opposite direction? Does colder weather in the winter—ranging from just a bit chilly to downright frigid—take a toll on your running too?

A recent review published in Temperature suggests it does. According to the study’s authors, when our muscles get too cold, neuromuscular function becomes impaired. On the run, this can contribute to stiffness, less power output, and decreased endurance, all of which may cause your winter workouts to feel harder than usual.

The good news? You can still run strong when the temps drop—as long as you make a few key training adjustments before, during, and after you log your miles in the colder months of the year. Here’s how to do just that.

There’s no exact number on the thermometer at which runners can universally expect their power, speed, or endurance to decline, Stephen Cheung, PhD, the lead author of the aforementioned review, tells Runner’s World.

Temperature is just one of the many factors that contribute to how well we perform in the cold, Cheung explains. “It’s a function of the environment, as well as how much heat you’re generating, the speed that you’re moving at, and of course, the amount of clothing that you’re wearing,” he says.

Each of these factors varies significantly from runner to runner and affects how quickly their bodies lose heat. You only need to consider those in your run group who run shirtless when it’s freezing versus those who bundle up when it’s 50 degrees Fahrenheit to understand that we all thermoregulate (maintain our internal body temperature) differently. And the equation only becomes more complex when you account for wind and moisture—both in the environment and what you create yourself from air resistance and sweat.

Heather Milton, CSCS, lead exercise physiologist at Primary MD and cofounder of Master My Run, agrees. “It would be great to be able to say, when the temperature outside reaches [a certain point], your pace slows down by this many seconds per mile—but that’s not how it works,” she says.

Keeping this in mind, Milton says the cleanest reference point for runners to begin paying more attention to how they prepare for runs in the cold is when the temperature drops below freezing. Generally speaking, the colder it gets from there, the more noticeable the negative effects on performance will be, she explains.

Cold weather leads to vasoconstriction, Milton explains, which means our blood vessels constrict more, so the muscles farther away from the core don’t receive as much blood flow. If your fingers or toes have ever gone numb in the cold, for example, you’ve experienced the joy of vasoconstriction. Our bodies prioritize heating our core over our extremities because it helps our vital organs maintain a consistent temperature, which is essential for survival in dangerous conditions.

This physiological response, however, doesn’t bode especially well for running performance.

“The cold slows nerve conduction so you have to work harder for the same power output,” Milton says. “And as temperatures drop further below freezing, that slower nerve conduction and the effects of vasoconstriction are going to be even more noticeable.” As a result, your overall exercise capacity decreases, meaning you may not be able to run for as long or at the same pace you normally would.

What else might you notice when temperatures drop? “Physically, if your muscles and tendons are cold, they’re going to be tighter, less elastic, and you won’t be able to contract them as strongly,” says Cheung. “Stiff muscles can also put you at greater risk for tears or strain injuries.”

Milton says this analogy, used by an old coach of hers, provides an easy way to understand what’s happening: When your muscles and tendons get too cold, they’re like a cold rubber band that can snap when pulled.

If you’re a runner who dislikes the cold, you might wonder if the smarter choice is to just run on the treadmill this winter. Don’t worry: As long as you prepare appropriately and use caution, you can absolutely continue logging regular miles outdoors.

Running well outside in the cold starts with warming up indoors first, says Milton. “An indoor warmup increases your internal metabolism and enhances blood flow, which helps prepare your muscles for contraction,” she explains. This also produces heat so your internal body temperature is more resilient to the cold outdoor environment.

Your winter warmup does not have to be any different or longer than your usual routine, Milton says. But you do want to make sure that you’re getting in a solid five minutes of prerun drills to adequately raise your body temperature and activate your muscles.

She recommends starting with slower, more controlled movements that help activate the core and the glutes, such as planks with leg raises, lunges with rotation, and hip CARs (controlled articular rotations). Finish with quicker drills that require your nerves to fire more rapidly, such as leg swings and butt kicks.

Equally important to warming up is layering up—with an important caveat.

When dressing for winter runs, keep in mind how you personally respond to cold temperatures, says Cheung, because everyone has different tolerances and susceptibilities. “I get very cold in my hands, so I’ll wear really thick gloves, even when I may be wearing relatively little on my torso,” he adds. “Pay attention to what’s an issue for you and adapt your clothing to both the conditions and your own peculiarities.” Cheung emphasizes this because you don’t want to end up sweating too heavily, which can then leave you chilled and clammy.

If you need a starting point for your winter running wardrobe, aim for two to three layers of breathable, moisture-wicking fabrics. If possible, choose a wind- and water-resistant shell for your outermost layer, suggests Milton. For your lower body, wear shorts over your tights to help keep your glutes warm. Finally, cover any exposed skin on your head, ears, and hands, and opt for a pair of wool socks—all of which can help ensure adequate blood distribution to the extremities, Milton adds.

When you head out for runs in the cold, slowly build up to your usual pace.

Gradually easing into your workout gives your muscles more time to adjust, and it can also help your airways get used to the colder temperatures, Milton says. You don’t necessarily need to go slower for your entire run, but you should try to match your rate of perceived exertion (RPE) with your typical effort, rather than aiming for your usual pace or heart-rate zone.

“Think about it the same way as if you were running on an extremely hot day,” Milton says. “Let’s say your RPE for an easy run is about 3 out of 10. Keep that 3 out of 10, even if your pace is a little slower.”

If you find that your run feels more difficult than usual, slow down if you need to, but don’t stop completely, Cheung advises. “Don’t take long breaks, because that’s when you aren’t generating as much heat. Just keep moving so you have that constant heat generation,” he says.

If you start to get cold on a run, try speeding up a little bit to stoke that metabolic heat production, Cheung says. But make sure you do it safely: Don’t force the effort in icy conditions or when your muscles feel especially stiff. Scouting out run routes with more tree coverage to block wind on blustery days can also help you stay warmer, he adds.

As soon as you finish your workout, change out of your sweaty running gear and into warm, dry clothes, Milton says.

Even if you’ve only sweated a small amount—for example, maybe you just have some dampness under your armpits—you still want to remove any wet fabrics against your skin. “Even that small amount of wetness can start to drop your core temperature,” Milton explains. If you wait until you’re near shivering, you’ll face an uphill battle to get yourself warm again.

You can also opt for a relaxing shower and hot chocolate—extra marshmallows.

Distributed LLM Training Across Metros: Streaming DiLoCo in MaxText

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Traditionally, training state-of-the-art Large Language Models (LLMs) relies on a monolithic cluster of accelerators, where all devices are collocated and tightly coupled via high-bandwidth to constantly synchronize gradients. However, physical and cost constraints make scaling a single slice increasingly difficult.

To scale further, we can connect multiple compute slices across geographically distributed datacenters. However, when training across multiple datacenters over wide-area networks (WAN), standard data-parallel training suffers from severe network stalls because slow WAN transfers cannot be fully overlapped with computation.

To address this, the Google DeepMind and Google Research teams developed DiLoCo (Distributed Low-Communication), a distributed optimization algorithm designed to make training resilient to these low-bandwidth, high-latency connections. In this blog post, we discuss the core concepts of DiLoCo, how it is implemented in MaxText (Google's open-source JAX-based LLM training framework), and the performance benefits it brings to large-scale LLM training on Google Cloud.

DiLoCo was introduced by Google DeepMind in the paper DiLoCo: Distributed Low-Communication Training of Language Models. Instead of synchronizing gradients across all devices at every single training step (as in standard Data Parallelism), DiLoCo partitions training across M independent compute groups, referred to as learners, and allows each learner to perform many local optimization steps independently before communicating across groups.

Despite communicating significantly less frequently (by a factor of H, e.g., 1-2 orders of magnitude less) than standard synchronous training, DiLoCo has been shown to achieve comparable convergence and model quality.

The Vanilla DiLoCo described above reduces total communication volume by a factor of H, however, all learners still transmit their weight deltas for the entire model all at once at every H-th step. On a thin network pipe, that full-model transfer creates a large periodic stall every H steps. This issue is alleviated by Streaming DiLoCo, which is described in the next section.

In multi-slice training, communication within a single slice is handled by high-speed Inter-Chip Interconnects (ICI). When scaling to multiple slices, synchronization across slices must traverse the Data Center Network (DCN) or, if the slices are geographically distributed, wide-area networks (WAN).

Within a single datacenter, DCN bandwidth is high enough that JAX and XLA can often hide standard Data Parallel all-reduce collectives by overlapping gradient communication with backward-pass computation. Across datacenters or congested links, however, bandwidth drops and latency rises sharply. Standard DP stalls at every step waiting for global gradient synchronization, collapsing Model FLOPS Utilization (MFU).

Streaming DiLoCo eliminates this synchronization stall by partitioning the model's parameters into P disjoint fragments. Over the course of H steps, every single fragment in the model is synchronized exactly once, achieving the exact same outer-loop cadence as vanilla DiLoCo without spiky bandwidth utilization.

Using a roofline model (similar to the JAX Scaling Book), we can calculate the network bandwidth required to prevent communication stalls for a model with ∣Θ∣ parameters (at b bytes per parameter, e.g., b=2 for bfloat16) and per-step compute time tstep=tfwd+tbwd. For example, the standard Data Parallel algorithm transfers 2∣Θ∣b bytes per sync via an all-reduce collective, which is a distributed reduce-scatter where each node reduces a different part, followed by an all-gather so all participants receive all parts. To achieve zero communication stalls, we overlap the reduce-scatter of the gradients with the backward pass, which produces them and takes tbwd time. Therefore, the peak required bandwidth is Bdp ≈ 2∣Θ∣b / tbwd.

Thankfully, females have not yet turned their back on the printed page, but ominous trends in reading habits for girls and boys suggest that at least the level of enthusiasm for reading among both genders is in decline. And as motivation to read slips, so does reading skill.

Where this relationship is most pronounced, however, is with boys, who have significantly lower levels of reading engagement than their female counterparts and fall well short of girls in reading achievement on global, national, and state-level reading assessments. When it comes to enjoyment of reading, time spent reading for pleasure, and diversity of text read (i.e., newspapers, magazines, fiction, nonfiction) girls in the U.S. and in most countries of the world have significantly and consistently higher levels.

I’ve often discussed in my books on the topic of boys and reading, as well as in speeches and lectures at conferences, that for those of us with sophisticated literacy skills, we hope the same for the guys within our care. This is because we desire boys, and indeed all youth, to experience the joy and benefits of the type of highly developed literacy abilities that we value and that have in very real ways contributed to our personal enrichment and professional accomplishments. However, there’s no shortcut to getting there. We can’t expect boys who rarely read to ever possess the level of literacy we wish for them. Parents who accommodate their sons’ non-reading habits are in some ways enabling stunted literacy growth.

Research tends to do a good job of documenting the decline in reading engagement and achievement without always specifying the causes. Thus, it’s helpful to look at the literature related to motivating students to read and then by inference appreciate the reasons for the lack of motivation. For example, researchers have discovered factors that positively influence motivation to read, such as (a) creating contexts for free self-selected reading, (b) giving students more text choices, and (c) structuring collaboration for reading and learning. Looked at from the opposite perspective, then, one can infer that students are disengaged when time is not set aside for free reading, when students’ choices about what to read are restricted or non-existent, and when students only read in isolation without opportunities to discuss and share texts.

These same lessons from research can help guide parents who are eager to create supportive environments for reading for their sons.

I’m fond of saying at the conclusion of my talks on this subject that “A boy’s love of reading doesn’t begin with scholarship, it begins with discovery." The more we do to help boys discover or rediscover the joy and pleasure of reading, the greater the chances they’ll become engaged and skillful readers for life.

Moving from Finland, where he has since 2025 been director of the Espoo Museum of Modern Art (EMMA), he said he wants to bring an outsider’s perspective and a focus on how the modern city connects with the world.

“The Stedelijk museum’s strength is in bringing large and Modernist exhibitions to the Netherlands,” he tells The Art Newspaper. “People go to the Rijksmuseum for Rembrandt and to the city [Stedelijk] museum to understand how Amsterdam has developed, to understand Modernism through the lens of art and design.”

Percy Jackson and the Olympians season 3 is fast approaching. Based on Rick Riordan's fantasy novel series of the same name, the Disney+ TV show follows the titular Percy Jackson, son of the Greek God Poseidon, as he embarks on quests with other demigods to stop mythic threats to the world. The series is centered around Camp Half-Bood, where Percy meets his best friends Grover and Annabeth, both of whom accompany him on his missions every summer. The show pulls from the pages of the novel series by adapting one book for every season.

Now, Percy Jackson and the Olympians season 3 is set to debut soon, adapting the third book in the series, The Titan's Curse. The new episodes see Percy on a quest to save Annabeth from the Titans, with him, Grover, and Thalia facing numerous mythological obstacles along the way. In addition, the show will focus on a conflict between the Gods and Titans growing ever closer, with both parties intersecting as Percy embarks upon his rescue mission.

In previous seasons, two episodes of Percy Jackson would be released for the premiere week, and season 3 is no different. Percy Jackson and the Olympians season 3, episodes 1 and 2 will be released at the same time, marking the first part of the eight-episode season to come. The show will continue airing weekly until its two-episode finale, episodes 7 and 8, on December 11, 2026. This means the entire season will be released in the span of just four weeks.

Episode 1 is titled "My Rescue Operation Goes Very Wrong," while episode 2 is titled "An Old Dead Friend Comes to Visit." Episode 1 is written by showrunner Craig Silverstein, and episode 2 is written by executive producer Albert Kim. Both episodes are directed by James Bobin, who has directed the first two episodes of every season of the show.

Percy Jackson season 3 will have a different release schedule than previous seasons. Following its two-episode premiere on November 20, the show will release two new episodes every Friday on Disney+ at 3:00 a.m. ET and 12:00 a.m. PT. This means episodes 3 and 4 will arrive on November 27, episodes 5 and 6 on December 4, and episodes 7 and 8 on December 11. It's a different release schedule from its prior seasons, which had two-episode premieres before releasing one episode per week. It means a shorter airing window, but a faster release for fans to enjoy.

Following the end of Percy Jackson season 2, the show laid the groundwork for what the next three years of Percy's life will be like. He's now tied to an ancient prophecy that will define how he approaches events moving forward, while also having new allies like Thalia on his side, despite some inevitable friction. Season 3 will also introduce new characters to Percy Jackson, such as siblings Nico and Bianca di Angelo, alongside the Greek Goddess Artemis, all of whom will be vital in the quest to come.

The new release schedule for Percy Jackson and the Olympians season 3 means viewers will get to watch the entire series play out over the course of a month. As the first time The Titan's Curse has ever been adapted onscreen, the upcoming episodes will deliver something non-book readers have never seen before. What comes next will be Percy's most harrowing journey yet, as he finds himself trapped in conflict while saving someone he cares very deeply about.

'GTA VI' Divides Congress: Tim Burchett Says Game Creates Criminals, Maxwell Frost Disagrees

"Grand Theft Auto VI" has sparked a showdown on Capitol Hill ... with one congressman comparing the game to a criminal training manual and another ready to buy a new console just to play it.

Jacob caught up with Reps. Maxwell Frost and Tim Burchett Wednesday ahead of the game's release ... and Burchett argued the violent game desensitizes kids while slamming parents who let their children play it.

Frost couldn't disagree more ... brushing off the criticism as overblown and revealing he's so excited for 'GTA VI' that he's buying a PlayStation 5 just to get in on the action.

The Florida Democrat insists it's up to parents to decide what their kids play ... telling us he wasn't allowed to play GTA growing up and had to settle for "The Simpsons: Hit & Run."

Now, he says he's got some serious catching up to do.

Burchett, on the other hand, compared the game to a how-to guide for stealing cars at gunpoint and shooting cops ... arguing there's a direct connection between violent video games and real-world violence.

The Tennessee Republican even told Jacob he believes mothers and fathers who let their kids play such games are bad parents.

Frost pushed back, pointing out that other countries have the same video games without America's level of gun violence.

Still, Burchett isn't calling for Congress to step in ... joking that expecting lawmakers to legislate morality is laughable given their own ethical track record.
