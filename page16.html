An Abu Dhabi court has ordered Circle and 17 other companies to keep tokens linked to Binance’s Oct. 29 theft frozen while the exchange tries to recover them.

Binance disclosed the order on Nov. 18 and said some issuers, exchanges and other partners ask for a court order before they will release or transfer assets they have frozen.

The exchange said the order from the Abu Dhabi Global Market Courts helps open that route, describing ongoing efforts to trace the stolen funds and giving no details of any transfer under the order.

The named firms include Circle, which issues USDC, and World Liberty Financial, the company behind USD1.

Reports noted that the firms must maintain the freezes pending further proceedings and cannot lend, swap, or mix the affected tokens with other assets.

The court did not accuse the firms of wrongdoing, and Dec. 1 is scheduled to consider whether to keep the injunction in place and on what terms.

The injunction covers specific assets Binance claimed are tied to the theft, and said it is still tracing the stolen crypto and taking legal steps to reclaim it. A transfer for firms seeking a court order depends on whether they have authority to release the specific balances they have frozen.

Tether falls outside the order covering the 18 firms, and the company said on Nov. 2 it had frozen more than $1.5 billion tied to the theft.

A spokesperson said Tether would work directly with Binance to reissue the tokens, in consultation with the relevant authorities.

Thomas Frank shares his feelings on Crystal Palace appointment

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Thomas Frank is back, and he sounds exactly like a man who has had enough time away to think clearly. Crystal Palace have appointed the 53-year-old on a two-and-a-half-year deal, and his first message was simple. He is refreshed, reflective and ready for another go in the Premier League.

Palace need that. They are bottom of the table, short on momentum and heading into a survival battle that will demand clarity more than noise. Frank arrives after a bruising end to his previous spell at Tottenham, where he was dismissed in February, less than eight months after taking charge. He could have stayed away longer. He chose not to.

There was no attempt to dress it up. Frank admitted the break had been enjoyable. He travelled to Japan and Australia, spent time in Denmark and relaxed with family and friends. As he put it, he had been "visiting friends and family, eating too well [and] drinking too much wine".

He had every reason to extend that time off too. "I was very aware I didn't want to do anything in the summer. I put a statement out there, I needed more time," he said. Then came the line that tells you where his head is now, "I can actually, honestly say I would have been fine if it was three, four, five, six, seven months more, because it's good to be back in the circus, but it's also unbelievably good not to be in, especially if you enjoy life, which I do."

Still, Palace convinced him. The club's internal pitch clearly mattered. Frank singled out chairman Steve Parish, along with Matt Hobbs and Iain Moody, as key figures in persuading him this was the right job at the right time.

That matters because sentiment gets you nowhere at the bottom of the Premier League. Palace need points, quickly. Frank's reign starts with a trip to Coventry City on Saturday, and there is no gentle easing-in period. The task is obvious, steady the team, sharpen the structure and lift belief.

Frank also made it clear that his time out of football was not spent feeling sorry for himself. "If you don't learn from any of your jobs, I don't think you reflect, I don't think you improve, and I don't think you get better," said Frank. "Of course there's a lot of things I have been reflecting over. The details, that will be for another time, but I can say I've learned, definitely."

This is where experience matters. Frank knows the scrutiny, understands the noise and appears entirely unbothered by it. "I think we all have an ego," he said. "Some are better at controlling it than others. For me, it's not the critics, it's more what I can learn, what I can prove, how I can get better."

He was even more direct when discussing outside opinion. "There's a lot of clever people out there, but the interesting thing is they only know 10 per cent of the truth. That's a tenth of the truth at Brentford, there's a tenth of the truth at Tottenham, and that's just the way it is.

"If you can't handle that as a coach or a player, then you shouldn't be in this business, and I'm very happy to be back in the game."

That is the point. No melodrama, no self-pity, no grand promises. Crystal Palace have hired a manager who sounds realistic about the mess in front of him and calm about the pressure around it. Right now, that is a sensible start.

Isaac Asimov’s Book Series Was Unfilmable — Now It's Apple TV's Greatest Space Opera

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sci-fi fans would collectively agree that some stories of the genre are better off staying on the page. For long, it was also believed that Isaac Asimov's books could not be translated to the screen, and for good reason. Especially when it comes to the Foundation book series, it was hard not to believe it was largely "unfilmable."

Some of the greatest sci-fi books are written in a way that it is easy to directly adapt them for the screen. Isaac Asimov's Foundation books, in contrast, defy almost every rule of visual drama. It unfolds less as a fictional drama and more as a deep dive into everything from sociological mechanics to human psychology. While at it, it also spans across centuries, shifting its focus from one character to another.

The books' massive time jumps, anti-cinematic action, and prioritization of ideas over character beats made it impossible to adapt for the screen. This explains why many planned big-budget adaptations of the books also struggled to see the light of day in the past. Fortunately, however, Apple TV's Foundation took a massive risk and achieved the impossible by successfully adapting Isaac Asimov's books.

Impossible feats demand extreme risks. Owing to this, Apple TV's take on the Foundation books significantly changed the source material's approach. The biggest adaptation challenge came from the books' revolving characters. Every few chapters, the books experience time jumps, where old characters die of age and new ones emerge.

On the screen, viewers need rootable visual anchors who appear for longer periods.

Apple TV's Foundation carefully navigated this issue by introducing cloning, cyro-sleep, and digital consciousness. These became narrative devices that allowed the series to logically sustain the same characters and cast members across centuries and seasons. Even an immortal humanoid, named Lady Demerzel, emerges in Foundation from Asimov's wider universe.

Unlike the books, Apple TV's Foundation also visualizes key conflicts and collapses from the original story instead of solely focusing on their political implications. Even "psychohistory" from Isaac Asimov's Foundation gets a visual and more tangible representation in the series.

Armed with the Socratic method, Apple TV's Foundation understands that "The beginning of wisdom is the definition of terms." Instead of treating Asimov's story as a plot to be faithfully transcribed, the show first understands the true essence of its terms. It then turns into a more sprawling space-opera by wisely redefining its ideas.

Many readers have complained that Apple TV's Foundation is too distinct compared to its source material. They argue that it is unfair to even call it a worthy adaptation. It is hard not to agree with them. An adaptation cannot be considered great if it is a far cry from its source material.

Similar to Stanley Kubrick’s The Shining and Denis Villeneuve's Arrival, Foundation fails as a book-to-screen adaptation on many levels.

However, if purely judged as a modern television space opera, Foundation is easily one of the best sci-fi shows of our time. While Asimov's books seemingly argue that historical forces matter far more than individual stories of heroism, Apple TV's Foundation carefully bends the original narrative to focus on almost mythic heroes and solo characters.

It is also arguable that a direct page-to-page TV adaptation of Isaac Asimov's Foundation books would likely have failed. Whether Apple TV's adaptation approach is ideal is still debatable, but it at least gives us an epic sci-fi space opera.

Central banks are buying approximately 60 tonnes of gold per month, compared with an average of 17 tonnes before 2022.

France and the Netherlands have also been moving gold reserves from foreign vaults to domestic storage. These developments indicate continued focus on gold as a reserve asset.

Titled Something Old, Something New, Something Borrowed, Something Blue, the exhibition brings together over fifteen works, and even includes new installations that revisit some of the artist’s earliest experiments with light and perception.

A rectangular opening glows violet against a darkened wall. Elsewhere, a luminous cube appears to float where two walls meet, with its sharp edges suggesting a solid object suspended in space. The room itself becomes difficult to read as the light alters viewers’ sense of depth and distance.

The show is on view through January 30th, 2027, and marks Turrell’s first presentation with Almine Rech in the United States. It arrives nearly four decades after their first collaboration in Paris, and returns to ideas that have occupied the artist since the 1960s.

Among the new works is the installation Shallow Space, part of a series in which James Turrell uses illuminated apertures to create unexpected depth within a wall. At first, the luminous surface can appear completely flat, like a field of color. As the viewer’s eyes adjust, the opening reveals an actual space beyond the wall, although its dimensions remain difficult to judge.

In Raemar II (2026), a glowing rectangular form casts violet light across the surrounding surfaces. Color spreads beyond its edges and gradually dissolves into the room. From a distance, the work resembles an illuminated painting, although its relationship to the wall is far more ambiguous.

The exhibition also introduces new pieces from the artist‘s Glassworks series, which uses precisely programmed LEDs behind etched glass. Colors change gradually across these luminous surfaces, and the apparent depth of each work shifts with them. Some passages of light appear dense and opaque, while others seem to recede far beyond the physical dimensions of the piece.

Another new installation, Afrum Violet (2026), returns to James Turrell’s Cross Corner projections. A sharply defined violet form occupies the intersection of two walls, where the geometry of the room helps create the illusion of a three-dimensional cube. The surrounding surfaces absorb a softer wash of purple, while the central form retains its remarkably precise edges.

Turrell began experimenting with these projections in 1966 at the former Mendota Hotel in Ocean Park, California, where he converted the building into a studio. By directing light onto the walls at specific angles, he discovered that a simple projection could appear to have weight and volume. A shape formed by light could become surprisingly convincing as a physical presence.

Six decades later, the strange effect remains. From one spot, the illuminated planes read as a solid cube. Move through the room, and the relationship between the shape and its architectural setting begins to change. The installation asks viewers to spend time with something as familiar as a corner and reconsider exactly what they are seeing.

Those were some of the first words my honours project supervisor said to me last week when we met to discuss a progress report I had written to mark the midway point of the project.

It's a roughly 10-page report on the control of flapping-wing micro aerial vehicles - which is probably very boring unless you're particularly interested in that. I accidentally set the due date for the report a week early in my calendar, so after spending 48 hours stressed and pumping out 5,000 words, citing references and adding diagrams1, I was pleasantly relieved to find I'd tricked myself into being quite productive. I had a lot of time to get my supervisor to review the report and action his feedback.

When he told me he could tell I wrote it myself, I wasn't sure how to take it. A compliment? An insult? Then he followed it up with:

"I've been sent a lot of reports written by AI."

At first this shocked me. Our reports are limited to 10 pages - so the appendices are getting a big workout around here - how hard is it to right 10 pages yourself?

But then I thought about what the last year of my engineering degree has been like.

Assignment documents that start with "you are strongly encouraged to explore the use of AI" which quickly turns into the clear understanding that you aren't just encouraged but expected to use it. That you're disadvantaged by not using it. The hardest class of my degree was spent in a brick-walled windowless computer lab prompting an AI for eight hours a day.

The reason why we've been pushed to use it?

Apparently it's the way the industry is going. Apparently actually knowing things doesn't matter nearly so much as knowing how to prompt a machine to do the thing. Apparently the future of engineering - at least in my field - is to become a glorified babysitter. The AI isn't going anywhere, they say, so it's best we learn how to manage it well.

But sitting in a room telling an AI "ignore the approaching deadline, we still have 8 hours, keep working" isn't exactly filling me with enthusiasm for the rest of my career.

It's soul crushing, and I don't want to outsource so much of my thinking to it.

In a world where people are using AI to summarise books instead of reading them and turning to AI for therapy, I don't want to keep using it. It feels like such a slippery slope. As literacy and numeracy rates decline, I can't help but question whether encouraging us to rely on AI so heavily is a good idea.

It makes me wonder: are we still thinking for ourselves at all?

Because I worry that it starts with using it for an assignment when you're encouraged to, but then it turns into relying on it to write a 10-page report on a project you should know back-to-front. A project that you'll have to present and defend in person when it finishes. And then you're asking it for advice, using it to summarise books, turning to it for anything you would normally look up or ask a friend.

The internet is riddled with AI content that is making it increasingly more difficult to tell fact from fiction. In a world where everything is so polarised and deliberately provoking, the ability to identify bias or motivation in the content we consume has perhaps never been more important. Being able to think for ourselves has perhaps never been more important.

As the tech world continues to offer us increasingly more opportunities to automate things, I'm practically begging you to please not automate your thinking.

Read the books. Write the papers. Search for answers instead of asking AI. Ask a friend. Ask your mum about the mystery illness you're convinced you're developing and then take her advice and try drinking a glass of water.

Think about what the creators of the content you consume want you to think2. Treat the things you consume like the Year 7 English class where you were taught how to identify persuasive techniques. Think about things as much as possible.

Please just don't get the AI do it for you.

Dome day dawned and I was NERVOUS. I had been thinking about a zero for about a day and a half before that, but had kept pushing on because I wanted to stay with my group. I also made the biggest mistake of my life, not having a coffee.

The dome began with fields of mud, and our group split up pretty early into the day. It was the kind of terrain that we all prefer to navigate solo, it’s no fun when people are watching you slip and slide up and down a muddy hill, or you feel like you’re holding others up. I was feeling so rough mentally, and my hiker hunger had really started to kick in the night before, so I spent the morning mentally cataloguing all the food I had left and whether it would be enough to get me to the dairy in Puhoi. When we approached the next big, forested hill section of the day we found a fellow hiker, Xavier, eating half his lunch. I knew that on the other side of this hill was a campsite where, if I really wanted to, I could stop for the night. I also knew that in less than a kilometre I’d hit the 500km mark.

I felt so sleepy, not just tired but burny-eyes-sleepy. I thought desperately about the single sachet of instant coffee deep in my food bag and sang “just keep swimming” to myself. By the time I was about ¾ through this hill I was once again mentally inventorying my meals, trying to decide if maybe I could break this section into two days.

Then I heard some girls ahead of me and like a puppy seeing its people again after a long work day, I perked up, thinking it was my friends. Unfortunately it was just two very clean-looking, white-sneakered day hikers.

Though they weren’t who I was hoping to see, the whiteness of their sneakers told me that from here, the trail would be more chill. I pushed on and eventually I could hear Sinèad’s accent floating through the trees. And there was our friend, Zoe, getting water from the stream! I crossed the stream and climbed up to the campground where I was delighted to find there was a picnic table so I wouldn’t have to sit on the ground.

I laid out my very wet tent to dry (still thinking about staying there), drank the last of my water, and filtered some into my mayo-turned cold soak jar to shake up my last instant coffee sachet. Sinèad said she watched me essentially reboot as soon as I started drinking the coffee.

It was 1:30 at this point, we’d been walking for six and a half hours, and I didn’t really have a sense of how much further there was to go. I decided to take a long lunch and to eat two wraps, food inventory be damned!

And then my saviours began to arrive. First was another set of day hikers, the older of whom helpfully told me I seemed very tired (I did not hold back when I agreed with him). Annabel and I climbed the third and final hill of the day which was actually suddenly easy terrain, and then descended a really well-graded, touristy track. I honestly felt like I was flying on that terrain and having Annabel around to chat to made it go really quickly. After eleven hours of walking (including the two hour lunch I took) we made it to camp. The dome cafe was an oasis, it had a bathtub and yes I did sit down in it. Wrapping up this autumn’s week of marquee evening sales in New York, Christie’s 21st-century auction on Thursday (19 November) proved rather lacklustre, bringing in $117.7m ($145.9m with fees). That tally fell short of Christie’s pre-sale expectations, which do not account for fees, and projected a total take in the range of $126m to $192m. It was also a lower result compared to the auction house’s last two equivalent sales in New York—namely the $132m ($162.6m with fees) generated across 42 lots last May, and the $99.5m ($123.5m with fees) brought by 45 lots one year ago.

Just five of the 56 lots offered Thursday night failed to sell, for a slim buy-in rate of 9%. Two lots were withdrawn at the last moment, including a Jeff Koons sculpture estimated to bring between $3m and $5m. Twenty-four lots were backed by third-party guarantees and nine were backed fully by house guarantees, representing around 59% of all the works offered. Auction records for three artists were broken over the course of the night.

It sold to a telephone bidder for $625,000 ($800,000 with fees). From an edition of six, it hailed from the collection of Antonio Homem Sonnabend, the adopted son of the late and legendary dealer and collector Ileana Sonnabend (1914-2007) and who ran his mother’s namesake gallery in its waning years. Antonio Homem died earlier this year, at age 86.

Another Lichtenstein, the painting Large Spool (1963)—a razor-sharp depiction of the titular object that has been featured in multiple gallery and museum exhibitions—squeaked by on the tails of a third-party guarantee at $7m ($8.6m with fees) against an ambitious estimate of $10m to $15m.

Still in the Sonnabend lots, Jeff Koons’s toy-like Hulk (Friends) (2004-12), in polychromed bronze and standing nearly 6ft tall, found a new home for $3.3m ($4.1m with fees). 6 Funniest Far Side Comics Starring the Devil

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Gary Larson’s loved sending people to Hell

In the 1980s and 1990s, nothing in the funny pages was quite as dark as The Far Side. Lucy van Pelt pulling the football away from Charlie Brown as he was about to kick it is about as dark as things got in Peanuts. And in Garfield, the lazy cat’s darkest running gag also revolved around a kick — when he’d kick Odie the dog off a table.

Meanwhile, The Far Side featured jokes about hangings and murder — death was a regular punchline. Larson also liked to make light of whatever came after death. Occasionally he’d make a joke about Heaven, but he clearly found Hell far more amusing. Many Far Side comics take place in the underworld — or some specific version of it — with Satan a recurring figure in his comic strips. Few characters in The Far Side appeared more than once, but the Devil himself made many appearances.

In this comic from June 11, 1988, a pizza gets delivered to Hell and Satan is clearly unhappy about it. “First of all, this is going straight back — and I’ll just have a little chat with whoever placed the order,” says Satan. The best little detail in the comic strip is the slogan on the pizza box. It says “Jerry’s Pizza: We Deliver Anywhere” and Jerry clearly lives up to her word.

I can understand the Devil’s annoyance. Pizza is one of the greatest pleasures on Earth, which means that pizza is absolutely disqualified from being served in Hell, even though there’s a perfect-looking pizza oven literally everywhere you look.

A recurring source of humor in Gary Larson’s comic strip was to take a common phrase or idiom and interpret it as literally as possible. In one comic from 1992, he picked apart the common phrase “ride off into the sunset” by depicting a burnt-up cowboy that had literally ridden into the sun. In another comic from 1985, he played on the phrase “dressed to kill” by showing two sharks in fancy evening attire.

In this comic from 1982, Larson dissected the phrase “To Hell and back” by turning Hell into a mere tourist destination. In it, a man is showing off slides from his vacation, which include a picture of his wife standing with the Devil. Especially fun here is how happy the Devil is, while the man’s wife looks bored by the encounter.

While Hell showed up in several Far Side comics, it wasn’t always the same Hell, or at least it didn’t appear to be. Several comic strips depicted very specific, different forms of Hell, each of which was specifically designed to torture a particular resident of the underworld. For example, this comic from 1990 has the caption “Bowler’s Hell.” It shows the bowler having made an impossible-to-hit split, with the Devil saying “Whoa! Another split?... What a bummer!” Clearly, endless splits like this would be the worst possible punishment for a bowler.

If a bowler’s version of Hell is endless splits, a classical conductor's version of Hell would have to be an orchestra filled only with banjos. Larson seemingly found banjos to be obnoxious instruments as a number of his comic strips feature them in a less-than-flattering light. In another, more Earthly Far Side comic, a different conductor is horrified by the sound of a banjo in his orchestra as he shouts “Stop! Stop! What’s that sound? What’s that sound?”

Still, it seems that, for Gary Larson’s musical tastes, banjos rank higher than accordions, which received even harsher treatment.

This Far Side comic contrasts Heaven and Hell. In Heaven, you receive a harp when you die, but in Hell, the instrument given out to all new inhabitants is an accordion. I seriously wonder if any accordion or banjo players were ever offended by Gary Larson’s repeated attacks on their instruments.

In yet another specific version of Hell, dogs who have been sentenced to eternal damnation are forced to work as their arch-nemesis: mailmen. Originally, I’d assumed that this comic strip was a parody of the movie All Dogs Go to Heaven, a truly beautiful animated film directed by Don Bluth. But the years don’t add up as this comic strip comes from 1986 while All Dogs Go to Heaven is from 1989.

And while I’m not the kind of person to get into a theological debate between any two parties, I think Don Bluth is right about where dogs go in the afterlife, whereas Larson is totally wrong about any dog going to hell.

Tottenham hopes remain high for the winger, who will likely remain in Germany all the while he is a consistent starter for Koln this season.
