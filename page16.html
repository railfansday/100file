Home Depot’s incoming CEO says homeowners are ‘feeling pressure.’ A quarter can’t cover a $5,000 repair

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Richard McPhail, a 21-year Home Depot veteran who was named the retailer’s next CEO on Tuesday, has been watching financial pressure build among American homeowners. On the company’s third-quarter earnings call the same day, he acknowledged that “a rising interest rate environment puts pressure on our customer and our business.”

McPhail, Home Depot’s CFO since 2019, takes over as CEO on Nov. 23. Ted Decker, who recently returned from a medical leave, will step down as CEO and remain chair of the board.

That’s a problem for a retailer that booked $164.7 billion in revenue last fiscal year, the bulk of it from people fixing up homes they already own. Homeowners are “one of the most resilient customer cohorts in the economy,” McPhail told Yahoo Finance in a February interview. “But they’re feeling pressure like everyone else.”

“If you look at housing turnover, that’s actually been frozen for three years,” he said, adding that turnover has sat at historical lows three years running. He also pointed to customers’ “concerns around housing affordability, general affordability” and “broader concerns over uncertainty and job loss.”

Owning a home has long been considered a path to financial security in America. But even homeowners with considerable housing wealth feel squeezed by the rising cost of living, and some struggle to afford the upkeep of their biggest asset.

Nearly two-thirds of homeowners (63%) say owning a home causes them at least moderate financial stress, up from 55% in June 2025, according to an October survey of 1,000 homeowners conducted for Hometap, a home equity financing company.

That stress comes even as Americans sit on a record $13.2 trillion in accessible home equity as of the second quarter of 2026, according to First American Data & Analytics. That’s the amount owners could borrow against while keeping their combined loan-to-value at 80% or below.

But housing wealth isn’t disposable income. Even owners who locked in rock-bottom pandemic-era mortgage rates aren’t immune to rising property taxes and insurance premiums or climbing utility bills. The typical homeowner now spends $16,566 a year on costs beyond the mortgage, about two-thirds of it on upkeep, according to Zillow.

Job worries are adding to the strain. In the Federal Reserve Bank of New York’s October Survey of Consumer Expectations, released last week, consumers put the odds of higher unemployment a year from now at 45.3%, the highest reading since April 2020. Their perceived chance of losing their own job also ticked up to 13.9%.

A LendingTree survey conducted in September found that 34% of homeowners had postponed at least one necessary repair or maintenance task over the past two years. Among those with outstanding repairs, 32% said they simply couldn’t afford the work.

A surprise $5,000 repair would be entirely out of reach for a quarter of homeowners surveyed. Another 30% could cover it only by substantially draining their savings.

Among homeowners who needed repairs over the past two years, 37% dipped into emergency savings, and 33% cut spending on essentials such as groceries, healthcare, or childcare to pay for the work.

Waiting tends to cost more. Among respondents whose homes currently need repairs, 34% said delaying the work drove up the price, and a quarter said it caused damage elsewhere in the house.

Decker sounded a similar alarm in February, telling analysts that “our customers are telling us that they’re not investing” in bigger projects, citing affordability, the jobs picture, and weak consumer confidence.

Even so, the business has grown this year. Home Depot reported third-quarter sales of $43.4 billion on Tuesday, up 4.9% from a year earlier, with comparable sales up 2.2%. “Third quarter results exceeded our expectations,” McPhail said.

Homeowners may be shelving kitchen remodels, but smaller repairs and maintenance jobs have kept customers coming in. In August, McPhail, then CFO, credited “broad-based demand across the business as customers continued to engage in smaller projects.”

Tight budgets are also pushing homeowners to pick up the tools themselves. Among LendingTree respondents who completed at least some repairs, 60% did some of the work themselves because they couldn’t afford a professional. Among millennials, that figure reached 72%.

At the other end of the market, Home Depot has been courting professional contractors, builders, and remodelers. SRS, the building-materials distributor it acquired for $18.25 billion in June 2024, delivered “almost $5 billion in sales in the quarter,” McPhail said. Executives said on the call that cross-selling between Home Depot and SRS is on track to generate $400 million in sales this year, a figure the company aims to double in 2027.

The company has also been investing in AI and digital tools that help pros plan projects, manage material lists, and track orders. Its express delivery service now reaches customers within two hours from virtually all of its stores, incoming president and COO Ann-Marie Campbell said on the call.

Home Depot now expects total sales to grow about 4.5% in fiscal 2026, with comparable sales up roughly 1.4%. McPhail is betting that speed will keep winning over a cautious customer: “Our investments in express delivery and in AI customer and associate enablement are making a meaningful difference.”

How Thinking Outcomes Can Be More Important Than Being Right

Posted November 18, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills

A few months back, I wrote a piece here about the viral conspiracy that Earth would lose gravity for 7 seconds on August 12th. I discussed how, despite not actually believing that the event would occur—because of a slew of reasons—the open-minded part of me (albeit still skeptical) nevertheless suggested developing a quick “just in case” protocol for the unlikely event. I received some positive feedback on how I exemplified the importance of cautious thinking throughout the piece and how, in context, we can engage two important dispositions towards thinking (Dwyer et al., 2016): open-mindedness and skepticism. On the other hand, though, some implied a level of foolishness in my thinking.

The latter cohort, I believe, missed my point, and so I aim to make it clearer in this follow-up. In that previous piece, I stated on multiple occasions that I didn’t actually believe the event would occur. In fact, I highlighted why it was nearly impossible.

But I used this event prediction as an example to make the point that cautious thinkers are typically happy to make minor mistakes in efforts to avoid risk, even if the risk is extremely low. That is, through cost-benefit analysis (consistent with many existing cost-benefit models we commonly see in psychology; e.g. Lave and March 1975; Thibaut and Kelley 1959; Uehara 1990), good thinkers may often choose a 99 percent likely mistake made out of caution, if the mistake yields minimal negative impact (e.g., looking foolish in front of one’s spouse). This will be selected over doing nothing potentially “foolish” (e.g., in light of being 99 percent “sure”), despite the disastrous impact that 1 percent chance would have because they failed to act (e.g., if the event occurred, death would be a near certainty if we were outdoors).

Simply, good thinkers don’t care about labels of wrong or right; they care about the outcomes of their decisions. It’s cautious thinking… and critical thinkers are cautious thinkers.

It’s much like hope for the best, plan for the worst. It’s why we develop contingency plans; it’s why we buy insurance. It’s largely why I don’t go outside in a thunderstorm, despite the odds against getting hit by lightning. It’s why I buy a lotto ticket once every few weeks, when the thought occurs to me on the day of a draw (i.e., I will not notice the loss of a couple of euros [thankfully], whereas winning the lotto would be life-changing). You never know.

With that, context (as well as moderation) is key. It might be foolish to buy too much insurance, to spend €200 weekly on the lotto, or to waste your time hiding in a cellar for three hours during your run-of-the-mill thunderstorm. Again, caution is considered in terms of cost (be it time, money, or status) relative to benefit or risk avoidance.

From an epistemological perspective, an established truth is that we cannot see into the future (as of yet, anyway [there’s some doozy of a meta-joke in there]). We do not know what’s going to happen. Objectively, we can place likelihoods on certain events happening, but that’s it. It’s our human interpretation that labels these probabilities as impossible, unlikely, possible, likely, highly probable, definite, etc. We might say definite, but that’s not exactly a guarantee. Consider the late Daniel Kahneman’s work on weather and stock forecasting (see also Kahneman, 2011). If we lived in a world of certainty (which we don’t), you’d never hear the phrase, “Well, that was unexpected.”

In moments of excitement and uncertainty, good thinkers are able to quickly evaluate probabilities, not just in terms of odds, but in terms of interpretation of outcomes (e.g., consider naturalistic decision-making [Klein, 2008]). The ability to do this quickly is a result of previous consideration and reflective judgment on priorities and values—things for which good thinkers would be concerned and cautious. They apply these considerations efficiently, if and when they need to. For example, despite less than a half-percent chance of your school burning down during your tenure in that school, how many fire drills were you a part of as a student? A lot, right? But you knew what to do in case it was for real.

Bizarrely, what’s your plan for a zombie apocalypse? Stop for a moment and think about it... Did your answer come to you fairly quickly? Even in the extreme unlikelihood of such a thing happening, it’s likely you thought about it or something similar before, at least once.

There’s nothing wrong with considering such things. Planning in such a way is a good cognitive exercise. It’s also good for creativity!

Cautious people often think in similar ways. Such cautious thinking might get accused of being “overthinking.” But that’s not necessarily a bad thing, unless it becomes a common or costly focus. Indeed, catastrophizing can be problematic. Moderation is important. But so too is caution, especially when it comes to ensuring good outcome-focused thinking. So, don’t sweat making small mistakes in life, especially if it’s done out of calculated caution. There’s nothing foolish about it.

Google’s AI feature in Search gets its own incognito mode

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google’s search engine has long offered an incognito mode for all those search queries you don’t want logged or associated with your account. Now, Google’s AI Mode in Search is getting a similar feature. In a blog post, the company announced a new feature that brings “temporary threads” to AI Mode, where the conversations you have won’t be resurfaced later in areas like your search history or recommendations.

Many leading AI chatbots already offer the option for temporary chats, Google Gemini included. However, there was not yet an option to have a temporary chat when you’re searching Google and are dropped into its conversational interface, known as AI Mode.

Google says that users won’t have to adjust their settings to take advantage of the new option. Instead, they’ll be able to click a temporary threads icon in the upper right corner of AI Mode to engage in a temporary chat. This essentially works the same as a temporary chat would in the stand-alone Gemini chatbot.

The option could be convenient to use at those times when you start digging deeper into a particular topic via AI Mode and realize that you don’t want the discussion to become a part of your search history because of its personal nature.

Once the temporary thread is started, the session becomes detached from your account, Google says. This means that the responses the AI provides won’t be informed by past preferences, nor will they impact future personalization. That could also be useful for those times when you want to learn about something as more of a one-off, but don’t want Google to start to think of the topic as one of your main interests.

When you close out of the thread, you will return to searching as usual.

The company says the option to engage in temporary chats in AI Mode is rolling out globally on desktop and mobile across all supported languages.

12 Powdered Gravy Mixes, Ranked Worst To Best

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

We may receive a commission on purchases made from links.

As most pro home chefs will tell you, making gravy from scratch isn't exactly rocket science. With the help of a little flour, butter, and broth, you could be on your way to a delicious gravy in just a few minutes. Still, there may be times when you simply aren't feeling up to it — whether you've had a rough week or are drowning in to-dos, packets of powdered gravy mix can prove a simple, flavorful fix.

Even so, the kind of store-bought gravy mix you choose matters — after all, anything you're planning to drown your meat and taters in had better be good. That's why I decided to make it my mission to test some of the most popular powdered gravy options in my area in hopes of offering my honest opinion about which truly deserves a spot in your kitchen

In the upcoming post, I'm giving a complete rundown of every store-bought powdered gravy mix I could find, including the pricing, size, and taste of each. Bear in mind that the details mentioned in this article are subject to change and that the gravy packet selection featured here may not be reflective of what's available in your area. With that, join me as I prepare, taste, and rank 12 store-bought powdered gravy mixes from worst to best.

Prices are as of the date of publication and may vary based on region.

Great Value Chicken Gravy Mix is a no-frills Walmart-branded product that is, surprisingly, one of the costlier options on the list when you consider its price per unit. Though tagged at only 92 cents per packet, each pack contains .87 ounces, meaning you'll pay roughly $1.06 per ounce of powdered gravy.

Great Value Chicken Gravy Mix is prepared using the standard 1 cup of water per 1 packet of mix, and its ingredients include monosodium glutamate (otherwise known as "MSG"), which may pose a problem for gravy lovers who may be sensitive to the ingredient. I tore open the packet, and its unappetizing, artificial smell hit me right away. Once cooked, the mixture turned creamy and thick, but its color seemed unnatural, especially when compared to homemade. I gave it a hesitant taste, and nope, not good — Great Value Chicken Gravy Mix tasted like stale chicken and literally made me gag.

Would I recommend purchasing Great Value Chicken Gravy Mix? No way. Not only does its bright yellow color not resemble any chicken gravy I've ever made, but the taste was barely tolerable. I'm sure you could try improving this store-bought gravy with Worcestershire sauce, but I say, if it's convenience you seek, there are other options more deserving of your coin than this one.

I hate to have to rank yet another Great Value powdered gravy mix so close to the bottom of the list, but it simply cannot be helped. Great Value Turkey Gravy Mix is a bit cheaper than its chicken-flavored counterpart — expect to pay 78 cents per .87 ounces, which works out to around 89.7 cents per ounce of powdered gravy. Great Value Turkey Gravy Mix doesn't contain the MSG that the chicken flavor includes, but that doesn't mean I recommend purchasing it — like the former, it has a weird smell and taste that I simply can't get past.

I prepared Great Value Turkey Gravy Mix according to the package directions and watched it bubble and simmer on the stove. The mix eventually turned the typical light brown color normally associated with turkey gravy and had an uber-thick consistency. I let it cool slightly before giving it a quick taste — sadly, it had the same stomach-churning qualities as the previous flavor. Great Value Turkey Gravy Mix tasted woefully artificial; in fact, the additives or chemicals present in the gravy were just about the only thing I could taste. Needless to say, I would never dish up Great Value Turkey Gravy Mix to guests at Thanksgiving — you'd be better off leaving not-so-Great Value picks like this one on the shelf.

Before offering my opinion here, I have to be honest and say I'm not a person who prepares peppered gravy often. That said, I'm willing to bet Southeastern Mills Peppered Gravy Mix doesn't quite encapsulate its classic flavor the way other options do.

Southeastern Mills Peppered Gravy Mix costs $1.22 per 2.75 ounces, with a unit price of 44.4 cents per ounce. As is typical for many peppered gravy mixes on the market, this packet contains a little more powdered gravy mix per pack than other varieties and flavors. Its preparation requires more water, too — you'll need 1 ¾ cups this time around versus the usual 1 cup.

Southeastern Mills Peppered Gravy Mix tasted annoyingly bland, with a flavor very similar to what you'd get if you added black pepper to plain cream. Though thick, it had no richness of flavor, and I couldn't imagine it doing much to elevate anyone's sausage gravy. I flipped the packet over and was surprised by what I saw on the ingredient list: Palm oil and modified food starch were the first ingredients mentioned, which is hardly a good way to start a high-quality gravy, if you ask me. As I said, I am by no means an expert in peppered gravy, but based on first impressions alone, I'd say skip this brand and opt for other, richer, more flavorful brands on the market.

If you're surprised to see McCormick Brown Gravy Mix ranked lower on the list, join the club. I, for one, adore brown gravy, and with McCormick being one of the most popular gravy and seasoning brands on the market, I figured I'd be in for a real treat. Expect to pay 97 cents for .87 ounces of it, making each ounce cost roughly $1.12.

When cooking up McCormick Brown Gravy Mix, I couldn't help but feel excited — its texture was thick and glossy, and the gravy had a deep brown hue that just screamed umami beef. It even had the savory, enticing aroma of a succulent pot roast, so imagine my surprise when I discovered the gravy tastes, quite literally, like nothing.

That's right — McCormick Brown Gravy Mix features a flavor so weak I could barely taste anything at all. To think McCormick Brown Gravy Mix smelled and looked so great without delivering flavor even half of what was teased is mind-boggling. Needless to say, McCormick Brown Gravy Mix isn't worth the buy — I'd recommend picking up some beef broth and making your own simple brown gravy or choosing one of the higher-ranking varieties over this one.

Though not the best out there, Amazon Grocery Brown Gravy Mix is a dark horse in terms of taste and performance. The gravy comes sold in an .87-ounce pack and was priced at 83 cents, about 95 cents per ounce. It contains artificial ingredients along with a host of additives, but hey, if that kinda stuff doesn't bother you, feel free to ignore those stats.

I went into my tasting of Amazon Grocery Brown Gravy Mix with low expectations — almost every Amazon Grocery item I've sampled in the past left me underwhelmed. According to the Google Threat Intelligence Group (GTIG) and Mandiant report, state-backed hackers are actively targeting crypto firms in North America and Europe by turning popular AI developer tools into malware delivery traps.

Researchers have linked a suspected North Korean hacking campaign to attacks on cryptocurrency companies. The group, known as Midnight Neptune, uses fake recruiter profiles and tampered software settings to break into company networks.

The attackers approach targets on professional networking platforms, posing as recruiters with AI-generated profile photos.

They then invite victims to fake online meetings, where simulated audio or video problems prompt them to run scripts to fix the issue. This social engineering trick, known as ClickFix, gets victims to execute malicious commands themselves.

Once inside a company’s network, the attackers tamper with its internal code repositories, modifying the configuration file used by Anthropic’s Claude Code.

The next time a developer launches Claude Code, the altered settings silently download a backdoor called SOMBERMEME.

The attackers also create GitHub Personal Access Tokens disguised as Microsoft Copilot to make their activity look like normal developer traffic.

The report also revealed a major advance in automated hacking that puts open-source Web3 infrastructure at risk.

Google researchers discovered an active tool called exploit_agent on an exposed testing server. It uses large language models (LLMs) to compare software code before and after security updates, identify newly patched vulnerabilities, write code to exploit them, and launch attacks — all without human involvement.

That puts open-source protocols at risk. Since code updates are publicly available, hackers can spot newly fixed vulnerabilities and exploit them before operators have a chance to install the patches. Even a brief delay can leave networks vulnerable.

Google Threat Intelligence Group (GTIG) reported that disclosed vulnerabilities and exploits nearly doubled between January and August 2026.

Exploited zero-day vulnerabilities also rose from an average of eight per month in 2025 to 11 in 2026, reaching 22 in August alone.

Criminals hijack corporate cloud accounts, steal AI computing power, and resell it through underground marketplaces known as LLM Mixers. The resulting fraud can exceed $100,000 in a single day and reach $1 million per victim.

Meanwhile, influence networks use more than 100,000 fake social media accounts to spread misinformation, fuel token hype, and trigger artificial market panic.

To counter these threats, Google is deploying AI security tools such as Big Sleep and CodeMender to find and fix software vulnerabilities before hackers exploit them.

The GTIG report highlights several security risks for the crypto industry.

Companies using AI coding tools should check their code repositories for unauthorized commands and keep third-party access tokens secure.

They also need to install software updates quickly, as AI-powered hackers can turn newly discovered vulnerabilities into working attacks.

Employees should never follow unexpected browser or video-call instructions asking them to copy and run computer commands.

Investors, meanwhile, should be cautious of sudden waves of online hype or panic, which fake social media accounts can create at scale.

Safe Places to Earn Interest – Match One to When You’ll Need the Money

Money you can’t afford to lose can still earn interest in high-yield savings, money market funds, CDs, Treasury bills, or I bonds. Pick the one you can cash out without a penalty by the month you’ll need the money, and skip any product sold as safe that pays far more than Treasury bills.

No investment pays a high return without the risk of a loss, and money set aside for a down payment, a tuition bill, or an emergency can’t take that risk.

Each option below is protected by FDIC insurance (NCUA insurance at a credit union), the U.S. government, or a fund’s short-term holdings. What separates them is how soon you can get your money back and what it costs to get it early, so the date you’ll need the money decides which one fits.

Here’s what protects each one, how fast you can reach the money, and the catch.

Use Treasury bills as the yardstick for any rate you’re offered. The Federal Reserve’s latest daily figures put the three-month Treasury yield, the annual rate the government pays to borrow for three months, at 4.32%. A product sold as low-risk that pays 7% or 8% does so because it carries a risk you can’t see. The SEC flags “high returns with little or no risk” as a sign of a Ponzi scheme. The one safe exception is the I bond, whose rate follows inflation and can top Treasury yields when inflation runs high.

Money you might need any day, like an emergency fund, belongs in high-yield savings or a money market fund, not a savings account earning the national average, which the FDIC puts at 0.39%. For an expense due within a year, buy a Treasury bill or a CD that matures before it. For a goal one to five years away, line up CDs to mature by the date, or roll Treasury bills over until then. Put only money you can leave alone for at least a year into I bonds, and use a short-term bond fund only for money that could ride out a small loss.

Before you buy a bond fund, look up its duration on the fund’s fact sheet. Duration measures how much a bond’s price is likely to move when rates change. By FINRA’s rule of thumb, a fund with a two-year duration loses about 2% for each percentage point that rates rise.

Say you’re saving for a down payment in two years. A CD ladder, a set of CDs that come due one after another before your closing date, has every dollar ready when you buy. So do Treasury bills you roll into new ones as each matures. Dividend stocks don’t, since a steady dividend won’t stop the share price from falling the month you need to sell. That’s the reason to keep money you’ll need within five years out of stocks.

There’s no question Andrew Scott, the 49-year-old Irish actor, has earned his position as one of the finest contemporary actors going. He’s in demand, but choosing wisely, and in roles that slyly play off his own status as an out gay man (a rare thing indeed) but with something off-center beneath, often a secret. He played Tom Ripley, one even more sociopathic than Matt Damon’s own iconically calculating Adonis, to devastating effect in the sinister Netflix series “Ripley,” there more explicitly grappling with the Patricia Highsmith character’s ambiguous (but was it?) sexuality. He played the Hot Priest who turned down a chance at heterosexual, secular love in “Fleabag,” of course, and starred in “All of Us Strangers” as a lonely adult orphan living in a glittering tower, bracing himself for romantic intimacy with Paul Mescal. I also saw him playing up to nine roles in Anton Chekhov’s “Vanya” onstage in New York City last year as men and women of all sorts, showing an actor who can shimmy between genders and across ranges. He has the unmistakable warmth of someone you should trust, but with intrigue behind the curtain of many of his performances to suggest that maybe you shouldn’t. He’s that good.

Scott has now taken on what will, to this point, I suppose, go down as “the role of a lifetime” in director Simon Stone’s rueful, pained, and unremarkable new film drama “Elsinore” as Scottish actor Ian Charleson, who died of HIV/AIDS complications while portraying Hamlet at the National Theatre in London in 1990. Charleson’s story is unlikely to be one most are familiar with. Even the most public of deaths among the most publicly gay of people often still remain in hiding, though Charleson wasn’t out himself. He resisted pressures from his inner circle to announce his disease amid a stage run that miraculously lasted 24 performances. Actors then were afraid of damaging their reputations. It’s not that different now, even as many advancements have made HIV/AIDS feel locally like a plague of the past.

A blond-headed Scott throws himself valiantly into the role of Charleson with the urgency and commitment of any of his best work, but the problem isn’t his: It’s that the movie surrounding him dulls the greatness of his performance with its conventional storytelling, and over-complication in post. “Elsinore” is one of those movies where you can feel the filmmaker — here, Stone, known for both stage and screen — actively laboring to “open up the play” with the opportunities of film. But “Elsinore” was never a play. Instead, it’s a movie where every emotional beat worth lingering on, especially Charleson’s swan-song performances as Hamlet, is churned into a frenzied montage of editing that won’t let the material take a breath. Or the audience sink in.

Charleson played an Olympian athlete in the Best Picture-winning 1981 film “Chariots of Fire,” and an Anglican priest in the next year’s Best Picture topper, “Gandhi.” That was after playing an outré bisexual in the 1978 film “Jubilee,” directed by his former lover, the very openly gay director Derek Jarman, who died of AIDS in 1994. Aside from a handful of film credits, including one alluded to in “Elsinore” as Dario Argento’s giallo “Opera,” Charleson otherwise kept to the stage. Scott’s Charleson at one point in this film opines about how much more satisfying stage work is for an actor without an editor there to chop you up to pieces.

Stone, working from a screenplay by Stephen Beresford (himself a playwright), does not follow his protagonist’s lead. Every thoughtful consideration of Kaposi’s sarcoma lesions, or the retching of blood into buckets under the care of dutiful practitioner Dr. Margaret Johnson (Olivia Colman) to show the terrible realities of the disease, is whipped into what feels like a feature-length trailer. The best scenes out of the “Hamlet” production detailed here are tossed into a rushed montage near the movie’s end that gives us no sense of what we are promised is a performance of enormity. (The play in 1990 was directed by Richard Eyre, later the filmmaker behind features like the campy diva battle “Notes on a Scandal.” He’s played in “Elsinore” by Johnny Flynn.)

A couple of things are going on: “Elsinore” triggers in a watchful gay viewer’s senses the signifiers of what would be the AIDS awards-bait movie of serious gravitas. Tom Hanks’ Oscar-winning performance in “Philadelphia.” Ed Harris in “The Hours.” Everyone who came out of the cringey “Dallas Buyers Club” with Academy Awards in 2014. There are others, though more lost to time. We become suspicious of this device, especially when paired with great acting. Is HIV/AIDS being exploited in the package of a would-be Oscar hit? Or are we showing young queer audiences how it was, how we got to where we are, and how where that is still isn’t perfect? It can be both, but the structure of “Elsinore” tips toward the former.

Then, there’s a veritable, cheeky who’s-who of British gay literati and luminaries lingering about “Elsinore’s” 1989 London milieu that’s irresistible in theory, but often drab in execution. There’s Eyre, of course, and Jarman (David Dawson, as excellent and tender and wounded here as he was in his screen breakthrough “My Policeman”). The afterburn of his relationship with Charleson lingers affectingly, though. After some drinks, he invites Charleson home, but they instead part with one of those tacit kisses shared by people who once loved each other but can’t make it work anymore. There’s too much history here. The lingering scorches of Charleson’s secret affairs and friendships with various (and out) men make for the best parts of “Elsinore,” but there’s no life to the telling. (Joe Locke plays a young man also ailing in Johnson’s AIDS clinic alongside Charleson.)

The pallid grayness of Mike Eley’s cinematography is probably supposed to mimic the finality of HIV/AIDS taking hold, but there’s no passion aesthetically to counterbalance what becomes absolute misery onscreen to watch. I’m not in the business of comparing one gay social drama to another, but Robin Campillo’s 2017 Cannes-winning French drama “Beats Per Minute” imbued an albeit more activist-oriented story of ACT UP fighting against AIDS suppression in Paris with such heat and spectacle. Watching someone die of AIDS in a room becomes a bore unless anyone is given a reason to keep on living.

That reason is, of course, supposed to be Charleson’s performance in “Hamlet,” which is relegated to a whirlwind sequence at the movie’s end after many pained rehearsals where he refused to drop out (and where Johnson encouraged him to). Scott has a mesmerizing hold on his scenes, and he’s compelling to watch in his repartee with Colman as Johnson, who treated the disease at a time when it was most stigmatized. She named her groundbreaking HIV testing clinic after him; that’s how much he impacted her. We are told how much Charleson mattered to people, but never shown it. Which unfortunately shuffles off “Elsinore,” named after the Danish town where “Hamlet” was set and where its main character died, off to the realm of HIV/AIDS movies seeking prestige over deep feeling.

Andreessen thinks both camps are overlooking that vast economic sectors—including health care, education, housing, law, and government—are blocked from rapid transformation by heavy regulation, limited competition, and other factors.

While this could limit AI-driven job losses in those sectors, it could also make their services increasingly expensive relative to other goods and services.

“[Health care] is already a fifth of U.S. GDP, and I don’t see why it doesn’t go to a fourth, a third and then half and then ultimately 80%,” Andreessen said.

The implications of this view depend on your values, he added.  

“You might interpret this as good news, because it means the AI revolution is not going to, you know, destroy all the jobs or kill everybody,” he said. “You could also view this as very negative, which is [that] productivity … simply may not grow very much.”

Andreessen is the co-founder and general partner of Silicon Valley venture capital firm Andreessen Horowitz and co-leader of Fed chairman Kevin Warsh’s Productivity and Jobs Task Force.

Andreessen’s comments on Monday marked a change in tone, if not substance, from many of his previous remarks on AI.

Andreessen has long conditioned his more bullish predictions about AI on the technology being allowed to proliferate freely throughout the economy. His comments on Monday suggest he is less confident about the prospects of that happening.

His argument is rooted in an economic phenomenon known as “Baumol’s cost disease,” which states that as technological advances make some goods and services cheaper to produce, goods and services that are less susceptible to productivity gains become relatively more expensive. This means that rapid technological progress in certain industries could leave Americans devoting an ever-larger share of their incomes to industries that have barely changed.

Here are the most common college costs you can pay with scholarship money.

Scholarships commonly pay toward the tuition and fees your college charges.

Tuition usually covers only the cost to enroll in classes. Your college will also charge fees for a variety of reasons, such as attending a lab portion of a class, access to on-campus technology, facility maintenance and student activities. You’ll have to pay many of these fees regardless of if you use those facilities or participate in campus activities. Fees are often charged with tuition on the same bill so that you pay them together.

Tuition and fees cost students at public four-year institutions an average of $32,730 for the 2026-2027 school year, according to College Board’s 2026 report. If you use scholarship money to pay for these costs upfront, it can help you save a good chunk of money.

Scholarship money also pays for textbooks and supplies you need to complete your courses. These can include physical books, e-books and software licenses to access online content.

Depending on the scholarship, the cost of additional supplies that are required for certain courses might be covered as well. For example, nursing students might have to purchase scrubs with their college’s logo or medical supplies such as stethoscopes for clinical shifts.

Some scholarships will pay for the purchase of a laptop because you’ll likely need one to complete your coursework; however, you’ll want to confirm that the money can be used for that purchase before buying one.

Your scholarship might help cover some of the costs of living on campus, including paying for a dorm room and meal plans (often called room and board). To apply the funds, you usually have to live in university housing, which might be in a dormitory or even university apartments.

Many students can purchase meal plans to eat meals in their college and university cafeterias. These plans are usually for a set number of meals each week and might be required for first-year students. Because meal plans are part of college expenses, some scholarships will let you apply the funds to purchase a meal plan.

Some scholarships might also allow money to pay for campus parking fees because those are paid directly to the college or university.

It shouldn’t be this easy to pedal 25 mph: Specialized’s Turbo Vado 3 Evo

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

This commuter bike will have you blazing through streets without any range anxiety.

I’ve had only one experience of pedaling an e-bike after it ran out of juice, but it was enough to make checking the battery display an anxiety-driven habit: “Can I afford to use the highest assist level up this hill? Should I switch to eco mode to preserve power and make sure I have enough to get home with the bike lights on?”

These aren’t fun questions to have rattling around in your head while riding home in the dark.

But those intrusive thoughts never crossed my mind while I was testing Specialized’s Turbo Vado 3 Evo. I didn’t experience range anxiety, even while blowing through city streets at 25-plus mph (40.2 kph). The bike has so much juice (840 watt-hours) and power (810 watts of peak power and 105 newton-meters of torque) from Specialized’s 3.1 motor system that I could ride as far and as fast as I wanted without worrying about having enough battery to get back home.

Given my preference for biking over driving, I need a bike that can get me across town at a decent clip without draining an entire charge. That’s exactly what the Evo delivered on my longest rides.

What sold me on the bike was a 24-mile round trip that took 83 minutes and used just 56 percent of the battery. According to Specialized’s app, which connects to the bike over Bluetooth, I averaged 235 W of assist power while moving an average of 17 mph, though I was generally closer to 20 mph on stretches where I didn’t have to slow down. I also hit a max speed of 27.3 mph with little effort.

The bike is a beast, weighing around 70 pounds for the large-size frame I tested. Despite being the heaviest e-bike I’ve ridden, the Evo makes pedaling easier than ever. The bike lacks a throttle, so it takes a few rotations to get moving, but once the assist kicks in, it’s remarkable how easily you can reach the 18–20 mph range.

That higher speed comes from the bike’s Sport mode, which became my default setting. It requires a bit more pedaling to reach full assist, which explains why my smartwatch recorded my heart rate in the moderate-to-vigorous zone if I was riding above 20 mph.) The other modes are Eco (minimal assist, with you supplying most of the power), Turbo (max power with little effort, making uphill climbs trivial), and Auto (which adjusts assist level to your pedaling). You can also tune each setting to your liking.

The added bulk is also meant to handle rougher terrain. I’ve had the occasional flat tire from road debris on slimmer bikes, so I appreciate the sturdier, all-terrain, flat-resistant tires. Still, the 70-pound frame makes me hesitant to venture too far from pavement. I won’t be bombing down any mountain bike trails, but it’s helpful to have the option to jump off a curb or ride on dirt or gravel roads.

The weight makes the bike markedly different from the previous Turbo Vado we reviewed; this isn’t a bike I’d want to ride without assistance. Even the Eco mode, which you’d have to use exclusively to get the bike’s purported 150-km (93-mile), five-hour range, felt a bit sluggish compared to the more powerful modes I preferred. Specialized also sells a 280-Whr range extender, but at $700, it feels like overkill.

The RockShox Psylo suspension fork, with 120 mm of travel, also made this the smoothest-riding e-bike I’ve tested. Other e-bikes I’ve ridden have felt stiff over even small bumps in the road, but the Evo’s bounciness made hitting bumps feel like a nice change of pace. I used to be scared of going over a curb, and that’s no longer the case. The 40 mm dropper seatpost is another nice feature, giving riders a bit of travel and letting them lower the seat to make it easier to touch the ground when stopped or dismounting.

Credit: Specialized Credit: Specialized

While other e-bikes I’ve owned initially offered good range, storing them in colder weather has been an issue for their longevity.

So I’m grateful that Specialized made the downtube battery removable. Being able to store the battery in my house helps since I keep my bikes in an outdoor shed—I no longer have to worry about it sitting in freezing temperatures. A full charge takes four hours, though you can tweak the battery to cap charging at 80 percent to further extend its life.

The other electric components are nice, too. On the Evo 5.0 I rode, a touchscreen lets you cycle through ride data, including your speed, estimated range remaining, and power assistance level. The front light even comes with low-beam (450 lumens) and high-beam (805 lumens) settings, making my night rides more visible.

If I have any complaints about my time with this bike, they boil down to two things. First, it’s incredibly heavy, making it difficult for many people to take up stairs. Second, the convenience of riding at high speed and making quick journeys has left my road bike gathering dust unless I’m specifically going out for a workout (and those are harder now that I’m so accustomed to riding the Evo). That’s more of a personal issue than a mark against the bike, though. It’s fun to ride, but maybe at the cost of consistent manual pedaling.

And then there’s the cost. I tested the Evo 5.0 ($5,200), which sits between the 4.0 ($4,500) and 6.0 ($7,000) in terms of cost, performance, and features. At $5,200, though, I would’ve liked to see a phone mount on the 5.0, which is a common enough feature on less-expensive bikes.

Those prices make securing the bike even more important, and Specialized offers a few safety features to thwart theft. As mentioned, you can remove the battery, lock the back wheel, and disable the power system; there’s also Apple Find My integration if the bike ever escapes your purview (a bummer if you use Android, though).

In all, Specialized has upgraded the e-bike experience enough for me to want to upgrade, though the price makes me blanch a bit. Maybe I can subsidize a purchase by selling an older e-bike.

Housing Starts Jump, But Drop in Permits Paints Dim Picture for Homebuilding

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Construction has seen a significant slowdown from continued market challenges in supply chain and affordability issues. Despite a surprising uptick in housing starts in this month’s New Residential Construction report, economists say the overall picture remains troubled.

The latest data from the Department of Housing and Urban Development and the Census Bureau saw housing starts jump 9.9% month-over-month to a rate of 1,309,000, also seeing a 2.8% year-over-year rise.

The rise was directly due to a leap in multifamily starts, up 25.5% month-over-month and 22% year-over-year to a rate of 433,000. Multifamily construction did see an uptick in building permits in September by 5.9% month-over-month and 1% year-over-year, which appears to have translated into the uptick in starts.

Single-family starts also saw a month-over-month rise of 4% to a rate of 864,000, but were still down year-over-year by 3.2%.

Notably, September’s original starts rate of 1,221,000 was negatively revised to 1,191,000, reflecting the continuation of the slower trend in construction. Single-family starts were also negatively revised from a rate of 859,000 to 831,000.

National Association of Home Builders (NAHB) Chairman Bill Owens said that the gain in starts this month is “encouraging,” but “does not necessarily signal a sustained recovery.”

“Builder confidence weakened in November as higher mortgage rates and affordability challenges weighed on buyers,” he continued. “Stronger demand is needed to sustain construction, while meeting the significant long-term need for more housing requires easing regulatory barriers to home building.”

Danushka Nanayakkara-Skillington—NAHB’s assistant VP for Forecasting and Analysis—agreed with Owens’ sentiment, adding that the gain is “welcome,” but the “market remains constrained by elevated borrowing costs, rising construction costs and weakening builder confidence.”

“The monthly gain should be viewed cautiously as affordability challenges and economic uncertainty persist,” she continued.

Looking regionally, the Midwest and the South were up month-over-month and year-over-year, while the Northeast and the West were down on both counts.

The Midwest was up 48.4% month-over-month and 17.2% year-over-year. The South was up 10.5% month-over-month and 12.2% year-over-year. The Northeast was down 20.8% month-over-month and 34.5% year-over-year. The West was down 2.6% month-over-month and 11.6% year-over-year.

Realtor.com® Senior Economist Joel Berner described the signals presented in this month’s report as “mixed,” and noted that single-family construction is still down year-over-year.

“This month’s data is an improvement, but from a very low baseline readout in both September and last October,” he continued.

Despite the rise in starts in October, building permits did not paint a picture of a continued upward trend.

Permits were down 7.4% month-over-month and 8.7% year-over-year to a rate of 1,295,000. Both multifamily and single-family permits were down month-over-month and year-over-year. Multifamily permits were down 5.2% month-over-month and 0.4% year-over-year to a rate of 475,000. Single-family permits were down 8.7% month-over-month and 12.9% year-over-year to a rate of 772,000.

Berner noted that this was permitting’s “lowest level since the early days of the COVID-19 pandemic in May 2020.” He also called this month’s permit data a “disappointment,” as it demonstrated that builders are “slowing down activity at the top of their funnel, which will result in slower months of starts and completions later on.”

Permits were also down both month-over-month and year-over-year in the Northeast, South and West, while the Midwest was up on both counts.

Housing completions had a more mixed result in October. Completions were up 6.5% month-over-month to a rate of 1,272,000, but were down 10.4% year-over-year. Both single-family and multifamily completions were also up month-over-month (6.3% and 9%, respectively), and were also down year-over-year (-10.7% and -9%, respectively).

“If you focus on home starts, this month’s new construction data is a welcome improvement from last month’s, but zooming out for more context shows a residential construction industry that is still struggling to find its footing,” concluded Berner. ”(S)oftening permits will put downward pressure on starts and completions in the months to come. In a country facing a supply gap of over four million homes, this represents a step in the wrong direction.”

The US Lost Its Measles Elimination Status. It’ll Take Years to Get It Back

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The US has hit terrible twin milestones over the past week: two more people died from measles, bringing this year’s death count to double digits, and the country officially lost its status as a country that had eliminated the highly infectious disease.

The US eliminated measles in 2000 and maintained that status for 26 years. Now, the nation faces an uphill battle to vanquish the virus once more, and it will take years to be measles-free again.

Noel Brewer, a professor of health behavior at the University of North Carolina, presented a report last week to the Pan American Health Organization showing continuous measles transmission for at least 12 months in the US—erasing one of the nation’s greatest public health achievements.

WIRED asked him how he felt after PAHO officials reached consensus the country was no longer measles-free. He paused.

“I have not actually thought about my reaction,” he says. After a beat, he says that the loss is “a somber moment for public health” that’s particularly wrenching because it’s a “completely avoidable situation.” Mexico and Canada have managed to wind down their outbreaks, but “measles in America is a runaway train,” Brewer says. “There's no end in sight, and it may even be gaining speed.”

PAHO director Jarbas Barbosa da Silva Jr. officially announced the change in status on Tuesday. “Losing a measles elimination status has broader implications than losing a public health designation,” he said at a press conference. “It means the virus has circulated for a prolonged period of time, putting families and communities at risk of preventable illness, hospitalization, and death.” Bolivia and Mexico also lost their elimination status this year; Canada lost it a year ago.

Brewer presented three key lines of evidence showing that measles has become endemic in the US. The outbreak in Utah and Arizona has gone on for more than 12 months, and the US has had measles cases every week since January 20, 2025. The genomic data also shows a great deal of similarity across states, indicating the clusters are closely linked—and each week, there are cases where the sources can’t be confirmed, indicating they didn’t come from travel or known contact with other patients.

“We have a problem on our hands,” says William Moss, epidemiology professor at Johns Hopkins. And it’s not going away anytime soon. To reclaim its status as measles-free, the US will have to prove it has gone a full year without continued transmission. That’s unlikely to happen under the Trump administration—and its actions have made it more difficult for future leaders.

“Reestablishing elimination is not just about reporting fewer cases—it also requires convincing the world that we have a strong enough surveillance system to find cases and deaths,” Jennifer Nuzzo, an epidemiologist and director of the Pandemic Center at Brown University, tells WIRED in a text.

The fact that the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and state health departments differ on how many people have died from measles “is a sign of dysfunction,” Nuzzo says. There have been 10 confirmed measles deaths so far this year—seven in Pennsylvania, two in Wisconsin, and one in New York—but the CDC only lists six.

Such chaos also breeds distrust, says Frederic Bertley, president and CEO of the Center of Science and Industry. That's “a huge problem” for a public trying to parse who is telling the truth and whether they should listen, he says.

Even as measles was eliminated, anti-vaccine rhetoric began to rise, especially around the measles, mumps, and rubella vaccine. Vaccination rates began falling—spurred in part by Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr., an environmental lawyer turned anti-vaccine activist who published a now-retracted article alleging a link between vaccines and autism in 2005. He continued crusading against vaccines as head of Children’s Health Defense and at the helm of the Department of Health and Human Services during President Donald Trump’s second term.

The Trump administration has done little to stop the outbreaks, cutting billions in funding from state, local, territorial, and tribal public health departments and offering conflicting messages on vaccines. During a televised February 2025 cabinet meeting following the first child death in a decade, Kennedy said “it's not unusual, we have measles outbreaks every year.” The CDC hasn’t held a single press conference on measles throughout the entire outbreak; as PAHO was making its measles status announcement on Tuesday, HHS sent out an email about Kennedy’s cooking show.

“The measles elimination designation is not what protects Americans. Vaccination does,” CDC director Erica Schwartz said in a statement. “Losing a designation doesn't mean we abandon an evidence-based approach. It means we remain focused on stopping outbreaks.” Kennedy posted on X that “I continue to recommend MMR vaccination as the most effective way to prevent measles.”

To regain its elimination status, the US needs to address barriers to healthcare access, missed vaccination opportunities, misinformation, and vaccine hesitancy, Barbosa said. That includes mounting wide-reaching vaccination campaigns, expanding laboratory testing capacity, and responding quickly to cases, including contact tracing. It’s the bread and butter of public health, but the longer it takes to create a comprehensive response, the more it will cost, experts warn.

Future efforts to contain measles will require “a stronger centralized federal response to these outbreaks” paired with support for “very local responses,” says Moss. That means identifying communities with longstanding under-vaccination and understanding why, he says, as well as improving trust in public health guidance.

“Immunity gaps” tend to concentrate in specific geographic areas and communities, and national or even state-level data can “mask these significant inequalities, providing an incomplete picture of the population's actual level of protection,” Barbosa said.

The CDC announced $100 million in August and September—more than a year after the outbreak began—to help fund state and regional efforts to combat measles, but Congress will need to allocate $100 million to $400 million more for measles elimination in coming months, Brewer says.

“More than 90 percent of children get the measles vaccine, Brewer points out. “This is a vaccine that has extraordinary public support, and it goes across the aisle.”

But to achieve herd immunity, vaccination rates need to be at least 95 percent due to how contagious the disease is. High transmissibility also means measles is very hard to uproot once it begins to spread.

Yet ending national measles transmission is “its own reward,” Brewer says. It spares children’s lives, keeps them out of hospitals, prevents blindness and other lifelong complications. “We no longer have that benefit,” he says. “Children are now being stalked by this incurable and deadly disease.”

Emotional intelligence, the ability to notice and manage your own feelings and read other people’s, is a skill you can build. When an email or comment at work makes you tense, put the feeling into one word, such as irritated or worried, before you answer. Naming it gives you a moment to choose your reply.

A curt email or a sharp remark in a meeting takes seconds, and living it down takes far longer. If coworkers say you come across harsher than you mean to, those first seconds are what they’re reacting to.

Emotional intelligence grows with training. A 2019 analysis of 58 studies, published in Human Resource Management Review, found that training programs raised participants’ scores by a moderate amount.

Besides giving you a moment, naming the feeling may weaken the emotional reaction itself. In a brain-imaging study published in Psychological Science, people viewed upsetting images, sometimes naming the emotion in them and sometimes doing other tasks with them. After you name the feeling, add a pause. Give a tense email or message an hour before you answer, or a night when the reply can wait. In a conversation, listen to understand rather than to answer, and ask about the other person’s side before you give yours. Friction that keeps coming back with one coworker calls for resolving the conflict itself, not just a calmer reply.

Jackets featuring decorative knots, elaborate loops, and ornate toggles have emerged as one of the season’s biggest outerwear trends, offering an alternative to standard buttons and zippers.

In particular, frog fastenings are having a resurgence, bringing a fanciful finishing touch to everything from minimalist wool peacoats to tailored military-inspired jackets. Typically constructed from fabric or braided cord, the fasteners consist of a knotted button and a loop. Originally designed to secure garments, the closures have evolved into a more ornamental role with intricate shapes and floral-like designs.

While they may seem new for the season, frog-fastener jackets have a history that spans centuries. In Chinese dress, knotted closures known as pankou have long appeared on garments such as the qipao and tangzhuang. By the 18th and 19th centuries, frogging had also become a feature of European military uniforms, worn by cavalry regiments whose fitted jackets were adorned with braided cords.

Fashion has revisited the detail since then. Oscar de la Renta incorporated frog fastenings into his designs in the 1990s, while Valentino explored versions for its fall 2015 couture collection. Meanwhile, labels like Khaite and Ann Demeulemeester embraced military-inspired looks featuring ornamental braiding and embellished fronts.

Today’s store-rack versions range from collarless blazers with understated knots to dramatic outerwear designs featuring detailed cording. Brands like Róhe have introduced contemporary takes on frogging, while Hong Kong–based fashion label Sau Lee has reimagined traditional Tang-style jackets with modern silhouettes.

Most camera specs between these phones are surprisingly comparable, so the differences you’re about to see are largely caused by some pretty massive differences in processing. I already covered Vivo’s wild new AI camera mode, so let’s go for something a little closer to normal smartphone camera use: a day at the Biltmore.

Perhaps the biggest difference between these phones is Vivo’s pursuit of “perfection,” while Google seems to embrace a more “natural” look. Rockstar revealed earlier this month that GTA 6 targets 30 frames per second on all platforms, news that wasn’t well-received by a growing cohort of frame rate-conscious players. While many modern games offer options for console players to adjust a game’s visual fidelity — often with a quality mode that prioritizes visuals at the cost of frame rate, a performance mode that prioritizes frame rate (usually aiming for around 60 FPS), and a balanced mode that’s somewhere in between — GTA 6’s technical demands apparently make it difficult to run at higher frame rates on modern systems.

"Our base presentation makes use of ray-traced reflections and global illumination," explained technology production director Marc Chambers. "On the PS5 Pro, this is enhanced with the addition of ray-traced shadows, as well as overall added clarity through the use of PlayStation Spectral Super Resolution (PSSR). The PS5 Pro features an upgraded graphics processor and upscaling technology that we leverage to increase the game’s visual fidelity. While the PS5 Pro’s central processor is slightly better than the PS5 base console, it is not powerful enough to run the game’s complex simulation at higher frame rates."

While Rockstar hasn’t officially announced whether GTA 6 will eventually be released on PC, rumors suggest a port could arrive as early as 2027. That said, Chambers’ comments cast some doubt on this quick timeline, making it clear that the game is quite graphically intensive. A PC version would presumably let users toggle ray tracing and other visual features, which, based on Chambers’ comments, sound like something Rockstar is hesitant to allow. It's unclear how much of the studio's disinterest in a performance mode on console is related to the game's core technical infrastructure versus Rockstar's preference for how this experience is presented: it's possible they technically could ​​​​​​release a performance mode on console, but simply don't think the visual trade-off is worth it.
