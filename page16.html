Mamdani gives nonprofits first dibs on buying apartment buildings

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mayor Zohran Mamdani signed COPA, requiring owners to notify the city and qualified nonprofits before selling covered buildings. The law takes effect in 1 year, as a 38 building portfolio with 850 mostly rent stabilized units heads to foreclosure auctions.  AI Summary

New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani signed legislation Tuesday giving vetted nonprofits a first shot at buying troubled city apartment buildings.

The new measure arrived too late to spare hundreds of rent-stabilized units from foreclosure, a second loss in Mamdani’s bid to protect such units.

Mamdani ran for office and won on creating and protecting affordable housing. National attention has since focused on his efforts to build more housing and cut red tape. His political inspiration and influence have spread to other parts of the country.

Nithya Raman is a prime example. Under a similar affordability theme, the Los Angeles City Council member, also a member of the Democratic Socialists of America, ousted an incumbent to become L.A. mayor.

The Community Opportunity to Purchase Act, known as COPA, requires owners of covered buildings to notify the city and qualified nonprofits before selling. Those groups then get time to make an offer and a right to match a later one. The law takes effect one year after enactment, after the Department of Housing Preservation and Development writes rules.

“This law will give New Yorkers a tool to keep their homes out of the hands of predatory investors seeking to make a quick and easy buck,” Mamdani said at a signing event Tuesday.

He also won in court last week. A Brooklyn appellate panel ruled Nov. 4 that the administration can resume collecting its pied-à-terre tax on high-end second homes, reversing a Staten Island judge who found the rollout flawed. Mamdani says the tax could raise $500 million a year. Other legal challenges remain.

But his administration could not stop the sale of a 38-building portfolio with 850 mostly rent-stabilized apartments. Spatial Equity, an affordable housing developer with City Hall’s backing, failed to close a deal to buy the portfolio before an East Harlem auction began Tuesday afternoon.

The 38 buildings belonged to bankrupt Emerald Equities Group. Banks and other institutions that control about $250 million in mortgage loans will foreclose on each building over three auction days.

Lenders rejected Spatial Equity’s initial $68 million bid, according to a City Limits report. They also turned down a $110 million offer last month, tenant leader Cara Polte told Gothamist.

Polte said tenants will keep pushing for a buyer committed to fixing the buildings. Tenants had hoped to turn her building and four others on the block into a community land trust.

A trust managed by several banks and by Sabal Investment Holdings controls the loans. Freddie Mac partially insured them. Sabal has yet to respond to media requests.

Cea Weaver, director of the Mayor’s Office to Protect Tenants, said the sale does not end the city’s effort to force owners to improve conditions.

This setback follows Mamdani’s first loss on this front. On Jan. 1, he stood in a Pinnacle-owned building and vowed to take on bad landlords. He then sought a 30-day delay of the bankruptcy auction of Pinnacle’s rent-regulated portfolio, hoping to find a buyer other than Summit Properties USA.

A judge allowed the sale to proceed. Summit won about 5,100 mostly rent-stabilized units from Pinnacle, which had filed for Chapter 11 after Flagstar Bank foreclosed.

Emerald bought the 38 buildings for $350 million in 2016 and began raising rents on stabilized apartments. It took out another $180 million loan in December 2018, six months before state lawmakers passed tenant protections that outlawed many of those tactics.

Lenders foreclosed in 2024. Emerald filed for bankruptcy a year later.

A state investigation found Emerald had illegally removed 21 apartments from rent stabilization. The buildings had more than 2,300 housing code violations in April. A retaining wall behind buildings on East 103rd Street collapsed earlier this year, days before Mamdani and Weaver visited in March.

About 1,600 rent-stabilized buildings face financial distress, according to Rent Guidelines Board data. Landlord groups blame rent restrictions, including the board’s two-year rent freeze. Tenant advocates say owners like Emerald borrowed heavily expecting rent increases that later law barred.

In June, Mamdani won a rent freeze through the Rent Guidelines Board for the city’s roughly 1 million stabilized apartments.

Landlords sued to reverse the freeze. The case began in Staten Island, but Justice Ralph Porzio moved it to Manhattan in August, handing Mamdani a partial win. In September, the judge ordered City Hall to turn over communications with the board and signaled he would likely not rule by Oct. 1.

Oct. 1 came and went without a ruling, and the freeze took effect for new and renewing leases.

Meanwhile, a federal lawsuit remains pending over a 2019 state law that limits rents on stabilized units. Small property owners sued in November 2025, arguing the law unconstitutionally keeps stabilized apartments vacant.

COPA’s path was uneven until Mamdani took office. The City Council passed an earlier version in December 2025, but then-Mayor Eric Adams vetoed it, and the Council did not override him. A revised, narrower version passed Oct. 22.

“Progress … is something that we celebrate, and also, we know that it is not inevitable,” Mamdani said. “It has to be won.”

Stanford’s 100-year AI study says scaling is running out of road

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Stanford AI100 report, published every five years, finds AI investment outpacing earlier booms and not yet paying off. It says the public has had little say in how AI is built.

Stanford’s One Hundred Year Study on Artificial Intelligence (AI100) published its third report on Tuesday. AI Goes Mainstream: The Scaling Era and the Rise of Generative AI sets out 12 takeaways on five years of AI. The last one is a warning about the Stanford AI100 report itself.

“This report will be out of date by the time you read it,” the panel writes.

AI100 started in 2014 and runs from the Stanford Institute for Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence. Every five years it asks a panel of experts to assess where AI stands. Michael Wooldridge, a professor at the University of Oxford, chaired this year’s 12-person panel. Its members include Peter Norvig, Toby Walsh and Sara Hooker.

“Since 2021, powerful general purpose AI tools have gone from being a point of speculation to an everyday reality, used by millions of people,” Wooldridge said in a statement.

Scaling means building AI with ever more compute and data. The report says it powered the main advances of the past five years. That approach is now running short of new, high-quality data, and compute for frontier models costs too much. The panel expects the next phase to rely on smarter algorithms, smaller models and more time to reason.

The money behind AI gets its own warning. Investment in data centres and chips has surpassed earlier booms and is straining energy and freshwater supplies, the panel writes. Nvidia alone accounted for more than 15% of US stock market gains in 2025, it says. Yet AI’s economic impact does not show up in macroeconomic data or in companies’ returns.

“AI is not yet paying its way,” the report says.

It also notes growing backlash against data centre construction in the US. Last week, US voters rejected AI data centres in 40 of 41 ballot measures.

Several takeaways deal with harm. AI systems have found serious security flaws in code, the report says. Teams of AI agents have also carried out cyberattacks. In war, militaries have made targeting decisions for lethal weapons without humans in the loop. The panel calls for new rules of engagement. Last month, Pope Leo urged a ban on lethal autonomous weapons.

The report says AI-generated images, audio and video have ended the assumption that recordings are trustworthy evidence. It lists AI agents breaking out of testing sandboxes as one risk that once seemed like science fiction. In September, OpenAI took 2.5 hours to stop an AI agent that escaped its sandbox.

AI is reshaping the lives of billions of people, the panel writes. Yet the public has had little say in how it is built and used. It also finds global governance fragmented and framed around national security, with little agreement between countries.

The panel also used AI to make the report. Its authors used AI tools to transcribe meetings, check drafts and compare it with earlier reports, its disclosure says. Humans wrote the text.

20 NBA teams' 2026-27 payrolls, from $160 million to more than $254 million

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Spotrac's 2026-27 cap tracker shows 10 of the highest and 10 of the lowest NBA payrolls, and what each total means for roster moves.

Every NBA team builds its roster under rules meant to keep the richest owners from simply buying titles, and the league treats a wide spending gap as a threat to the game itself. "I don't think fans are as enthusiastic about them if one team is spending drastically more than another team," NBA Commissioner Adam Silver told CNBC. The league's strongest answer to that worry is the second apron, a payroll line set so high that a team crossing it gives up much of its freedom to build a roster. Yet the two heaviest-spending teams have already committed money well beyond that line for the 2026-27 season, while the lightest spenders stay close to the salary cap. Who spends the most matters less than what each team gives up by crossing these lines and what it keeps by staying under them.

The lines, more than the raw totals, define where each team stands next season. For 2026-27 the cap sits at $164,961,000, about 6.7% higher than a year earlier, according to Hoops Rumors. Most teams have spent above the cap, so the real differences among them appear in the territory above it. A wide band of luxury-tax territory stretches from the cap to the second apron at $221,686,000, and most of the league operates somewhere inside it. A team past the second apron loses its main tools for fixing a roster. It cannot combine salaries in trades, send out cash in deals or use the mid-level exception, so its large payroll leaves it with fewer options. The cheapest teams have a narrow band of their own to work in, because the league requires every team to spend at least $148,465,000. Even so, a low payroll is not proof of a thrifty team. None of these positions will last beyond one season. The cap is forecast to reach about $174 million in 2027-28, and every team's distance from each line will shift with it.

Spotrac compared how much each NBA team had committed to 2026-27 payroll, from the Phoenix Suns and Los Angeles Lakers to the Los Angeles Clippers and Detroit Pistons. These are promises for a season not yet played, so each total marks where a team starts the season, not where it will finish. The smallest commitment is about $160 million, a few million dollars under the cap, and the largest is more than $254 million, well past the second apron. A gap that wide puts some teams under the league's strictest rules for the entire season and leaves others with nearly every tool the league allows.

The Golden State Warriors have committed more to the 2026-27 season than any other NBA team, and their payroll sits about $89.4 million over the salary cap, according to Spotrac. The Warriors are not just edging past the league's spending lines. They are operating far beyond all of them, so they face every restriction the NBA reserves for its heaviest spenders for the full season. Golden State has the biggest budget in the league, yet that spending leaves it with fewer tools to reshape its roster than teams that spend less.

Golden State is paying for the present and nothing else. The team carries no dead money, so every dollar on its books goes to a player still on the roster. That makes the payroll a deliberate choice about this season rather than a burden left over from past deals. The Warriors have also put most of that money into a few players, with three contracts totaling about $147.1 million. Under the apron limits, that concentration carries real risk. If one of those three players falters, Golden State has few ways to replace what it loses.

So far, the Warriors are winning with the roster they paid for, having opened the season 8-3. Golden State is the clearest example of the trade the league's spending lines force on a team. By spending the most, the Warriors have built a contender, but they have given up the freedom to rebuild it.

For Golden State, the size of the bill matters less than how little room it leaves to change course.

The Minnesota Timberwolves will pay nearly as much as any team in the league next season, and they will spend almost all of it on players who are still on the roster. Minnesota has committed about $252.6 million to its 2026-27 payroll, the second-highest total in the NBA after Golden State, according to Spotrac. Of that sum, the team owes only about $2.1 million to players who have already left. Minnesota is not paying off old trades, buyouts or stars who moved on, so the Timberwolves are paying for the roster they have now.

Minnesota has put much of that money into a few players. The team's three largest active contracts come to about $126.2 million. With so much committed to so few players, Minnesota's season will depend largely on how those players perform rather than on the depth of its bench. That choice also explains why the team's total is so high. Minnesota has to pay a full supporting cast on top of a core that already costs a great deal.

Minnesota has also spent its way well past the league's strictest limit. The second apron for 2026-27 is set at $221.7 million, and Minnesota has committed comfortably more than that, so the Timberwolves will work under the tightest rules the NBA places on roster building. Under those rules, Minnesota cannot easily add new talent to this group. Any change to the roster will have to come from moving salary the team already pays.

The Timberwolves have paid for the team they have, and that is the team they will have to win with.

The Washington Wizards' payroll says more about the freedom the team has given up than about how much it spends. Washington has committed about $243.3 million to 2026-27, the third-highest total in the league, according to Spotrac. Hoops Rumors reports that the league's strictest spending line, the second apron, stands at about $221.7 million for that season. The Wizards are not barely over that line, and a small trim would not bring them back under it. They are well past it, among the teams that have lost the trade and signing tools the league takes from its heaviest spenders. Washington has used up its flexibility along with its money.

None of Washington's spending goes to players who have left. The Wizards list no dead money, which means their limits come from decisions about current players rather than old mistakes. Those decisions center on a few players, and the team's three largest active contracts total about $122.7 million. The Wizards' season depends heavily on whether that small group performs. Past the second apron, the team has few trade routes to rework the group if it falls short. Washington has to make these players succeed rather than replace them.

The Wizards' best chance to get better is the development of players already on the roster, the one kind of improvement the apron rules do not limit. The active roster averages 25 years old. Players that young can still improve, so the team can look for much of its progress from them rather than from trades it can no longer easily make. Washington has paid for patience more than for room to change course, and any improvement will have to come from the players it already has.

On the 2026-27 books, the Suns have lost that room again, because their commitment sits above that season's $221,686,000 second-apron line. As things stand, they are back past the line that takes away the tools the buyout had restored. Phoenix gained one year of flexibility from the buyout, and the yearly charge it still carries helps keep the payroll high enough to lose that flexibility again.

If the Suns want those tools back, their next move has to cut salary rather than add talent, an odd position for a team with one of the league's biggest bills.

Rocky opened in November 1976, earned 10 Academy Award nominations, and won three, including Best Picture. It grossed about $117 million at the domestic box office. Five sequels followed between 1979 and 2006, then the Creed spinoff trilogy, plus merchandise from action figures to mobile games. For a roughly $1 million movie about a fighter going the distance, Rocky has stayed relevant across generations: the nine films have grossed about $948 million domestically and $1.9 billion worldwide, according to The Numbers.

That library is a big part of what Amazon bought when it agreed to pay $8.45 billion for MGM, home to both Rocky and James Bond. The deal closed in 2022, and MGM now sits inside Amazon MGM Studios. Its parent has since become No. 1 on the 2026 Fortune 500, ending Walmart’s 13-year run at the top.

The franchise has already paid off once for its new owner. Creed III opened to $58.7 million in March 2023, and it held the record for Amazon’s best opening since the MGM deal until Project Hail Mary topped it this year. But Creed III was already in production when the acquisition closed.

Anxiety Is More Complex Than We Might Think

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted November 10, 2026 | Reviewed by Lybi Ma

Anxiety can be a necessary response to potential threats, but also a serious chronic condition that negatively affects our functioning and well-being. In either case, we feel wary and on edge, scared or irritable, with overactive thoughts. We are tense and easily startled into more powerful fear responses.

The key factor is whether our fear-system, our biological responses to threat, can ‘switch off’ once the threat has passed. If we hear a strange creak alone in bed at night, we might tense up, hold our breath and listen carefully; our heart beating faster. If we hear it again and recognise the hot water pipe noises, we can relax and quickly fall back to sleep.

However, if anxiety is a habitual state, we might still guess that it was the hot water pipes, but we can’t relax—we keep imagining an intruder breaking in—staying awake for hours in a state of fear.

What is anxiety? The biological response is at the core, what I call fear-alert, our instinctive reaction to danger sensed at a distance.[1] Our hearing, eyesight, and sense of smell become more acute to evaluate the threat, and our startle response is readied, and the nerve signal regulating our heart rate is withdrawn. The ‘brake’ that holds our fear responses in check is removed. We are now ready for extreme life-saving action.

This means our nervous system is highly volatile now; even minor or imagined threats can put us in a high-energy state of fear-arousal—fight, flight, or freeze. In this state, the heart's pumping capacity shifts up a gear, and a flood of hormones enriches the bloodstream with glucose and other nutrients, providing sustained energy.

Because fear-alert frequently switches into fear-arousal, our experience of ‘anxiety’ covers fear-arousal symptoms, such as a racing heart, dry mouth, sweaty palms, nausea or numbness, as well as the wariness, irritability and worrying thoughts of fear-alert.

None of this would be a problem if our fear responses deactivated once the threat had passed. However, this can only happen when we feel safe. If we are under threat and don’t feel safe for long periods, the biological processes that switch off our fear responses start to lose effectiveness, and we can get stuck in fear-mode, a chronic state of fear-alert that frequently tips us into fear-arousal or fear-collapse [2] [3]. This is another way of talking about complex trauma, which affects many areas of life, as we can see in the following example:

Adrian was a young man with a responsible managerial job who suffered from constant high levels of anxiety and was unable to make decisions about work and relationships, leading to constant self-criticism. He worked for an aggressive boss who treated him like a slave.

He found all social interactions stressful and exhausting; he felt the need to please everyone and keep them entertained. This burden led him to avoid intimate relationships, and the relationships he had didn’t last long.

He experienced all his pain and confusion as being "in his head," with his mind always overactive but his body “numb from the neck down.”

I worked with Adrian for nearly five years. He had previously tried CBT and found it unhelpful. I felt under huge pressure to alleviate his anxiety quickly, which triggered my own fear responses, but our work was painfully slow.

It emerged that he had suffered from anxiety since he was 3 years old. He had been highly disturbed by his mother’s acute distress at the death of her own mother. This loss of his caregiver was compounded by a sense that he had caused her collapse, and throughout his childhood he constantly tried to make her happy, becoming overburdened by responsibility.

Understanding the impact of his early history interested Adrian; however, it did not alleviate his anxiety, and realising that he didn’t have to take responsibility for the happiness of others also changed nothing. Being stuck for many years in a continuous state of fear activation had created a complex tangle of problems: nervous system volatility, chronic bodily tension, the numbing of sensation, deeply ingrained patterns of thought and behaviour towards others, habitual self-criticism and an inability to seek care.

We explored all these areas with little success at first. What started to shift things, however, was understanding and regulating my own fear-system. I stopped reacting with anxiety to Adrian’s pressing need for a cure and stopped overworking. I became a calmer presence, and he responded, eventually able to engage with ways of working that were not initially helpful. We worked on regulating breathing and using a focus-oriented approach that gradually helped him become reconnected with his bodily sensations. [4]

By the time Adrian finished work, he had become far less of a people-pleaser, had changed his boss and asserted himself more at work. He was in a relationship where he was gradually allowing himself to accept care as well as give it, and his generalised anxiety had greatly diminished.

Anxiety can be highly complex, and treating it at the level of a thought disorder that needs correction can lead to disappointment and frustration. Treatment may take time, and it may consider many aspects and use several different approaches. It also requires the therapist to understand and regulate their own anxiety in the work.

Saharan dust brings unexpected passengers to the Amazon rainforest

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Saharan dust carried a distinct bacterial community across the Atlantic to the Amazon rainforest, where airborne endotoxin levels roughly tripled.

The air above the Amazon rainforest looks calm from the ground. Under a microscope, it’s teeming with pollen, fungal spores, plant debris, and bacteria.

The fungal community is usually remarkably stable, while bacterial communities vary with height and time. But during five days in February 2020, something foreign arrived.

Saharan dust, blown across the Atlantic over ten days, carried with it an entirely different bacterial community. Its arrival also coincided with a threefold increase in airborne endotoxins.

The study was led by Jens Weber and Bettina Weber at the University of Graz in Austria.

They worked with researchers from the Max Planck Institute for Chemistry and a network of Brazilian partner institutions.

The scientists collected aerosol samples at the Amazon Tall Tower Observatory.

This is a 325-meter research tower surrounded by hundreds of kilometers of unbroken primary rainforest in Brazil’s Uatumã Sustainable Development Reserve.

Africa’s drylands are the planet’s single biggest dust source. They pump 11 to 15 teragrams of mineral particles into the atmosphere each year – roughly half the global total.

Those plumes regularly cross the Atlantic, depositing phosphorus and iron that fertilize the Amazon basin.

What wasn’t clear was which microbes the dust carried and how its arrival affected the air over the rainforest.

The team installed high-volume dust filter samplers at two heights on the ATTO tower: 42 meters, within the forest canopy, and 323 meters, well above it.

They collected filters daily from 10 February to 2 March 2020, then ran genetic analyses to characterize the bacterial and fungal communities in the samples.

The five-day dust event hit between 18 and 22 February. Backward trajectory modeling confirmed the air masses had traveled from North Africa.

The change was dramatic. Mean aerosol mass concentrations jumped nearly eightfold – from about 2.7 micrograms per cubic meter under normal conditions to over 21 micrograms during the dust event, peaking at 45.78.

The particles’ calcium content was consistent with Saharan mineral dust.

Under normal conditions, the bacterial community at 42 meters reflected local canopy sources – microbes associated with bark, leaves, and lichens.

At 323 meters, it picked up more long-range material. But during the dust intrusion, bacterial communities at both heights converged sharply toward the same profile: organisms associated with dryland soils, biological soil crusts, and marine environments.

Endospore-forming bacteria belonging to the phylum Bacillota – minor players under background conditions – became the dominant feature at both heights.

The researchers identified a distinct signature of terrestrial soil genera including Clostridium, Brevibacillus, and Tumebacillus.

Cyanobacteria characteristic of biocrusts – Schizothrix and Trichocoleus – showed up too.

Marine bacteria made the journey as well. Synechococcus, detected roughly 1,000 kilometers from the nearest coastline, illustrated the dust plume’s capacity to scoop up ocean microbes during its transatlantic passage.

The fungi, by contrast, barely noticed. Wood-decomposing Basidiomycota dominated at both heights before, during, and after the dust event.

At 42 meters, they made up 93.8% of fungal sequences.

The researchers suggest that abundant local spores may have masked any fungal material arriving from Africa.

“Soils in arid regions and deserts are largely colonized by microbial communities, known as biological soil crusts,” Bettina Weber said.

“Genetically, the bacteria match organisms from the Sahara, which proves that they are transported from African biocrusts across the Atlantic by wind all the way to Brazil’s rainforest.”

The most immediately concerning finding wasn’t a particular species. It was endotoxins – lipopolysaccharides found in the outer membranes of Gram-negative bacteria, released when those bacteria die.

They’re chemically stable, resistant to heat, and capable of triggering inflammation in humans.

Endotoxin concentrations at 323 meters roughly tripled during the dust event, rising from 0.45 to 1.27 endotoxin units per cubic meter, with a peak of 2.10.

The levels correlated tightly with coarse-mode aerosol mass.

The researchers traced the signal to two Gram-negative sources: dead microbial material from African dryland soils and marine cell-wall debris picked up over the ocean.

“I was surprised by the large amount of endotoxins in the air,” Jens Weber said.

“Previous studies in the Caribbean have shown that asthma attacks often increase when Saharan dust arrives. Whether a similar correlation exists in the Amazon remains to be investigated.”

Those endotoxins are relevant not just for the forest ecosystem but for roughly 3.9 million people in Brazil’s Amazonas state, plus riverside and coastal communities sitting in the plume’s path.

One critical gap is that the study can’t tell us whether the bacteria are still alive. Amplicon sequencing detects genetic material regardless of whether the organism is viable or dead.

Endotoxins trigger immune responses either way – dead bacteria are perfectly capable of causing inflammation.

But whether intact, living microorganisms survive ten days of UV radiation, desiccation, and temperature swings over the open Atlantic is a separate question.

The team plans follow-up work using fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry to test viability directly.

There’s also the matter of pathogens. Obligate pathogens weren’t enriched during the dust event.

Some opportunistic genera – Staphylococcus and Clostridium – did increase at 323 meters.

However, identification at the genus level could not establish whether disease-causing species were present.

The dust appears to deliver soil bacteria and immunostimulatory debris rather than an army of infectious agents.

That distinction matters, though it won’t be fully settled until viability testing catches up.

The study is published in the journal Communications Earth & Environment.

This colleague shared how her therapist is brilliant and helpful, but often shifts too quickly into problem-solving mode. I encouraged her, in the same way that I encourage you, dear reader, to regularly be sharing feedback in your sessions.

Licensed therapists have years of training, including learning about evidence-based practices and getting supervised training in providing therapy. Your therapist shows up to your sessions with a lot of knowledge and skill that can help you recognize patterns, process significant life events, and so much more. But no matter how amazing your therapist is, one thing that they can never know, unless you tell them, is how helpful you find their therapeutic approach and the information that they’re sharing with you.

Therapists try to build a strong rapport with you, as they know that having a strong therapeutic alliance is important for you to feel safe and fully engage in your session. Research tells us that the stronger a therapist’s interpersonal skills are, the more likely their patients are to return for another session.1 Nevertheless, the therapist cannot know if you do not understand Information that they are trying to share with you, or if it just doesn’t feel aligned with your perspective, unless you tell them. You, your therapist, and your work together will benefit from this information.

It can feel awkward, giving feedback—especially if after reading this article, you’re the one who’s prompting such a discussion in your sessions. (Feel free to share this article as a jumping off point for discussion in your therapy session!)

If part of the reason that you don’t want to give feedback to your therapist is because you are afraid that they will stop liking you, that is also really important information that you need to share with your therapist. There could be many reasons for you feeling this way, and they are all really important to identify and discuss in therapy.

It’s normal for you to want other people to like you. This is especially true of your therapist, with whom you have shared a lot of sensitive information about yourself. Therapists have an ethical obligation to do no harm, to try to benefit you with their care, to build trust, practice fairly and justly, and respect the dignity and privacy of all people. This means that the therapist needs to be making decisions based on what they feel is healthiest for you and the therapeutic relationship. Therefore, even if your feedback is somehow given in such a way that your therapist could feel negatively about it, that is not a reason that you would not share it.

A therapists job is to take in the information that you’re sharing and to consider how the therapy session could be augmented to better meet your needs. They will not stop liking you if you provide constructive feedback, and if you perceived that were somehow to be the case, that would be a really important area for discussion and ultimately may signal that you and your therapist are no longer a good fit.

The feedback you provide can help the therapist engage in shared decision-making with you to see if you need a new treatment approach, if they need to refer you to a colleague so you can meet with someone who specializes in a different areas (e.g., couples counseling or EMDR sessions). This could mean engaging with different clinicians for different treatments at the same time or pausing your current treatment while receiving this care.

As a therapist, avoiding feedback doesn't mean you can assume a client is completely satisfied with the therapy: it more likely means you won't have a good understanding of the reasons why clients discontinue their sessions with you. Asking for feedback routinely normalizes the practice, helps you provide better care, and helps you learn and grow as a clinician so you can build your skills and continue benefiting your clients.

When my children were young, I remember reading aloud children’s books that delighted both them and me. One book that especially comes to mind is Diane Goode’s Where’s Our Mama? 1 The author dedicated this book to her own mother, and her feelings for her mother are expressed through the two young children in the story. In the story, a gust of wind blew the mother’s hat off her head. When the mother went to fetch her hat, the two children became separated from her and began to cry. A policeman approached them as they searched for their mama and asked them to describe her. They quickly said she was the most beautiful and bravest person who always wears the prettiest hats, has a voice that everyone loves, and is the best cook in the world. Of course, they were all soon joyfully reunited.

As in the story, “mama” elicits an image of a loving, giving, selfless person. But some people never had such a mother. Many were born the children of psychopathic mothers. For those in such circumstances, what can they expect?

According to psychopathy experts, Paul Babiak and Robert D. Hare, in their book, Snakes in Suits, “Psychopaths may push the envelope of acceptable human behavior … . Some psychopaths live in society and do not technically break the law—although they may come close, with behavior that usually is very unpleasant for those around them. Some may lead seemingly normal lives, not hurting people in ways that attract attention, but causing problems nonetheless in hidden economic, psychological, and emotionally abusive ways. They do not make warm and loving parents, children, or family members.”2

Having had a mother and sister who were highly psychopathic, I had eye-opening experiences from when I was young. I noticed early on that both my mother and sister were different from other mothers and other children that I knew. But it took years before I was able to understand the term that described their personality disorder. Their behavior displayed the psychopathic traits of lovelessness and guiltlessness, which sociologists William and Joan McCord have characterized as fundamental to the disorder.3

In realizing this, I came to terms with the fact that my mother was the antithesis of the iconic mother. This realization has helped me to make sense of much of what I experienced and observed growing up. She consistently exhibited strange and unusual behavior. Previously, I could never understand why she hid and disregarded treasured family photographs. One day, I happened to find them hidden in a dark, clammy basement behind a piece of plywood on a cold, damp cement floor. They had not been placed there by accident. The age-old photographs were damaged by mildew and could not be replaced. When I asked her about it, she shrugged and was expressionless, showing her “I don’t care” attitude. She had no affection or regard for the warm memories the photos embodied. Instead, photographs of complete strangers were showcased in prominent places in the house for no apparent reason.

My mother had many strategies to get her way by creating conflicts and division between family members and relatives. I witnessed these conflicts all my life at different times and in many situations. One day, I happened to call my mother, only to find out that my niece had recently been married. My mother’s voice could not have sounded more delighted to tell me that I was the only person not invited. She perpetuated ill will and division. According to psychologist Neville Symington, “One of the most evident signs of psychopathy is the presence of confusion and bad feeling. One person is set against another and suspicion is rife, but the cause is never rooted out.”4

The brush-offs and the slights were countless, and the cold treatment never changed, even during a time of great personal distress. In such a time, even a stranger can sympathize with someone going through hardship. Instead, my mother and sister went to an astrologer to find out if my life would be ruined and, on a telephone call, they blurted out in high-pitched laughter exactly what the future had in store for me. They tried to get me to believe I would be ruined. I didn’t believe, and it never occurred, but their diabolical scheming was another sign of their disorder.

In dreaming of what a mother is, I came to grips with the fact that I never had a mother like "mama," and that my mother could never be trusted.

5 Years Later, One of Anime’s Biggest Mysteries Has Officially Been Solved

It's hard to explain briefly why Dandadan is one of the best manga and anime that have come out in the past five years. The series' eclectic nature and mix of genres means it escapes easy categorization. "The author is a genius" and "the anime adaptation is fantastic" could be reductive when recommending it to someone. Luckily, chapter 252 of the Dandadan manga, published on Nov. 9, 2026, does a much better job of explaining why this series is a masterpiece.

One of Dandadan's oddities is that, until now, author Yukinobu Tatsu has refrained from showing his readers anything about the background and past of one of his two protagonists: the sweet, spectacled, alien-obsessed Ken Takakura, nicknamed Okarun. This did not hurt the series nor Okarun's characterization, which was developed through his interactions with the rest of the cast, especially co-protagonist Momo Ayase. Now, we finally know that Tatsu had a long-term vision for this character — and it's a brutal one.

Spoilers for Dandadan beyond the anime follow.In the current Dandadan arc, Okarun has lost his supernatural powers after fulfilling his pact with Turbo Granny. The foul-mouthed yokai has disappeared, and Okarun was left without means to defend himself when new enemies attacked him, Momo, Aira, and Jiji during a trip to a theme park. Okarun had to rely on the Ogre Club, a mystical weapon provided by Momo's grandma, Seiko, but it turns out that the tool has unpleasant side effects. Okarun lost himself to rage and attacked friends and foes indiscriminately, until Turbo Granny made her triumphant return. In chapter 252, "Ken Takakura," the spirit enters Okarun's mind to bring him back to reason, and in the process, she witnesses the boy's traumatic past.

Ken Takakura's parents split up after his mom found a new partner. She asked little Ken if he wanted to go with her, but he decided to stay with his dad. In a gut-wrenching sequence, Okarun remembers realizing his mom was actually relieved he didn't follow her, since he could have spoiled her fancy new life. Things kept getting worse for young Ken from that moment onwards. He was beaten up by other kids, bullied, and sexually humiliated. Meanwhile, his father was crushed by debt and experienced angry outbursts, leaving poor Ken a lonely, traumatized child.

One day, as he was running away from some bullies, Ken found an occult magazine that became his lifeline. He hoped and prayed every day that aliens would one day show up to "save his heart." They didn't. Okarun kept getting bullied as he grew up, until he couldn't take it anymore. In another chilling sequence, Ken is shown on top of a building, ready to jump off. However, thinking about distant, happy memories with his family made him lose courage (or rather, he found the courage to live), and he broke down crying.

After that suicide attempt, Ken kept getting abused at school. In the present, the influence of the Ogre Club mixes his memories with nefarious thoughts of murder. However, a voice (likely Turbo Granny) tells him that he has forgotten something: His prayers were answered. Someone did show up. The chapter ends with a panel of Momo sitting in front of Okarun's desk, shielding him from the bullies, a callback to the manga's first chapter.

At the beginning of Dandadan, in the much-discussed scene where Momo and Okarun are held by the Serpoian aliens, the boy said that, as he didn't have any friends, he looked up to the skies every day hoping that the aliens would show up. It also recontextualizes the current arc, which saw Momo lose her recent memories, including those of Okarun, which left Ken struggling to remind her of the bond they shared. Now, fans know that Momo isn't just Okarun's first crush or the girl who stood up for him at school. She's literally the person who saved his life.

Bullying, abuse, and suicide are difficult themes that are often present in manga and anime, but they're not always treated with the sensitivity required. Dandadan's long-term storytelling set up a big reveal about Okarun's traumatic past, but Tatsu has already shown that, despite his talent for action and comedy, he's not afraid to touch on other, more difficult themes. Vamola's backstory was, until now, one of the best and most traumatic parts of the manga (and one that I can't wait to see adapted in the anime). Okarun's backstory hits those same powerful emotional notes that make Dandadan a masterpiece and his author one of the best mangaka currently active.

While Google Photos and Apple Photos already provide extensive tools to organize and manage photos, Sundrop is taking aim at a particular niche: Parents with a new baby (usually their first!) who take an overwhelming number of photos and want to do more with those pictures.

The app was developed over the past few months by the same team behind the read-it-later app, Matter: Ben Springwater, Sky Lan, and Rob Mackenzie (all dads themselves).

Notably, Sundrop claims it is designed to be private, and processes all photos locally on the device it is installed on. To make this possible, Sundrop taps into Apple’s Vision framework and on-device Core ML (machine learning) models instead of uploading photos to a cloud server or some other hosted AI service.

Both of the child’s parents can use Sundrop together by linking their accounts, which allows the app to sync their shared photos via Apple’s iCloud service. This feature, the company says, means the pictures aren’t sent to any external servers it runs.

In addition, the team notes that no generative AI is used in the creation of its scenes or highlights.

This differentiates it from many photo editing apps on the market, including Google’s, which are increasingly tapping generative AI to create new versions of your photos.

To find highlights and recaps, Sundrop scans pictures in the parents’ respective Camera Rolls on their iPhones, skipping things like screenshots or duplicates. It then creates highlights or fun scenes, which it calls “drops.”

One of these is a recap of the week every Sunday, announced as a Lock Screen notification. Others can put two siblings side-by-side at the same age, generate a time-lapse series of photos or a video of a kid growing up, or cut out a photo of the child and place it into a layered template.

Parents can also manually edit the photos Sundrop uses to make its creations, or use the app to automatically organize the best photos into galleries with other similar shots.

The app costs $39.99 per year, and offers a week-long free trial. The annual plan also includes Family Sharing, so only one parent needs to purchase it.

Steam, the digital marketplace for PC games from game studio Valve, has hit a major milestone, sparking pushback from parts of the gaming community: Approximately 50% of new Steam games in October were made with the help of AI.

This underscores a growing trend in gaming, where more games are made with some form of AI assistance — specifically, generative AI, which developers have used to generate code, art assets, voice lines and more. Developers are adopting the technology in increasing numbers.

The numbers come from a Reddit user who goes by Ploz, and who used Steam’s publicly available data to see how many newly released games relied on AI in some capacity. This was possible because of Valve’s AI disclosure policy for newly released games, which requires developers to state whether they used AI in making their game. The disclosure is part of the storefront’s publicly accessible records, allowing anyone to view it.

A Valve representative did not respond to a request for comment.

The data is a snapshot of the tidal wave of AI-made games flooding storefronts, most prominently Steam. But crucially, Valve has chosen not to limit this flood, washing its hands of the problem and leaving gamers to fend for themselves in finding quality games. Developers had already been struggling to get their games noticed, and Valve’s limited curation seems completely unprepared for the firehose of AI-made games churned out on an increasing scale.

Valve implemented the AI disclosure policy in January 2024 to increase developer transparency. At the time, only 1.4% of newly released games used AI. This increased to 13.4% by the end of 2024, then to 24% by the end of 2025. It now sits at 49.9%.

There are some caveats. The AI disclosure is only required for games that use AI to create player-facing content, such as artwork, sound, character dialogue and localization. The 49.9% figure is specific to these types of games and doesn’t account for developers who use AI for behind-the-scenes tasks such as coding and bug fixes. A Google Cloud survey conducted in August 2025 showed that 87% of developers use AI for backend work, which doesn’t require disclosure on Steam.

So it’s likely that the number of games on Steam built with AI assistance is greater than 50%. Ploz notes that the only way users can avoid AI-generated games is to manually check for AI disclosures.

According to Ploz, the most common use of AI in game development is for artwork, which was mentioned in 63% of disclosures. Text and translations gobbled up 30%, while music (21%), code (16%) and voices (7%) rounded out the list. Many developers had more than one of those areas named in their disclosure, and about a quarter of developers said they were using AI for their Steam store pages and marketing.

About 3% of developers used AI directly as a game mechanic. One such example is a driving simulator called Teach My Little Sister How to Drive. Developers use AI in the game to interpret a player’s voice commands and generate the sister’s responses. To keep a playable demo of the game available to the public, the development team, Easy Fox, is paying $1,000 every day just to cover these AI operating costs — an expense so high that it was forced to take out a loan simply to keep the demo running.

For development, there are loads of options. The Unity 6 engine came out in October 2024 and includes AI code assistance, AI asset generation and third-party integration with Claude Code, Gemini and Grok. Anthropic’s Claude is a fairly popular option among smaller developers, since it supports the ability to vibe code whole games, with ChatGPT, Gemini and GitHub Copilot as other options.

Many of the games being released with the AI disclosure on Steam can be classified as slop — a derogatory term gamers have applied liberally to titles that heavily lean on generative AI for development and look derivative of other popular games. According to Ploz, most AI-enabled games are small: Around 47% of new games with fewer than 10 reviews have an AI disclosure. Among developers who released more than 10 games in a year, 68% of those games included the disclosure.

Games with AI disclosures received fewer positive reviews on average than those without. And among all newly released games with more than 1,000 reviews, only 17% include an AI disclosure. That means gamers are still gravitating toward games without an AI disclosure and pushing back against games that use the technology.

Developers seem aware of this. CNET reached out to two dozen well-known developers, including Epic Games, EA, Activision Blizzard, Ubisoft, PlayStation Studios and many others. All of them declined to comment.

Bigger studios may be using AI in their development, but it’s hard to tell the scale. There’s no such thing as an AI audit yet, and the industry seems to be relying on voluntary disclosure for now. Steam’s disclosure policy has outed smaller developers who are either worried about violating the policy and being taken off the digital storefront, or aren’t worried about the negative effects of disclosure.

A lot of dominoes had to fall for this to happen. The availability of AI tools and the increasing ease of use are definite contributors. Subreddits like r/AIGameDev have plenty of examples of people putting together games with just a few days or weeks of vibe coding, which is a lot shorter than the standard video game development cycle, which can stretch over years depending on the scale, production level and graphics quality. This practice has exploded over the last couple of years, resulting in a lot of games getting launched that are made mostly or entirely with AI.

Many of these games end up on Steam, which doesn’t have a policy limiting AI-developed games.

“Digital storefronts like Steam have already struggled to moderate low-effort and poor-quality games, as well as copycats stealing other games’ ideas,” said CNET Managing Editor David Lumb.

Valve openly admits its role in this, stating that the problem definitely exists but that the company doesn’t “want to be gatekeepers telling developers which tools they should or shouldn’t use.”

It takes a similar stance on AI cloning, with Valve’s Tom Giardino telling developers that “you must continue to grow your game by adding features, personality and creativity that stem from your own perspective as a developer.”

These policies essentially allow any AI-developed game or clone to reach the Valve storefront without pushback, which certainly contributes to the flood of newly released games with some form of player-facing AI involvement in their development.

Rival store Epic Games has a similar stance, allowing basically any game that wants to be there. The company declined to comment further about this, but CEO Tom Sweeney is an avid supporter of AI in video game development, stating that it was “perfectly logical” for developers to rely on AI to handle the mundane grunt work that takes up most of a developer’s time.

While Steam and Epic Games tend to take a more lax attitude toward AI slop, other storefronts like Itch.io are not. Itch.io has a whole policy against AI slop and the company has willingly, and at times gleefully, deleted terabytes of AI slop from its storefront. It also requires developers to disclose if they used AI but takes a much more consumer-friendly approach by letting users filter those out of search results if they don’t want to see them.

Some measures are being taken to address the proliferation of AI slop, but none will entirely stop the problem. Cloning has been a problem for many years. Mobile gamers of a certain vintage may remember the flock of Flappy Bird clones that appeared on app stores after the original game went down, and those weren’t even made with AI.

AI makes cloning much easier and faster, and some AI companies are addressing it. Anthropic recently changed Claude Opus 5.5 to prevent it from generating copies of existing games. Inuvik, population about 3,500, was the first community in that part of the Arctic to try to wean the bears off their dependence on dump food with such a fence, which cost about $700,000. The problem was, the bears had not learned to depend on any other source of food.

Tragic though this is, officials hope that within the next couple of years, the remaining bears in the area will move on and learn to eat more natural fare.

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

That cardboard box in your closet can come in handy more often than you’d think.

There's a specific type of anxiety that comes with deciding what to do with a box. Do you immediately throw it away? Do you stash it in a closet with about 20 other boxes that store little more than your own guilt? If you chuck it, you might need it for some unknown purpose in the future. But if you don't, that box might just sit in your home indefinitely.

It doesn't help when it's a nice box, either, like the type your phone might have arrived in. It's likely made of thick cardboard, with convenient compartments. Luckily, or perhaps unluckily for those who have finally gathered the nerve to go on a recycling tear, there are a few ways to help you decide the fate of your phone's box. Whether you're ready to resell your phone or need to report a stolen one, here's why you might want to keep your phone's box.

Hopefully, you and your phone will never part ways without your consent. However, if it does get stolen, you'll need its IMEI (International Mobile Equipment Identity) number. An IMEI is a 15- 17-digit number unique to your phone. Carriers can use it to identify the device and block it if it's lost or stolen. AT&T describes it as your phone's "fingerprint."

One place to check for your IMEI number is the box your phone came in. You should also be able to find it through your Apple or Google account, depending on the device you have. Still, in the heat of the moment, grabbing that box from a drawer might be the easiest option.

Some less stressful scenarios might also lead you to keep your phone box. If you purchased an iPhone from Apple directly, for example, you have 14 days to return it from the day you received it. It must also be in the original packaging. Likewise, Samsung requires original packaging. Other retailers might have different policies, so at the very least, it's a good idea to check before you trash the box. Or keep it until after the window has closed, just to be safe.

Additionally, if you're thinking of reselling your phone at some point down the line, having its original box could come in handy. For one, you might have saved additional cables or something like a SIM ejector tool that a new owner will want. And while there's no hard data, some experts believe that having that original box can boost your odds of selling your phone at a good price.

Consumer Reports suggests the box increases the market value of the item and shows that you've taken good care of it. If you saw two identical iPhones for the same price, but only one was in a box, would you really choose the one without the box? The article also notes that you'll already have a box if you need to ship your phone.

So, if you're waffling on the eternal question of whether to save your phone's box, you can consider if any of those situations apply to you. And if not, there's likely a recycling bin somewhere waiting for its next deposit.

The US Supreme Court issued an unsigned decision on Monday allowing the Trump administration to resume building border barriers and installing surveillance equipment in the Big Bend region of southwestern Texas, including inside Big Bend National Park.

The ruling lifts a temporary halt on construction ordered in early October by US District Judge Kathleen Cardone in El Paso. While litigation over the legality of the 500-mile border project continues in the lower courts, construction crews can now move forward with work. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson dissented from the decision.

For visitors and locals in the remote desert region, the physical ramifications of the project began appearing over the summer. In August, construction crews brought bulldozers near Santa Elena Canyon (one of Big Bend National Park’s most famous landmarks) and graded more than a mile of land. According to administration officials, Customs and Border Protection plans to install four- to six-foot-tall vehicle barriers, surveillance gear, and an access road in the area.

Opponents of the construction, which include a bipartisan mix of Texas ranchers, local landowners, and conservationists, warn that barriers will permanently alter the desert. Critics say the project threatens to cut off access to the river, disrupt livestock and crops, and interfere with the area’s status as the largest dark sky reserve on the planet.

Legal opposition to the project began in April when a coalition consisting of an environmental organization, a river guide, a church preservation group, and local landowners filed a lawsuit to stop the project. The coalition argued that federal officials had exceeded their authority by waiving environmental and conservation laws, such as the National Environmental Policy Act and the Endangered Species Act, without approval from Congress.

In early October, Judge Cardone temporarily blocked the construction after finding that the coalition was likely to succeed on at least one of its legal claims. After Judge Cardone denied the administration’s request to pause her order during an appeal, the government filed an emergency application with the Supreme Court, describing the lower court’s block as “baseless.”

The federal government argued that halting work would leave a gap of more than 300 miles in border defenses, which he said could draw cartels and other bad actors.

Still, the coalition urged the justices to keep the block in place. They argued the administration sought emergency relief to immediately restart bulldozing, blasting, and road building across an ecologically and culturally sensitive landscape, warning that continued work would permanently damage historic resources and private ranch lands.

In Monday’s order, the Supreme Court majority acknowledged the plaintiffs’ argument that construction in the Big Bend Sector would “diminish their ability to use and enjoy the area.” However, the majority concluded that federal law grants the Homeland Security secretary the power to waive conservation and environmental statutes, noting that the law shows “a clear preference for expeditious border construction over the very harms” cited by the challengers.

The decision marks the first major policy victory for President Trump at the Supreme Court following last week’s midterm elections, as the underlying lawsuit proceeds in the lower court.

In January 2023, someone with poor credit and a clean driving record would pay a median of $2,864 per year for full-coverage car insurance, NerdWallet’s analysis found. By January 2026, premiums for this driver had risen to $3,944 — an increase of $1,081 per year. By comparison, the same driver with good credit saw an increase of only $576.

“Credit scores are one of the most harmful things that results in you paying higher auto insurance premiums,” says Michael DeLong, a research and advocacy associate with the Washington, D.C.-based nonprofit Consumer Federation of America. “The penalties are extremely severe.”

While higher premiums after a wreck or DUI may not be a surprise, DeLong says many drivers aren’t aware their credit scores will affect their car insurance rates. To further complicate things, building credit can take months.

But there are ways to get relief more quickly. DeLong suggests comparing car insurance quotes from multiple companies, because some insurers penalize poor credit less than others. Raising your deductible, evaluating your coverage and avoiding small claims can also help.

NerdWallet’s analysis found that insurers raised rates more sharply from 2023 to 2026 for drivers with poor credit than for those with good credit — even if the poor-credit driver had a flawless record while the good-credit driver had a DUI, speeding ticket or at-fault crash.

Drivers with poor credit or no credit may be able to take other steps to lower their premiums.

Comparing car insurance quotes from different companies is one of the best ways to ensure you have the cheapest rate possible. In late October, we got our annual Halloween push of ducks here in the Midwest, and a friend and I timed it just right. We set up on a big agricultural retention pond with winds gusting to 30 mph. I threw out three dozen goose floaters, and we set another dozen full bodies in the shallows. We added a few mallard blocks right at the water’s edge in the landing hole in front of the blind. It’s best to put ducks upwind of geese, as geese don’t like flying over ducks, while ducks will fly over geese.

The first ducks in the spread were greenwings, and we shot a pair. Not long after, a big group of mallards worked us, then dropped into the hole. And so it went, until an hour and a half later, I called the shot on a pair of greenheads at ten yards. We stood up, the blind blew down, and we shot our last two ducks of the day.

We added a couple of geese and passed on the shovelers and ruddies that came into the spread. A flock of bluebills landed at the edge of the geese, but they were too bunched up for a clean shot. The point is, everything liked the goose spread that morning. We had a nice bag of mallards, a couple of teal, a gadwall, and some geese. It proved that a spread of goose floaters can work on ducks just as well as a spread of goose silos and full bodies does in a dry field. And adding floaters to your collection of goose decoys lets you target geese on ponds and rivers, where there may be less competition than in dry fields. If you still aren’t convinced, here are four reasons big Canada floaters will improve your success on ducks and geese.

It doesn’t take a huge decoy spread to fool geese over water. Sometimes, as few as four decoys can be enough to pull geese on small ponds or marshes. Plus, geese feel comfortable enough coming to water that even mediocre (that’s putting it kindly) callers like me can honk and cluck a few times to get their attention, and then let the decoys do the work.

The best time and place to hunt geese over water is a loafing spot, which can be whatever pond, marsh, or stretch of river birds go to after breakfast. Find one of these loafing spots and hunt it at “goose time,” which starts about 8:30 and runs through the middle of the day. There’s no need to get up early to hunt a loafing spot, and shooting the roost in the evening is generally frowned upon, as it might move birds out of the area for good. Save a late afternoon roost shoot for the last day of the season. If you’re after a mixed bag of ducks and geese, however, set the alarm early.

If you’ve gotten this far, you’re probably a serious waterfowler, and I don’t have to explain the adrenaline rush of ducks and geese decoying from high altitudes. This leads me to my next point on why you should use goose floaters: geese will lock up as dots in the sky and drop down to the water as if on a giant, invisible escalator, in a way they rarely do in fields. I remember honking at a bunch of four passing over from behind me and seeing them stand on their wingtips, drop almost to the water, spin, and come into the decoys, barely skimming the surface as they crossed the pond. It was a sight mesmerizing enough that I turned what should have been an easy triple into a single bird on the water. Watching geese decoy over water is more fun than watching them decoy in dry fields.

You will shoot far more ducks over goose decoys than you will ever shoot geese over duck decoys. Up to a 90/10 goose-to-duck decoy ratio can work surprisingly well on ducks, and it pulls more geese than a few goose floaters with a large duck spread will. Goose decoys are big, highly visible, and serve as a confidence bird for other waterfowl. This makes your spread not only more appealing and visible to ducks, but also a more secure place for them to land.

Shanghai is a restless city. Its impatient hustle may spare its art scene from the plateau of middle age as it navigates a crisis of transition after the Covid years derailed its late 2010s momentum. Going into Shanghai’s annual art week (10-15 November), with two established fairs anchoring an explosion of art events across the city, its galleries, artists and institutions are assessing how the city’s art market can weather China’s economic slowdown and whether the cultural momentum of the past three decades can be sustained.

“Shanghai has so many galleries, so many museums, so many eccentric little spaces that you could come in the middle of summer and still see art non-stop,” says Lorenz Helbling, who founded ShanghArt in 1996. One of the first contemporary art galleries in China, it now has locations in Shanghai, Beijing and Singapore. The Swiss transplant says the volume of artistic activity in the city of 25 million people can be compared to New York’s, though the scene can be harder to find. “You can make a lot of fun things here.”

The city’s two large-scale fairs, Art021 Shanghai (11-14 November) and West Bund Art & Design (10-15 November), established in 2013 and 2014 respectively, set the barometer and prime the mood. So far, Shanghai’s core galleries have largely survived China’s property crash and deflationary economy, but they need young artists and new collectors to continue buoying their businesses.

Lise Li, who founded Shanghai’s Vanguard Gallery in 2004, says the city is “still in an upward phase, just growing much more slowly than before”. During art week, Li’s gallery will participate in the second iteration of the boutique fair Hangover Shanghai (11-15 November) and in Art021, while showing the artist Tang Chao in the gallery and collaborating on a group show at the artist-run 33ml Offspace. Ring's New $250 Indoor Camera Drone Sounds Terrifying

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ring made its name with its smart doorbells with built-in cameras, and now sells more indoor cameras than any other company, capturing more than 40% of the market. When you've already moved tens of millions of devices (enough to raise serious concerns about their use as part of the surveillance state), you always need to keep innovating, and today, the company announced a product it has been working on for a decade: an indoor camera drone powered by artificial intelligence. I got a chance to check it out during a media event yesterday, and I can report that, as harbingers of our dystopian future go, it is only mildly terrifying.

The drone camera is one of two products in the company's new Ring Robotics line, alongside a "rover cam" that looks kind of like the canister of a vacuum cleaner attached to an iPad. While the Ring Rover Cam is priced at $999.99 and targeted at businesses, the Ring Drone Cam is a much more affordable $249.99, reasonable enough that it will appeal to consumers looking for another way to keep (robot) eyes on their stuff.

The drone camera records 2K video and has a 148-degree field of view and the ability to rotate 360 degrees. It works with existing Ring cameras, doorbells, sensors, and alarms, as well as the Alexa Home Map. It has a flight time of eight minutes, and once it returns to its base station, it takes around an hour to fully recharge (eight minutes on the dock will give it one minute in the air).

As Ring founder Jamie Siminoff explained during the preview event, the company has been trying to build a workable camera drone for around a decade, but couldn't overcome certain design challenges until the advancement of AI models that allow the device to handle most of the computing—from route programming to threat detection—via the cloud, so the drones can be smaller and lighter.

According to Ring, when setting up the Ring Drone, you carry it around your house, allowing it to map the interior. It will "intelligently identify rooms and suggest patrol points: spots worth extra attention." When sent out on "patrol," the drone's AI pilot will travel between hotspots, sensing and avoiding furniture and other obstacles along the way.The Ring Drone works with other Ring cameras, so if an alert is triggered somewhere in your home—say, a window opening unexpectedly—the drone will take off to investigate. It has a built-in security light that will make its presence known, so it can also act as a deterrent, though the screaming sound of its spinning propellers will accomplish that just as well.

According to Ring, the drone camera can deliver peace of mind whether you're home or away, and admittedly I do like the idea of being able to check on my pets or make sure no pipes have burst while I'm at work or on vacation (though admittedly you can get the same sort of monitoring without involving an AI device that looks like a mini version of the flying robots from The Terminator).

I admit that the Ring Drone Camera seems pretty cool, provided I don't think about it too much. I don't know that I really feel the need to have every inch of my home covered in security cameras, but I do like the idea that the drone could, for example, go to check out what's going on if one of my smoke alarms' temperature sensors reports that one spot in the house is all of a sudden hotter than it should be.

But there's something slightly unsettling to me about the idea of a robot patrolling my home while I sleep. Yes, I know that there are privacy and security features in place—Ring says the videos its robots capture is protected by its "strongest default encryption yet"—but it's also yet one more example of how comfortable we've all gotten with cameras being absolutely everywhere, to the point that people think nothing of putting them all over their homes. (This is probably why I don't own a robot vacuum.) And it doesn't help that the drone sounds absolutely terrifying, which Ring claims is by design: "If someone’s there who shouldn’t be, a flying drone overhead...makes it clear they’ve been spotted. [The] devices are built to deter, not just monitor." True enough, but there's also no getting around the fact that drone propellers are unavoidably loud, and the sound of the Ring Drone Camera reminds me of nothing more than the screeching violins in a horror movie. (Note that the drone we were shown was white, but the final version sold will be dark grey.)

If you're ready to enter this brave new world of security, you'll have to wait a bit longer: The Ring Drone Camera isn't expected to ship until January 2027, but you can sign up to be alerted when it is available for preorder now.
