HBO’s ‘Harry Potter’ Officially Changes a Classic Book Moment in First Full Scene Release

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

For millions of Harry Potter fans, that iconic at Hogwarts is one of those moments that's practically impossible to forget. It's a sensory overload with the singing Sorting Hat, the Great Hall, hundreds of floating candles, and nothing short of a mountain of food per person. Chris Columbus brought the scene to life in 2001's Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone, and it's remained one of the most recognizable moments in the franchise ever since. Now, with HBO preparing to take audiences back to Hogwarts this Christmas, we're finally getting a proper look at how the new version compares.

HBO has officially released the first full scene from Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone, and despite the fact it's recognizable, it's also distinctly different from what's come before. The approximately 90-second clip shows Harry arriving in the Great Hall alongside Ron, with Albus Dumbledore preparing to begin the feast. One of the first differences comes when the students begin chanting Dumbledore's name, something that doesn't happen in J.K. Rowling's original novel. Harry then asks Ron whether Dumbledore is a little mad, whereas in the book he directs that question to Ron's older brother Percy.

However, the biggest addition comes when Dumbledore and Professor McGonagall share a private conversation about Harry, with McGonagall commenting on his resemblance to his father, James Potter. The exchange doesn't appear in the novel, which tells the banquet scene from Harry's perspective. It's the first look at how the series will start showing us different perspectives than that of the titular character.

The series is led by Dominic McLaughlin as Harry Potter, Arabella Stanton as Hermione Granger and Alastair Stout as Ron Weasley, alongside John Lithgow (Conclave) as Albus Dumbledore, Janet McTeer (Ozark) as Minerva McGonagall, Paapa Essiedu (I May Destroy You) as Severus Snape, Nick Frost (Shaun of the Dead) as Rubeus Hagrid, Lox Pratt (Lord of the Flies) as Draco Malfoy and Warwick Davis (Willow) as Filius Flitwick. Season 2 is already building its own impressive cast too, with Kit Harington (Game of Thrones) playing Gilderoy Lockhart, Lenny Rush (Doctor Who) voicing Dobby, Billy Barratt (Invasion) as Tom Riddle and Rafe Spall (Jurassic World: Fallen Kingdom) as Arthur Weasley.

The series is written and executive produced by Francesca Gardiner (Succession), with Mark Mylod (The Last of Us) directing multiple episodes. Rowling also serves as an executive producer alongside David Heyman, who produced the original film franchise.

Bitcoin’s next big threat could come from two oil tankers in the Middle East

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Brent spot was more than $30 below its October average before the strikes. US rate expectations are the next test.

UK Maritime Trade Operations reported two Hormuz tanker strikes on Saturday in which projectiles hit outbound vessels, putting a recent drop in oil prices to the test. Recovering shipments had helped bring Brent down. For Bitcoin, cheaper crude could take some pressure off US inflation and interest-rate expectations if the decline holds.

UK Maritime Trade Operations (UKMTO) reported that a projectile struck an outbound tanker’s stern near Khasab, Oman, at 5 a.m. UTC. Its second warning described a hit to an outbound tanker’s engine room 50 minutes later. UKMTO said no casualties had been reported at the time and the environmental impact was unconfirmed. The warnings gave no estimate of cargo losses or delays.

The US Energy Information Administration said Brent spot was more than $30 a barrel below its October average of $124 as of Nov. 18. It credited rising Hormuz flows with part of the drop. The International Energy Agency had also credited recovering Gulf exports with easing the oil market in its Nov. 13 report.

In the seven days through Nov. 18, UKMTO counted 115 outbound transits, down from 139 the previous week. Its totals combine vessel tracking with US-reported facilitated crossings that UKMTO could not independently verify.

The vessel tracker hormuz.now counted five outbound commercial crossings on Saturday, Nov. 21, and three on Friday. Its Saturday logs show four outbound crossings after the second reported strike, including two tankers. The tracker counts commercial vessels of at least 80 meters with transponders switched on. The amount of oil shipped after the strikes remains unclear.

Reuters reported that front-month Brent futures settled Friday at $92.57 a barrel, up 59 cents. Treasury data show the two-year yield fell to 4.63% from 4.76% on Thursday. Both moves preceded the strikes.

A December rate hike was still widely priced in. In its Friday market recap, Kiplinger cited CME FedWatch for a 96% chance of a quarter-point increase. Traders can compare that reading with the next one and watch whether early-2027 fed funds futures point to further tightening.

Federal Reserve staff estimated that oil prices fed through to core inflation more strongly after the pandemic in a model pooling five advanced economies, including the US. A 2023 IMF working paper found that US monetary tightening weighed on broad crypto prices as investors took less risk. A lasting oil rebound could keep expected borrowing costs higher if traders also anticipate higher inflation and tighter Fed policy.

Research Says Cold Muscles Produce Less Power. Here’s How to Protect Your Performance on Frigid Runs.

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Experts share how to prepare, pace, and recover so you can run strong when temperatures fall below freezing.

When temperatures rise, running performance tends to drop. Both the research and anyone who’s put in miles on a hot summer afternoon can confidently tell you that much.

But what about when the mercury moves in the opposite direction? Does colder weather in the winter—ranging from just a bit chilly to downright frigid—take a toll on your running too?

A recent review published in Temperature suggests it does. According to the study’s authors, when our muscles get too cold, neuromuscular function becomes impaired. On the run, this can contribute to stiffness, less power output, and decreased endurance, all of which may cause your winter workouts to feel harder than usual.

The good news? You can still run strong when the temps drop—as long as you make a few key training adjustments before, during, and after you log your miles in the colder months of the year. Here’s how to do just that.

There’s no exact number on the thermometer at which runners can universally expect their power, speed, or endurance to decline, Stephen Cheung, PhD, the lead author of the aforementioned review, tells Runner’s World.

Temperature is just one of the many factors that contribute to how well we perform in the cold, Cheung explains. “It’s a function of the environment, as well as how much heat you’re generating, the speed that you’re moving at, and of course, the amount of clothing that you’re wearing,” he says.

Each of these factors varies significantly from runner to runner and affects how quickly their bodies lose heat. You only need to consider those in your run group who run shirtless when it’s freezing versus those who bundle up when it’s 50 degrees Fahrenheit to understand that we all thermoregulate (maintain our internal body temperature) differently. And the equation only becomes more complex when you account for wind and moisture—both in the environment and what you create yourself from air resistance and sweat.

Heather Milton, CSCS, lead exercise physiologist at Primary MD and cofounder of Master My Run, agrees. “It would be great to be able to say, when the temperature outside reaches [a certain point], your pace slows down by this many seconds per mile—but that’s not how it works,” she says.

Keeping this in mind, Milton says the cleanest reference point for runners to begin paying more attention to how they prepare for runs in the cold is when the temperature drops below freezing. Generally speaking, the colder it gets from there, the more noticeable the negative effects on performance will be, she explains.

Cold weather leads to vasoconstriction, Milton explains, which means our blood vessels constrict more, so the muscles farther away from the core don’t receive as much blood flow. If your fingers or toes have ever gone numb in the cold, for example, you’ve experienced the joy of vasoconstriction. Our bodies prioritize heating our core over our extremities because it helps our vital organs maintain a consistent temperature, which is essential for survival in dangerous conditions.

This physiological response, however, doesn’t bode especially well for running performance.

“The cold slows nerve conduction so you have to work harder for the same power output,” Milton says. “And as temperatures drop further below freezing, that slower nerve conduction and the effects of vasoconstriction are going to be even more noticeable.” As a result, your overall exercise capacity decreases, meaning you may not be able to run for as long or at the same pace you normally would.

What else might you notice when temperatures drop? “Physically, if your muscles and tendons are cold, they’re going to be tighter, less elastic, and you won’t be able to contract them as strongly,” says Cheung. “Stiff muscles can also put you at greater risk for tears or strain injuries.”

Milton says this analogy, used by an old coach of hers, provides an easy way to understand what’s happening: When your muscles and tendons get too cold, they’re like a cold rubber band that can snap when pulled.

If you’re a runner who dislikes the cold, you might wonder if the smarter choice is to just run on the treadmill this winter. Don’t worry: As long as you prepare appropriately and use caution, you can absolutely continue logging regular miles outdoors.

Running well outside in the cold starts with warming up indoors first, says Milton. “An indoor warmup increases your internal metabolism and enhances blood flow, which helps prepare your muscles for contraction,” she explains. This also produces heat so your internal body temperature is more resilient to the cold outdoor environment.

Your winter warmup does not have to be any different or longer than your usual routine, Milton says. But you do want to make sure that you’re getting in a solid five minutes of prerun drills to adequately raise your body temperature and activate your muscles.

She recommends starting with slower, more controlled movements that help activate the core and the glutes, such as planks with leg raises, lunges with rotation, and hip CARs (controlled articular rotations). Finish with quicker drills that require your nerves to fire more rapidly, such as leg swings and butt kicks.

Equally important to warming up is layering up—with an important caveat.

When dressing for winter runs, keep in mind how you personally respond to cold temperatures, says Cheung, because everyone has different tolerances and susceptibilities. “I get very cold in my hands, so I’ll wear really thick gloves, even when I may be wearing relatively little on my torso,” he adds. “Pay attention to what’s an issue for you and adapt your clothing to both the conditions and your own peculiarities.” Cheung emphasizes this because you don’t want to end up sweating too heavily, which can then leave you chilled and clammy.

If you need a starting point for your winter running wardrobe, aim for two to three layers of breathable, moisture-wicking fabrics. If possible, choose a wind- and water-resistant shell for your outermost layer, suggests Milton. For your lower body, wear shorts over your tights to help keep your glutes warm. Finally, cover any exposed skin on your head, ears, and hands, and opt for a pair of wool socks—all of which can help ensure adequate blood distribution to the extremities, Milton adds.

When you head out for runs in the cold, slowly build up to your usual pace.

Gradually easing into your workout gives your muscles more time to adjust, and it can also help your airways get used to the colder temperatures, Milton says. You don’t necessarily need to go slower for your entire run, but you should try to match your rate of perceived exertion (RPE) with your typical effort, rather than aiming for your usual pace or heart-rate zone.

“Think about it the same way as if you were running on an extremely hot day,” Milton says. “Let’s say your RPE for an easy run is about 3 out of 10. Keep that 3 out of 10, even if your pace is a little slower.”

If you find that your run feels more difficult than usual, slow down if you need to, but don’t stop completely, Cheung advises. “Don’t take long breaks, because that’s when you aren’t generating as much heat. Just keep moving so you have that constant heat generation,” he says.

If you start to get cold on a run, try speeding up a little bit to stoke that metabolic heat production, Cheung says. But make sure you do it safely: Don’t force the effort in icy conditions or when your muscles feel especially stiff. Scouting out run routes with more tree coverage to block wind on blustery days can also help you stay warmer, he adds.

As soon as you finish your workout, change out of your sweaty running gear and into warm, dry clothes, Milton says.

Even if you’ve only sweated a small amount—for example, maybe you just have some dampness under your armpits—you still want to remove any wet fabrics against your skin. “Even that small amount of wetness can start to drop your core temperature,” Milton explains. If you wait until you’re near shivering, you’ll face an uphill battle to get yourself warm again.

You can also opt for a relaxing shower and hot chocolate—extra marshmallows.

From the Archive: Ina Garten Tapped a Few Famous Friends for Her '90s Farmhouse Renovation

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Take a look inside the sunny, five-room farmhouse.

Welcome to Archive Dive—my new excuse to obsess over the absolute treasures hiding in House Beautiful’s 130-year-old archives. Every month, we’ll dig up a favorite story from the past (epic room makeovers, wildly bold design swings, and style moments that were way ahead of their time) and talk about why they still resonate today. It’s part history lesson, part design deep-dive, and 100 percent a love letter to the iconic taste that’s defined HB for over a century.—Jo Saltz, House Beautiful Editor in Chief

After Ina Garten moved the now-shuttered Barefoot Contessa specialty store from Westhampton to East Hampton, she and her husband, Jeffrey, decided to put down roots in the neighborhood. But rather than buy a new-build home, Garten transformed an old farmhouse into a light-filled, five-room retreat designed for hosting—and feeding—guests.

In our latest Archive Dive, we revisit the 1994 renovation, which includes Garten’s search for the right paint color, her no-nonsense decorating approach, and her love of the property’s grounds. She took each project in stride with help from famous friends like Martha Stewart and the late interior designer Robert Currie—and Jeffrey, of course. The result was a relaxed home with an entertaining-ready atmosphere and a garden that would make any green-thumbed foodie proud. Read on for the original story.

With the help of Robert Currie, the owner of a fabulous food emporium in the Hamptons planned her nearby house for the casual ease of the legions of guests she feeds in her spare time.

Are you full of self-doubt in the dark of the night? Should you buy that old house? Should you change your career? Then follow in Ina Garten’s (bare) footsteps. She has done both with such breathtaking speed and success that the average worrier blinks in awe. She bought a Westhampton, New York, specialty store (oddly shaped breads, ravishing vegetables, seafood salad to go) from a business opportunities ad practically on the spot.

“I didn’t know how to slice smoked salmon or tell whether the Brie was ripe,” she confesses. But in no time, she turned her emporium, Barefoot Contessa, into a foodie’s fairyland.

After Garten moved the store to East Hampton she converted a cramped, run-down old farmhouse in the village into an airy five-room refuge and party venue for herself and her husband. “I just trusted that if the house had good ‘bones,’ I could figure it all out,” she laughs. No insomnia. No agonies. And, incredibly, no mistakes.

She’s a nurturer, too, a definite Earth Mother, which is certainly why she fled her career in nuclear energy policy planning for the far more congenial field of food. Her personal policy is summed up simply: lots of entertaining.

When her husband—a former Columbia professor, author, and investment banker—worked in New York, she encouraged his uptight fellow bankers to loosen their ties and shed their jackets by dishing up platters of fried chicken and other finger foods. In East Hampton she gives Sunday afternoon parties with plenty of easy food “like crabcake sandwiches,” and leaves all the doors open so guests will feel free to wander out and eat on the porch, or anywhere else that seems inviting.

She often serves in her sunny kitchen, which contains the requisite serious fridge and stove, but lacks the usual array of self-important utensils. This is a kitchen with nothing to prove.

The toughest decisions of her fix-up were the wall colors. She thought at first that she might like the whole house vanilla (a true foodie’s choice), but test patches were too white or yellow.

“I had samples all over the walls, and the painter was yelling ‘Stop already’,” but finally she hit on a toasty khaki for her bedroom. She was still stuck for the first floor when she spotted the bedroom primer paint: khaki halved with white. A mental lightbulb turned on, and the painter went to work. The house is now two values of that changeable shade with browny greens in the upholstery derived from the tapestry-covered living room sofa.

There is not a stick of unnecessary furniture in the farmhouse either, just several old oversize tables, a few pieces of portly upholstery, and in the bedroom, a brass bed (of which a friend said, “That’s not a bed, it’s a temple”), plus two chairs. No windows are curtained.

“Everything,” Garten feels, “should do something, have a function, not just sit there. I like things to be spare.” And bare as well, like her new floors, which are crafted of newly purchased old wood. Are the original floors still underneath? Nope. With a sanding and a coat of polyurethane, they are reincarnated as a kitchen countertop.

Garten actually bought the house for its grounds, because she yearned to garden. She had lived for a time in rented houses, but when she found herself planting expensive trees in rented lawns, she realized it was time to own some land.

Outside her kitchen doors she has cut square beds for tomatoes, which mix quite cheerfully with dahlias, and for roses knee-deep in catmint. She likes to combine “mess with structure,” but her structures are always imaginative. Instead of a single stake for each tomato plant, for instance, she does a row of tepees. Or she lines her disheveled strawberry beds with a spine of iris.

A Manhattan florist friend, Tom Pritchard of Pure Mädderlake, helped her with the garden design and did a very clever thing. He took two chairs out into the garden and walked around with her, scoping out the best seating spots. That’s how she decided which vistas to emphasize and which had too fine a view of the neighbor’s patio. Gardening has become a passion since she moved here. Every surface in her house is piled with well-thumbed horticultural tomes; for the time being, her food banks are relegated to the bottom of the pile.

Garten acknowledges that she has had a lot of help. Her good friend, the late Robert Currie, designed all the seating, shopped with her, and arranged the furniture. He also taught her to avoid like mad “anything that was too trendy.” Stephen Scanniello, the rose dean at the Brooklyn Botanic Garden, chose a hundred great roses for her, and Martha Stewart passed judgment on her new shower stall. No less helpful was her very supportive husband.

But husbands don’t ponder that midnight ceiling with you. And talented friends aren’t around at 2 a.m., when the real bullets are bitten. Some lucky people, though, do it all with ease—Ina Garten for one. Warm and comfy, like her own new house, she just turns over and goes back to sleep.

Architect Labs, a Palo Alto startup, said in a research blog post that its AI systems discovered, designed, and verified Frostbite, a general-purpose CPU with a new instruction set “built from the ground up.” Across 25 workloads, a four-tile Frostbite cluster delivered 4.59 times the performance per watt of a similarly sized “silicon-proven” dual-issue, in-order CPU core. Those numbers come from a simulated 7nm design that has not yet been made in silicon, and the company says it can’t name the comparison core for legal reasons.

Conventional CPUs have hardware that “rediscovers, at runtime, which instructions actually depend on each other.” Multicore designs also need deep cache hierarchies and coherence protocols, which keep every core’s view of shared memory consistent, but scale poorly and cost area and power. Architect Labs’ alternative is what it calls “multithreading for free,” where the program itself exposes the parallel work, without threads, locks, or out-of-order hardware required for that discovery.

Frostbite’s programs are compiled to interaction nets, a model of computation from 1990 in which a program is a graph of nodes and computation happens by rewriting pairs of connected nodes, and the chip is designed around this distinct paradigm. Pairs of nodes being rewritten never overlap, so they can run in any order, or all at once, and arrive at the same result. Every value is also used exactly once, so data can stay in small local memories instead of large shared caches.

An example of the difference given by the team is a 5x5 chess-like board with five queens, placed so none can attack another. A typical program searches for solutions one board at a time. As an interaction net, the same search breaks into 3,183 small operations, and the longest chain that has to run in order is only 262 steps, so on average about 12 operations are ready to run at once at each step. Nothing about the program was rewritten to get there, as the parallel work was already intrinsic.

Each of the chip’s tiles, small identical processing units linked by an on-chip network, has its own small local memory. Four tiles, plus four L3 nodes, form the cluster that’s benchmarked against the conventional core for the performance claims, but the full layout has 16 tiles. The design reached 800 MHz in early synthesis but ended up at 500 MHz in the final layout. It may come as some surprise that the AI which built Frostbite started with what the company calls “zero architectural priors,” though the team first picked the workloads it had to do well on, and this sort of blank slatism may have its advantages in avoiding bias. Instead, the agents read papers and benchmarks, built Python models, and then selected one concept for focus. The original 141-page PDF output, which contained a programming model, runtime, and high-level blocks, was deleted after the first register-transfer level (RTL) design was generated, because “many of the numbers and estimates were well off what was physically possible.”

That PDF was only ever a starting point. Once the agents picked an architecture, they moved away from English — as it can’t get every detail right or catch every mistake, according to the team — and wrote the spec in the company’s own language, called ASL. ASL translates to Lean, a proof checker, so it can be machine-checked, which the company says stops a large class of bugs and cuts down on agents gaming their objectives. From this, the agents devised Arcmodel, a software model of the whole chip written in ASL, before any circuit design existed.

Few images in horror are as recognizable as pig blood raining down on freshly-crowned prom queen Carrie White. Carrie's bloody prom night is such a famous scene that its cultural influence extends beyond movies. The rock band Hole's second, and best, album, 1994's "Live Through This," features cover art of a prom queen holding flowers close to her face with a wide-eyed smile but tear-drenched makeup. Featuring model Leilani Bishop and photographed by Ellen von Unwerth, the cover represented Hole's inflammatory frontwoman Courtney Love's attempt to recreate the pivotal scene from De Palma's "Carrie."

"We were on the phone for one hour. I didn't say much but listened, and Courtney had the idea of reenacting the scene of the movie 'Carrie,' which I loved, too," as von Unwerth once told fashion magazine AnOther. "Live Through This" earned much critical lauding and commercial success in its day and is still ranked as one of the best albums of 1994, if not in alternative rock history. The aggressive songs on it both cut deep and flat out rock.

Even the "Carrie"-inspired album cover art is famous in its own right. Rolling Stone has ranked "Live Through This" as the 13th best album cover ever. Later female musicians like Olivia Rodrigo have staged "Live Through This" homages featuring themselves, much like how the original album cover paid homage to "Carrie." That homage isn't skin deep, either. The "Carrie" allusions on the album's cover reflect the album's themes of how the world likes to (metaphorically) drop pig's blood on women, especially ones who step into the spotlight.

The title "Live Through This" immediately suggests someone going through a tough time and, barely, finding the strength to, well, live through it. As Courtney Love has explained, the title comes from a line of dialogue in a different movie — "Gone with the Wind" — and was largely inspired by the microscope the media had put her life under. (Love's husband, Kurt Cobain, died by suicide in April 1994, about a week before the release of "Live Through This.")

"Softer, Softest" features the verse "burn the witch, just bring me back her head," referencing the historical persecution and execution of women as "witches." Besides the album cover, that's the line that most directly connects "Live Through This" to "Carrie," a story all about religious trauma. The withdrawn and telekinetic Carrie White is the kind of woman who'd be burned as a witch in darker ages.

Carrie's pain is often explicitly feminine, too, as the story infamously kicks off with her getting her first period. The allusions to "Carrie" in "Live Through This" infuse subtext that, with all that the world makes women live through, it's miraculous that more of them don't explode like Carrie White did.

Like so many things with AI, it’s not that large language models opened up some wholly new capability. For as long as there have been gadgets, a few users have been hacking, modding, jailbreaking, and tweaking them to their liking. What is new is that it’s easy now. People all over the world are discovering that they can use large language models to hack their hardware simply by prompting Claude Code or Codex to do it. It has led to a boom in developer-friendly gadgets and created a market for better software on just about every device you can think of.

For one developer in India who goes by Gathin T, the whole thing started almost on a whim. “One afternoon, I was fiddling with my TV, and it was just really shitty,” he tells me. His was a set made by Xiaomi and running Android TV, with so many ads and so much interface chaos it was hard to even change inputs. And he got to thinking: If you can install another launcher on your Android phone, maybe you can do the same to your TV. “The first thing that came to mind was to remove the bloatware from the TV,” he says. “Then I thought, why not make it look better?”

Gathin prompted Claude Code and discovered that with a couple of permissions tweaks and a toggle of USB debugging mode, his chatbot could control his TV as long as they were on the same Wi-Fi network. An hour and a half later, Gathin had redesigned his TV to his liking — no more onscreen ads, a simpler interface, easier access to his PlayStation on another input. He posted on X about what he’d done and promptly went viral. So Gathin put together his work, bought a domain, and launched CleanYourTV as a tool for helping other people solve their own television interfaces. It became one of the most popular repositories on GitHub, got thousands of pull requests and feature ideas, and inspired countless people to point their own chatbot at their big screen.

Gathin is careful to note that the only TVs that work this easily are based on Android. (He has Claude Code-d his way through other operating systems, but those involve more explicit jailbreaking and rooting, so he’s more reluctant to share the process so widely.) But for vibe hackers everywhere, the good news is that Android kind of runs everything. From dishwashers to digital photo frames to luxury cars, even gadgets that don’t look like Android often run Android. Even when they don’t, odds are good that there’s another way in. If there’s a hidden API, your LLM can find it. If the firmware file lives on a website somewhere, your LLM can unpack it, explore it, and repackage it to your liking.

Again, few of the technical capabilities here are new, and enterprising developers have been doing this kind of work for many years. Some have even been using AI for several years to make the work easier. But when Claude Code first had its “oh my gosh it works” moment in late 2025, developers began leaning on the tools even more — and non-developers began discovering they, too, could build software now. And over the course of 2026, more and more people began pointing that capability at their hardware.

Now, it seems, all hardware is hacker hardware. Camera enthusiasts embraced the possibilities of vibe hacking as fast as anyone, using LLMs to repackage their camera software in all sorts of ways. Firmware hacks like Magic Lantern are a staple of the camera community, but suddenly it seems like everyone is using AI to add new recording capabilities, support higher resolutions, or even add new looks and color profiles to their shots. Many are using the tools to give their old gear new life; other, more enterprising vibe coders are pointing their bots at their brand-new hardware.

The music world is being similarly upended. Budding musicians are turning cheap synths into powerful new kinds of gear, and a cheap gadget known as the M-Vave FM-1 has become a popular target for custom firmware and new features. M-Vave has even released an open-source firmware, embracing the hackers as other companies have tried to keep them out. The FM-1 is cheap, it’s easy to hack, and it’s raising questions about what higher-end manufacturers can even do to compete.

Gamers, who are also using AI to decompile and mod the games they love, are using LLMs to add new features to their consoles. One vibe coder found a way to play PlayStation 2 discs on a PlayStation 5; lots of gamers have built scripts that allow them to use their favorite controllers on other devices.

In the games world in particular, this has caused the industry to fight back. Game makers are looking for ways to protect their work from being decompiled and made available for free and on other platforms. Luke Dicken, a longtime gaming executive who was recently Take-Two’s head of artificial intelligence, recently predicted to Kotakuthat the industry will start to embrace “more invasive DRM, platform-level solutions, and probably more of a push towards streaming solutions since nobody can decompile what they can’t get their hands on.” Hardware makers will push harder to lock down their systems; companies across the board will pursue legal action to protect their intellectual property.

All this hardware access also creates new risks for users. When you install a vibe-coded version of your camera’s firmware, you are now well outside the bounds of what the camera manufacturer is equipped to help you solve. You might be stuck asking Claude to fix the problems Claude created. As Ritchie Roesch wrote recently at Fuji X Weekly, “It’s not so much if someone’s camera will become a permanent paperweight, it’s a matter of when and how many, and whether it will be yours.”

The safer (and less litigious) route for the enterprising vibe hacker lies in an entirely new kind of hardware. ESP32 modules, which package networking chips, microphones, speakers, and more into a simple and hacker-friendly package, are suddenly everywhere in hardware communities. (Think Raspberry Pis, but even cheaper and more versatile.) So sure, you can use Claude Code to jailbreak and de-Amazon your Kindle, but why do that when you can buy a device like the M5Stack PaperMono, an ESP32-based e-paper device you can make into whatever you want?

ESP32 modules are perfectly suited to help you build the kind of hyper-specific device Apple or Samsung would never consider. With a few dollars and a few prompts, you can build a way to see real-time sea conditions, rig up a monitor for your whole DJ system, or just play Metal Gear Solidon the go. You can even have a dedicated device for your family’s shopping list.

The arms race between device manufacturers and vibe hackers is only going to continue. As AI models get more powerful, and both the tools and their users get more sophisticated, it’ll be easier and easier for anyone to point their bot at their TV, their car, or their washing machine and see what happens. Some manufacturers will try their best to keep the hackers out; others will look for ways to safely embrace them. Some users will find incredible new functionality for their tech; others will find they’ve created extraordinarily large and expensive paperweights.

Before long, we’re going to see a lot of ESP32 devices turned into more consumer-friendly hardware. Meta is already leading the charge with its Muse Charm and the Muse Gadgets program, through which you can either buy a nicely packaged ESP32 module or use your own to quickly spin up a dedicated AI device. This kind of gadget — just a simple computing module in a pretty box — is going to be everywhere.

At the same time, we’re already seeing some vibe hackers turn into proper software vendors. Gathin, for instance, has spent a lot of time reckoning with the responsibility he feels for what happens when people use CleanYourTV to hack their televisions. He’s tweaked the prompts he shares to first take a backup of your TV’s software in order to make it easy to restore if something goes wrong, and to make note of every change it makes along the way. The CleanYourTV system also checks its progress as it goes and spins up agents to manually test if something has gone wrong, and Gathin hopes it’s increasingly foolproof.

But it’s not foolproof. It might never be. Devices change all the time; every software update introduces new possibilities and new problems. So Gathin recommends the same thing I hear from vibe coders and vibe hackers all the time: Never give the bots access to something you can’t afford to lose. “Even for fiddling with stuff,” he says, “I have an external computer which runs through Linux. So I just use it like a sandbox environment to test things out.” If you’re going to try and vibe-hack your phone (which lots of people are doing) or your computer (which lots of people are also doing), do it carefully. And make lots of backups.

This makes it easier for developers to put their apps on the HomeView — if an audio or video app supports CarPlay, it already works on the HomeView (there’s no support for other types of CarPlay apps).

In practice, this creates frustrating limitations, including no search function, requiring a user profile on the HomeView, and your phone on the same Wi-Fi network. It also results in slower performance and a laggy, inconsistent experience compared to the native apps.

I tried several apps: Pocket Casts, Amazon Music, YouTube Music, Spotify, and Audible. They all appeared in my HomeView app drawer, and I could access them, choose content via streamlined interfaces, and then play audio on the HomeView or AirPlay from it to any speakers.

But CarPlay apps don’t work as well as the built-in audio apps — Music, Podcasts, and Safari. They take longer to load and respond more slowly. You also can’t type a search query in any of them, as you can in the native apps, because they don’t have a search field or keyboard. Instead, you have to scroll through content to find what you want or use Siri voice search.

Me: “Siri, resume my Audible book.” Siri: “Shuffling latest songs from Apple Music.”

There’s no search box because you’re not supposed to type while driving; only I’m not driving — I’m in my kitchen! This shows how much of a CarPlay port the apps are; they haven’t been updated to account for the fact that this device is stationary and in your home. Case in point, when I first pulled up the Apple TV app (the only CarPlay video app currently supported), a warning popped up telling me to make sure I was stationary before watching any video.

Playing audio through CarPlay apps is also buggy. It didn’t always bring up the music control screen, so I had to use my iPhone to control it. Plus, the Audible app often refused to load at all, and didn’t work with Siri voice search. Me: “Siri, resume my Audible book.” Siri: “Shuffling latest songs from Apple Music.”

CarPlay support feels like a stopgap, and Apple needs to work with developers to refine the software and add HomeView-specific controls for a better, more integrated experience.

Much of the confusion around the HomeView stems from treating it like a smart speaker when it’s really a smart display. I get it; I’ve spent the last decade frustrated by smart displays from Google and Amazon that promise a lot but rarely do it all well.

If you don’t care about the other features of a smart display and just want a smart speaker, then the reason to buy a HomeView over a HomePod is if you have a use for it as a touchscreen music controller. This lets anyone in the household easily browse and control what’s playing. Bear in mind, though, Apple Music (with a subscription) is the only app anyone can use to play music on the HomeView, as CarPlay apps require a user profile.

If you want a smart speaker primarily to play music and are happy using your voice or phone to control it, go for a HomePod. If you use Spotify or other third-party music services, consider an Echo Dot or Google Home smart speaker; both let you set a default speaker and a default music provider — such as Spotify.

If you already own HomePods and use Apple Music, the HomeView’s touchscreen will fit in nicely as a simple way to control audio. Just don’t buy one expecting it to fully replace a dedicated smart speaker.

The U.S. Let Volvo Keep Selling Cars. Polestar Got A No. What About Mercedes?

The Volvo EX-90 and Polestar 3 roll off the same assembly line in South Carolina. They share much of their hardware and, according to Polestar, run the same software. However, when each manufacturer asked Washington for permission to keep selling them in America, they got different answers.

Now Congress is considering a tougher law aimed at keeping Chinese cars out of the U.S., one that could reintroduce headaches for Volvo and put another major automaker in a tough position. Congress is close to a deal on legislation that would ban cars from automakers with more than 15% Chinese ownership stake, as Bloomberg reported. This would be a tough pill for Mercedes-Benz, as around 20% of the company's stock is owned by Chinese investors.

But the publication reported that Republican and Democratic lawmakers are nearing a deal that could spare Mercedes. They want to send a bill to the president by the end of the year, but its terms have not yet been finalized.

Volvo EX90 assembly at the Volvo Ridgeville factory in South Carolina

The Volvo and Polestar decisions came under a Commerce Department rule finalized in 2025 aimed at controlling connectivity and self-driving technology. Washington’s concern is that a foreign adversary could use those systems to collect data on Americans or even remotely control vehicles. The new restrictions on certain Chinese- or Russian-linked software take effect for the 2027 model year. Hardware restrictions will follow in 2030 and apply to both gas and electric cars, including ones assembled on U.S. soil.

Chinese giant Geely has a majority stake in Volvo Cars, so the Swedish automaker had to apply for specific authorization to keep importing and selling connected cars. The Department of Commerce granted it. Volvo told InsideEVs it still has to meet the same software requirements as every other automaker. The Swedish foreign trade minister confirmed his government worked closely with Volvo to achieve compliance.

Polestar is another Sweden-based automaker that is controlled by Geely. It applied and was denied authorization to keep selling cars from model year 2027 onward. So why was Polestar axed despite Volvo being saved? Polestar told its dealers it had asked the Department of Commerce that very question and had yet to get an explanation, pointing to the common software and the shared South Carolina production line.

Even though Polestar is a Volvo spinoff and also has a Swedish connection, the country’s trade minister said Polestar didn’t want the same assistance as Volvo. That’s one difference we know between their applications, but the U.S. government hasn’t explicitly said why things shook out this way, and Polestar did not appeal the decision.

Mercedes-Benz EQS SUV production in Alabama

The proposed Connected Vehicle Security Act would write existing restrictions into federal law and extend the U.S. sales ban to automakers with more than 15% Chinese ownership, even if they build their cars in America.

China’s state-owned BAIC Group owns 9.98% of Mercedes-Benz. Geely founder Li Shufu holds another 9.69% through an investment company. Taken together, that’s just shy of 20%, and over the new proposed threshold.

For reference, Geely owns 79% of Volvo and Geely founder Li Shufu reported beneficial ownership of 60.5% of Polestar’s shares in a September 2026 SEC filing.

That doesn't mean Mercedes will be locked out of the market, though, even if the bill passes as currently written.

“The U.S. is an extremely important market for us, and we're not going to do anything to jeopardize our success in the past and our success in the future,” Jason Hoff, CEO of Mercedes-Benz North America, said during a media roundtable with InsideEVs last week.

When asked whether the company had a plan for its Chinese shareholders to divest, Hoff declined to comment on specific conversations Mercedes has with shareholders. He was more direct about the legislation itself.

“You know, we always have and always will support anything that protects the national security of the United States. So we've never opposed this bill, but you know, in the end, we'll also take whatever steps we need to comply with it if it does get passed," Hoff said.

Mercedes says its Chinese shareholders have no seats on its management board and no say in day-to-day decisions. They could still have voting rights as shareholders. That matters because, according to Bloomberg, lawmakers are considering an exception that would let companies exclude passive investments with no voting rights from the proposed 15% ownership calculation.

It’s still unclear how Mercedes would prove its Chinese stakes qualify. In the meantime, House members have asked Mercedes to provide documents, answer two dozen questions by December 1, and arrange a meeting with its U.S. boss.

Randy Flores brings a former player's perspective to Houston Astros GM job - and knows its limits

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In his first year as St. Louis Cardinals scouting director, Randy Flores was watching a pitcher who threw a four-seam fastball and a curveball. The mix can be the backbone of a north-south pitching profile. But to Flores, a former major-league reliever, it looked suboptimal.

"I'm like, 'No, no, no - you sink it and slide it,'" said Flores, reciting an approach he gleaned from former Cardinals pitching coach Dave Duncan.

As a slight left-hander who did not throw especially hard, Flores carved out a playing career that lasted parts of eight major-league seasons. So, his methods had merit. But as an executive now, he recalls the scouting anecdote as something of a cautionary tale.

"Being a former player, if you're anchored too much to, 'This is how it was done when I was playing,' it's the wrong thing," Flores said.

Exploring other perspectives and means and finding a balance has been part of Flores' evolution as an executive, one that led to his hiring this month as general manager of the Houston Astros. It is also an arc to which only a handful of the majors' current highest-ranking baseball executives can relate.

Of the current presidents of baseball operations, Jerry Dipoto (Mariners), Chris Young (Rangers), Craig Breslow (Red Sox) and Buster Posey (Giants) played in the majors. So did general managers Chris Getz (White Sox) and Flores, whom the Astros hired after 11-plus seasons in St. Louis' front office.

That many top executives did not play in the majors, or at all professionally, illustrates how it is far from required. It might even invite the question of how such experience informs former players' approach to the role, if at all, as Flores settles into running a baseball operations department for the first time.

"You probably have more empathy … for what a player goes through, from the moment they sign their first professional contract to when they get to the big leagues," Dipoto said at last week's GM meetings in Las Vegas.

Dipoto and Breslow were relievers in the majors, like Flores. Dipoto posited that background could help "to understand the life of the guy who (is) not always the most thought-of player on the roster."

"Outside of that, I think it's just business as usual," Dipoto said. "We each went through our own, I guess, apprenticeship (after playing).

"You take the lessons you learn and apply them, just like any other general manager does when they get their first job. We just maybe have that slight - I don't even want to call it an advantage - that slight difference in that we have lived life in these players' shoes and probably as a result have a little bit more empathy rather than just frustration. It's a hard game."

Mariners GM Jerry Dipoto, a former major league reliever, is among a half-dozen ex-players who head up baseball operations departments. He said that experience includes empathy for players' journeys through pro ball. (Lindsey Wasson/AP)

After his playing career, Dipoto held roles in scouting before becoming general manager of the Angels in 2011 and for the Mariners in 2015. Breslow spent five seasons in the Cubs' front office, working heavily in pitching development, before the Red Sox hired him to lead their baseball operations.

"My hope would be it adds a level of credibility to the role and a level of empathy," Breslow said, "given that there really isn't anything I ask player to do that I haven't kind of been through myself, having been traded, promoted, released, started games, come in from the pen.

"And I think understanding the mindset of a player and how they're likely to respond and react to different suggestions and requests, I'm hopeful (that) is helpful in terms of understanding how to approach certain conversations."

Flores, 51, spent several years out of professional baseball after pitching his final season in the minors in 2011. He returned to the University of Southern California, his alma mater, to pursue a master's degree with a self-described goal to "chart a career path combining business and sports." He started a company that captured and provided amateur game video for use by recruiters and scouts.

In 2015, the Cardinals sought a scouting director to replace Chris Correa, who was dismissed and later imprisoned for illegally accessing the Astros' computer database. John Mozeliak, then St. Louis' GM, recalled that the Cardinals did not want to overhaul their draft process, which was producing results. Flores, who played five seasons in St. Louis, interviewed and landed the job, his first in a front office.

"There's more to leading a department than just believing in a model and running with it," Mozeliak said this month. "But I think his interpersonal skills are outstanding. I think he has the ability to walk into any type of room and be able to speak to the group."

New Astros GM Randy Flores (left), pictured with former Cardinals teammate Matt Morris, pitched in the majors for parts of eight seasons before moving into front-office work with St. Louis. (MARK HUMPHREY/AP)

Flores oversaw St. Louis' amateur drafts from 2016-26, including for the past eight seasons as an assistant general manager. In recent years, Flores gained experience in other areas of the Cardinals' operations including pro scouting and salary arbitration, he said at his introductory press conference in Houston.

"He's definitely ambitious," said Mozeliak, who left St. Louis after the 2025 season and is now the Angels' interim general manager. "And there was a certain point where he started to ask to get more exposure to different elements of baseball operations. So as he was having early success on the scouting side, it was just natural to keep letting him evolve and giving him exposure to more of the things that you really see under the hood when you're working with or as a general manager."

Chaim Bloom joined the Cardinals as an advisor in early 2024 and succeeded Mozeliak as their president of baseball operations. In their two seasons overlapping in St. Louis, Bloom said he found Flores "gifted in how he can navigate and understand any type of person or group of people you come across in this game" and appreciated "the way he challenged me to think differently about some things."

"It's hard to go into specific detail on that," Bloom said. "But just in terms of some of the discussions we would have on players or how to play the draft - and (also) some things in the pro space, just thinking about different acquisitions - the way he's able to assimilate information, hear different voices and kind of distill it down into something that's actionable, I found that really helpful."

For executives with playing backgrounds, the exchange of viewpoints is a two-way street. It's incumbent on former players to consider how their experience might limit how they view situations, said Brandon Gomes, who spent five seasons as a reliever with the Rays and is now the Dodgers' general manager under president of baseball operations Andrew Friedman.

"Obviously, we have a lot of fantastic executives that never played that have other really helpful perspectives without a bias of having played," Gomes said. "I think you just have to be aware of it; we all have biases. So just being aware it could be there and being a good listener, understanding that you may be too far anchored in that, is a good way to go about it."

Changes within the game can require it. Flores noted during his Astros introduction how access to the kind of data and technology available today might have impacted his own playing career and said he considers analytics "the fabric (and) foundation of … evidence-based decision-making" as an executive.

While data use is prevalent in front offices, though, those who can weigh it against firsthand experience playing in major-league games are less so.

"(Flores) is someone that anchored to these modern tools to help with the decision-making," Mozeliak said. "But he also has great feel for what happens inside a clubhouse or on the field. So being able to pair those two skill sets, I think, will just allow him to make better decisions along the way."

The Astros wagered as much in hiring Flores as general manager, handing him a major-league roster that finished 81-81 in 2026 and a farm system rated among the sport's thinnest by outside evaluators. Astros owner Jim Crane, who maintains involvement in baseball operations along with advisor and Hall of Fame first baseman Jeff Bagwell, touted Flores' playing career afterward as part of his "well-rounded" résumé.

Flores said last week that early on as a scouting director, he felt the need to guard against "attaching too much to my bias of my empirical experience" in evaluating players. Still, those years as a pitcher form an inextricable part of the path that led Flores to the role he assumes now in Houston.

"I understand how hard it is to play this game and I think I have some empathy for that," Flores said. "And I do believe the modern tools and curriculum and the technology and coaching that are available help drive player improvements at a pace much faster than I experienced as player via trial and error.

"So my hope is that I understand how difficult that road is, but I also drive our organization to help those players be the best version of themselves."

First, you note that $200k of premiums would be paid through year 5, which I assume are paid at the rate of $40k per year. But the cash surrender value is only $131k at the end of the 5th year. Unfortunately, that's typical for a "permanent" life insurance product like indexed universal life. This cost is solely the result of your employer deciding to "pay" you with an IUL product rather than cash. (Also, the agent, who I expect is the friend of somebody highly placed in your company, probably made somewhere in the range of $40k in commissions for selling this policy.)

Second, while I stand by my suggestion of doing the simplest indexing strategy of S&P 1 year point to point with a cap, I don't feel that so strongly as to preclude other choices. All of those options should have generally the same "expected value", so pick whichever you want.

Third, upon reflection, I'd like to suggest an alternative way to factor this policy into your overall financial picture, presuming that you're able to partially surrender the cash surrender value while you remain employed. Central to this approach is realizing that the surrender value is really a "fixed income" investment (that is, not an "equity" investment, since you're credited with an interest rate each year which happens to be based on an external index with a floor of zero.)

As to this though process - I'd pick an indexing strategy, and then just let the policy sit and accumulate value for maybe 5 years or so, being careful to retain the annual statements that you'll get shortly after each policy anniversary. At the end of 5 years, the illustration shows that you should have $131k of surrender value. At that time, you can go through a two-step thought process, as follows ----
---- How does my overall asset allocation look, including this $131k of surrender value as a "fixed income" asset? If, for example, your targeted asset allocation were to be 100% equities at this stage in your life, that would suggest that you should withdraw cash from the policy and reinvest the proceeds in equities, ignoring income tax consequences (discussed below). On the other hand, if I want to have this $131k invested in fixed income assets, then go to step 2 below.
---- Does the rate I'm earning on the IUL surrender value look good compared to alternatives? You've now got 5 years of interest credit history, so you can do a geometric average of the five credited rates. There's a chance that the geometric average after 5 years will be somewhere in the range of 5-7% per year, which you may find attractive compared to alternatives. However, if the geometric average is pretty low, it would be in your best interest to partially surrender the policy and reinvest the proceeds in some other fixed income asset outside the IUL, subject to income tax consequences.

As to income tax - presuming that the premiums are paid fully by the employer, I would expect that any partial surrender would be taxed as ordinary income to you. (Note that I am not a tax expert.) InfoQ Homepage News PostgreSQL 19 Released with Online Table Repacking and Query Planning Controls

Nov 22, 2026 2 min read

The PostgreSQL Global Development Group released PostgreSQL 19, introducing changes to database maintenance, query planning, replication, and application development. The release focuses on reducing administrative overhead and improving performance, with new tools for reorganizing tables without downtime and controlling query plans.

One of the main additions is REPACK, which reclaims unused table space and can reorder data to match an index. Its CONCURRENTLY option allows the operation to run without blocking reads or writes, making it a potential replacement for common uses of VACUUM FULL and CLUSTER. PostgreSQL 19 also introduces pg_plan_advice and pg_stash_advice, extensions that let administrators influence query plan selection and apply stored advice to queries by identifier, without changing application code.

Other maintenance improvements include parallel index vacuuming, changes to autovacuum prioritization, and additional monitoring views. EXPLAIN ANALYZE and related logging now report asynchronous I/O activity and WAL generated for full-page images. PostgreSQL also reports that changes to foreign key checks made bulk inserts nearly twice as fast in testing. Query planner improvements include more efficient anti-joins for eligible NOT IN clauses and eager aggregation before joins.

The release adds several features for replication and application development. Logical replication now supports sequence values, and PostgreSQL automatically raises the effective WAL level to logical when the first logical replication slot is created. The new WAIT FOR LSN command lets applications wait until a standby has replayed a specified WAL position before querying data, providing read-your-writes consistency. Developers also get INSERT ... ON CONFLICT DO SELECT for get-or-create operations, JSON output from COPY, additional SQL/JSON string methods, and IGNORE NULLS support for window functions.

Security and compatibility changes include server-side TLS Server Name Indication (SNI), which allows a server to present different certificates based on the hostname used by the client. PostgreSQL 19 also warns after successful MD5 password authentication. MD5 was deprecated in PostgreSQL 18 and is planned for removal in a future release, with SCRAM recommended instead. JIT compilation is now disabled by default because its cost estimates proved unreliable, while LZ4 becomes the default TOAST compression method when supported by the build.

A Common Meat Triggers a Unique Muscle-Building Peptide, Study Finds

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A new study found that lean ground pork triggered a distinctive increase in a naturally occurring peptide linked to muscle growth.

A new study suggests that the way the body processes protein after exercise may depend on more than how much protein a meal contains. Researchers found that lean ground pork produced a distinctive increase in a naturally occurring peptide linked to muscle protein synthesis, the biological process responsible for building and repairing muscle proteins.

Published in Molecular Nutrition & Food Research, the research examined how different protein sources affect amino acids and peptides circulating in the bloodstream after resistance exercise. The findings suggest that the structure and composition of whole foods may influence how protein is digested and made available to the body.

Researchers at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign analyzed blood samples and muscle protein synthesis measurements from two randomized controlled trials involving healthy young adults.

Following resistance exercise, participants consumed one of five nutritional treatments: low-fat ground pork, high-fat ground pork, black beans and rice, an isolated nutrient beverage containing amino acids and other nutrients, or a carbohydrate-only control drink. Each protein-containing treatment supplied approximately 20 grams (0.7 ounces) of protein, allowing researchers to compare the body’s responses to different nutritional sources.

Among the five treatments, lean ground pork was the only one to produce a statistically significant increase above baseline in circulating dileucine, a peptide composed of two linked leucine molecules. Blood concentrations rose approximately 90 to 120 minutes after consumption. Dileucine has been associated with muscle protein synthesis, although scientists are still investigating its specific role in muscle metabolism.

Peak dileucine concentrations after lean ground pork consumption were significantly higher than those measured after the isolated nutrient beverage and carbohydrate control. However, the differences compared with high-fat ground pork and the beans-and-rice meal were not statistically significant.

Lean ground pork also produced the highest peak concentrations of leucine, an essential amino acid involved in regulating muscle protein synthesis. These levels were significantly higher than those following high-fat ground pork, beans and rice, or the carbohydrate control, but were not significantly different from the isolated nutrient beverage.

“These findings suggest that the body’s response to protein is influenced by more than the amount consumed,” said Nicholas A. Burd, PhD, corresponding author and researcher at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign. “Seeing how the whole-food matrix affects the release of amino acids and peptides into the bloodstream gives us a clearer picture of how dietary protein may actually drive muscle protein synthesis and remodeling.”

Across the protein-containing treatments, researchers identified a modest but statistically significant positive relationship between peak dileucine concentrations and changes in muscle protein synthesis. The same association was not observed with conventional leucine measurements.

Importantly, this does not establish that dileucine directly stimulates muscle growth or that lean ground pork produces greater muscle gains than other foods.

One possible explanation involves the food matrix, which encompasses a food’s physical structure and the interactions among its components. Researchers proposed that grinding meat could disrupt connective tissue, potentially making proteins more accessible to digestive enzymes and changing how amino acids and peptides enter the bloodstream.

The results add to research investigating how whole-food protein sources influence the body’s response to exercise. Although the protein content of a meal remains relevant, differences in food structure, fat content, and digestion may affect the timing and composition of nutrients reaching the bloodstream.

“Consumers often think about protein in terms of how many grams are on their plate, but science is showing us there is much more to the story,” said Kristen Hicks-Roof, PhD, RDN, LDN, Senior Director of Human Nutrition at the National Pork Board. “What’s exciting about this research is that it helps us better understand how a familiar food like ground pork delivers protein and how the body responds to it after exercise. Ground pork represents an important opportunity to connect emerging protein science with practical, nutritious meals people already enjoy.”

Further studies will be needed to determine whether these short-term biological responses translate into meaningful differences in muscle growth and recovery. Researchers also need to examine how dileucine behaves when protein sources are consumed as part of typical mixed meals and whether similar responses occur in older adults and other populations.

Irwin, Alexander V. Ulanov and Nicholas A. Burd, 19 October 2026, Molecular Nutrition & Food Research. DOI: 10.1002/mnfr.70628

Disney Just Hit a 52-Week High. 3 Reasons It Still Has Room to Run.

Record parks and experiences demand contradicts fears of a consumer spending slowdown.

By Daniel Foelber – Nov 22, 2026 at 7:45AM EST

10 stocks we like better than Walt Disney ›

Walt Disney (DIS -1.89%) closed at a 52-week high on Nov. 18 -- the day after it reported its fourth-quarter and full-year fiscal 2026 results. Disney is now up 7.6% year to date and just a hair below its highest level in over four years.

Here's why Disney is breaking out despite a sell-off in the major stock market indexes, and whether the consumer discretionary stock has room to run from here.

Fiscal 2026 was arguably Disney's best year since before the COVID-19 pandemic. Overall revenue grew 8% year over year, and margins improved, driven by a 12% increase in operating income.

A major reason for Disney's increase in profitability is that Disney+ is now a key contributor to entertainment earnings, whereas just a few years ago it was losing money. In fiscal 2026, the entertainment subscription video-on-demand (SVOD) segment, which mainly comprises Disney+ and Hulu, generated $2.57 billion in operating income, up from $1.3 billion in fiscal 2025. That's a significant 47.2% of total entertainment operating income for the fiscal year -- proving that streaming is helping to offset the declines in linear cable networks.

Disney's sports segment grew revenue 5% year over year and operating income by 7%, which may seem weak at first glance. But sports holding its own is impressive, considering margins remain under pressure due to media rights costs, the challenges of integrating ESPN with Hulu and Disney+, and the risk of sports streaming cannibalizing traditional cable customers.

Experiences had another excellent year, including notching over $10 billion in revenue in a single quarter (the fourth quarter of fiscal 2026). Overall, the experiences segment generated a whopping 28.3% operating margin for fiscal 2026 and contributed 56.7% of Disney's total operating income.

For fiscal 2027, Disney is forecasting another year of double-digit adjusted earnings-per-share growth, including 8% adjusted operating income growth.

Disney is benefiting from an in-person experiences boom. In an era of rising screen time, folks are yearning for in-person connections with their families and others. And Disney's experiences drill into the core of that desire.

While the parks continue to be Disney's ultimate cash cow, its latest results were a reminder of just how important Disney's other experiences have become -- namely its cruise business.

The cruise industry is on fire. On its third-quarter fiscal 2026 earnings call, Carnival said it is already half-booked for full-year 2027, even with occupancy and pricing at record levels. Royal Caribbean Cruises (RCL -2.20%) is coming off an exceptional summer sailing season.

Disney's multiyear ramp-up in its cruise fleet is paying off big-time. It launched two ships in the late 1990s and two more in the early 2010s before a decade-long pause. But since 2022, it has launched four ships -- doubling its fleet. The company plans to launch five more ships over the next five years -- bringing its total fleet to 13 ships. And on the Nov. 17 earnings call, CEO Josh D'Amaro said that Disney would add even more ships if it could get ones that met the company’s standards, given cruise ships are much more flexible than building land-based resorts.

Disney has shifted more from a motion picture company to an experiences powerhouse -- realizing that experiences present a more attractive risk/reward profile than trying to smash a home run with a box-office hit (although it still relies on new content to drive fresh storytelling). Management is guiding for $9 billion in fiscal 2027 capital expenditures (capex) -- which is significant relative to its operating expenses. A lot of that is going to experiences, including building out the cruise fleet and some major park renovations.

Avengers Campus at Disney California Adventure Park is set to double in size in 2028. Tropical Americas is expected to come to Animal Kingdom as soon as next year, alongside a Monsters Inc.-themed land in Hollywood Studios. Construction is underway at a new Cars-themed land and Disney Villains Land in Magic Kingdom. There's also a Spider-Man-themed land in the works at Shanghai Disneyland. And the grand prize is a new Disneyland in Abu Dhabi, which is expected to open between 2030 and 2033.

In sum, Disney's latest fiscal year and guidance validate that it is putting capex to good use on projects that will be embraced by guests and benefit its bottom line.

Since Josh D'Amaro took over as CEO earlier this year, a major theme has been returning capital to shareholders.

In Disney's third-quarter fiscal 2026 earnings report in August, the company said it believes its shares are undervalued, which is why it accelerated stock buybacks. The move has proven correct so far, given Disney's recovering stock price. And despite Disney's massive fiscal 2027 capex forecast, it still expects to generate $11 billion in free cash flow and target $9 billion in stock buybacks.

The same day as its earnings release on Nov. 17, Disney announced a 33% dividend increase -- boosting the semiannual dividend from $0.75 per share to $1 per share for an annualized yield of 1.7%. It's worth noting that buybacks are still a much larger share of Disney's capital return program -- which leaves plenty of room for future dividend hikes.

Disney has a reasonable valuation -- even after its recent run-up. The stock trades at just 17.4 times adjusted fiscal 2026 earnings -- which is more than reasonable for an industry-leading entertainment giant growing earnings by double digits.

Disney has an unmatched intellectual property portfolio, vastly improved streaming profitability, ongoing cash flows from legacy linear networks, a resilient sports segment, and a high-margin experiences segment.

Add it all up, and Disney remains a balanced buy for value investors who believe consumers will continue to pay a premium for high-quality in-person experiences. The larger dividend provides an added incentive to hold the entertainment stock over the long term.

For many of us, this seems like a new problem. But for one veteran game developer, the scenario is all too familiar. Howard Scott Warshaw is the developer of numerous Atari 2600 games including Yars' Revenge and Raiders of the Lost Ark. But perhaps his most famous (or infamous) game is 1982's E.T. the Extra Terrestrial, the notorious tie-in game that was so terrible Atari buried unsold copies of it in the desert.

E.T. has been cited as a contributing factor in the US video game industry crash of 1983—one of many poor-quality 8-bit titles that hollowed out the gaming market in the States. It's here where Warshaw sees a parallel to today's emerging scenario, though he doesn't necessarily believe it will end in the same way.

In a talk he gave at the DevGAMM Lisbon conference last week (via GamesIndustry.biz) Warshaw remarked upon how AI coding tools have seemed to rapidly sped up iteration times on game development. "People who used to spend weeks, months or years programming something can now do it in days with AI support," he explained.

"So what does this mean in game development? What it means is we're going to see a lot more games produced. You ever hear of the crash of '83 or '84, the big video game crash? They said the problem that happened in the crash, and I think that was true, was there was a glut of crap on the market, because the machine opened up and anybody could just pop a game out, and they did."

Warshaw pointed out that, at the time "good games were still being made" but these games were "way overshadowed by the crap: and that created a real problem in the game industry." He sees a similar situation emerging now thanks to generative AI tools. "AI is going to have the same impact on a larger scale, because on one level, so many more people can now realise and create a video game than ever could before just using AI. And that's okay, but there's going to be a lot of crap, because most games suck. That's just the way it is."

The reason for this, Warshaw says, is that, like all artistic endeavours "there's some things in life that everybody can see themselves doing, and the truth is most people who do it can't do it very well. And what AI is going to do is make that more possible for people who can't do it to do it." Yet while he thinks that most of the games that result from these ideas "aren't going to be very interesting" he also believes that "it's also going to enable the people who really do have some innovative, interesting and solid ideas to realise those, too."

COP31: Climate deal 'not adopted' as EU, others reject draft declaration over fossil fuels omission

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The summit was extended as nations worked to reach a deal, but they couldn't agree on including fossil fuels. European nations, small island states and activists have criticized oil-producing countries for the impasse.

Diplomats and delegates from nearly 200 countries are meeting in Antalya, Turkey

The summit's president acknowledges that a deal was 'not adopted'

The final declaration omits any mention of fossil fuels

The EU says it cannot accept a deal that 'greenwashes fossil fuels'

Fossil fuels are a major flashpoint at this year's summit, which comes amid soaring energy prices and tensions over the US-Israeli war with Iran

These are the major developments from the closing phase of the UN's COP31 Climate Summit in Antalya, Turkey, on Friday, November 20 through Sunday, November 22:

The COP31 climate summit has failed to agree on a final joint declaration.

During a plenary session, the summit's Turkish president acknowledged there would be no climate deal.

"I understand that there is no agreement on this text. For that reason, these decisions have not been adopted," Murat Kurum, who is also Turkey's environment minister, told a closing plenary in the Mediterranean city of Antalya.

The rift centered on whether to include the phaseout of fossil fuels in the final declaration. Scientists say the burning of fossil fuels such as coal, oil and gas is the main cause of global warming.

European nations, smaller island states and climate activists have criticized oil-producing countries for the omission.

Several nations continued to raise serious objections to the draft.

Antwi-Boasiako Amoah, chair of the African Group of Negotiators, took a shot at the presidency for how it has handled the negotiations.

"With all due respect Mr. President, you have not shown respect to a group of 54 countries (from Africa) and my country (Ghana) for that matter," Amoah said.

Panama's representative said the Central American nation was amongst those who suffer most from the consequences and issue of climate justice.

Panamanian Environment Minister Juan Carlos Navarro said the talks were being held hostage by a "petroleum mafia" and need to be reformed.

He said science must be the basis for the cover decision, adding that those who contributed to this crisis must shoulder the responsibility to deal with it.

"We are running out of time," Navarro told attendees. "Panama cannot support this decision. The planet cannot continue to wait."

Ireland's Climate Minister Darragh O'Brien has said the European Union is "profoundly disappointed" with the current draft deal on the table at COP31 because it "fails to meet the moment" and that the bloc will not agree to it.

"We will not endorse a cover decision that does not respond to the science, that disregards the implementation gap, and risks deepening the chasm between global commitments and the action required to correct the overshoot and bring 1.5°C within reach," O'Brien said.

"We will not endorse a cover decision that does not even mention fossil fuels, the driver of climate change. And we will not accept negotiating backward in time. And we will not accept a COP that greenwashes fossil fuels," he added.

Former Greenpeace executive Jennifer Morgan said it would be better to end the COP31 summit with no deal than a watered-down declaration.

Her remarks come amid deep divisions over the omission of fossil fuels from the draft declaration.

Morgan rejected the notion that it would be better to accept the current draft than have no resolution at all, as the Turkish COP president has suggested.

"I think this is the day for the first time in my 31 COPs that I thought it was better not to have a decision that sets us back, that goes against what small islands and vulnerable people are saying," Morgan added.

She praised Germany for standing up to pressures to accept the current deal.

"Of course it would have been better to have a strong outcome here with everyone moving forward, but I agree with the German minister who just said if the text stays the way that it is, then it should not be accepted," Morgan said.

The US climate activist blamed oil-producing nations for the impasse.

"Saudi Arabia's fingerprints are all over" the current draft proposal, she said.

The omission has created a deadlock at COP31, which was due to conclude on Friday but has been extended as delegates seek consensus.

Morgan also blamed this year's Turkish presidency for failing to bring everybody together and instead letting countries that rely on fossil fuels dictate the terms of the agreement.

"Last year we had Brazil with a very strong presidency. They worked all year to bring countries together and they managed. It was not easy last year," Morgan said.

"This year we had presidencies that, in my view, were not well prepared. They did not bring countries together  (...) it's been shuttle diplomacy with no text for four days. That's not the way you get an agreement here," she added.

German Environment Minister Carsten Schneider has said the latest version of the draft agreement is "not acceptable."

Schneider said that the draft decision "not only fails to provide an adequate response but actually takes a step backward. A step backward from previous decisions on climate protection is the last thing Germany can support."

Schneider said that delegates had been working hard for days to reach a unanimous decision on "the world's greatest challenge: human-induced climate change," noting that this summer had "shown that the nature of this warming not only costs lives — as in Nepal — but also changes our world for the worse."

"It is up to us, as humanity, to do something about it. That is why a conference dedicated to climate protection — whose sole purpose is to protect the climate — must send a clear signal that we are taking action against this threat."

Schneider said he still believed most nations are willing to work to enact climate protection, but singled out those who still rely on fossil fuels to power their economies.

"The overwhelming majority of nations are committed to climate protection. Some, however, are still making huge profits from oil and gas and are unwilling to scale back their production or to pioneer a clear roadmap for reducing greenhouse gas emissions in this area," he added.

France has called a new draft deal proposed at COP31 a "backward" step.

French Ecological Transition Minister Monique Barbut criticized the deal for failing to mention fossil fuels, casting fresh uncertainty over whether nations will be able to reach an agreement.

"Time is ticking and we are not there yet," French Ecological Transition Minister Monique Barbut said in a statement.

COP31 President Murat Kurum has expressed his frustration over the deadlock at the summit.

The latest version of the draft deal again fails to mention fossil fuels, the main cause of global warming.

Over 40 hours after its scheduled conclusion and with no compromise in sight, the summit risks losing the numbers required to make any decision as delegates leave for home.

"If it is not adopted, I believe those who push the COP process into a deadlock will need to reflect on their position," Kurum said.

"It is our wish that this resolution be passed. We shall shortly be expressing our determination to ensure that this resolution is passed at the general meeting," he said.

"It is up to the parties involved. If there is sincerity here, and if there is a genuine desire to get things done, I believe that this can be achieved in this chamber. We shall see about that in a moment," Kurum added.

The third draft makes no mention of the words  "fossil," "oil," "coal," or "phase out."

However, as in the second draft, the latest version retains a goal to boost electricity's share of global energy use to 35% by 2035, as well as efforts to expand the use of "clean energy."

The draft text also does not stipulate that the "35-by-35" electrification target should be met using renewable energy sources.

It also urges countries to "accelerate" carbon capture and storage — a controversial technology backed by the fossil fuel industry.

It's understandable to get caught up in negative daily news headlines. On any given day, it might seem like the stock market is going to crash, that the world is ending. But remember: Stock markets are about the future, not today.

Stock investors are constantly evaluating what's likely to happen next -- which companies are poised to keep growing, which industries are about to benefit from positive economic changes, and which countries around the world are due for a stock market surge. It happens every December. You bring home a full, bright red poinsettia, set it on the table, and a week or two later the colorful bracts start to curl and fall. By Christmas morning, the plant looks tired and half bare.

Some of that can be blamed on poinsettia care after you get home, like a cold windowsill or a pot left sitting in water. Much of it starts at the store, though. The plant was already on its way out before it ever went in the cart.

There's an easy way to tell a fresh one from a tired one, and hardly anybody looks. Forget the red for a second. Down in the center of each cluster of colored leaves are some tiny yellow-green buds, and those say a lot more about how long the color will hold up. It's kind of a strange thing to judge a plant by, but trust the process.

Those colorful "petals" on a poinsettia aren't petals at all. They're bracts, and they color up in fall as the nights get longer. Right in the middle sit the real flowers, small knobby clusters called cyathia. Each one is a little cup holding a single female flower and several male flowers, often with a yellow gland on its rim.

Cyathia go through the same cycle on every plant. They start out tight and green or red-tipped, open to shed yellow pollen, and then drop off. After the cyathia fall, the colored bracts usually follow before long.

Some of the newer poinsettia varieties hang on to their cyathia longer. So it isn't a sure thing, but it's still one of the better clues you get in the store. Any color works for this check, whether you're eyeing red or the pink poinsettias at Walmart (which come in a four-pack for maximum festive joy).

Bend down and look closely at the center of a few bracts. Plants on the same table can be at different stages, so check more than one. Ideally, the cyathia are mostly closed, green or tipped with red, and fresh-looking. Some yellow showing is fine on its own. That's often just the gland on the side of the cup – it doesn't mean the flower has opened.

Now compare that to a plant where the buds have split open and yellow pollen is dusting the bracts below them. Classic red poinsettias come in a four-pack from Costa Farms, and you can order them on Amazon – and the same quick look at the center works on a plant that shows up at the door.

The green leaves under the bracts deserve a look too. Check for yellow leaves low on the stems, or bare spots where leaves have already dropped. Cold, poor light, and watering trouble can all cause that. Pass on a plant that droops or looks wilted, along with any that have torn, bruised, or creased bracts. Bluish or whitish edges on the bracts can be a sign of cold injury.

Flip a few leaves over as well. Whiteflies, such as the silverleaf whitefly (Bemisia tabaci), are common on poinsettias, and a puff of tiny white insects lifting off when you brush the plant is a bad sign.

Lift the pot if you can. Soggy soil in a heavy pot may mean it's been sitting in water inside its foil, and roots kept that wet can rot. Pay attention to where the plants are displayed, too. Poinsettias crowded by the automatic doors can catch a blast of cold air every time the doors open, and plants left packed in plastic sleeves for days can droop and drop their flowers early – ethylene gas builds up inside the sleeve.

When you get home, use a soil moisture meter, like the Xlux moisture meter available on Amazon, to take the guesswork out of watering.

The journey home is where poinsettias tend to get hurt. When it's below 50°F (10°C) outside, sleeve or wrap the plant before it leaves the store, even for the short walk to the car. Just a few minutes of cold air can damage the bracts and leaves, and the damage may not show right away. Save the garden center for your last stop so the plant isn't stuck in a cold car while you're in other stores, and warm the car up first. Once you're inside, get the sleeve off pretty quickly.

Pick a bright room for your poinsettia. Keep the plant near a window but out of hard, direct sun, and well clear of heat vents, the fireplace, and any door that lets in a draft.

Normal house temperatures work fine, roughly 65 to 70°F (18 to 21°C) during the day, with nights around 60°F (16°C) or a little warmer. Startups are betting on America’s loneliness epidemic, and Americans are buying in. Apps such as 222, Real Roots, and Timeleft are turning friend-matching events into a growing consumer market, and some users pay hundreds of dollars to make new friends offline.

The business is working, at least for now: each of the three companies brings in millions of dollars in revenue a year, according to the Times. But the model carries an unusual tension. The better it works, the less its customers may need it.

These companies are tapping into demand for in-person connection at a time when many people struggle to build relationships in a digital age. Rather than meeting people the old-fashioned way, users are matched with strangers based on shared interests and personality, then sent to curated events.

222 began as a college research project. Its co-founders later started hosting dinners in their backyard, and the project became a full-time business in 2023, according to the Times.

The company has since raised $13.7 million in venture capital, according to Business Insider, including $10.1 million in 2025. It uses machine-learning models trained on users’ feedback after each meetup to decide who should meet whom.

“I think our generation craves the in-real-life environment of meeting each other in a more intimate setting, without digital preconceptions,” Arman Roshannai, one of 222’s co-founders, told the Times.

222 charges $22 a month to match users and curate events, before the cost of the activity itself, such as a yoga class or a meal. Interested in dinner and collage-making? That will be $38, not including food and drinks.

The company now operates in 21 cities, employs 27 people and hosts at least one experience a day. Its co-founders told the Times that revenue tripled last year and is on track to quadruple this year, into the high seven figures.

Competitors are scaling, too. Real Roots, which coordinates activities for groups of women, is in 120 cities, while Timeleft, which matches strangers for dinners, is in more than 200. Newer entrants keep arriving, including the professional-networking app Clockout, whose founder raised $3.5 million to help Gen Z feel less alone.

All of them are competing for users who want a genuine relationship beyond social media. Ironically, building these friendships starts with an app.

About 21% of U.S. adults reported feeling lonely in a May 2024 survey by Making Caring Common, a project of the Harvard Graduate School of Education. Among those lonely adults, 81% reported anxiety or depression.

Sixty-five percent of lonely respondents said they felt fundamentally disconnected from others or the world. And when all respondents were asked what contributes to loneliness in America, technology topped the list, cited by 73%.

Others pointed to families not spending enough time together, people working too much and mental health challenges that strain relationships.

Founders in the space argue that this gap is a market in its own right. As the co-founder of group-travel startup WeRoad argued in a Fortune commentary, people who haven’t found meaningful connection online are now willing to pay for it in person.

Turning friendship into a business doesn’t guarantee meaningful connections. Some users have complained on social media that they didn’t click with their group or that the experiences felt awkward and forced, the Times reported.

There is also a larger question about the sustainability of the business model. If the product works and customers develop genuine friendships, they may no longer need the service.

That creates an unusual challenge for companies built around solving loneliness. Their success hinges on helping people establish relationships, and every relationship that sticks could mean one fewer customer coming back.

The companies appear to be designing around that problem. If a Real Roots group gets along, its members can pay the company to keep arranging meetups for the same group in the weeks that follow. Business Insider reported that 222 is building tools that help users plan their next meetup together and says its users are split roughly evenly between those looking for friends and those open to romance.

For investors, the question isn’t simply whether Americans will pay to make friends. It is whether friendship can be a durable business without turning human connection into a subscription.

From Diego Forlan’s unforgettable double to Juan Mata’s spectacular volley, Manchester United have enjoyed some memorable victories at Anfield over the years.

With Mauricio Pochettino preparing for his first match in charge against Liverpool on Sunday, we look back at five occasions when United emerged victorious from one of English football’s fiercest fixtures.

United travelled to Anfield on 19 April 1997 knowing Liverpool could overtake them at the top of the Premier League with a victory. Instead, centre-back Gary Pallister scored twice with headers from David Beckham deliveries, either side of John Barnes’s equaliser.

Andy Cole added a third after the interval, heading home from Gary Neville’s cross following an error by Liverpool goalkeeper David James. The victory helped Sir Alex Ferguson’s side take a decisive step towards retaining the Premier League title, which they secured the following month.

Fresh from winning the Treble, United returned to Anfield for a remarkable match in September 1999. Jamie Carragher headed into his own net inside three minutes before Andy Cole doubled United’s advantage.

Sami Hyypia pulled a goal back for Liverpool, but Carragher’s second own goal restored the visitors’ two-goal lead. Patrik Berger reduced the deficit again, and United faced a difficult finish after Cole was sent off for a second bookable offence. Despite playing the closing stages with 10 men, Ferguson’s side held on for a 3-2 victory.

Diego Forlan had endured a frustrating start to his United career before producing a performance that supporters would remember for years. The Uruguayan opened the scoring after Liverpool goalkeeper Jerzy Dudek failed to gather the ball, allowing him to finish from close range.

Forlan added a second just minutes later, beating Dudek at his near post to put United in control. Sami Hyypia pulled one back for Liverpool, but Ferguson’s side held on for a victory that proved valuable in their successful pursuit of the 2002-03 Premier League title. Forlan’s double also inspired a terrace song that remains familiar to United supporters.

Few United victories at Anfield have been as dramatic as the one secured by John O’Shea in March 2007. Liverpool had enjoyed the better opportunities, and United’s task became harder when Paul Scholes was sent off late in the second half.

With a goalless draw appearing increasingly likely, Cristiano Ronaldo’s free-kick was parried by goalkeeper Pepe Reina. Substitute O’Shea reacted quickest, firing the rebound high into the net in stoppage time. The unexpected winner gave United three valuable points on their way to reclaiming the Premier League title.

Juan Mata produced one of the most memorable individual performances in this fixture during Louis van Gaal’s first season as United manager. The Spaniard opened the scoring after 14 minutes, finishing a move involving Ander Herrera, before doubling United’s lead with a spectacular scissor-kick volley after half-time.

Liverpool captain Steven Gerrard had been sent off just 38 seconds after coming on as a substitute at the interval. Daniel Sturridge reduced the deficit, while Wayne Rooney missed a late penalty, but United held on for a 2-1 victory. Mata’s second goal remains one of the outstanding moments of United’s Premier League visits to Anfield.

Those five victories underline the different ways United have succeeded at Anfield, from outstanding individual performances to late winners and determined defensive displays. Pochettino now has an opportunity to begin his managerial reign with a memorable result against United’s biggest rivals.

Scientists Discover That a Simple Change in Walking Habits Could Improve Blood Sugar Control

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Scientists have discovered that a simple change in how daily exercise is distributed could improve blood sugar control, particularly in people at risk of type 2 diabetes.

How you distribute exercise throughout the day may influence how well your body regulates blood sugar. A randomized trial published in Obesity found that frequent, short walks improved several measures of glucose regulation more than completing the same amount of walking in one continuous session.

The differences emerged after six weeks, despite both groups performing similar amounts of physical activity and burning comparable amounts of energy.

Researchers recruited 30 inactive adults with overweight or obesity who reported sitting for more than six hours daily. Participants were assigned to complete 45 minutes of brisk walking five days per week for six weeks, either in one continuous session or in approximately five-minute breaks spread throughout the day.

Continuous glucose monitoring showed that the short-walk group increased its time within the target blood sugar range from 82% to 94%. In contrast, the continuous-walking group experienced a decline from 89% to 80%.

Participants taking frequent walking breaks also had more stable glucose levels, largely because they experienced fewer unusually low readings rather than lower average or peak blood sugar levels.

Laboratory tests showed that participants who took frequent walking breaks experienced more favorable changes in glucose tolerance, indicating differences in how their bodies cleared glucose from the bloodstream. However, neither approach produced a detectable improvement in insulin sensitivity.

Researchers suggest that repeated muscle activity may influence glucose uptake and utilization, although the exact mechanisms remain uncertain.

“Our findings suggest that how we distribute physical activity throughout the day may be just as important as how much we do,” said corresponding author Audrey Bergouignan, PhD, Director of Research at the French National Centre for Scientific Research (CNRS) and Associate Professor at the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus. “Rather than relying solely on a single exercise session, regularly interrupting prolonged sitting with short walks may offer additional benefits for blood glucose regulation, especially in people at risk of developing type 2 diabetes.”

The findings suggest that interrupting prolonged sitting may offer glucose regulation benefits beyond those associated with total exercise duration.

However, the trial was small and lasted only six weeks. It also lacked a non-exercise control group, and participants’ diets were not controlled during glucose monitoring. Further research is needed to determine whether these improvements translate into long-term health benefits, particularly for people with prediabetes or type 2 diabetes.
