Liverpool remain unsure over Virgil van Dijk contract decision

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Liverpool are facing a significant call on the future of Virgil van Dijk, with fresh reporting from Sport Italia indicating that the club remain hesitant over the defender's preferred contract terms.

Van Dijk, now 35, remains one of the defining figures of this Liverpool side. Even through major change, including the end of the Arne Slot era and the arrival of Andoni Iraola, his place in the team has carried real weight. His level, leadership and authority have kept him central to Liverpool's defensive structure, while younger players around him continue to develop.

The issue is not whether Van Dijk still contributes. By all accounts, he does. The issue is what Liverpool are prepared to commit to next, and on what financial and sporting terms.

The update from Italy points to a familiar modern stand-off between club caution and player expectation. Sport Italia state, "Gerry Cardinale is following the Reds captain's situation and dreams of a big signing this summer. The sticking point is a renewal: the Dutchman is asking for a two-year contract, while Liverpool still seem unconvinced."

That immediately places AC Milan into the conversation, albeit at a watching distance for now. The same report adds, "Virgil van Dijk's future could become one of the most interesting situations of the upcoming summer transfer window. AC Milan is in the background, with Gerry Cardinale eyeing the possibility of bringing one of the most authoritative defenders in the international scene to San Siro."

There is still an important caveat. Liverpool hold the contract, and the club's decision remains the key factor. As Sport Italia put it, "But before imagining the Dutch centre-back in a Rossoneri shirt, Liverpool must be taken into account."

That is where the matter sharpens. "This is precisely where the crux of the matter lies: Van Dijk wants a two-year extension, while Liverpool do not appear willing, at least for now, to fully grant his request."

This feels less about emotion and more about policy. Van Dijk is Liverpool's highest-paid player, and by the start of next season he will be 36. Clubs at the elite end have to judge not only present performance, but the durability of that performance over the life of a deal.

Even so, there is a strong football case for meeting him somewhere close to his request. Liverpool are still in a period of squad adjustment. Defensive planning matters, and certainty matters more. Van Dijk's ongoing importance, on the pitch and inside the dressing room, means a short-term approach carries risk if there is no fully trusted successor ready to step in immediately.

Jeremy Jacquet's progress has been noted, while other younger options still need time and evidence at the top level. In those circumstances, allowing this situation to drift could create a problem that feels avoidable today and expensive tomorrow.

AC Milan's presence in the background may prove useful leverage, even if the outcome still points towards Anfield. A player of Van Dijk's standing will always attract attention when contract uncertainty appears. That is particularly true when the destination is a club with stature, ambition and a clear desire to add elite experience.

Liverpool, then, have a choice to make. They can hold firm on age-based caution and test how far the situation goes, or they can recognise that a proven, still-performing captain may justify an exception. The coming months should reveal whether pragmatism or principle wins out.

This is exactly the sort of story that raises nerves, because it feels painfully familiar. Liverpool have already seen major figures move on, and there has to be real care over how many pillars of the side are allowed to drift towards uncertainty at the same time.

Van Dijk still looks like someone this team depends on. Not in a sentimental way, not because of what he was, but because of what he still gives now. He organises, he settles games, he protects those around him and he sets standards. That sort of presence is hard to replace even when recruitment is strong.

If the concern is wages, then fine, structure the deal sensibly. If the concern is age, build in protections. But a flat refusal on two years would feel short-sighted unless Liverpool are absolutely certain the next leader of the defence is already in the building.

That certainty does not seem obvious. And if it is not obvious internally, then this should not be allowed to become a saga that opens the door for AC Milan or anyone else. A calm compromise still feels possible, and it would be far better than gambling on a clean transition that may not be ready to happen yet.

The 8 Best Miniseries of the Streaming Era, Ranked

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

We are living in the streaming era of television. Though Netflix launched streaming video-on-demand as far back as 2007, they didn't produce their first original series, Lilyhammer, until 2012; and it wouldn't be until 2013, with the release of shows like House of Cards and Organge Is the New Black, that streaming would truly gain cultural hegemony over the entire televisual medium. Gone was the era of network television, and in came the era of streaming.

Miniseries in particular find their roots in the British serials of the 1950s, but it was when American television adapted the format in the 1970s that it really became a cultural phenomenon. From classics like Roots to 21st-century masterpieces like Band of Brothers, the era of network TV delivered many a great miniseries. The streaming era of television has definitely not fallen behind, though, giving us in the likes of Adolescence and xxx some of the greatest miniseries of all time. Only streaming originals can qualify for this list.

After actor Stephen Graham read a news article about a young boy stabbing a girl to death, he proposed telling a story inspired by this wave of violent attacks by young boys against girls in Great Britain through the miniseries format. He and Jack Thorne then co-created Adolescence, a scathing critique of incel culture, male rage, toxic masculinity, and the "manosphere." It's an awfully timely yet sensitive topic, but the series treats it with precisely the care it deserved.

The result is one of the best miniseries of the last five years, composed of four episodes all shot in a single take, which lends the series an air of urgency and raw authenticity that elevates both its emotional and intellectual impacts tremendously. Bolstered by its exceptional cast (including Graham himself and a revelatory Emmy-winning turn by young Owen Cooper) and its excellent writing, it's one of the most important crime drama series of the 2020s.

Beth Macy's non-fiction masterpiece Dopesick: Dealers, Doctors, and the Drug Company that Addicted America deals with the origin and evolution of the opioid epidemic that began in the United States around 1996 with the release of OxyContin. In 2021, Hulu adapted that story in Dopesick, which just so happens to be one of the most perfect miniseries of the last 25 years. It's every bit as ambitious, expansive, and insightful as the book it's based on.

It's such a devastating investigative drama that it's most definitely not an easy watch, but boy, is it an important one. It's a 360-degree systemic portrait of absolutely every link in the chain of the opioid crisis, from the Purdue Pharma boardroom to the victims of the crisis. Elevated by its incredible cast (including the likes of Michael Keaton and Peter Sarsgaard) and its non-linear structure, it's about as urgent as streaming-era miniseries get.

Far more than just one of the best movies of the last 100 years, Francis Ford Coppola's The Godfather is one of those artistic masterpieces so great and so important that they straight-up transcend their medium. What really makes it such a special movie, however, is that the story behind it is every bit as interesting and compelling as the film itself; and that story is the subject of Paramount+'s The Offer.

Making-of dramas can either be so full of propulsive energy that they're irresistibly entertaining, or so cold in their approach that they feel like Wikipedia pages come to life. The Offer definitely belongs to the former group. The larger-than-life performances, far from just being impressions, are truly phenomenal; the writing turns Hollywood politics into a genuinely momentous thriller; and the show's approach to the story should be enough to make any cinephile melt in enjoyment.

Western television hadn't really been a particularly prominent part of the mainstream for several years now, but Taylor Sheridan apparently never got the memo, because with his Yellowstone universe, not only did he become one of the kings of streaming-era television, he also made Western shows mainstream again. Case in point: the Paramount+ prequel 1883, one of those modern Western shows that are absolute masterpieces.

Visceral, poetic, and emotionally devastating, it's not only one of the best television prequels of the streaming era, but also one of the most unflinchingly realistic Westerns that the small screen has seen in years. Leaving behind the soap-opera tone of the franchise's flagship show and stripping the Western genre of its nostalgia and problematic myth-making, it's a series fully dedicated to showing just how unforgiving the Oregon Trail really was. The characters are engrossing, the performances are top-notch (Sam Elliott's in particular), and the visuals are some of the best of the whole franchise.

There are plenty of great Netflix shows with under 30 episodes—many of them miniseries—, and one of the best is undoubtedly The Queen's Gambit. Based on the eponymous 1983 novel by Walter Tevis, it rapidly became one of the biggest Netflix sensations of the COVID-19 pandemic lockdowns, launching Anya Taylor-Joy's mainstream popularity into the stratosphere.

Pivot[s] around many of the usual clichés that tend to make sports drama miniseries trite.

One of the most kinetic, visceral, and utterly addictive character studies that the small screen has seen in years, Queen's Gambit proved that it was perfectly possible to tell an absolutely fascinating and exhilarating story built around chess. Pivoting around many of the usual clichés that tend to make sports drama miniseries trite, Queen's Gambit is elevated by its stunning visuals, its tight pacing, and its constant intelligent subversions of the "tortured woman genius" trope.

Four Netflix characters, four completely different ways of showing up for somebody. This is the one who fits how you actually do friendship, and what having them in your life would be like.

You do not do friendship at half volume, and neither does he. What you want is somebody who says yes to the plan before you have finished describing the plan.

You are the friend who makes things happen, and so is she. Together you would be mildly exhausting to everyone around you and completely delighted with yourselves.

You have no patience for pleasant lies, and she has no ability to tell them. What looks like coldness from the outside is the most reliable friendship on this list.

You look after whoever is having the worst day. So does he. It is the least strategic way to move through the world and the best possible quality in a friend.

History has seen many creatives put on the crown of Television's King of Horror, and there's a strong argument to be made that the man currently wearing that crown is Mike Flanagan. Though he's also a filmmaker, Flanagan is perhaps best known for his work on the small screen, with the Netflix miniseries The Haunting of Hill House often being credited as the project responsible for turning him into a household name in the field of mainstream horror.

One of the scariest TV shows of all time, Haunting of Hill House is a landmark in the history of streaming-era horror television. The 2010s saw the rise of "elevated horror" on the big screen, referring to a form of horror filmmaking that proved the genre could be full of artistic merit, and this was one of the first true "elevated horror" projects that the small screen ever saw. Rooted in themes of grief and family dynamics, Haunting of Hill House is a masterclass in atmosphere and non-linear storytelling.

It's an absolute tragedy that it was canceled after only one season, but Scavengers Reign was originally pitched as a self-contained miniseries anyway, so the series fortunately managed to come to a satisfying conclusion. Though fans have been clamoring for a follow-up for years (and creators Joe Bennett and Charles Huettner have confirmed that there are definitely ideas in the tank), regardless of whether it ever does actually come, this will be one of the best miniseries of the 2020s anyway.

There are plenty of sci-fi miniseries that belong on everyone's bucket list, and this is arguably one of them. Proof that animated science fiction deserves far more respect than many people are willing to give it, Scavengers Reign is a cartoon unlike any other, with some of the most layered world-building and most deliciously complex character work that any sci-fi miniseries has seen in years. HBO Max really dropped the ball with this one.

Back in 1989, five Black and Latino teenagers were wrongfully convicted of the rape and beating of Trisha Meili in New York City, a case that came to be known as the Central Park jogger case. They were exonerated in 2002 after DNA evidence matched another man's confession, but the damage was done, and the foundations for a gripping Netflix miniseries created and directed by Ava DuVernay were laid down. One of the most hopeless Netflix shows to date, When They See Us is the best that streaming-era miniseries have had to offer thus far.

Part psychologically intimate character study; part razor-sharp critique of systemic injustice and tabloid sensationalism; part unflinching depiction of coercion and systemic failure within the American justice system, When They See Us is a must-see for anyone who enjoys watching flawless television. Indeed, it's one of the most flawless shows of the last decade, complete with some exceptional tour-de-force performances, deeply layered writing, and top-notch production values.

Mata’s second goal remains one of the outstanding moments of United’s Premier League visits to Anfield.

Instead, the former Paris Saint-Germain midfielder hit the ground, with attendants rushing to help him after his collapse.

‘I Started Strength Training at 74. At 78, I Can Deadlift 132 Lbs—Here’s How.’

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

“While I love lifting weights and hitting new PRs, the greatest reward is how much easier everyday life has become.”

If there’s one thing I’ve learned as I’ve gotten older, it’s that being active and being strong aren’t necessarily the same thing. For most of my life, I’d been recreationally active, and had loved kayaking, swimming, and taking my dog on long walks. I’d never done any kind of structured strength training, until right before my 75th birthday. One day, I was sitting on a curb outside my house, and when it was time to stand up, I couldn’t. I felt embarrassed, and the idea of falling and not being able to get up or being in a situation where I couldn’t take care of myself was scary.

I wanted everyday activities, like climbing stairs, carrying groceries, and getting up off the floor, to feel easier. I also wanted to trust my body more, and feel confident in my ability to handle whatever life threw my way. So, I joined Aspire Athletic Club, a gym near my home in Schenectady, New York, and began working out once a week with the owner, Chris Wertman. In the beginning, even simple movements were challenging, so I started with basic bodyweight exercises like the box squat, step-up, and bird dog. The focus was on learning proper form and getting comfortable with the movements.

After a few weeks, I gradually incorporated 5- to 8-lb dumbbells into the movements, and a month or two later, I noticed a real difference in my strength: I could do more repetitions, lift even heavier weights, and move with greater confidence. It was so encouraging to see the hard work pay off, and it motivated me to keep going. About a year into consistent training, I was feeling stronger and ready to take on more. I began training full-body twice a week and incorporated more challenging exercises like squat variations, the walking lunge, weighted glute bridge, and chest fly to build overall strength.

I’d never deadlifted before, so seeing someone my age lift with that kind of strength was incredibly inspiring. That decision marked the beginning of a new chapter in my strength training journey—I told my new trainer of about six months, Jack Tyree, that I wanted to lift heavier weights and try deadlifting. I even began training for my gym’s upcoming annual deadlift exhibition, which was in eight months.

Since I’d just been training with dumbbells, I tried working with a kettlebell while I got comfortable with the movement and proper technique. As I got stronger and more confident, I graduated to the barbell and continued adding more weight each week. Then, this past May, I deadlifted 132 lbs for a single rep at the exhibition, setting a personal record. I never imagined I’d be able to do something like that, but I was so proud of how strong I’d become—and I was excited to see how much further I could push myself.

Six months after the exhibition, I now strength train three days a week: one day focused on upper body, one on lower body, and one dedicated to mobility and balance. Jack still programs all my workouts, and every session includes six to eight exercises, with 3 or 4 sets of 6 to 8 reps each. I use a mix of free weights and weight machines, and some of my go-to moves include the farmer’s carry, curtsy lunge, lat pulldown, scapular pushup, squat, and bench press. Deadlifts are still my favorite; I can now do three reps at 132 lbs, which I’m really proud of.

Outside the gym, I love getting outside and staying active, so I walk a few miles with my dog every day for my cardiovascular health. In addition to mobility and balance work at the gym, I also try to stretch at home whenever I can to ease muscle soreness and improve my range of motion, especially in my shoulders and hips.

I know nutrition is an important part of recovery too. Although I don’t follow a strict nutrition plan or count macros, I try to eat protein at every meal to support muscle growth. Usually, it’s chicken, red meat, or a scoop of protein powder mixed into my morning oatmeal. I also prioritize whole foods and aim to incorporate a variety of fruits and vegetables throughout the day.

When I first started strength training, I didn’t know where to begin. So, having someone guide me through the movements, teach me proper form, and help me build strength safely made all the difference. My trainers have helped me recognize what I’m capable of, push beyond what I thought was possible, and progress at a pace that feels right. They also create workouts that challenge me while keeping my goals in mind. Having their support and encouragement has given me the confidence to try things I never imagined I could do. Whether it’s deadlifting heavy weights, bench pressing a barbell, or taking less rest between working sets, my trainers have taught me not to put a ceiling on what I think I can accomplish.

Before I strength trained, I felt weak and unsteady, but I didn’t give up and accept that I’d feel like that for the rest of my life. In some ways, I completely started over with exercise at 74 because I tried something brand new—and that’s affected my life outside of the gym.

Now, at 78, I feel stronger than ever; I can get up off the floor with ease, carry my own groceries into the house, shovel snow, garden, and play sports with my grandchildren. These are things I want to keep doing independently, and having the strength to do them confidently means the world to me. While I love lifting weights and hitting new PRs, the greatest reward is how much easier everyday life has become.

Learning to lift in my mid-70s wasn’t easy, and building that strength didn’t happen overnight, but I kept showing up, putting in the work, and gradually accomplishing what I once never thought was possible. Now, as I approach 80, I’m still getting stronger, and I want other women to know they’re never too old to get started, and they may be capable of far more than they realize. If I can go from struggling to get up from a curb to deadlifting 132 pounds, who knows what else is possible?

Sony Bravia 9 II Review (2026): Adjustable and Bright

The current flagship television from Sony, the Bravia 9 II, is an RGB TV model (Sony calls it “True RGB”) that brings to life even the darkest scenes in Game of Thrones. Much brighter than the midrange Bravia 7 II, the 9 II's best qualities are related to its black levels, picture quality, and excellent viewing angles that allow me to see Jon Snow and the GoT dragons perfectly, even when I'm sitting off to the side.

This premium Sony television carries a premium price of $3,600 for the 65-inch model, but that’s still $1,400 less than the LG Micro RGB Evo, which retails for $5,000. I ended up preferring the overall picture quality of that LG model and some other OLED models, but—after my usual rigorous testing—I felt the Bravia 9 II came close to matching the high caliber of those other options. In fact, it’s just a notch or two lower than the absolute best television you might find.

The Sony Bravia 9 II is all black, sits on two feet, and includes four HDMI ports (only two are HDMI 2.1, though, which allows for higher-bandwidth streaming and sound at 48 Gbps). Setup was a breeze; Google TV was up and running in less than five minutes, which is an impressive feat, given that both Samsung and LG televisions typically add a few extra steps to the install process, such as authenticating your login (with Samsung) or relying on an app (with the LG ThinQ).

The premium Sony Bravia 9 II also offers exceptional sound. It includes two upfiring tweeters for more realistic Dolby Atmos than the Bravia 7 II, which lacks these extra channels. When I watched House of the Dragon, the room-filling audio made me feel like I was flying a dragon or piloting a ship.

Viewing angles on the Bravia 9 II are about the best I’ve seen on any television. I tested this element by camping out in a chair and playing video games off to the side, and there wasn’t a hint of screen glare. These perks are, again, a sign of its premium quality, verified through my usual benchmarks. Skin tones looked varied and vivid; one person had a little more red in her face than what I noticed on the LG Micro RGB Evo or the Samsung Micro RGB R95H.

In a demo-reel benchmark test I use for all televisions, I was shocked by the Bravia 9 II's stellar performance. I’ve watched the same short segment with a fence in front of a winter landscape countless times to test various models, but here, the green grass in the background looked supremely vivid and clear. It’s because the Bravia 9 II has such exceptional screen brightness, color accuracy, and absolutely no glare.

The Sony remote is just OK. I wish the Home button were centered; since it's not, I kept clicking the mic for the Google Gemini voicebot (which is in the center) by mistake. I prefer the bare-bones Samsung remote or the one included with an Apple TV 4K streaming device. Just give me a Netflix button and volume controls, and I’m good. Still, the layout of this remote mostly makes sense, and I like that it’s backlit.

The Bravia 9 II didn’t pass all of my tests for performance while I watched shows and movies, but it did allow me to tweak more settings than I’m used to. For example, the TV automatically calibrated colors and tones for Spider-Man: Brand New Day, but it still looked too dark. I was able to manually adjust the black level and used Vivid mode to make Spidey’s suit look extra red.

However, compared to the picture quality of an OLED like the Samsung S95H, the Bravia 9 II's deep blacks looked less realistic. That's because on OLED TVs, the LEDs can be turned off entirely. The Bravia 9 II performed OK for Toy Story 5, but in darker scenes late in the film, I noticed the Samsung had inky blacks that reminded me of a high-end projector.

My main takeaway is that it's nice that you can tweak settings on the Bravia 9 II to great effect, thanks to an RGB panel that’s super-bright. The movies Awake and The Creator tested my patience a little in that regard. I adjusted the black level to precisely 61 (out of 100) and could then see faces, a predawn mountain scene, and a beach landing with better clarity. Knowing you can achieve those results is nice; actually doing it does take time.

While playing Resident Evil: Requiem to test brightness, picture quality, and latency, I confirmed that it's possible to adjust the black level to liven up dark scenes. I also noticed that the latency, at a 120-Hz refresh rate when I used one of the HDMI 2.1 ports, was almost nonexistent. That helped with every slight twitch when a seven-headed monster from the dark underworld of hell started attacking me.

Similar games, like Senua's Saga: Hellblade II, looked too gray and washed out on the midrange Bravia 7 II and some LED models I’ve tested. I also played End of Abyss, a game that takes place almost entirely in dark hallways, and the Bravia 9 II rendered those scenes with inky blacks—granted, not as black as an OLED would, but better than many LED TVs.

Bravia 9 II lived up to its flagship-quality promise for sportscasts, making my beloved Minnesota Vikings' purple jerseys look vivid and clear and the grass bright green—all without displaying any glare at all. News programs were bright, with strong color accuracy, but not oversaturated—a good compromise. In fact, it was so clear, I felt as if I could reach out to clip an MS Now analyst's nose hairs.

The Bravia 9 II is near the top of the leaderboard of the best televisions to buy right now. You'll just want to consider the technology it uses. RGB models use red, green, and blue LEDs to create realistic colors that, in theory, improve color accuracy and vividness. Unlike an OLED TV, an RGB TV still shines those LED colors through an LCD panel. So you have to pick your poison. The Bravia 9 II looked super bright and vivid, especially during NFL games. It’s just a hair less colorful and vivid compared to the LG Micro RGB Evo.

Knowing you can tweak the settings for the results you want and that anyone in your household will be able to see America’s Got Talent from any angle in the room is a major plus. But is the Sony Bravia 9 II worth $3,600 when so many TVs cost around $2,000? I would say it depends on what you watch. I want to see dragons at night and monsters in dark hallways, but for some animated shows, sports, and network TV, you might just want your device to work well, right out of the box, without worrying about cinematic quality. Yes, a flagship lets you do a lot more, but at a much steeper price.

Injuries to several stars and key contributors have shaped the league conversation through the opening month. Some teams have responded well to the adversity. 6 Funniest Far Side Comics Starring the Devil

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Gary Larson’s loved sending people to Hell

In the 1980s and 1990s, nothing in the funny pages was quite as dark as The Far Side. Lucy van Pelt pulling the football away from Charlie Brown as he was about to kick it is about as dark as things got in Peanuts. And in Garfield, the lazy cat’s darkest running gag also revolved around a kick — when he’d kick Odie the dog off a table.

Meanwhile, The Far Side featured jokes about hangings and murder — death was a regular punchline. Larson also liked to make light of whatever came after death. Occasionally he’d make a joke about Heaven, but he clearly found Hell far more amusing. Many Far Side comics take place in the underworld — or some specific version of it — with Satan a recurring figure in his comic strips. Few characters in The Far Side appeared more than once, but the Devil himself made many appearances.

In this comic from June 11, 1988, a pizza gets delivered to Hell and Satan is clearly unhappy about it. “First of all, this is going straight back — and I’ll just have a little chat with whoever placed the order,” says Satan. The best little detail in the comic strip is the slogan on the pizza box. It says “Jerry’s Pizza: We Deliver Anywhere” and Jerry clearly lives up to her word.

I can understand the Devil’s annoyance. Pizza is one of the greatest pleasures on Earth, which means that pizza is absolutely disqualified from being served in Hell, even though there’s a perfect-looking pizza oven literally everywhere you look.

A recurring source of humor in Gary Larson’s comic strip was to take a common phrase or idiom and interpret it as literally as possible. In one comic from 1992, he picked apart the common phrase “ride off into the sunset” by depicting a burnt-up cowboy that had literally ridden into the sun. In another comic from 1985, he played on the phrase “dressed to kill” by showing two sharks in fancy evening attire.

In this comic from 1982, Larson dissected the phrase “To Hell and back” by turning Hell into a mere tourist destination. In it, a man is showing off slides from his vacation, which include a picture of his wife standing with the Devil. Especially fun here is how happy the Devil is, while the man’s wife looks bored by the encounter.

While Hell showed up in several Far Side comics, it wasn’t always the same Hell, or at least it didn’t appear to be. Several comic strips depicted very specific, different forms of Hell, each of which was specifically designed to torture a particular resident of the underworld. For example, this comic from 1990 has the caption “Bowler’s Hell.” It shows the bowler having made an impossible-to-hit split, with the Devil saying “Whoa! Another split?... What a bummer!” Clearly, endless splits like this would be the worst possible punishment for a bowler.

If a bowler’s version of Hell is endless splits, a classical conductor's version of Hell would have to be an orchestra filled only with banjos. Larson seemingly found banjos to be obnoxious instruments as a number of his comic strips feature them in a less-than-flattering light. In another, more Earthly Far Side comic, a different conductor is horrified by the sound of a banjo in his orchestra as he shouts “Stop! Stop! What’s that sound? What’s that sound?”

Still, it seems that, for Gary Larson’s musical tastes, banjos rank higher than accordions, which received even harsher treatment.

This Far Side comic contrasts Heaven and Hell. In Heaven, you receive a harp when you die, but in Hell, the instrument given out to all new inhabitants is an accordion. I seriously wonder if any accordion or banjo players were ever offended by Gary Larson’s repeated attacks on their instruments.

In yet another specific version of Hell, dogs who have been sentenced to eternal damnation are forced to work as their arch-nemesis: mailmen. Originally, I’d assumed that this comic strip was a parody of the movie All Dogs Go to Heaven, a truly beautiful animated film directed by Don Bluth. But the years don’t add up as this comic strip comes from 1986 while All Dogs Go to Heaven is from 1989.

And while I’m not the kind of person to get into a theological debate between any two parties, I think Don Bluth is right about where dogs go in the afterlife, whereas Larson is totally wrong about any dog going to hell.

Tennessee football SEC tiebreakers: Rooting guide to make title game

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Tennessee football needs help, even if it wins out, to make the final SEC championship game.

As the regular season enters its final two weeks, the SEC championship game picture is only slightly less murky, but a clear top four of contenders - Georgia, Alabama, Tennessee and Texas - have emerged at the center of messy tiebreaker scenarios.

Hoping to keep their College Football Playoff hopes alive, the No. 9 Vols (8-2, 5-2 SEC) play No. 23 LSU (6-4, 3-4) on Nov. 21 (3:30 p.m. ET, ABC) at Neyland Stadium in Tigers coach Lane Kiffin's first visit to Knoxville since 2021.

Here's what needs to happen for the Vols to earn a spot in the 2026 SEC championship game.

Tennessee makes the SEC championship game if ...

Texas, by virtue of its win over the Vols at Neyland Stadium on Sept. 26, controls its own destiny to play in conference championship game on Dec. 5 in Atlanta. So there is only one way for the Vols to make the SEC championship game: win out while Texas loses to either Arkansas or Texas A&M, who have a combined record of 3-11 vs. conference opponents this year.

Things could get more complicated in the fight for seeding if Georgia drops its SEC finale at South Carolina on Nov. 21, while Tennessee, Texas and Alabama win out.

In that case, the SEC would have to resort to its final tiebreaker mechanism, "capped relative scoring margin," to get an opponent for Georgia, who would take the top seed based on a higher combined winning percentage for its conference opponents.

Alabama is expected to come out on top in the capped relative scoring margin tiebreaker, while Tennessee assumes third place and Texas falls to fourth. But that metric takes into account margin of victory for games that have yet to be played, so there's no way to know who it would favor when the season ends.

A Georgia loss to South Carolina and Texas loss to either Arkansas or Texas A&M would clear the way for Tennessee to assume No. 1 spot over Georgia by virtue of a better record vs. common conference opponents, as long as the Vols win out.

Upsets could force the SEC to dig deep into the tiebreaker system during these final two weeks of the regular season. But for Tennessee, winning their final two games vs. LSU and Vanderbilt, while getting some help from Arkansas or Texas A&M, is the only path to the SEC championship game.

Veron: ‘Nico Paz does things I’ve only seen Messi and Maradona do’

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Juan Sebastian Veron believes that the sky is the limit for Como and Argentina star Nico Paz, whom he tips to reach the game’s highest possible heights: ‘At 22, he does things I’ve only seen Messi and Diego Maradona do’.

The former Sampdoria, Parma and Lazio midfielder spoke in-depth about the qualities and potential that Nico Paz possesses in an interview with La Gazzetta dello Sport.

Nico Paz has established himself as one of the finest players in Serie A over the last two seasons, has recently introduced himself on the Champions League stage and is now being tipped for greatness by legendary former players in his homeland.

The Como attacker provided 12 goals and seven assists with Como last season, and already has six goals and eight assists from 14 appearances at club level in 2026-27.

Back in Argentina, he is one of the leading candidates to inherit Lionel Messi’s legendary no.10 shirt for the national team, for whom he has scored five goals in five games this season.

“Nico Paz has incredible talent. The future is all his if he continues to act with his current level of professionalism,” said Veron in his interview with La Gazzetta dello Sport.

Veron would later explain that comparing young players to Messi and Maradona has been the ‘death’ of several young Argentine forwards, but he feels that Nico Paz has earned the right to those comparisons.

“At 22, he does things that I’ve only seen Messi and Diego Maradona do. I don’t mean to say that he will reach those levels, defining an era like Diego and Leo did, but Nico Paz has the qualities to attempt the feat. And I wish him success in achieving that goal.”

Veron believes that Nico Paz has two standout traits: “The personality he shows on the pitch and the way he is able to make plays so naturally. You can tell he has that personality because his teammates always look for him, give him the ball even when he’s being marked, knowing he’ll dribble past opponents and leave them for dust.

“Nico is a leader in the way he moves, the way he occupies space on the pitch and the way he handles certain moments of play. He chooses when to speed up or slow down depending on the state of play, he can attack from the right, left, through the middle. He’s always at the core of the game.”

Veron was asked if he sees any of the qualities that he possessed as a player in Nico Paz.

“I don’t like to make comparisons with players from different eras, but I think that Nico Paz is like if you moved Veron 20 metres further forward. Looking for shots, goals and assists and that last ball in behind,” he replied.

“He brings a lot of value to Argentina, which is trying to rebuild in the post-Messi era. He’s definitely more of a striker and less of a midfielder when you compare him to me, but he also reads the game well and understands the rhythm of it in his head.”

Despite his earlier claims, Veron warns that making comparisons to legendary former players can often be detrimental.

“I know Lionel Scaloni well. He’s right to want to divert media attention away from Nico Paz. We’re incredible at burning talent in Argentina. If only you knew how many no.10s with great potential have been ‘killed’ by comparisons to Maradona. It takes patience and a bit of luck.”

Veron was also asked for his thoughts on Fiorentina’s Franco Mastantuono.

“I can’t know whether he’ll become a superstar or not, but I do think that Serie A is the most suitable league for a player like him,” he said.

“He has the chance to gain experience, to feel the studs of opposition defenders, to learn the technical and tactical traits that Italians are masters at teaching.

“However, he needs to find consistency in his performances and that can only happen if he plays regularly as a starter, as is currently happening at Fiorentina. He was closed out at Real Madrid by all the other players.

“Nico Paz has become a leader by playing regularly. I’ve never known somebody who is the best on their team on the bench.”

Asked if Nico Paz and Mastantuono are the future of the Argentina national team, Veron agreed: “I think so. We are the land of no.10s and these two guys can help us dream. As long as we let them grow in peace.”

For years, I was a girl who would say “yes” when I meant “no.” It was only after I decided to stop drinking that I realized how much my “yes-girl” tendency had fueled my drinking cycle over the years.

Over the years, both through my own recovery and my work in helping others break their drinking patterns, I have come to see that most people who eventually come to question their relationship with alcohol don’t have a “drinking problem;” instead, they have a “drinking solution.” In my case, my inability and unwillingness to say “no” had created a lot of problems that I ended up turning to alcohol to solve.

I would say yes to a football weekend marathon when I didn’t even know what a quarterback was, and then turn to a 6-pack to make time pass faster. I would say “yes” to hosting the relative whom I couldn't stand, then try to push away my resentment with a bottle of wine. I would say “yes” to helping out at a neighbor’s garage sale after a long week and end up trying to recover from exhaustion with a few cocktails.

As I started to help others break their old drinking patterns, I realized I was far from the only person who got caught in this pattern. In fact, I came to nickname this particular drinking pattern “the Yes-Effect Drinking Loop.” It’s one of the 10 most common invisible drinking loops that keep an unwanted drinking pattern alive.

In most unhealthy drinking patterns, drinking is only what we see on the surface. The Invisible Drinking Loop is a term I created to help people understand the hidden forces that keep their drinking patterns alive. An invisible drinking loop is often driven by the need to solve a problem, fueled by the belief that alcohol can help, and maintained by the habit loop in which drinking has become the default routine to cope with or deal with certain situations or emotions.

In the “Yes-Effect Drinking Loop,” drinking has become the solution to deal with the side effects that arise when a person struggles to say no.

As is true with most drinking loops, simply stopping drinking doesn’t break the loop.

With alcohol out of my life, I found myself sitting through events that I had no interest in, battling the desire to reach for a drink to make time pass faster, hanging out with people whose company I didn’t enjoy, missing the quick mental escape alcohol could offer, and dealing with overwhelming cravings by the end of the day after I once again stretched myself too thin in the effort to please others instead of honoring my own limitations.

It took me a long time to realize that simply stopping drinking doesn’t solve the problems; instead, it exposes them. I needed to learn to find another way to address the problem that I once attempted to solve through drinking, and that meant learning how to say no.

Learning to say no after years of saying yes is not easy. When faced with a choice between disappointing others and disappointing myself, my default response was to choose the latter. Somewhere along the line, I had been conditioned to believe that fulfilling others’ expectations, even those of people whom I wasn't even that close to, was a way to keep myself safe, liked, and in good standing.

Yet, I failed to see the hidden cost of all the efforts to keep others happy; I kept myself unhappy. So much so that alcohol became the only way I knew how to push down my disappointment, exhaustion, and resentment.

Learning to say “no” started with a radical decision to honor my own preferences rather than automatically prioritize others’ expectations. Don’t get me wrong, it doesn’t mean becoming self-centered or never making compromises in life. But it does mean taking an intentional pause before giving in to my compulsive need to keep others happy. And when faced with the choice between disappointing others and disappointing myself, I wanted to make that choice consciously, rather than by default.

Of course, giving myself permission to say no was one thing. Actually knowing how to say it was another. After years of automatically saying yes, saying no felt like a skill I had not yet learned.

That's why Skill Expansion is one of the core pillars of my approach to breaking an old drinking pattern. Truly growing beyond alcohol often requires us to develop new skills and tools to navigate the challenges we once relied on alcohol to handle. For the Yes-Effect Drinking Loop, learning to say no is one of those skills.

Fortunately, like most skills, saying no is something we can learn through practice. Personally, I've found that having a few ready-to-use ways to say no makes a world of difference. That's why I've put together 7 of my favorite scripts for saying no with grace.

A Pop-Star Acting Newbie, the Afterlife and “Dirty Energy”: The Making of ‘La Bola Negra’

Directing duo Javier Ambrossi and Javier Calvo stretch a love story across the Spanish Civil War, three generations and.the great beyond.

Javier Calvo was traveling with his filmmaking partner, Javier Ambrossi, when he read Alberto Conejero’s play La Piedra Oscura (The Dark Stone) and the idea for La Bola Negra (The Black Ball) took hold. Conejero’s drama, set in a military hospital during the Spanish Civil War, imagines the last night of Rafael Rodríguez Rapún — a Republican lieutenant and the real-life lover of playwright Federico García Lorca — in conversation with Sebastián, the young Nationalist soldier who is to execute him at dawn.

“I imagined this little gay boy having to leave his town, all this tragedy, and how this happened to my country only 90 years ago, and having a gay character in the civil war,” says Calvo. “We never talk about that. Where were we? What happened to us? It hit me, and I started to cry. The film, co-written with Conejero, braids three timelines. In 1932 Granada, Carlos (Milo Quifes), a young upper-class Andalusian, is blackballed from an exclusive casino because he’s gay — the premise of Lorca’s unfinished play La Bola Negra, of which only four pages survive. In 1937, Sebastián (singer- songwriter Guitarricadelafuente) keeps watch over Rafael (Miguel Bernardeau). And in 2017 Madrid, Alberto (Carlos González) learns that he is Sebastián’s grandson. Sebastián has left him the rest of Lorca’s play — pages the film imagines into being — which he had promised Rafael he would save. Those pages — verified by Glenn Close, who plays a Spanish-speaking American scholar named Isabelle Durand — lead Alberto and his relatives to discover that Sebastián spent most of his life secretly living with another man.

“This is a movie about what we pass on to the next generation,” Ambrossi says. “It’s about inheritance.”

The co-directors, known as Los Javis among their collaborators because of their shared first names, reassembled their usual crafts team to begin shaping each timeline: cinematographer Gris Jordana, editor Alberto Gutiérrez, production designer Roger Bellés, costume designer Ana López Cobos, composer Raül Refree and casting directors Eva Leira and Yolanda Serrano.

The directors were already keen on Guitarricadelafuente and Bernardeau for their respective roles, as well as González for the part of Alberto, which Ambrossi calls “a creation.”

“He’s so comedic and flamboyant, but we wanted to give him a role where he can prove the actor that he is because sometimes queer people are given the same [stereotypical] roles,” Ambrossi says.

Quifes, who like Guitarricadelafuente makes his screen debut in the film, was found through an open casting call. “He has a sense of melancholy about him, but it’s not about hopelessness; there’s a sense of hope and beauty,” says Leira. “And he has such a beautiful gaze. It’s a gaze that comes from ancient times.”

Los Javis also knew they wanted to shoot in 35mm from the outset, which aligned with DP Jordana’s approach. “You don’t have to fight the format to get images,” she says of 35mm film. “If you use the right light direction, the rest just comes together. It’s like magic.”

Light also drove the design of the opulent casino in Granada, the only set built from scratch. Everywhere else, the production restored existing buildings, hiring local artisans who still work with prewar methods. “At first glance, it looked like something crazy and unnecessary when we built it in the middle of Spain, but when you open the windows, you see Spain, not a green screen,” says production designer Bellés, who commissioned artist Ignasi Monreal to paint the mural on the ceiling in the style of Joaquín Sorolla, “one of the great painters of Spanish light,” he explains.

The windows were made of sugar glass so that they could shatter safely when the casino explodes near the end of the film after being overtaken by a mob as Carlos makes a final case for admission. The remaining structure was then filled with fake snow as the scene transitions to Carlos lying on a mountainside, realizing he has died from the bomb’s explosion. The shot, captured at an altitude of 3,000 meters (more than 9,800 feet high) in the snowy region of the Sierra Nevadas, brought to life elements described in Lorca’s poem — like the skiers on the slopes — which also inspired Guitarricadelafuente’s original song, “La Nieve (The Snow),” that plays in the background.

“With Federico García Lorca, there’s a lot of powerful imagery and symbolism, like in the beginning of the film, the horse running past Sebastián is a representation of untamable desire,” Guitarricadelafuente explains. “ ‘La Nieve’ symbolized the end of a journey and approaching death. I had to leave the emotional baggage of Sebastián to fully understand what he was carrying to be able to write that.”

Refree took a hands-on approach with Penélope Cruz, who portrays Nené Romero, a cupletista cabaret singer. “She told me, ‘I’m not a singer,’ but as soon as I heard her singing … I fell in love with her singing,” he says. “The problem was that she hadn’t created the character yet.”

Over the course of several days, Refree went to Cruz’s home for rehearsals, and she began to craft the persona of Nené, who serves as a portal to a world in which Sebastián can be free to be his true self when he sees her perform “Las Tardes del Ritz (Afternoons at the Ritz)” and “Batallón de modistillas (Seamstress Battalion)” on the base.

“My goal was to have this kind of music that she could be influenced by when talking to the soldiers and to create this evocative thing, like making two men play together as a couple,” Refree explains of the first number. “The second one needed a dirty energy that would make the soldiers lose control and want to touch her. So we created something very rhythmic and feminine at the same time.”

Wardrobe also helped shape Nené. Cobos drew from research on 19th century cuplé singers, like María del Consuelo Vello Cano, to sketch Cruz’s feathery, embellished ensemble.

“When she saw the white dress, she said, ‘This is it; she has to be wearing white,’ and Gris also said it would be helpful because we don’t have a lot of lights in the theater,” says Cobos. “[For the second number,] because I know Penélope’s a big fan of Mugler, I went through their images and found these overalls and dresses that reminded me of military costumes.
