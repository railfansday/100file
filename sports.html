Johnny Gaudreau’s Number Will Be Retired at Boston College After NHL Star’s Tragic Death

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Boston College will retire Johnny Gaudreau's jersey number during a ceremony on Feb 12

He helped the Eagles win the 2012 NCAA championship and earned the 2014 Hobey Baker Award before joining the NHL

Gaudreau and his brother Matthew were killed by an alleged drunk driver in August 2024

Boston College will honor late NHL star Johnny Gaudreau by retiring his number early next year, the school announced.

Two and a half years after Gaudreau was killed alongside his brother, fellow Boston College (BC) graduate Matthew Gaudreau, by an alleged drunk driver, the school will retire his No. 13 jersey with a ceremony on Feb. 12, the university said in a statement on Wednesday, Nov. 18.

"We are proud to honor Johnny Gaudreau by retiring his No. 13 at Boston College," the university's Director of Athletics, Blake James, said in a statement. "Johnny was an extraordinary player who helped bring a national championship to the Heights and became one of the most accomplished players in our program's history. He will always hold a special place in the Boston College family."

(L-R) Matthew and Johnny Gaudreau in 2014 after winning the Beanpot TournamentCredit: Richard T Gagnon/Getty

Gaudreau, known as "Johnny Hockey," played for the Eagles for three seasons before he was drafted by the Calgary Flames in 2014. During that time, he won the 2012 NCAA National Championship and in 2014, when he led the nation in scoring, was awarded the Hobey Baker Award, given annually to the best men's college hockey player. In just 119 games with BC, Gaudreau racked up 78 goals and 97 assists.

He became an NHL star after leaving BC, playing 11 seasons with the Flames and the Columbus Blue Jackets, as well as the U.S. national team.

His No. 13 will be just the third men's hockey number retired at BC, after David Emma's No. 16 and Mike Mottau's No. 3.

Gaudreau's wife, Meredith, celebrated the news on her Instagram Stories Wednesday.

"Whole different level of everything ❤️," she wrote alongside a video of longtime BC coach Jerry York talking about Johnny. "My husband!! soooo proud of you."

Johnny and Meredith Gaudreau with their children Noa and JohnnyCredit: Meredith Gaudreau/Instagram

A day before BC announced they would retire Johnny's number, his childhood ice rink shared plans to erect a statue of him and Matthew outside the facility.

"We hope every young player who walks through those doors sees it and is inspired to play the way John and Matty did: with heart and love for the game, and to live the way they did, with kindness on and off the ice," Guy and Jane Gaudreau, the brothers' parents, said in a statement. "If it encourages even one kid to pick up a stick and follow their example, their legacy lives on."

With Kyle Shanahan and Brian Flores, Vikings-49ers will be a matchup for the modern NFL

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

In the long-running anime One Piece, there's an incident at the World Government headquarters, Marineford, known as "The War of the Best." The strongest pirates in the world of One Piece fought the strongest Marines the World Government had to offer, and it ultimately became the most important arc in the One Piece world, shaping the direction of the story that's still being told today.

In the NFL, it feels like we have a game every year that is similar to War of the Best -- two titans facing off against each other, where the outcome will shape the next wave of the NFL on both sides of the ball:

Kyle Shanahan vs. the Legion of Boom in 2016.

Sean McVay vs. Vic Fangio, then Bill Belichick, in that year's Super Bowl.

Andy Reid vs. Lou Anarumo in the second half of the 2022 AFC Championship.

Sean McVay vs. Mike Macdonald last season.

This year, the new NFL meta will be defined Sunday night in Mexico City, where the San Francisco 49ers and Shanahan will face the Minnesota Vikings and defensive coordinator Brian Flores in one of the most anticipated matchups of the year. Both the Niners' offense and Vikings' defense are resetting what their respective sides can and should do, and seeing which side blinks first could be a blueprint for how offenses and defenses attack each other in the near future.

If we recall a story I wrote earlier this season, NFL teams are blitzing at an astronomical rate. Over ten weeks, defenses league-wide have a 32.3% blitz rate, the second-highest since 2010 and one of only four seasons with a blitz rate over 30% (2011, 2013, and 2010). The number jumps even higher when teams go under center to an astounding 50.5%, the highest since 2010 and the only season TruMedia has recorded in that time period with a blitz rate over 50% against under-center offenses. When these defenses blitz, they have a 54.9% defensive success rate overall, and that number drops to 50.1% when offenses are under center.

At the forefront of this are Flores and the Minnesota Vikings, who are redefining what it means to be aggressive. The Vikings currently sit with an absurd 72.6% blitz rate, by far the highest for any team through 10 weeks since 2010. In fact, you have to go all the way back to the 2006 Miami Dolphins to find a team that went higher than that (what was Nick Saban cooking in 2006?). The way that Minnesota can dictate the flow of not only a play, but the entire game through their pressure looks that completely stymie opposing offenses.

The Vikings are essentially playing rock-paper-scissors on every play, daring you to throw anything other than rock. One way they can dictate the flow is by forcing teams to leave extra pass protectors in to help against pressure looks.

Imagine you're Packers quarterback Jordan Love. You come to the line of scrimmage, and this is what you see:

The alarm bells are ringing. This is the most obvious Cover 0 all-out blitz look, eight guys up at the line of scrimmage. The common answer for the Packers and Love has been to keep the RB and TE in the protection, calling it max protection. This is the Packers throwing rock, hoping to catch the Vikings in a Cover 0 look and hit them for an explosive.

The Vikings actually spin everyone out of this except the four defensive linemen and run Cover 3, frazzling Jordan Love before he ends up throwing it 10 yards short of the first-down marker on third down. The Vikings got the Packers to throw rock, then they throw paper.

This ability to force offenses to keep seven people in to protect the QB is unprecedented at the NFL level. According to Sumer Sports, the 2026 Vikings are facing dropbacks where all five eligible receivers are out to catch a pass on 35.7% of their dropbacks, the lowest in the NFL by a significant margin. They're the only team in the NFL below 50% in that metric, with the New York Giants in second place…at 58.5%! Flores and the defense force offenses to play a completely different sport when they play Minnesota, using pressure to dictate the terms of the passing game. They force you to waste backs and tight ends in pass protection, then force you to play with a hand behind your back.

If you want to get four out in the passing concept, good luck, because that doesn't work either.

You're probably wondering, "Yes, the Vikings defense is amazing, but what does that have to do with the 49ers and how this will decide the state of NFL schematics?" The answer lies in the protection plans. The Vikings see the lowest amount of five-out in the passing concept in the NFL. Well, guess which team leads the NFL in percentage of dropbacks with all five eligible receivers out in the passing concept? That's right, Kyle Shanahan and the San Francisco 49ers.

I'd describe the Niners' offense as "malleable." They can use multiple players in multiple ways, keeping their best players on the field while putting linebackers in a blender. They do that by getting all five guys out in the passing concept. If we include chip blocks, the Niners get all five eligibles out at an 80.1% clip, the highest in the NFL (not including chips, that number drops to 60.8%, second in the NFL behind the Pittsburgh Steelers). Against pressure looks where linebackers are mugged, or showing that they're blitzing, the 49ers like to get everyone into the concept and spread teams out. The Niners (and Bengals as well) are zagging when everyone else is zigging, spreading out and getting five eligibles into the concept against the blitz.

With simulated pressures, there's always a law of equivalent exchange: for every guy you add, a guy has to drop. For most defenses to stay structurally intact while showing these pressures in two-high shells, someone has to drop from the defensive line. Shanahan knows this, and knows he has the personnel to live in five-out looks (we'll get to that later). If we're going to the rock, paper, scissors comparison again, the Niners are choosing to throw paper instead of rock, because if they can find the weakest point in the pressure, they'll hammer it.

This 3rd down against the Cowboys is a great example. Dallas lines up in a pressure look that has seven at the line of scrimmage. The Niners see this and have five guys as eligible route runners. Instead of checking into max protection, they stay with what they have, giving RB Christian McCaffrey a choice route on the backside. Cowboys spin out and drop into Cover 2, but CMC has already found the hole, and Brock Purdy finds him to move the chains.

DAL comes out with 7 up at the LOS on 3rd down, but drop everyone into the coverage. SF gets 5 out into the look, and run CMC on a choice route underneath that picks up the first down. Their ability to get 5 out into the concept is huge against pressure looks pic.twitter.com/L9sYfVOUuJ

This touchdown for McCaffrey against the Raiders is another way that the 49ers can undress the pressure looks by getting five receivers out into the concept. San Francisco motions FB Kyle Juszczyk into the backfield, then bumps him out right before the snap. The Raiders' secondary bumps with him, then sends the blitz while dropping the 2 DTs. However, LB Quay Walker hesitates just a little bit on his blitz, giving McCaffrey enough time to get wide open.

The reason the 49ers can live this way is not only because of Kyle Shanahan, but because of the personnel on the field. LT Trent Williams is a mute button at left tackle. Having him on the field lets the rest of the line slide the other way and leave Williams on an island. Having McCaffrey also helps in a big way: a guy who can run the offense as well as anyone and run choice routes against zone coverage. But this all comes together because of Brock Purdy and his ability to create out of structure. If the Niners go to five-man protection, and there's someone hot off the edge, Purdy is trusted to make them miss and extend the play. That's why he became the starter in the first place: he can go off schedule and keep plays alive. Like this play against the Dolphins, where Miami sends an overload to the defense's left and drops the EDGE to the numbers side. The Niners' OL squeezes to the right, leaving the left EDGE unblocked. Purdy spins out and makes the first Dolphin miss, buying time to make this throw to WR Mike Evans.

These two coordinators have squared off before as members of the Vikings and Niners with Purdy under center, but I'm almost tempted to throw both games out. In their 2023 game, LT Trent Williams didn't play and it was before players like LBs Blake Cashman and Eric Wilson were added to the Vikings' roster. The 2024 tilt is more accurate, but even then it feels like watching two completely different teams. The Vikings weren't leaning nearly as much into their blitzing (the 2024 Vikings only had a 42% blitz rate, still a league high then but a far cry from where we are right now). Conversely, the Niners' offense feels more mature now than it was in 2024, with Purdy playing at a much higher level (plus, McCaffrey didn't play). It's a testament to how much both coordinators have grown and how their respective teams have grown with them, to become the Charizard version they both are today.

This battle between the two most prominent minds in the sport feels like it's going to set the trends for what teams will look like in the future. Already this season, we've seen teams lean more into seven- and eight-man fronts at the line of scrimmage, trying to force offenses to condense their formations and limit receiving options. The best offenses, however, have decided to spread out and get all five eligibles into the passing skeleton, forcing defenses to show their hand and find eligible playmakers in space.

Two of the most prominent minds at the forefront of this push-and-pull will go to battle in Mexico City. What happens during the game could shape how teams approach offensive and defensive design.

3 Winter Fueling Mistakes You’re Probably Making—and How to Fix Them

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Sports dietitians share seasonal nutrition tweaks to help you stay healthy and energized through the colder months.

For many runners, winter presents a greater challenge than other seasons when it comes to fueling. Our regular eating schedules shift around social gatherings, holiday menus influence our usual food choices, and staying hydrated can take more effort without higher temperatures to drive our thirst.

As we approach the start of winter, it’s a good time to check in on your nutrition habits and make tweaks to set you up for stronger running through the season.

Three registered dietitians, who have years of competitive athletic experience themselves, share the most common fueling mistakes they see runners make this time of year and explain exactly how to fix each one.

Vitamin D deficiencies are common among athletes in winter, Maddie Alm, RD, a registered dietitian and the founder of Fueling Forward Sports Nutrition in Colorado, tells Runner’s World. That’s because shorter days and colder temperatures can mean less exposure to the sun’s UVB rays, which help our bodies produce vitamin D.

Alm explains that this vitamin not only supports strong bones, but also helps keep our immune and digestive systems functioning at their best. It plays an important role in brain health and mood, too. According to the National Institutes of Health, low blood levels of the vitamin have been associated with a higher risk of depression.

The Fix: Get outside daily to catch some sunshine, and focus on eating more vitamin D–rich foods, with the goal to consume 600 international units (IU) per day.

Good sources of vitamin D include milk, yogurt, cottage cheese, tuna, salmon, egg yolk, and mushrooms. Some cereals and orange juices are also vitamin D–fortified. For reference, three ounces of salmon contain 450 IU, one cup of cow’s milk contains 180 IU, one large egg yolk contains 40 IU, and one cup of fortified cereal contains 40 IU.

It’s a good idea, Alm adds, to check your levels through bloodwork once or twice a year and to consult a professional if your numbers come back low. If you have a hard time hitting your daily quota through food alone, you may benefit from taking a vitamin D supplement.

Runners can also overlook vitamin C intake this time of year, says Sakiko Minagawa, RD, a registered dietitian for the Division of Sports Medicine at Nationwide Children’s Hospital in Ohio and two-time Olympic Marathon Trials qualifier. Vitamin C, also called ascorbic acid, supports immune health, which is especially important during cold and flu season.

Our bodies also need vitamin C to produce collagen, a protein that’s essential for tissue growth and repair. “While vitamin C may not prevent you from outright getting sick, some research shows that it may decrease the duration of cold symptoms,” Minagawa explains.

The Fix: Up your consumption of foods with vitamin C, such as bell peppers, citrus fruits, greens, and strawberries. Women should aim for 75 milligrams per day, while men should aim for 90 milligrams daily.

You’ll get about 210 milligrams from one large red or orange bell pepper, and 90 milligrams in a cup of raw strawberry halves—each of which achieves your recommended daily dose.

Because vitamin C helps with iron absorption, Minagawa suggests pairing the two whenever possible. Some meals she recommends are a spinach salad with strawberries, an omelet with spinach and red bell peppers, and tomato soup with lentils or black beans.

In the winter, you might not end your run with a dry mouth and sweat-soaked shirt like in the summer, but it’s still critical to hydrate. “Even when it’s cold, you still sweat,” says Minagawa, which means you still need to replenish fluids throughout the day, as well as before and after a workout.

Similarly, your midrun fueling and hydrating remain just as crucial for performance. It’s true: Tearing open an energy gel with gloved hands can pose a real challenge. But, if you’re doing long runs through the colder months, you still need to consume enough carbohydrates during your effort.

The Fix: Minagawa suggests carrying a water bottle that you can easily drink from and refill throughout the day. If you have a hard time sipping on plain water, remember that soups, broths, and hot chocolate can also help you stay hydrated—and can double as recovery fuel.

Don’t forget about fresh fruits and vegetables, either, as they can help with hydration. Some great in-season options that can contribute to your overall fluid intake include grapefruit, clementines, pomegranate, kiwi, squash, Brussels sprouts, sweet potatoes, pears, kale, and cabbage.

During your winter long runs, experiment with fueling options other than energy gels and chews. Some great alternatives you can easily carry in a small plastic baggie include salted Medjool dates, boiled red potatoes, dried fruit, pretzels, and even candy.

Bad weather conditions, scant daylight hours, and more limited racing opportunities make winter a time for many athletes to dial back their training. Taking an off-season can benefit you in the long haul, but Minagawa encourages runners to still stay active—even if that means swapping some runs for walks, bike rides, easy swims, or elliptical sessions.

You don’t want to depart from those year-round healthy habits, Brianna Butler, RD, a registered dietitian, co-owner of Stef & Bri Wellness in Texas, and former professional WNBA athlete, previously told Runner’s World. The same applies to your nutrition.

“The biggest mistake runners make is that, when it’s the off-season, they’re off everything—they say, ‘I’m not doing anything, I’m going to eat whatever’—they’re just winging it,” Butler says. “It shouldn’t be all or nothing. We want to make sure we’re nourishing our bodies well year-round.”

If your training load changes significantly, pay closer attention to your hunger cues to make sure your nutrition matches your energy needs.

The Fix: Maintain a daily exercise routine you enjoy, prioritize nutrient-dense meals and snacks, and resist making drastic changes to your diet.

If you do find yourself feeling sluggish this time of year, it can be tempting to repent with cleanses, detoxes, and extreme exercise regimens. But such quick fixes rarely pay off in the long run, Alm warns, as they put you at risk for injury, burnout, and subpar seasons to come.

Alm says not to stress if you ditched your typical eating and training schedule for a few weeks and are feeling less energetic as a result. When you return to your usual healthy habits, your body will naturally find its sweet spot. “Trust the process and don’t panic,” Alm advises, because our bodies may change as we move through different training phases. “It is okay to not be at your leanest year-round.”

The smartest path forward? Do your best to move your body daily, fuel it with what it needs, and give yourself permission to rest when you’re tired. That way, you’ll find yourself feeling strong and ready to train hard again come spring.

Fifa’s World Cup TV rights documents indicate hydration breaks will stay in 2030

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Controversial breaks were financial boon for broadcasters

Fifa flags possible benefits but says no decision made

Fifa’s tender documents for TV rights for the 2030 World Cup includes a provision for broadcasters to sell adverts during hydration breaks in a strong indication the controversial stoppages will be retained.

A three-minute break midway through each half was introduced for the first time at all 104 matches at last summer’s World Cup in the interests of player welfare, a radical move that also provided a commercial boon for broadcasters, particularly the rights holders in the United States, Fox Sports and Telemundo.

Fifa insists it has yet to decide whether hydration breaks will be used at the 2030 tournament, which will take place largely in Morocco, Spain and Portugal but will also feature three matches in South America, but the Guardian has learned that the possibility and potential commercial benefits are flagged to broadcasters in the tender documents.

Fifa has awarded World Cup rights to Netflix in Spain and to the public service broadcaster SBS in Australia, and the sales process for the US and Puerto Rico is ongoing, with Thursday the deadline to bid.

The tender documents are also understood to reference to cooling breaks, a welfare mechanism used by Uefa whereby play is stopped for between 90 seconds and three minutes if the on-field temperature reaches 28C.

Fifa sources told the Guardian the tenders were designed to cover all potential options.

Fifa used cooling breaks on an ad hoc basis before triggering the wholesale adoption of hydration breaks for the 2026 World Cup, despite 31 of the 104 games being played in climate-controlled stadiums, after high temperatures led to stoppages in about 60% of matches at the 2025 Club World Cup in the US.

Fifa said the move was to protect the integrity of the World Cup by ensuring all matches were played in the same conditions, although there were also major commercial benefits for broadcasters.

The Hollywood Reporter reported last summer that hydration breaks were generating between $2m and $9m in advertising revenue a game, leading to claims that Fox brought in an estimated $250m.

Fox’s overall advertising yield from the tournament was $835m according to the media sales analyst Guideline, almost double the $485m rights fee paid to Fifa.

The US rights are expected to fetch between $1.5bn (£1.14bn) and $2bn in an auction that will feature bids from Netflix and all of the streaming services alongside traditional broadcasters.

Mandatory hydration breaks will not be used at next year’s Women’s World Cup in Brazil, where temperatures are forecast to be cooler than they were in many cities at last year’s men’s tournament.

Sean Connery Wasn’t the First James Bond: Meet Barry Nelson in the ‘Casino Royale’ You’ve Never Seen

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Ever since George Lazenby first replaced Sean Connery as James Bond in 1969’s “On Her Majesty’s Secret Service,” fans have argued over who makes the best 007 — and those arguments are likely to resume once Robert Pattinson takes the screen as the latest Bond in director Denis Villeneuve’s new iteration. Although Craig has his partisans, Connery tends to be the consensus choice for the best Bond, though I’ve argued here for the merits of 1970s and ’80s Roger Moore. All the Bonds have their virtues, but Connery benefited from being there first — for anyone who discovered the series in its initial heyday, Connery simply is James Bond. Yet Connery actually wasn’t the first actor to play Bond. That honor goes to journeyman MGM contract player Barry Nelson, who played Bond in a “Casino Royale” adaptation eight years before Connery made the character famous in “Dr. No.” Connery may have been the first 007 to grace the big screen, but Nelson established Bond on the small screen in an episode of the CBS anthology series “Climax!” opposite Peter Lorre in the Le Chiffre role that would later be inhabited by Orson Welles (in the goofy 1967 “Casino Royale”) and Mads Mikkelsen (in the 2006 adaptation that introduced Craig to the series).

The 1954 “Casino Royale” is a fascinating artifact that includes elements that would go on to characterize future James Bond movies, while also going in a few other very different directions. The key difference is in the scale, stripped down by necessity for television production. The hour-long “Climax!” episode is all interiors, taking place entirely in the hotel and casino of the title. It’s more a James Bond play than a movie, with Bond and Le Chiffre facing off in a series of verbal — and ultimately physical — jousts in confined spaces, and it’s surprisingly effective.

It’s also a very strange beginning for a franchise that would come to be known for its globetrotting locales and elaborate action set pieces. Not that this “Casino Royale” was officially part of the James Bond franchise as we know it. In fact, although there have been three adaptations of Ian Fleming’s first James Bond novel, only the third incarnation with Daniel Craig was from EON Productions, the company that controlled the character from “Dr. No” through the Craig era before selling the rights to Amazon.

The 1954 “Casino Royale” came about when producer Gregory Ratoff optioned the rights to Fleming’s novel in May of that year. The novel wasn’t yet a hit, and Fleming was sorely in need of cash, which led him to make one of the worst deals in movie-rights history when he sold the book outright to Ratoff for $6,000 after the TV episode aired. This left “Casino Royale” out of the deal when EON secured the screen rights to James Bond as a character, so Bond producers Albert Broccoli and Harry Saltzman could only sit back and grit their teeth when Ratoff turned “Casino Royale” into a comedy in 1967 and cast Peter Sellers, David Niven, Woody Allen and others as rotating Bonds.

EON ultimately made a deal to bring “Casino Royale” under their umbrella and used the novel to reboot the franchise with Craig, but in 1954 it served as the source for a much more modest affair. The biggest similarities between the 1954 “Climax!” episode and the Daniel Craig movie come in the time spent on extended gambling sequences, and the centering of a brutal torture scene. Given censorship restrictions of the time, Nelson’s torture in a bathtub is considerably less graphic than the mallet to the testicles inflicted on Craig, but it’s rough enough stuff for a 1950s TV production.

Certain aspects of Nelson’s characterization, like Bond’s mix of sexual swagger and icy indifference, would later be adopted by most future 007s, to varying degrees. The biggest difference between Nelson and future Bonds is a simple one: He’s American. Although Broccoli and Saltzman flirted with the idea of an American Bond throughout the series’ history (going so far as to cast James Brolin in “Octopussy” before Moore decided to return at the last minute), to date they’ve never actually pulled the trigger on casting outside the UK.

Nelson is probably best known to modern audiences as the affable hotel manager Stuart Ullman in Stanley Kubrick’s “The Shining,” but in the 1950s he was a sturdy character actor and occasional leading man who bounced around television after an initial sojourn as a studio contract player. As a young man, he appeared in several important MGM films, including “Shadow of the Thin Man,” “Bataan,” and “A Guy Named Joe” (the source material for Steven Spielberg’s “Always”). Later on, he popped up in “Airport” and had a successful Broadway career. He was nominated for a Tony acting opposite Liza Minnelli in Martin Scorsese’s 1977 production “The Act.”

In 1954, though, he was in the middle of a prolific and productive era as a TV stalwart, both as a series regular (on “The Hunter” and “My Favorite Husband”) and a frequent guest star. He doesn’t exhibit a ton of range as American “Combined Intelligence” agent “Jimmy” Bond, but that too is in keeping with the overall conception of the character, at least until Craig tried to bring a little more emotional depth to the role.

In Americanizing Bond, the “Climax!” edition of “Casino Royale” ends up making a number of related changes that are small but make the film feel just a bit off for fans of the EON productions. American Felix Leiter (later played by Jeffrey Wright) becomes Brit Clarence Leiter, for example. Although there’s none of the Bond gadgetry that would become famous later, there are hints of what’s to come in the form of props like a villain’s cane that hides a gun, and the opening credits for the “Climax!” series itself feature a gun barrel pointed at the viewer that predicts the iconic graphics of later Bond title sequences.

The 1954 “Casino Royale” is noteworthy for another reason: its composer, Jerry Goldsmith, who would ultimately score hundreds of major films, including “Planet of the Apes,” “Chinatown,” “Basic Instinct,” and most of Joe Dante’s work. Yet in some ways what’s fascinating about “Casino Royale” is how unremarkable it is — it’s a decent, solid piece of entertainment from an era when proliferating anthology shows provided similar programs every week.

If Fleming had his way, it would have only been the start for 007 on television; he wrote several outlines for episodes of a James Bond series before CBS canceled the project. Luckily, he repurposed most of what he wrote as short stories for his 1960 book “For Your Eyes Only,” and television’s loss was cinema’s gain. By 1962, James Bond belonged to the movies — and to Sean Connery.

OHSAA public-private playoff split headed for statewide vote

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Suburban League, a conference of 16 schools in Northeast Ohio, has officially gotten the ball rolling on a public-private divide for high school playoffs.

The league submitted a petition for a membership vote on separate tournaments for public and private schools to the Ohio High School Athletic Association on Nov. 16. The petition received 152 signatures, well exceeding the required 75-signature threshold.

Now, the question of whether public and private schools should compete in separate postseason tournaments will go to a vote for OHSAA member institutions. The OHSAA's next referendum period begins in May 2027, with each high school voting on all ballot proposals from May 1 to May 15. There are 691 public-school and 121 private-school OHSAA members, each of whose principals cast a vote in the referendum period.

If approved in May, separate state tournaments would likely be implemented for the 2028-2029 school year. According to the National Federation of State High School Associations (NFHS), only Alabama, Maryland, Texas, and Virginia currently hold separate public and private high school state tournaments.

This isn't the first time the question of public vs. private athletics has been brought to the OHSAA's members for a vote. Petition attempts in 1978 and 1993 both failed, but renewed discussion over disproportionate private-school titles led to the development of the OHSAA's competitive balance plan in 2014.

OHSAA member schools adopted competitive balance measures beginning in 2017, placing schools in appropriate postseason divisions for baseball, basketball, football, soccer, softball, and volleyball.

The competitive balance formula looks at a school's enrollment and assigns each rostered player a tier based on considerations like which school district the student lives in or whether they come from an assigned feeder school. The OHSAA then adjusts the enrollment count accordingly, with more local players lowering the enrollment number and more out-of-district players raising it. That adjusted enrollment figure then determines the school's tournament division.

Competitive balance has had some success at bringing state titles closer to a proportionate public-private split. In football, for example, private schools won over 50% of the state championships in the 16 years before the plan's implementation. Since 2017, private schools have won just 20% of the football titles.

While nearly every sport has seen more public schools win championships since competitive balance was adopted, some sports still seem to favor private schools. Girls volleyball has hardly been affected as private schools have won over 64% of state titles since 2017, with three Greater Cincinnati-based private schools winning this year alone. Both boys soccer (48%) and girls soccer (42%) also see high rates of private schools titles.

In OHSAA fall 2026 state finals so far, public schools won four of seven titles in volleyball, four of five titles in boys soccer and three of five titles in girls soccer.

While I was excited to run, I also didn’t want to miss out on any of the socializing that happened that year. Despite all of that, I had caught the marathon bug and after finishing the New York City Marathon in 2022, I went on to run the Paris and Chicago Marathons in 2024.

While training for those first few marathons, I didn’t have any real plan, except to not let it interfere with my social calendar. My strategy consisted of building up mileage in order to reach 20 miles about four weeks out from race day. In between, I pieced together training information from influencers on Instagram and Google searches (this was pre-ChatGPT!).
