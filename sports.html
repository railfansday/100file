Columbia coach Megan Griffith returns from suspension for big game vs. Michigan

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Without head coach Megan Griffith on the sidelines for its first three games of the season, the Columbia women's basketball team didn't just survive — it thrived.

The Lions went 3-0 against Davidson, Rhode Island and Belmont, a trio of squads that ranked in the top 110 of NET last year, a metric the NCAA uses to sort teams. Columbia won those games by an average margin of 13.3 points with associate coach Tyler Cordell making the in-game decisions and adjustments.

"Yeah, she should just take my job," Griffith said with a laugh during a Zoom call with reporters. "What I loved about it was how she just jumped right into it. I think Ty really understood the game planning aspect from my standpoint and how I think about it, and she put her spin on it. Which for her, that is so easy because she built the program with me."

Griffith is now back on the sidelines for Columbia following a three-game suspension, returning to the lead chair on the bench for perhaps the toughest stretch of the Lions' schedule. On Thursday, Nov. 12, they'll host No. 2 Michigan at Levien Gymnasium in New York City, travel to No. 18 Maryland on Sunday, Nov. 15, then host No. 5 Duke on Saturday, Nov. 21.

Columbia announced it was suspending Griffith — an alum of the Ivy League institution who is in her 10th season leading the Lions — in October following a review of the program by an outside firm after the university received a complaint about her behavior during practices from past seasons.

In a statement, Columbia said the review did not require notice to the NCAA of any rules violations and did not involve any allegations of violations of the university's anti-discrimination policies, Title IX or related policies. According to the statement, "Griffith expressed regret for the times in which her interactions with the team did not align with the university's expectations."

USA TODAY Sports was provided with a statement from current Columbia players, too. They said they "fully stand with and support" Griffith and that the complaint was "not indicative of our experience as part of this program."

During a call with reporters on Tuesday, Nov. 10, Griffith didn't discuss the reasoning behind her suspension, but talked about the positives that she took away from it.

"What a unique experience this has been. The biggest benefit was, I think, bringing my staff into how I game plan and think about the game, bringing our players more deeply into it, and then I got to watch a lot and coach the details of the game in a very different way," Griffith said. "I got to observe from a very different vantage point of a game. And obviously, that's not what I wanted. You want to be with your team, right?

"But it was a great opportunity to learn in a different way, and I think our team and my staff did a phenomenal job of stepping up and battling together."

Griffith, 41, played at Columbia from 2003-2007 and was a two-time All-Ivy League selection. After playing professionally in Europe, she joined the coaching ranks by becoming an assistant for Courtney Banghart at Princeton during a stretch where the Tigers made three NCAA Tournaments.

In 2016, Griffith took the reins at her alma mater and has turned Columbia into a consistent mid-major power. In the last five seasons, the Lions were 125-35 with three regular season Ivy League titles, two at-large NCAA Tournament appearances, a runner-up finish in the WNIT in 2023 and a WBIT championship last season. Columbia also won an NCAA Tournament game in 2025, defeating Washington in the First Four.

The March Madness bids and victory — and the WBIT title — were the first in Columbia's history. The program that Griffith has turned the Lions into is one that Power 4 teams and national title contenders are willing to play in the non-conference slate now, because they know Columbia will provide a competitive game and a sufficient measuring stick.

And for Columbia, pulling off an upset in one of these games can be the bullet point on the resume it needs to secure a berth in March Madness.

"It's a gauntlet. I love a challenge," Griffith said. "We have to prove a resume for March. Not that March hinges on (these games), but it gives you another chance, right? So, very intentionally, we always look for a couple of these Quad 1 games.

"Teams like Michigan, teams like Duke, they're not afraid to play anywhere. Those are teams trying to prepare themselves for hostile environments and playing against strong teams. I think that's just a good sign of a program that's really trying to test themselves for March and make a deep run."

Led by reigning Ivy League Player of the Year Riley Weiss, Columbia also brought back key contributors in Mia Broom, Fliss Henderson and 6-foot-3 German forward Hilke Feldrappe. Through the transfer portal, former Big Ten Rookie of the Year Mary Ashley Stevenson has also joined Columbia this season after spending one season apiece at Purdue and Stanford.

"They can turn it on when they want. I think we need to realize they can turn it on more. I think we're sometimes assessing the game and I'm like, 'Impose yourself. Be tone-setters,'" Griffith said. "I just want to see what we're made of a little bit more, right? Let's really test ourselves. We're throwing ourselves fully into the fire here."

To have a chance against a pair of teams in Michigan and Duke that made the Elite Eight last season and a consistent winner in Brenda Frese's Maryland, Columbia will need to push itself to play at its very best.

The 2026 NFL trade deadline has come and gone, and while we did see a couple of blockbuster trades in recent weeks, such as the Chicago Bears trading for Jeffery Simmons and the Jacksonville Jaguars swinging deals for three players in one day, the final 24 hours of the trade deadline were relatively quiet.

On Tuesday, the Minnesota Vikings brought back Kirk Cousins to replace Kyler Murray, and the Kansas City Chiefs added a new wide receiver in Josh Downs. However, there were plenty of other teams perceived to be "buyers" who stayed put, while other "sellers" were hesitant to part ways with their assets. In all, 23 teams combined to make 18 trades with 19 players and 34 picks changing hands. It was the Indianapolis Colts who led the way with an NFL-high four trades for six draft selections, although star running back Jonathan Taylor was not moved.

So, who won and who lost at the trade deadline? Let's take a look.

The Vikings and Las Vegas Raiders made the deal of the day, as new general manager Nolan Teasley sent a third-round pick to "Sin City" for Kirk Cousins. It feels like everyone is a winner in this situation.

The Vikings are a winner because they absolutely had to do something at the quarterback position. Kyler Murray wasn't cutting it, and then Carson Wentz registered one of the worst performances by a Vikings quarterback ever on Monday night with an 8.9 passer rating and a 32% completion rate. The Vikings were wasting the efforts of the best defense in the NFL and found themselves in a spot where they were forced to make an addition. Bringing back a familiar face who has been performing well this season with 20 touchdowns and eight interceptions is a good move.

Cousins actually has the most passing touchdowns (20) and career passing touchdowns (318) by a quarterback changing teams midseason, all-time. During his six seasons in Minnesota (2018-2023), he ranked top five in completion percentage, passing yards and passing touchdowns while going 50-37-1 as the starter.

The Raiders are a winner because they got a third-round pick for their rental quarterback in a season where they likely aren't making the playoffs. The Vikings didn't enter this year expecting to trade for a quarterback in November, which allowed the Raiders to drive up the price.

Kirk Cousins is a winner because he upgrades his own situation. He goes from 4-5 to 6-2, and has the opportunity to spark a legitimate Super Bowl run. That may sound like an exaggeration, but with Justin Jefferson, Jordan Addison and T.J. Hockenson, the Vikings have everything in place to make a run. All they needed was better quarterback play. It could be a storybook ending for Cousins.

Fernando Mendoza is a winner because he's the new starter in Vegas, although the No. 1 overall pick has to begin his NFL career against some aggressive defenses with the Seattle Seahawks on Sunday and then the Denver Broncos the following week.

Another winner is Justin Jefferson, because he gets his quarterback back. JJ began his NFL career with three straight 1,400-yard seasons — including a 1,809-yard campaign in 2022. Jefferson barely crossed 1,000 yards receiving last season. Now, he can suit up next to the best quarterback he's ever played with again. His 98.9 receiving yards per game when catching passes from Cousins are the most by a QB-WR duo all-time (min. 50 games).

Tuesday morning, it was reported that the Cincinnati Bengals were sending a sixth-round pick to the Philadelphia Eagles for pass rusher Arnold Ebiketie. This wouldn't have been a massive story, except the Bengals front office didn't realize they would also be giving up a third-round pick in the deal!

Nick Korte of Over The Cap pointed out that Ebiketie was signed as a compensatory free agent by the Eagles. The Bengals lost three compensatory free agents and signed two, with Cordell Volson not qualifying as a CFA lost since he hasn't played a snap this year. So, adding Ebiketie means the Bengals would lose a 2027 compensatory third-round pick that was coming after Trey Hendrickson signed with the Baltimore Ravens.

From the NFL Collective Bargaining Agreement:

"If a Club trades for a player who was a CFA during that League Year, whether the player was another Club's CFA or the acquiring Club's own CFA, the player will be considered a CFA gained by the acquiring Club and will be included in the Compensatory netting process described above."

It's believed Cincinnati initially thought Volson counted toward the formula.

Naturally, when the Bengals discovered this, they backed out of the deal. This isn't even the first time Korte has had to correct the league about compensatory picks. He actually helped the Bengals secure a top-100 pick in the 2024 NFL Draft after a compensatory mix-up.

Duke Tobin and the Bengals look silly because they almost gave up a third-round pick for Ebiketie. They should hire Korte in the front office after helping them twice in three years.

There's no questioning the Jaguars' goal this season: They are all-in on a Super Bowl. Here's what they traded just this week:

Jacksonville saw Eric Murray go down with a season-ending injury, so they targeted Bates to replace him. He's tied for the second-most interceptions (28) since entering the league in 2018 and is the only NFL player to have recorded a pick-six in each of the last three seasons.

The Jaguars also lost defensive tackle DaVon Hamilton for the remainder of the season after he tore his quad, so the front office went out and acquired a couple of legitimate starters in Godchaux and Davis.

The Jaguars are "winners" because it's exciting when a team gets this aggressive and pushes all of their chips to the middle of the proverbial table. Fans have all the reason in the world to be excited about this run for the No. 1 seed in the AFC.

However, while Jacksonville's roster got better this week, I am questioning all the capital the Jags just spent.

Trading second- and fifth-round picks for Bates is expensive, but giving up first- and second-round picks for Davis was shocking. He's a solid player in the middle, but no one had any idea the Eagles would fetch that kind of return for him. He's recorded 31 tackles and 1.5 sacks in nine games played this season, and he recorded a career-high 4.5 sacks in 2025. Just eight months ago, Davis signed a three-year, $78 million extension that made him one of the highest-paid defensive tackles in the NFL.

If the Jaguars win the Super Bowl, then we can't complain about the compensation. But Jimmy James Gladstone is clearly in an "eff them picks" mood.

Fernando Mendoza inherits a 4-5 Raiders team and NFL history offers two wildly different outcomes

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

If not listed, please contact your TV provider.

In 2016, the Rams were 4-5 when they benched Case Keenum and handed the offense to No. 1 overall pick Jared Goff. He lost all seven of his starts, threw five touchdowns and seven interceptions, and LA finished 4-12. Jeff Fisher was fired with three games remaining, the Rams hired Sean McVay that offseason, and the rest is history.

Two years later, the Ravens were also 4-5 when Lamar Jackson, the final pick of the first round in 2018, replaced an injured Joe Flacco. He won six of his first seven starts, and Baltimore won the AFC North before losing to the Chargers in the wild-card round. Jackson threw just five touchdowns, but his ability to run transformed everything about the offense.

Same record. Same point in the season. Two completely different outcomes.

And now comes Fernando Mendoza, the No. 1 pick in April's draft, who inherits a 4-5 Raiders team after Las Vegas traded Kirk Cousins to Minnesota on Tuesday for a third-round pick. Mendoza will make his first start Sunday against the Seahawks, and while it's tempting to think nine weeks on the bench may have helped prepare him for what's next, history suggests it could be a little more complicated than that.

Since 2000, 148 quarterbacks have started at least one game as NFL rookies. Just 22 made their first start between Weeks 10 and 13, and their collective debuts were not pretty.

They completed just 53.8% of their passes, averaged 5.8 yards per attempt and threw interceptions on 5.3% of their dropbacks – the worst marks of any group. Only five of those 22 won their debuts, and their interception rate was more than 1.6 times that of rookies who started in Week 1.

How rookie QBs performed in their first career start, 2000-2025

Of course, circumstances matter. Some quarterbacks sat because they weren't ready. Others played because the starter got hurt or was ineffective. But the takeaway remains: Sitting for half a season doesn't guarantee a smoother transition to the top of the depth chart.

Five teams had winning records when they turned to a rookie in Weeks 10-13. They were a combined 30-19 before the change and went 10-20 afterward. The 17 teams with losing records fared slightly better when they installed a rookie under center, improving their winning percentage from 28.9% to 33.7% – though they still lost twice as often as they won.

Team performance before and after a rookie's first start, Weeks 10-13

Only three of those 22 teams made the playoffs. The 2011 Texans turned to T.J. Yates after injuries to Matt Schaub and Matt Leinart. The 2017 Bills benched Tyrod Taylor for Nathan Peterman despite sitting at 5-4. Peterman threw five interceptions in the first half of his debut, Taylor reclaimed the job at halftime, and Buffalo eventually made the playoffs with Taylor back under center.

Jackson was different. Baltimore initially needed him because Flacco was injured, but he played so well that they stuck with him even after Flacco was healthy. Obviously, that's a blueprint the Raiders would love to follow, but it's fair to say the organizational infrastructure isn't quite there yet.

And then there are the more immediate concerns for the Raiders rookie…

Mendoza's first three starts come against Seattle, Denver and Cleveland, which also happen to be three of the league's best defenses. The schedule gets less daunting after a Week 13 bye, though matchups against the Broncos and Chiefs make up two of the Raiders; final four games. So yeah.

There is a bigger issue: Mendoza could play well and still lose a bunch of games, and Vegas' first nine weeks help explain why.

Kirk Cousins wasn't the reason for all the losing when Vegas traded him. He had thrown 20 touchdowns and just eight interceptions, and the team was averaging 25.4 points per game. Their passing offense ranks fifth in yards per game and ninth in EPA per play, so Mendoza isn't walking into a no-win situation.

The problems lie everywhere else. Las Vegas ranks dead last in yards per carry (2.9), 31st in rushing yards per game (71.3) and 30th in rushing success rate. The defense hasn't been much better, allowing 35.7 points per game over the last six weeks, five of them losses.

That's a tough combination for any quarterback, future Hall of Famer or fresh face of the franchise. Kubiak's system can help, but even the best-designed offense needs a run game that provides some balance.

Back in June, I wrote that Mendoza might be the most NFL-ready quarterback in the class but had landed in the least quarterback-friendly situation. My rookie QB model had him completing 61.4% of his passes, averaging 6.6 yards per attempt with an 82.1 passer rating.

Which brings us back to Goff and Jackson.

They started their careers headed in opposite directions, yet both eventually became two of the NFL's best quarterbacks. Goff didn't reach his current level until Detroit surrounded him with a strong offensive line, dependable running game and playmakers (and early on, Ben Johnson). Baltimore built its offense around Jackson's unique abilities rather than asking him to become something he wasn't.

The Raiders already have their quarterback of the future. Winifred Notty is the sanest person she knows, or so the heroine (or anti-heroine, depending on how you might see it) of Zachary Wigon’s “Victorian Psycho” is quick to tell anyone who will listen. Or not, as star Maika Monroe delivers that damning and darkly funny insight into Winifred’s soul via a prim voiceover that seems to exist for her own benefit as much as the audience she seems to imagine.

That’s tangled enough, but there’s also this: Winifred doesn’t really know that many people, mostly because just about everyone she’s come across in her life is dead. Still, sane! Ish. Well, it’s not called “Victorian Psycho” for nothing. Adapted for the screen by novelist Virginia Feito from her own work, the book comes recommended alongside its predecessor, “Mrs. March,” for anyone drawn to entertainment with “psycho” in the title.Beloved scream queen Maika Monroe (“It Follows,” “The Guest,” “Longlegs”) may be switching sides here, offering a twist on who horror fans root for once the blood starts flowing. (Hint: it’s the psycho.)

When we first meet Winifred, the Victorian-era governess is bound for her next assignment to teach the children of Mr. and Mrs. Pounds (Jason Isaacs and a sneaky-great Ruth Wilson, respectively) at their sprawling Yorkshire estate. “This won’t be like the other times,” she tells herself, winking mightily at how all those other times turned out. Our Winifred sports dead eyes and a rictus grin she works so very hard to fix in place. It’s even better when she attempts to imitate those around her, all the better to approximate “being human.”

No question, there’s something very wrong with Winifred. But what? You’ll find out soon enough. Possessed of “a darkness” she cannot shake, Monroe’s Winifred practically vibrates with the need to hold things together. It does not always work for her, but it is nearly always funny, deliciously and dementedly so.

Wigon and Freito don’t balk at the “psycho” promise of their story, as Winifred happily whacks away (often literally) at everyone icky, from gardeners to nurses (Thomasin McKenzie) and, yes, at least one baby. Killing scenes are elegantly, amusingly cut by editors Dustin Chow and Lance Edmands and designed for maximum gasps. Blood flows, then spurts, then pools. Sometimes it gets on Winifred’s face or her collar. She moves on, grimly and happily.

Cinematographer Nico Aguilar brings a similar energy to his work, shooting in and through keyholes, swinging his camera like a clock pendulum, even briefly adapting the perspective of a swallowed (redacted, for spoilers) as it travels into Winifred’s belly. It’s playful and gross, wholly fitting the material. Mostly, it’s so dark and fucked up you can’t help but laugh. OH! Did they do that? Yes, yes they did.

Possessed by the notion that she’s meant to be a part of the Pounds family (who would want to be? these aristocrats are monstrous), Winifred continues to fray. Bloodlusty audiences will be charmed by her go-for-broke attitude (more like go-for-the-throat), plus a steady stream of visual cues as to her current state. Dreams and nightmares merge, she’s haunted by the Pounds’ family mascot of a wild boar (particularly in an ever-morphing tapestry), and she simply can’t stop sneaking into places to stare at people while they sleep.

Clocking in at a tight 90 minutes, some changes to the also-slim book are smart, while others dilute its darkest impulses. Gone is the countdown to a Christmas massacre, which frames the novel; hell, gone is the whole Christmas massacre. Freito’s script leads up to a different sort of celebration, doing away with many of the most colorful supporting characters in the process (Mrs. Fancey, we hardly knew ye!).

That choice isn’t disastrous, although the execution of the big! final! event! is oddly watered down. Here it’s imagined as a battle of Winifred’s wills… but, erm, what wills? The book and the film each offer enough reasons for why our heroine is the way she is, Wigon’s film oddly bisects her spirit by implying she’s not fully on board with all the evil she commits.

For fans of the novel and its wonderfully unrepentant star, this choice might rankle; it’s also odd that Freito would go this route with her own adaptation. “Well, that’s not my ‘Victorian Psycho’ — and she’s not. That’s such a shame because for much of the film, she’s even better. By tempering her impulses and inner monologue, the film ends with less of the giddy gore that makes the rest of it such a dark treat. Dare we say it? We would have loved for this one to go full, ahem, boar.

At the Stadium of Light on Sunday, Hume was starting to sit up when Rogers caught him. Chelsea were losing 1-0, and frustrated. Their £117million arrival from Villa apologised immediately, so we will believe him when he says it was an innocent collision rather than retribution.

Chelsea boss Xabi Alonso needs his side to develop an aggressive streak

Top 25 EFL Championship goalscorers since the 2004 rebrand

Goalscorers are often the difference between a Championship side merely competing and one capable of going up, which makes them among the most valuable commodities in the game. Football League World have looked at the top 25 since the inception of the division in 2004.

Of course, a reliable striker can turn draws into wins, settle tight games, and provide the consistency required over a gruelling 46-game season. However, the problem for Championship clubs is that when one of those forwards hits the ground running, they do not always get to enjoy the benefits for very long.

A prolific season in the second tier can put a player firmly on the radar of top flight clubs looking for a proven goalscorer, while the alternative is often just as painful for the Championship club when the striker scores enough goals to help fire them to promotion and then faces a new challenge in the top flight.

It is little surprise, then, that centre-forwards regularly command some of the biggest transfer fees and salaries in football. There have been plenty of prolific names in the Championship since the division was rebranded ahead of the 2004/05 season, with some strikers establishing themselves as second-tier specialists and others using it as a springboard towards the Premier League.

Some of the division's most exciting modern goalscorers are not yet eligible for this particular list because they simply have not spent long enough in the Championship. Middlesbrough's Will Lankshear, Wolverhampton Wanderers' Raul Jimenez, West Ham's Taty Castellanos, and Southampton's Cyle Larin are among the forwards currently capable of making their mark, but their Championship stories are only just beginning.

But who has actually scored the most goals since the Championship era began? Including play-off goals as well as goals scored during the regular league season? We have taken a look and ranked the top 25, here.

His Championship career took Martyn Waghorn to a number of clubs, including Leicester City, Ipswich Town, Derby County, and Coventry City. He was particularly effective during his time at Ipswich, where his goals made him one of the division's leading forwards. His 78 goals put him just ahead of Britt Assombalonga and David McGoldrick below him in the standings, but there are the likes of Kieffer Moore and Ben Brereton-Diaz closing in on his tally from the crop of currently active players.

Joel Piroe has established himself as one of the Championship's most reliable modern goalscorers, reaching 78 goals during spells with Swansea City, Leeds United, and now West Ham United. The Dutchman has been particularly prolific in the second tier and remains an active player capable of climbing this list further. Piroe will hope more goals can help the Hammers achieve their ambitions this season, with his 78 goals just ahead of Waghorn on a goal-per-game basis.

Across England's top two divisions, Chris Wood has enjoyed an outstanding career, with his Championship goals coming for the likes of Leicester, Leeds, Millwall, Ipswich, and Birmingham City. His 78 goals match Waghorn's tally as well as Piroe's, but his superior goals-per-minute ratio means he sits higher than both. Wood has not played a minute of Championship football since leaving Leeds for Burnley and has gone on to score 92 goals in the Premier League as well.

One of the Championship's most experienced forwards during the 2000s and early 2010s, Marlon King scored regularly for clubs including Watford, Coventry, and Birmingham. He eventually reached 80 goals in the division, underlining his consistency across a lengthy second-tier career which began prior to the Championship's rebranding. King's physical presence and eye for goal made him a valuable Championship striker for many a year.

Premier League pedigree did not prevent Kevin Phillips from continuing to score at an impressive rate in the Championship. After his famous goalscoring exploits with Sunderland, Phillips found the net regularly for clubs including West Bromwich Albion, Birmingham, Blackpool, Crystal Palace, and Leicester. His 82 Championship goals highlight that his finishing ability remained intact well beyond his top-flight peak and well into his 30s.

During spells with the likes of Watford, Middlesbrough, and Blackburn Rovers, Danny Graham became one of the Championship's most dependable goalscorers. His movement and finishing made him a consistent threat for years, while his goals helped earn him opportunities in the Premier League. Graham's 83 goals leave him inside the division's top 20 scorers since the 2004/05 rebrand.

Peter Whittingham was one of the defining Championship players of his generation, spending much of his career with Cardiff City after an earlier spell at Aston Villa. His left foot, set-piece ability, and eye for goal made him an influential attacking midfielder or winger rather than a conventional striker. His 84 goals are an impressive return for a player operating primarily from midfield. Although he was an attacking player, he is one of only two midfielders on this list.

Dexter Blackstock never scored more than 13 in a season and only hit double figures three times as well, but consistency rather than one spectacular scoring season was the hallmark of his Championship career. He represented clubs including QPR and Nottingham Forest, becoming a familiar figure across the division. Blackstock's 84 goals put him level with Whittingham, although his goals-per-minute record places him slightly better on this particular ranking.

After scoring a few for Newcastle United and then joining Fulham, Aleksandar Mitrovic wasted little time establishing himself as one of the Championship's most fearsome strikers. He scored for Newcastle before his remarkable Fulham spell produced a record-breaking individual season and helped secure promotion. His 85 goals are a remarkable total considering how much of his career has been spent outside the Championship. He has the best goal-per-minute ratio of any player on the list.

Over the 31 completed 20-team Premier League seasons, fewer than half of them were won by the team that conceded the fewest goals. Sometimes, as was often the case during the Pep Guardiola era, teams can be the best at both. Of the 15 times since 1995-96 that the best defence triumphed, they were also the most prolific attack on nine occasions.
