Lane Kiffin on Arch Manning, Manning family ties before LSU vs. Texas

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

LSU football hosts Texas on Saturday night. For Texas QB Arch Manning, it marks a return to Louisiana. Manning, who played high school football in New Orleans, was the top-ranked recruit in the class of 2023.

Manning comes from a football family. His grandfather, Archie Manning, is an Ole Miss and New Orleans Saints legend. LSU coach Lane Kiffin, who was Ole Miss' head coach from 2020-25, has plenty of connections to the Manning family, from coaching at Archie's alma mater to recruiting Arch out of high school.

Kiffin was asked about his relationship with the Manning family earlier this week.

"If you think about it, I left them twice. You've got to remember the Tennessee part, too, and Peyton, so you probably forgot about that," Kiffin joked.

Kiffin was referencing his exits from Tennessee, where Peyton Manning played, and Ole Miss, both of which came with controversy.

Kiffin said the family has "been awesome, the places I've been, and very supportive while we're there," and that he still talks with them once in a while through text.

after Kiffin left for LSU, Archie Manning recorded a Cameo video for an Ole Miss fan in which Manning called Kiffin a "narcissistic, jerk coach."

The video gained steam on social media, and per Kiffin, Manning apologized.

"He actually left me a message right after apologizing, but he got caught up in the moment after I left and thought it was just a Cameo to the people, and called me a few names," Kiffin said. "But that's okay. I've been called worse, and I'm sure I'll be called the same when we go back to Tennessee, just like Ole Miss."

Kiffin made Arch Manning a priority during his time at Ole Miss. In 2021, he grabbed a "We Want Arch" sign from the crowd before the Rebels' game against Texas A&M and traveled to Baton Rouge to watch Manning and Isidore Newman play Episcopal in the state playoffs.

"I went and saw him play one time … and got to see him just have fun with his teammates," Kiffin said.

Kiffin also saw the other side of Manning's recruitment.

"That's a lot to deal with, even when he was in high school, the expectations and pressure and everything around him," Kiffin said. "When he'd come visit, you kind of see more of the pressure side of what he'd deal with."

Manning chose Texas and Steve Sarkisian, Kiffin's longtime friend and former USC co-worker, over Ole Miss. Saturday will be the first time Kiffin coaches against Manning.

"I really enjoyed the time around Arch. Great kid," Kiffin said. "I think he's an awesome kid, and I hope he does really well, obviously, outside of Saturday."

Additionally, United have not reached out to U.S. Soccer yet to activate Pochettino's buyout clause, according to The Athletic. The exit clause is reportedly set at $10 million but only covers Pochettino; United would have to compensate U.S. Soccer further to hire Pochettino's coaching staff, who he has worked with for the entirety of his decades-long career.

For the USMNT, the timing is awkward. Though the next World Cup is still four years away, Pochettino signed a new four-year deal in August and is currently with the team at U.S. Soccer's national training center in the suburbs of Atlanta and led Wednesday's training session. The USMNT are preparing for the two-legged Concacaf Nations League quarterfinals against Haiti, the first of which will take place on Saturday at Miami's Nu Stadium while the second will be on Tuesday at Cincinnati's TQL Stadium.

Defender Antonee Robinson addressed the reports on Tuesday, admitting that this is not the first time the players have been put in this situation during Pochettino's tenure.

"That news kind of got sprung on us, like the rest of the world, I'd say," Robinson said. "Similar things happened in the past, happened at the World Cup where huge teams lose their manager and they start looking at possible candidates and for us to have a manager who's linked with those sorts of jobs means we're very lucky to work with a top-level manager but it never seems to leak into the work we do, whether it's the World Cup or whether it's going to be this camp."

Pochettino is the first USMNT head coach with genuine international appeal, U.S. Soccer more often than not picking from a domestic talent pool in the team's modern history. His tenure has reflected just that – he became the federation's highest-paid coach ever when he initially joined in 2024, the salary and the scope of his role only increasing in his recent contract extension. U.S. Soccer surprisingly plucked him out of the European club game, where he had spent the entirety of his coaching career beforehand, making a name for himself at Tottenham Hotspur before later stops at Paris Saint-Germain and Chelsea. Naturally, clubs of that ilk never lost interest and over the course of his time with the USMNT, he was linked to a handful of jobs. The most notable approach came from AC Milan, who were considering the move in the build-up to the World Cup but ultimately hired ex-Manchester United coach Ruben Amorim instead.

"Even when we first met a couple summers ago, there were lots of other people interested in having Mauricio and team join," U.S. Soccer CEO J.T. Batson said in May. "He had standing offers from other places to come. He wanted to be here. He's a big believer in what we're doing at U.S. Soccer. He's a big believer in soccer in America and he's a big believer in this men's team and so, throughout this entire process, when you have top-class talent, whether it's salespeople, whether it's marketing people or whether it's coaches, other organizations want them and throughout this, Mauricio and team have been incredibly transparent. They've been great partners and are, of course, excited about the summer … There's been a longer list of outreach than what has even been reported and so they've been great about that."

Pochettino has also been open about his desire to return to the Premier League during his stint with the USMNT, something the move to United would accomplish.

United are currently in the relegation zone with one win in 10 games, the least of any team in the Premier League this season.

Pochettino's track record with the USMNT is not exactly notable – he has 21 wins in 45 games, nearly all of them against teams below them in the FIFA rankings. They stumbled in a variety of ways when they attempted to punch above their weight, most notably in the World Cup round of 16 loss to Belgium that was tinged by the Folarin Balogun affair. His attempts to lead the USMNT to a statement-making run at a World Cup on home soil were mixed at best, since the team could not pass the last 16 hurdle they have been stuck at since 2010, but Pochettino still found a way to recalibrate the U.S. team in his two years in charge.

The specifics of the round of 16 elimination were a surprise, even if the exit was not. The U.S. team's player pool, talented as it was at the World Cup, was not considered amongst the world's elites so a genuinely deep run over the summer felt improbable. Pochettino, though, managed to get the best out of the group and had the USMNT playing in a way it rarely had previously – in an entertaining, high-intensity, attack-minded system. The style is a hallmark of Pochettino's coaching career but has been historically difficult to integrate at the international level, with teams usually gathering for 10 days at a time weeks or months apart, and has historically not been the USMNT's strategy. Pochettino's predecessor Gregg Berhalter, for example, was more reserved in his tactical approach and before him, the U.S. team were known more for grit than style.

Slowly but surely, Pochettino's approach worked with the generation of players Berhalter brought in. He correctly identified that the player pool's strengths were in attack, forwards Balogun and Christian Pulisic continuing to serve as central figures for the team. More notably, though, Pochettino found use for players Berhalter had previously cast aside – midfielder Malik Tillman became a key player, his versatility something Pochettino counted as an asset. Forward Ricardo Pepi also rose up the ranks in the build-up to the World Cup, his first after being one of the last omissions from Berhalter's 2022 team.

Pochettino's legacy might actually be the player pool shakeup he oversaw on the road to the World Cup, which felt rushed because of the short timeline but was ultimately useful. Defender Alex Freeman was the breakout of the Pochettino years, going from relative obscurity at Orlando City to a World Cup starter and goalscorer in less than 500 days. Midfielder Sebastian Berhalter, the son of Pochettino's predecessor, was not on the national team's radar until his first call-up in 2025 and looks like a national team fixture for years to come. The player pool expansion project will only get more exciting from here with the USMNT's up-and-comers, with some like Cavan Sullivan and Adri Mehmeti poised to be some of the best players in the history of the men's program.

The coach's departure will sting in some ways but his tenure ensured that the USMNT would be in a position of strength, which was hardly the case when he arrived. He inherited a team that had lost its way after a group stage exit in the Copa America and will leave behind a team with a clear enough roadmap to the 2030 World Cup. The Pochettino years prove that the next head coach should primarily be a strong tactical fit, no matter the candidate's global appeal, because he was right to identify that an attack-minded approach would suit the player pool. That's especially true with the up-and-comers since many of the brightest prospects are attackers.

There is no reason U.S. Soccer should not hire the best fit for the job but for perhaps the first time in the program's history, the USMNT's prospects might truly rest with the players at their disposal. Pochettino's successor will be chiefly tasked with getting the best out of them, much as he did with the U.S. team's core. As an exciting generation of talent finds their footing with the senior team, that's exactly how it should be.

Legally restrict heading a football in training, says MP

Guidance already exists to restrict heading in training in England and Scotland

An MP is calling for legal safeguards to limit repetitive heading in football training, amid growing concerns about the risk of brain injury.

Grahame Morris, the Labour MP for Easington, submitted a parliamentary motion on Tuesday urging the government to take action to reduce the number of times a player can head the ball in training.

Researchers have found footballers are three and a half times more likely to die from a neurodegenerative disease than the general population.

The Early Day Motion (EDM) describes the need to protect players' brain health as a "significant public health and player welfare issue".

"The aim of this EDM is to raise awareness of the growing body of evidence linking repeated head impacts, particularly from heading a football, to an increased risk of neurodegenerative disease," Morris told BBC Sport.

The motion encourages the government "to take a more active role in prevention, education and player protection" Morris added.

Morris's motion supports a national petition submitted in September by the charity Head Safe Football, and calls on the government to take a number of actions:

Impose legal limits on repetitive heading in football training

Establish a cross-departmental task-force involving medical experts, players representatives, campaign groups and football authorities to develop "enforceable heading safeguards"

Recognise chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) as a public health concern

Create a national action plan to protect players' brain health

Improve education and awareness of the risks associated with heading, including in schools and through the PE national curriculum

EDMs are seen as a means for MPs to express their opinion on a subject and to canvass support for their views. No matter how many MPs sign up to an EDM there is no guarantee that it will be debated in Parliament.

Football does already have restrictions around heading.

The Football Association has phased out deliberate heading in grassroots football matches for children aged 11 and under.

It has also issued guidance recommending limits on heading during training of no more than 10 headers per week at amateur level.

In the professional game in England, clubs have been advised to limit players to 10 "high force" headers a week in training.

Scotland has gone further, banning heading in training on the day before and after matches in the professional game.

However, campaigners such as Dr Judith Gates, founder of Head Safe Football, believe that voluntary guidance is not enough.

"What we are finding is that coaches and parents don't know of the guidance, or the ban. Or they do not know why it exists," she told BBC Sport.

"There needs to be greater clarity and greater specific restrictions in order to protect players of today and tomorrow."

Dr Gates, whose husband the former Middlesbrough player Bill died with CTE, a degenerative brain disease associated with repeated head impacts, accepts that enforcing a legal limit would be challenging.

The calls for greater protection came as Prime Minister Andy Burnham called, in an interview with the Telegraph,, external on English football to formally recognise the game's dementia crisis and establish an "industrial injuries" compensation scheme for former players.

In a statement to BBC Sport, he said: "I've spoken to families affected by dementia and brain injury and heard what they have been through.

"Their determination to get this issue taken seriously has been extraordinary, and they should never have had to fight so hard to be heard."

Burnham's intervention follows inquests into the deaths of former England World Cup winner Nobby Stiles and Scotland defender Gordon McQueen, which found repetitive heading had contributed to their deaths.

The Football Association has not financially contributed to the fund, but said it continued to invest in research and had introduced guidance across professional and grassroots football.

FAMU football QB Isaiah Knowles turns 'what-if' into lasting impact

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Isaiah Knowles' daily routine looks quite different nowadays.

Instead of suiting up as Florida A&M's starting quarterback, the graduate student is now holding a practice itinerary, teaching, and observing the offense from the sidelines during games.

"Man, I wish I was able to play," Knowles told the Tallahassee Democrat. "But given the cards have been dealt, I'm just happy that I got an opportunity to help in any way that I can."

After winning back-to-back Southern Intercollegiate Athletic Conference Offensive Player of the Year awards and an NCAA Division II Black College Football National Championship at Albany State, Knowles made the no-brainer decision to transfer to FAMU to follow head coach Quinn Fordham Gray Sr.

The Tampa native entered as the presumed starter for Gray's first season at FAMU following their successful tenure together in the Peach State.

But his time under center was cut short to two games.

On Sept. 6, Knowles broke his right throwing-hand thumb in the Rattlers' Orange Blossom Classic football game against South Carolina State. While attempting a pass, his hand hit off of a teammate's helmet.

The quarterback attempted to pop his thumb into place himself. FAMU trainers tried also to no avail.

Knowles ran to the sideline, and then X-rays later revealed the severity of the injury that ended his season and ultimately his college football career.

"They told me there were too many bones moving," Knowles recalled the injury, "So we covered it up, walked back to the X-rays, and that's when they told me what happened."

Knowles' injury was the first domino to fall in a disastrous FAMU season as the Rattlers (2-8, 1-5 in SWAC) are on track for their fewest wins since 2015.

"You know, I look at that kid every day and just think, 'What if?' But you can't harp on things that happened in the past that aren't in your favor," Gray said of Knowles. "Losing a guy like that was tremendous for us in terms of our development this season."

After two starts in relief of Knowles, backup Dustin Fletcher suffered a similar throwing-hand thumb injury and has been out since mid-September.

So, the Rattlers have turned to true freshman quarterback Armond Parker, who's started six games this year fresh out of high school.

Parker was an early FAMU enrollee, arriving on campus this past January for organized team activities and spring football training camp, wanting to learn everything from Knowles, who's spent four years in Gray's offensive system. They've "been attached at the hip" ever since, and Knowles has "been there every step of the way."

"It's a testament to his character," said FAMU running back Jamal Hailey. "Still waking up at five in the morning, showing up so he can be there so our freshman quarterback can be prepared week to week."

Knowles began watching games from the coach's box for an aerial view of the offense.

That stopped once Parker asked him to be on the sidelines to be a mentor, explain situations and playcalling rationale, and to bring a calming voice during the madness of a college football game.

"He keeps me confident, making sure I know what I'm doing, and takes me through progressions with my decision-making," Parker explained Knowles' impact. "With him being on the sideline, that's a big help for me — keeping my head up, keeping me confident."

Parker has experienced freshman highs and lows.

He's fifth in the Southwestern Athletic Conference with 1,790 passing yards and 14 touchdown throws in eight appearances out of 10 possible games.

However, the 18-year-old is still in his developmental phase, completing 56% of his passes and also tossing 11 interceptions, many of which turned into pick-sixes.

"Helping him battle through adversity, especially as a freshman quarterback," Knowles said of Parker. "See him grow each and every game; I think FAMU's going to be in good hands."

After his arm was in a sling for a few weeks after the injury, Knowles has recovered from surgery and began treatment last week. His range of motion and strength are beginning to return, and he should be able to throw in December.

Knowles is hoping to get an invite to February's sixth-annual HBCU Legacy Bowl, an all-star showcase game for draft-eligible players from Black Colleges. Knowles shared that the Legacy Bowl invited him the last two years, but he wasn't leaving college yet. FAMU coaches have been in constant contact with the Legacy Bowl, hoping Knowles gets the nod to make the trip to Atlanta.

Knowles will then perform before scouts at FAMU's Pro Day, typically held in March.

The National Football League, Canadian Football League, United Football League — the opportunities are endless for aspiring pro players.

Knowles is encouraged by scouts telling him that they have enough film on him from Albany State that the season-ending injury may not hinder his professional football dreams as much as one might expect.

"Lord willing, I go get an opportunity somewhere," Knowles said. "If not, I'll jump right into coaching."

Knowles time in college is coming to an end.

He began at the United States Naval Academy before transferring to Gray's inaugural Albany State recruiting class ahead of the 2023 season.

This Saturday, Knowles will be one of many Rattlers as part of the pregame Senior Day ceremony before FAMU battles Mississippi Valley State with a 3 p.m. kickoff on Ken Riley Field at Bragg Memorial Stadium. He has a bachelor's degree in exercise science from Albany State and is in the process of a second bachelor's in interdisciplinary studies from FAMU.

"He's going to be missed as he's missed now," Gray said. "That's a part of the game. You got guys that come through your program and are there for a certain period of time. And ultimately, they leave you to become those great citizens that you hope to develop them into, to become the great husbands and fathers that they are truly meant to be."

On Saturday, Knowles will be joined by his fiancée, Jayda, and parents Chuck and Linda. And even though his time donning the Orange and Green on the field was brief, his impact on the program in a lone year could be everlasting.

"It's going to be a surreal moment," Knowles said. "Definitely looking forward to walking there with my family, dapping up Coach Gray on the field, and getting loved on."

West Ham United are still suffering because of years of bad recruitment, Claret & Hugh has been told by a Hammers insider today. The source, who did not wish to be named, described the situation as a mess and laid the blame for the club’s current predicament firmly and squarely at muddled, poor recruitment.

Speaking to Claret & Hugh, the source said:

“It’s all down to recruitment and it’s been bad, a real mess.”

“It’s a hodge podge with players being brought from everywhere by loads of different people. We’re years behind and needed a complete reset, we had to start again and consider how we recruit.”

Honest words, and not ones that many West Ham fans would disagree with. The club has spent years changing managers, recruitment chiefs and transfer strategies, seemingly without allowing any one approach enough time to work. The result is a squad assembled under several different regimes, with little evidence of a consistent long-term plan.

It has always been Daniel Křetínský’s wish at West Ham to have a director of football in charge, and whilst we don’t have that exact job title at the club currently, Nils Koppen, as Director of Player Recruitment, is as good as a director of football in all but name.

What is clear is that the club are going to have to rebuild the recruitment structure brick by brick. And it’s worth pointing out that even as recently as the summer, there was still something of a mix-up, with a blend of recruitment ideas and Nuno Espírito Santo being allowed to bring in three players in Joël Piroe, Manor Solomon and Morato.

Kretinsky is a big believer in the structured recruitment model

That is hardly the definition of a joined-up recruitment strategy, although it’s also important to recognise that Koppen arrived with very little time to prepare for the summer window. The bigger issue is whether West Ham are finally prepared to commit to a consistent approach rather than changing direction whenever the manager changes.

We’re semi-lucky in one respect because we do have another transfer window coming up in January, giving Koppen and Scott Parker an opportunity to address some of the more obvious problems within the squad.

But this is definitely a time when West Ham have to commit to a transfer policy and allow it to play out and bear fruit over a number of seasons. Toying with an approach and then giving up, as we did with both Tim Steidten and Kyle Macaulay, is precisely the sort of short-term thinking that has contributed to the problem.

The club cannot keep appointing recruitment specialists, allowing them to start rebuilding the squad and then abandoning the entire project when results turn sour. At some point, West Ham have to decide what sort of players they want, how they’re going to identify them and who is ultimately responsible for bringing them in.

January offers an opportunity to begin correcting some of the mistakes, but the problems described by our source won’t be fixed in a single transfer window. If West Ham are genuinely years behind in their recruitment, the rebuild needs to be given the time and consistency that previous attempts have lacked.

Meet the youngest top-flight manager in Europe

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Jordan Hadaway has been in charge of Llandudno since August 2023; at 18, he was managing Caerwys and a 41-year-old captain; now 25, he has a UEFA A Licence and took the Seasiders back to the Cymru Premier with promotion in 2025/26

Football Journalist @DanLong__

As football has evolved, so have routes into football management.

Once almost exclusively the preserve of former professionals, a playing career is no longer a prerequisite.

That has opened the door to the younger generation. Thirteen managers and head coaches in England's top four divisions are under 40, with Brighton's Fabian Hurzeler the youngest at 33.

But the Cymru Premier - formerly known as the Welsh Premier League - is home to the youngest top-flight manager in Europe.

Last season, Jordan Hadaway guided Llandudno to promotion from the Cymru North. In July, he turned the grand old age of 25.

Hadaway already had the presence of mind to focus on coaching, rather than playing, by 14.

He completed his Level 1 Leaders Award two days after his 16th birthday and was working towards his UEFA B Licence by 18, when he was in charge at Caerwys and working with a 41-year-old captain.

Later, he coached for the Real Madrid Foundation and, in 2024, he passed his A Licence. He counts Nigel Adkins and Robert Earnshaw among those to have helped him along the way.

During his time at Caerwys, Hadaway wrote to Roy Hodgson, who was then in charge of Crystal Palace.

"He was the oldest manager in league football and I was the youngest manager in football, so my college tutor said to me 'Why don't you just write to him?'" he tells Sky Sports.

"I got an email from his personal assistant and he invited me in, but the day I was due to go down, Covid happened. By the time everything opened back up, he had been sacked!

"It was a bit of a nightmare because I really wanted to go and meet him, but I guess things happen for a reason, don't they?"

It is clear his strong work ethic has helped him progress.

"While I was studying at Liverpool Hope University, some days I'd be up at 2.30am or 3am going to work in the bakery for a family friend as they'd lost staff because of Covid," he says.

"I'd stay there until 9.30am, go to a lecture from 11am until 2pm and then I'd coach from maybe 4pm until 9pm. It was a full-on period in my life!"

And it has not slowed down, given he now balances his part-time managerial role with a full-time role as a teacher.

In December 2022, Hadaway left his role as assistant manager at Cefn Druids to become Llandudno U19s manager, as well as the club's head of youth development.

By the start of the 2023/24 season, he had been named interim first-team manager of a club whos squad and staff had been decimated after its artificial playing surface was condemned by the Welsh FA.

"Everyone had walked away and we were two weeks away from starting the season," he recalls.

"The chairman said to me: 'Will you just try and keep us in the league and make sure we have a club? I don't care if we get relegated if that's what has to happen as long as we've got a team and a club at the end of it.'

"Everyone around me, I'll be honest, told me don't take it. My mum even says to me today: 'I can't believe you took that job'.

"But I saw it as an opportunity to go and try and prove people wrong at a very big club in Wales that has an amazing facility and a good fanbase.

"I had the smallest budget in the league by a country mile. I had one player two weeks before the start of the season and no management team. I had to put all that together in two weeks. We were favourites to get relegated, but I never once thought we would.

"We managed to finish 12th out of 16 and we got to a cup final that year. It's quite incredible, really."

Two years later, he took the club one step further.

"My aim was always to be a Welsh Premier League manager by the time I was 27, 28. Did I ever think I was going to get there? No. It's amazing.

"I'm lucky I've had very good players and very good staff that have enabled me to push the club to that standard. It's not all about me, it's about everybody involved.

"I got quite emotional on that afternoon we got promoted. It was an achievement that probably will stick with me and be one of my best forever."

Asked if he has aged beyond his years with the way his career has progressed, Hadaway quips: "Yes - I think my hairline's shown that!

"A lot of people say I'm the oldest 25-year-old they've ever met. I think I am mature, but I'm mature because of the people I've grown up around. The people you spend the most time with have the biggest impact on you."

That maturity is reflected in his attitude towards what comes next.

Many would leap to the conclusion that, if he is doing well at 25 in Wales, a career over the border in England will be on the cards soon.

That may well come to be true, but Hadaway is in no immediate rush.

"Don't get me wrong, I would love to do it [work in the Premier League or EFL]," he adds.

"However, I love my job and I love supporting young people that need the support in school, and I think it would have to be a very good opportunity to take me away from what I'm doing currently. I'm also not naive.

"But I don't think I should get given a chance because I'm the youngest manager in top-flight football - I think I should be given a chance because I'm good enough.

"If we had this interview again in 10 years' time and I was still at Llandudno, I would be happy, because I'm not someone that chases the stars. I'm someone that likes to make sure I'm doing a good job where I'm at and make sure that when I leave, I've left it in a very good place and it's stable."

For someone who has accomplished so much so young, perhaps Hadaway's strength is in knowing there is no need to rush.

Not every successful career starts spectacularly well but Andoni Iraola's journey to Liverpool will take some beating. Seven years ago, he was sacked from his first job in management after a winless streak in the Cypriot top division with AEK Larnaca.

Iraola learned from the experience and rebuilt his career back in Spain with Mirandes before really impressing with Rayo Vallecano and Bournemouth to earn his opportunity at Anfield. Nevertheless, it must be curious for his old colleagues to witness the rise.

Were there any believers that day in January when Larnaca drew 2-2 at home to Ermis Aradippou, the team that would go on to finish bottom of the table? It extended the run of league games without a win to six and Iraola paid the price for that streak with his job.

Were there clues beyond the results that Iraola was destined for bigger things and merely the victim of a trigger-happy firing culture at the Cypriot club? Speaking to David Catala, who played under him at Larnaca, including in that final game, he did see promising signs.

"He already had very clear ideas of what he wanted," Catala tells Sky Sports. "You saw it and, for sure, all the players were speaking about how he was going to become a fantastic coach. But to reach this level? To say that he would be at Liverpool? That is a big claim."

Iraola had spent his entire playing career in Europe at Athletic Club prior to a brief sojourn stateside with New York City. His first foray into the managerial game with Larnaca put him outside of his comfort zone, even if there was a Spanish constituency in the club.

His initial impact was impressive. Three rounds of qualifying for the Europa League group stage were successfully negotiated with wins over Dundalk, Sturm Graz and Trencin. Just as significantly, the players at Larnaca readily embraced Iraola's illuminating ideas.

"To be in the Europa League for a club in Cyprus was amazing," explains Catala. "And the work that he did was good. These were the same ideas that he wants at Liverpool, actually, pressing high and wanting to be very aggressive with and without the ball."

In Cyprus at that time, this was particularly rare. "The rhythm of the game there was very slow. I was able to play there until I was 38 years old because that rhythm was not so high." Iraola had a different vision of the game, learned under Marcelo Bielsa in Bilbao.

Catala explains: "Everything was more explosive with him, more rhythm, more intensity. You saw how Marcelo did it with Athletic Club. I remember sometimes it was like that for us. Crazy things like following man-to-man all over the pitch. That was crazy as players."

He recognises now that it was a taste of the future of the game. "Football has gone in that direction. The way you have to press. It changed the mentality of the players." Even the game in that country. "He changed a little bit the rhythm of the league in Cyprus."

For a while, it worked. At the start of December, 10 games in, Larnaca were second in the table to Limassol, the only team to beat them during that period. Iraola's side were actually ahead of the six-time reigning champions and the eventual title winners APOEL.

But it all unravelled quickly. There was a 5-1 home defeat to Bayer Leverkusen in Europe that was sandwiched between a trio of away trips on the island that yielded no wins. A home defeat by the odd goal increased the pressure before the brief Christmas break.

A loss away to Omonia followed by that draw against strugglers Ermis sealed his fate. There were some formation tweaks but nothing major. "He did not change too much in terms of his thinking about the way of playing against different teams," recalls Catala.

"As soon as you lose games, especially against the lower teams, immediately the fans are speaking with the owner and the owner is going crazy. You cannot have the stability that maybe you need when you are a young coach. This is what happened to Andoni."

"Cyprus is not an easy place for anyone to work but it is a good place for a young coach to start their journey. He had the opportunity to coach a team in the Europa League, for example. Unfortunately, football everywhere is now very unstable for all coaches.

"It was a short period. But he would have learned many things from his experience in Cyprus about how to manage situations. It was his first job, you have to take something from that. For sure, he has improved a lot since then and he has developed himself."

Same record. Same point in the season. Two completely different outcomes.

Mendoza will make his first start Sunday against the Seahawks, and while it's tempting to think nine weeks on the bench may have helped prepare him for what's next, history suggests it could be a little more complicated than that.

Since 2000, 148 quarterbacks have started at least one game as NFL rookies. Just 22 made their first start between Weeks 10 and 13, and their collective debuts were not pretty.

They completed just 53.8% of their passes, averaged 5.8 yards per attempt and threw interceptions on 5.3% of their dropbacks – the worst marks of any group. Only five of those 22 won their debuts, and their interception rate was more than 1.6 times that of rookies who started in Week 1.

How rookie QBs performed in their first career start, 2000-2025

Of course, circumstances matter. Some quarterbacks sat because they weren't ready. Others played because the starter got hurt or was ineffective. But the takeaway remains: Sitting for half a season doesn't guarantee a smoother transition to the top of the depth chart.

Five teams had winning records when they turned to a rookie in Weeks 10-13. They were a combined 30-19 before the change and went 10-20 afterward. The 17 teams with losing records fared slightly better when they installed a rookie under center, improving their winning percentage from 28.9% to 33.7% – though they still lost twice as often as they won.

Only three of those 22 teams made the playoffs. The 2011 Texans turned to T.J. Yates after injuries to Matt Schaub and Matt Leinart. Peterman threw five interceptions in the first half of his debut, Taylor reclaimed the job at halftime, and Buffalo eventually made the playoffs with Taylor back under center.

Jackson was different. Baltimore initially needed him because Flacco was injured, but he played so well that they stuck with him even after Flacco was healthy. Obviously, that's a blueprint the Raiders would love to follow, but it's fair to say the organizational infrastructure isn't quite there yet.

And then there are the more immediate concerns for the Raiders rookie…

Mendoza's first three starts come against Seattle, Denver and Cleveland, which also happen to be three of the league's best defenses. The schedule gets less daunting after a Week 13 bye, though matchups against the Broncos and Chiefs make up two of the Raiders; final four games. So yeah.

There is a bigger issue: Mendoza could play well and still lose a bunch of games, and Vegas' first nine weeks help explain why.

The problems lie everywhere else. Las Vegas ranks dead last in yards per carry (2.9), 31st in rushing yards per game (71.3) and 30th in rushing success rate. The defense hasn't been much better, allowing 35.7 points per game over the last six weeks, five of them losses.

Which brings us back to Goff and Jackson.

The Raiders already have their quarterback of the future.
