Sean Connery Wasn’t the First James Bond: Meet Barry Nelson in the ‘Casino Royale’ You’ve Never Seen

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Ever since George Lazenby first replaced Sean Connery as James Bond in 1969’s “On Her Majesty’s Secret Service,” fans have argued over who makes the best 007 — and those arguments are likely to resume once Robert Pattinson takes the screen as the latest Bond in director Denis Villeneuve’s new iteration. Although Craig has his partisans, Connery tends to be the consensus choice for the best Bond, though I’ve argued here for the merits of 1970s and ’80s Roger Moore. All the Bonds have their virtues, but Connery benefited from being there first — for anyone who discovered the series in its initial heyday, Connery simply is James Bond. Yet Connery actually wasn’t the first actor to play Bond. That honor goes to journeyman MGM contract player Barry Nelson, who played Bond in a “Casino Royale” adaptation eight years before Connery made the character famous in “Dr. No.” Connery may have been the first 007 to grace the big screen, but Nelson established Bond on the small screen in an episode of the CBS anthology series “Climax!” opposite Peter Lorre in the Le Chiffre role that would later be inhabited by Orson Welles (in the goofy 1967 “Casino Royale”) and Mads Mikkelsen (in the 2006 adaptation that introduced Craig to the series).

The 1954 “Casino Royale” is a fascinating artifact that includes elements that would go on to characterize future James Bond movies, while also going in a few other very different directions. The key difference is in the scale, stripped down by necessity for television production. The hour-long “Climax!” episode is all interiors, taking place entirely in the hotel and casino of the title. It’s more a James Bond play than a movie, with Bond and Le Chiffre facing off in a series of verbal — and ultimately physical — jousts in confined spaces, and it’s surprisingly effective.

It’s also a very strange beginning for a franchise that would come to be known for its globetrotting locales and elaborate action set pieces. Not that this “Casino Royale” was officially part of the James Bond franchise as we know it. In fact, although there have been three adaptations of Ian Fleming’s first James Bond novel, only the third incarnation with Daniel Craig was from EON Productions, the company that controlled the character from “Dr. No” through the Craig era before selling the rights to Amazon.

The 1954 “Casino Royale” came about when producer Gregory Ratoff optioned the rights to Fleming’s novel in May of that year. The novel wasn’t yet a hit, and Fleming was sorely in need of cash, which led him to make one of the worst deals in movie-rights history when he sold the book outright to Ratoff for $6,000 after the TV episode aired. This left “Casino Royale” out of the deal when EON secured the screen rights to James Bond as a character, so Bond producers Albert Broccoli and Harry Saltzman could only sit back and grit their teeth when Ratoff turned “Casino Royale” into a comedy in 1967 and cast Peter Sellers, David Niven, Woody Allen and others as rotating Bonds.

EON ultimately made a deal to bring “Casino Royale” under their umbrella and used the novel to reboot the franchise with Craig, but in 1954 it served as the source for a much more modest affair. The biggest similarities between the 1954 “Climax!” episode and the Daniel Craig movie come in the time spent on extended gambling sequences, and the centering of a brutal torture scene. Given censorship restrictions of the time, Nelson’s torture in a bathtub is considerably less graphic than the mallet to the testicles inflicted on Craig, but it’s rough enough stuff for a 1950s TV production.

Certain aspects of Nelson’s characterization, like Bond’s mix of sexual swagger and icy indifference, would later be adopted by most future 007s, to varying degrees. The biggest difference between Nelson and future Bonds is a simple one: He’s American. Although Broccoli and Saltzman flirted with the idea of an American Bond throughout the series’ history (going so far as to cast James Brolin in “Octopussy” before Moore decided to return at the last minute), to date they’ve never actually pulled the trigger on casting outside the UK.

Nelson is probably best known to modern audiences as the affable hotel manager Stuart Ullman in Stanley Kubrick’s “The Shining,” but in the 1950s he was a sturdy character actor and occasional leading man who bounced around television after an initial sojourn as a studio contract player. As a young man, he appeared in several important MGM films, including “Shadow of the Thin Man,” “Bataan,” and “A Guy Named Joe” (the source material for Steven Spielberg’s “Always”). Later on, he popped up in “Airport” and had a successful Broadway career. He was nominated for a Tony acting opposite Liza Minnelli in Martin Scorsese’s 1977 production “The Act.”

In 1954, though, he was in the middle of a prolific and productive era as a TV stalwart, both as a series regular (on “The Hunter” and “My Favorite Husband”) and a frequent guest star. He doesn’t exhibit a ton of range as American “Combined Intelligence” agent “Jimmy” Bond, but that too is in keeping with the overall conception of the character, at least until Craig tried to bring a little more emotional depth to the role.

In Americanizing Bond, the “Climax!” edition of “Casino Royale” ends up making a number of related changes that are small but make the film feel just a bit off for fans of the EON productions. American Felix Leiter (later played by Jeffrey Wright) becomes Brit Clarence Leiter, for example. Although there’s none of the Bond gadgetry that would become famous later, there are hints of what’s to come in the form of props like a villain’s cane that hides a gun, and the opening credits for the “Climax!” series itself feature a gun barrel pointed at the viewer that predicts the iconic graphics of later Bond title sequences.

The 1954 “Casino Royale” is noteworthy for another reason: its composer, Jerry Goldsmith, who would ultimately score hundreds of major films, including “Planet of the Apes,” “Chinatown,” “Basic Instinct,” and most of Joe Dante’s work. Yet in some ways what’s fascinating about “Casino Royale” is how unremarkable it is — it’s a decent, solid piece of entertainment from an era when proliferating anthology shows provided similar programs every week.

If Fleming had his way, it would have only been the start for 007 on television; he wrote several outlines for episodes of a James Bond series before CBS canceled the project. Luckily, he repurposed most of what he wrote as short stories for his 1960 book “For Your Eyes Only,” and television’s loss was cinema’s gain. By 1962, James Bond belonged to the movies — and to Sean Connery.

Wolves’ £16m signing of Marshall Munetsi had threatened to become an expensive false start.

Having arrived from Reims in February 2025, the Zimbabwe international initially established himself under Vítor Pereira, making 12 Premier League starts during the second half of the campaign.

But his influence diminished following the Portuguese manager’s departure, with Rob Edwards ultimately sanctioning a loan move to Paris FC in January.

It was a decision that offered Munetsi the chance to rediscover his confidence and regular playing time.

Now back at Molineux, he has become an important fixture in Cesar Peixoto’s side.

Munetsi arrived at Wolves from Stade de Reims for a reported £16m in February 2025, becoming the most expensive Zimbabwean footballer in history in the process.

His physical presence and ability to contribute at both ends of the pitch made him an intriguing addition - and he wasted little time making an impression.

He scored his first goal for the club against Everton in March and became a regular under Pereira.

However, the managerial change that followed proved less favourable for the midfielder.

After Edwards took charge of Wolves, Munetsi found opportunities increasingly difficult to come by, making just one start before being allowed to return to France on loan on 16 January 2026.

For a player who had arrived with considerable expectations, it was a disappointing turn of events.

Yet, rather than allowing his confidence to suffer further on the fringes of the squad, the move to Paris offered a valuable opportunity to get his career moving again.

Wolves should try and sign Chelsea's Ryan Kavuma-McQueen, who is also on the radar at Sheffield United due to his performances at Portsmouth.

The change of scenery appeared to suit Munetsi.

He made 15 Ligue 1 appearances for Paris FC during the second half of the season, scoring four goals and reminding Wolves of the qualities that had prompted them to invest so heavily in him.

His performances also ensured that his future remained an interesting talking point heading into the summer.

With Wolves preparing for their first season outside the Premier League in nine years, Munetsi returned to Molineux facing another opportunity to establish himself. Under Peixoto, he has taken it.

The 30-year-old has featured in all 15 of Wolves' Championship fixtures so far, starting 14, and has become a consistent presence in midfield alongside Andre.

His experience and willingness to do the less glamorous work have proved valuable in a division where the demands are considerably different.

Munetsi's resurgence is part of a wider pattern at Molineux, with several players returning from loan spells.

Fer Lopez has arguably been the most prominent, emerging as Wolves' leading Championship goalscorer following his return from Celta Vigo.

Nasser Djiga and Tommy Doyle have also added further options following spells at Rangers and Birmingham City, respectively.

Munetsi believes the returning players have helped change the atmosphere around the club after a difficult period.

"With the difficulties we had last year, it was tough for everybody at the club, but us coming back, we came with a bit of freshness," he explained.

Wolves' decision to send him to France may initially have looked like an admission that the £16m investment had not worked out.

OHSAA public-private playoff split headed for statewide vote

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Suburban League, a conference of 16 schools in Northeast Ohio, has officially gotten the ball rolling on a public-private divide for high school playoffs.

The league submitted a petition for a membership vote on separate tournaments for public and private schools to the Ohio High School Athletic Association on Nov. 16. The petition received 152 signatures, well exceeding the required 75-signature threshold.

Now, the question of whether public and private schools should compete in separate postseason tournaments will go to a vote for OHSAA member institutions. The OHSAA's next referendum period begins in May 2027, with each high school voting on all ballot proposals from May 1 to May 15. There are 691 public-school and 121 private-school OHSAA members, each of whose principals cast a vote in the referendum period.

If approved in May, separate state tournaments would likely be implemented for the 2028-2029 school year. According to the National Federation of State High School Associations (NFHS), only Alabama, Maryland, Texas, and Virginia currently hold separate public and private high school state tournaments.

This isn't the first time the question of public vs. private athletics has been brought to the OHSAA's members for a vote. Petition attempts in 1978 and 1993 both failed, but renewed discussion over disproportionate private-school titles led to the development of the OHSAA's competitive balance plan in 2014.

OHSAA member schools adopted competitive balance measures beginning in 2017, placing schools in appropriate postseason divisions for baseball, basketball, football, soccer, softball, and volleyball.

The competitive balance formula looks at a school's enrollment and assigns each rostered player a tier based on considerations like which school district the student lives in or whether they come from an assigned feeder school. The OHSAA then adjusts the enrollment count accordingly, with more local players lowering the enrollment number and more out-of-district players raising it. That adjusted enrollment figure then determines the school's tournament division.

Competitive balance has had some success at bringing state titles closer to a proportionate public-private split. In football, for example, private schools won over 50% of the state championships in the 16 years before the plan's implementation. Since 2017, private schools have won just 20% of the football titles.

While nearly every sport has seen more public schools win championships since competitive balance was adopted, some sports still seem to favor private schools. Girls volleyball has hardly been affected as private schools have won over 64% of state titles since 2017, with three Greater Cincinnati-based private schools winning this year alone. Both boys soccer (48%) and girls soccer (42%) also see high rates of private schools titles.

In OHSAA fall 2026 state finals so far, public schools won four of seven titles in volleyball, four of five titles in boys soccer and three of five titles in girls soccer.

My 3:45 goal didn’t go to plan, but the NYC Marathon still delivered a win.

I figured I’d never get in, so why not? I played soccer growing up, and when the world shut down during COVID-19, running became the easiest way to get out of the house and see my friends. That’s where my running journey began.

My NYC Marathon training also happened during my senior year of college, which brought its own challenges. While I was excited to run, I also didn’t want to miss out on any of the socializing that happened that year. I did my best to make it to every party, every club soccer game, and anything in between while running as much as possible. I was scared that running was going to infringe on my everyday life and I wouldn’t have time to keep having fun. Despite all of that, I had caught the marathon bug and after finishing the New York City Marathon in 2022, I went on to run the Paris and Chicago Marathons in 2024.

While training for those first few marathons, I didn’t have any real plan, except to not let it interfere with my social calendar. My strategy consisted of building up mileage in order to reach 20 miles about four weeks out from race day. In between, I pieced together training information from influencers on Instagram and Google searches (this was pre-ChatGPT!). Now that I’m a Runner’s World+ member, I have all those resources right at my fingertips.

Although I caught the marathon bug almost instantly, I also felt defeated after New York. I knew I could have done better. Between New York City in 2022 and Paris in 2024, I never ran more than 10 miles at a time, but I was running enough that I knew I could do it again if I really wanted. I followed a similar approach for Chicago in 2024: Build up to one long run about a month out, then fill the rest of the weeks with shorter runs and one medium-length run. It wasn’t much of a plan, but it worked for me. My times kept improving, and I broke four hours for the first time in Chicago.

Looking back, I realize that my no-plan approach was partly a way to protect myself. If I never said my goal out loud, I couldn’t feel disappointed if I missed it. Breaking four hours in Chicago changed that. For the first time, I wanted to see what I could do with a real plan.

I was now living in the city I had expected to call home ever since I left college, so I really wanted this race to feel meaningful. For the first time in my marathon career, I downloaded a Runner’s World training plan, the 16-week Break 3:45 plan to be exact, printed it out, and started tracking all of my runs. I wanted to beat my time in Chicago and get as close to 3 hours and 45 minutes as possible.

In the beginning, this felt super daunting. I had never done an interval run in my life nor had I ever intentionally sought out hills to make my runs feel harder than they already did. Nonetheless, I found my old Garmin Forerunner 55 and learned how to upload intervals to run up and down the West Side Highway. When the plan called for hills, I made my way back to the Queensboro Bridge for the first time since 2022, ready to face it with excitement instead of fear. Even while pretending to be excited, the incline is no joke!

For a while, I really felt like I had gotten the hang of things. I actually started to recognize what different paces felt like during the intervals, instead of sprinting and pulling back and looking at my watch while I ran. And, I could now confidently run over many of the bridges in New York City, notably the Williamsburg and Manhattan, even though they aren’t even part of the marathon course. I wasn’t following the plan exactly, but I was able to reorganize the days of the week to hit all the workouts.

Near the end of August (roughly week eight of the plan), it became hot all the time, I was constantly traveling for weekend trips, and I had started to lose the excitement of running. So while I was making sure to hit all of the long runs, the interval training fell off completely. I was a little disappointed in myself because the interval training runs were workouts that made me feel like I was getting stronger each week. I had never done this type of workout before, so I could tell I was getting better each time.

To find the joy of running again, I really started to lean on my friends. Every week, I’d send out a few texts to see who was available and wanted to go for a run with me. I ran the loop in Central Park almost every week and met up with my friends in Brooklyn to run every Friday morning. I started to explore parts of the city I had never been to before, which helped ground me in my training and remember that running is supposed to be fun and is an exciting way to explore the city.

So much of marathon running is mental, so knowing that I could run 20 miles (and have run 26.2 before), was enough for me. So, while I started with a plan, I started reverting back to my tried-and-true method—the just make sure you run the long runs plan.

Outside of the training plan itself, I leaned on Runner’s World a lot. I didn’t have access to this in the past so I didn’t feel confident about planning for prerun meals or even how to take gels during the race. I also found that being able to read runner stories online made me feel more connected to the community, which helped me mentally with my training.

Plan or no plan, I’m proud of myself! My official time was 3:57:39, so while I did not achieve my goal of breaking 3:45, I did run a sub-4 race, and following the Runner’s World plan helped me get there. The last time I ran this race, I was a senior in college and thought I had it all figured out. I was going to move to New York City, get a real adult job, and start the life I had been imagining. Similar to my experience following the Runner’s World plan, this is not how everything went. Instead, I moved to a different city after graduating, eventually found my way back to New York, and my life is so much more full than I ever expected because of it. Maybe things aren’t always supposed to go to plan, and that’s a good thing!

As a reminder, players must receive votes on at least 75% of the ballots cast to be inducted into the Hall of Fame with the 2027 class. Those who get at least 5% of the vote can remain on the ballot the following year, and so on, for a maximum of 10 voting cycles. Players who are on the ballot for 10 years without getting 75% of the vote, in addition to players not getting 5% of the vote, fall off the ballot in ensuing years, though that doesn't mean the doors to the Hall are permanently shut.

The results will be announced on Jan. 19, with the induction set for Cooperstown over the summer.

Here's everything else you need to know about the Baseball Hall of Fame this offseason.

As alluded to earlier, there are two different votes each offseason. The BBWAA vote is for former players who have been retired from Major League Baseball for at least five years. It's the most common method in mainstream Hall of Fame ballot discussions. When you hear stuff like "first-ballot Hall of Famer," it means via the BBWAA vote.

Players who don't make it via the BBWAA vote can get extra chances via committees. That's how, for example, Jeff Kent made it last year. Formerly called the Veterans Committee, the Hall of Fame now uses Era Committees, which are divided into two categories: contemporary baseball (1980 to present) and classic (prior to 1980). The contemporary ballot is then split into two, one for players and one for non-players like umpires, executives, pioneers and managers.

Sure enough, this time around, it is a non-player Contemporary Baseball Committee, which looks at "managers, umpires and executives whose most significant career impact was realized since 1980."

NBC Ignites The Return Of Its Unfairly Cancelled 5-Season Gritty Crime Series

Attending events like SCAD TVfest and San Diego Comic-Con over the course of her career, she has been immersed in the world of network, streaming, and cable TV.

Fall premiere season is usually when networks deliver new shows and ramp up existing series before the winter hiatus. NBC, however, is revisiting one of its most shocking recent cancellations. The network may still air several crime dramas, including Chicago P.D. and Law & Order, but one show's abrupt ending changed the balance of an entire franchise. A recent milestone episode proved how much NBC's fall 2026 schedule was shaken up by the decision, while also igniting a return from cancellation.

Though both Chicago P.D. and Law & Order have reached double-digit seasons, Law & Order: Special Victims Unit recently celebrated a massive milestone. SVU's 600th episode brought back Christopher Meloni for a case related to one of Benson and Stabler's earliest '90s-era investigations. It was Meloni's first non-cameo appearance since 2023 and the first time he reprised his longtime role since Law & Order: Organized Crime ended after 5 seasons.

Organized Crime's cancellation was arguably inevitable after NBC cut it from its Law & Order Thursday night block. The fifth season of Meloni's show moved to Peacock. While that opened the show up to the kinds of swearing and violence that can't happen on network TV, it also split the series from SVU. One of Organized Crime's original selling points was the overlap between Mariska Hargitay's Olivia Benson and Meloni's Stabler. Both shows suffered after the move to Peacock. Now, thankfully, NBC has set up a well-deserved return for the spinoff, though Meloni's recent role on SVU wasn't actually the catalyst.

After the 600th episode of SVU officially sealed Stabler's fate by sending him far away from the NYPD (and Benson), NBC ruled out the possibility that his inclusion was meant to set up Meloni's full-time franchise return. Instead, the next episode's preview provided hope that SVU (and perhaps even Law & Order) can revisit Organized Crime from time to time. OC series regular Rick Gonzalez will return as Detective Bobby Reyes in SVU's fall finale.

The preview doesn't give much away about what brings Bobby back. Gonzalez may not have much more of a role than NBA superstar Jalen Brunson did in his Law & Order: SVU cameo. Still, it will be Gonzalez's first return to the franchise since Organized Crime's 2025 finale, and his first appearance on Special Victims Unit since 2023. Bobby getting a moment in SVU's fall finale certainly doesn't confirm Organized Crime's full-fledged return, but it's a good sign. Clearly, the franchise isn't abandoning all the characters, even when Stabler isn't available.

NBC's logline for the episode, "Untouchable," sheds more light on the case that brings Bobby back:

When a nightclub dancer is assaulted by a VIP, the squad’s investigation is thwarted by a powerful network determined to protect one of its own. As the case heads to trial, Benson and Carisi are divided on how to handle a witness’ testimony.

The synopsis indicates the fall finale in December will largely focus on the Special Victims Unit squad. Still, SVU is also turning it into an event by bringing in an Organized Crime alum. Ideally, Bobby will appear long enough to update viewers on other OC characters whose stories ended when Peacock canceled it.

Before SVU's 600th episode, an Organized Crime character's appearance would likely have signaled that Stabler would return sooner rather than later. Perhaps that would have happened if Organized Crime hadn't been canceled, but SVU didn't bring Christopher Meloni as a guest star to set him up for future episodes. In fact, the way Stabler's journey ended in the episode felt more like a permanent goodbye than another temporary split between the former partners.

Stabler wasn't in a good emotional place when Benson chased him down to collaborate on a case. He hadn't returned her calls for the past 18 months, and he was still on bereavement leave after his brother died in Organized Crime's series finale. The duo finally delivering justice on a case they'd botched three decades ago didn't spark a desire to return to the NYPD. Instead, he decided to put more distance between him and Benson since moving to Europe.

Olivia Benson has had seven love interests in Law & Order: Special Victims Unit, but only one of them can be considered the best of the bunch.

Rather than returning as a detective, Stabler decided to take a road trip to Arizona to visit his daughter and grandchildren. He invited Benson (and her son) to come along, but she obviously declined. The hot-and-cold nature of their relationship since his return to the U.S. didn't exactly suggest they'd have a peaceful time together, and SVU's lead workaholic character couldn't just leave the show for an extended vacation.

Stabler, however, is taking an indefinite break, and Meloni has moved on to a brand-new drama on Hulu called The Land. SVU technically didn't rule out a future role, since Stabler was still alive by the end of the 600th episode; he's just not coming back anytime soon, if at all. For now, fans can at least look forward to Law & Order: Special Victims Unit revisiting Law & Order: Organized Crime with Rick Gonzalez's return in SVU's fall finale, airing on Thursday, December 3 at 9 p.m. ET on NBC.
