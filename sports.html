IMAX is about to make history in 2027 with the biggest U.S. sporting event of the year.

IMAX is a premium theatrical format that has shown countless movies and events over the years. Then, two months later, IMAX will host the first-ever theatrical screening of the Super Bowl on Sunday, February 14, 2027. Deadline reports that Disney and the National Football League (NFL) have secured this history-making deal so that football fans can watch Super Bowl LXI on the big screen.

The sporting event rotates which network airs the game every year. ABC is the broadcasting host for 2027, and parent company Disney has decided to make the Super Bowl as accessible as possible.

Aside from airing on ABC, it will also be available on ESPN, Disney+ and Univision, and alternate telecasts on ESPN2 and Disney Channel. Now it's been confirmed that IMAX will be yet another way for viewers to tune in to the game as the winning teams from the American Football Conference (AFC) and National Football Conference (NFC) face off.

Ken Potrock, the president of Major Event Integration at Disney, said in a statement that the Super Bowl is already "one of the great shared experiences in sports," and this new partnership with IMAX "takes it to a historic new scale."

IMAX CEO Rich Gelfond added that the Super Bowl's "one-of-a-kind global cultural" experience makes it the perfect event to screen in theaters.

Ken Potrock: “Watching the Super Bowl with your fellow fans is one of the great shared experiences in sports, and this first-of-its-kind collaboration with the NFL and IMAX takes it to a historic new scale.”

Rich Gelfond: “The Super Bowl is a one-of-a-kind global cultural event imbued with spectacle, sound, drama, and heroes — all the makings of a great IMAX experience.”

From Dune: Part Three in December to the Super Bowl in February, IMAX is gearing up for a busy season in the coming months. Now, the Badgers take on their rivals, the Minnesota Gophers, in the season finale on Black Friday, looking to reclaim the Axe Trophy for the first time in three years.

Last week, ahead of the Purdue game, in our weekly Reacts poll, I had asked whether eight wins would constitute a successful season for Luke Fickell and the Badgers. At the time, Wisconsin was 6-4 coming off the Maryland loss that felt like another two steps back following the win over Rutgers.

The inability to stack consistent performances, plus the way the Badgers were losing, felt like Wisconsin was stuck in a cycle as they've been over the past few seasons.

So, even with potential wins over Purdue and rival Minnesota, a majority of fans felt like the season wasn't a success with eight wins.

Now, when you back to the preseason outlook of the team, I had said eight wins was my minimum benchmark for this Badgers team given the amount of investment into the program and also the easier schedule Wisconsin was facing. There were games against Western Illinois, Eastern Michigan, Michigan State, Rutgers, Maryland, and Purdue, which felt like very winnable games.

Then, there was also the Minnesota and Iowa rivalry games, and I felt Wisconsin had to win at least one. Finally, out of the USC, UCLA, Notre Dame, and Penn State bunch, I thought the Badgers needed to win one of those as well.

Well, Wisconsin won five of the very winnable games, losing to Maryland. They lost to Iowa, but are 7.5-point favorites over Minnesota, according to FanDuel. And they beat USC and Penn State, while playing UCLA, a potential College Football Playoff team, close.

Still, though, heading into the final game of the season, there are key questions about the future of this program. It's not just that the Badgers are losing games, it's how they're losing those games.

Wisconsin lost by 30 to Iowa, which has been a common theme in the rivalry game. They completely floundered against Maryland after leading by a touchdown late. And the problems have been the same: poor passing game, run blocking struggles, mental mistakes, and coaching decisions.

But the Badgers did make progress this year, winning games that they haven't done in the past. Now, the Penn State win has been diminished, given that the Nittany Lions have looked really poor in Big Ten play, including a loss to Rutgers at home this weekend. But the USC win was quite impressive, given the way Wisconsin did it with an elite defensive performance.

A win over Minnesota, to me, would bring back some pride in the program and meet the benchmark for what I considered a quality season to start the year. But the Badgers aren't supposed to be a middling program.

They're one that now has the resources to exceed expectations, especially when facing a more viable path to the College Football Playoff next season with another doable schedule and the new 24-team rule. The expectations for the program has to continue to grow, and that's where the decisions have to come this offseason.

Wisconsin has taken a step forward this year. But they need to take a bigger one to reach the heights that they want to get to. Hypothetically, if they beat Maryland, Wisconsin is sitting with a 8-3 record, including 6-2, in Big Ten play with losses to Notre Dame (a top five team), UCLA (a top 10 team), and Iowa (a top 15 team). That's quite a strong resume and probably has Wisconsin in or near the Top 25 with the USC win.

They'd also still be in the Big Ten Championship race as one of four two-loss Big Ten teams.

Now, Wisconsin got back on track with the win over Purdue, and they did so in resounding fashion. There's still another game to play, plus a bowl game, so the Badgers can finish the season strong with an eye on 2027.

Carles Puyol Named His Toughest Opponent Out Of Rooney, Henry & Drogba

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Carles Puyol is widely considered one of football's finest defenders, having played a pivotal part in Barcelona and Spain's successful eras throughout the 2000s and 2010s, leading both sides to an impressive collection of major trophies.

A six-time UEFA Team of the Year nominee, Puyol secured six La Liga championships during his career at the Nou Camp, alongside three Champions League crowns, two Spanish Cups and several other domestic and international accolades.

On the international stage, he was a key figure in Spain's defence during their back-to-back tournament successes, helping La Roja claim Euro 2008 and the 2010 World Cup. He featured in every game of the latter competition and netted a crucial semi-final winner against Germany.

Although he previously identified players such as Arjen Robben and Sergio Aguero as some of the toughest rivals of his career, Puyol was recently challenged to select the most difficult opponent from three former Premier League greats: Thierry Henry, Didier Drogba and Wayne Rooney.

Puyol, speaking with former Manchester United centre-back Rio Ferdinand, was asked to identify the toughest challenge between Henry, Drogba and Rooney. He singled out the first two, highlighting Henry's display in the 2006 Champions League final:

“Henry and Drogba. Henry in the final in 2006 was amazing, and Drogba, many times, fighting.”

Henry and Puyol first crossed paths in the 2006 Champions League final, which saw Barcelona come from behind to secure a 2-1 victory thanks to goals from Samuel Eto’o and Juliano Belletti.

The pair also went head-to-head at the 2006 World Cup in Germany, where France eliminated Spain with a 3-1 win in the Round of 16. Franck Ribery, Patrick Vieira and Zinedine Zidane were all on target for Les Bleus.

In 2007, Henry joined Puyol at Barcelona, arriving for a reported transfer fee of £16m after spending eight successful years with Arsenal, where he established himself as the club's record goalscorer with 228 strikes and one of the greatest strikers in football history.

Henry spent the next three seasons alongside Puyol at the Nou Camp, playing an important role in Barcelona's remarkable 2009 campaign. The Catalan side claimed six trophies that year, completing a historic treble as part of an unprecedented sextuple.

The former France international recorded 121 appearances for Barcelona, registering 49 goals and 27 assists, before making the move to New York Red Bulls in July 2010.

The Barcelona captain won just the opening three encounters against Drogba, while their final meeting came in the 2011/12 Champions League semi-finals, when Chelsea progressed with a 3-2 aggregate triumph.

When Huge Is Not Big Enough

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted November 23, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

Fans of the X-Men film franchise, which premiered in the long-ago year of 2000, were treated to the original version of Hugh Jackman’s now-longstanding portrayal of the character Wolverine. Appearing frequently shirtless in the movie, at the time Jackman seemed muscular enough to be super-heroic. He has played Wolverine nine more times in the decades since then, but rather than growing older and more relaxed, Jackman’s physique has seemed to develop in the opposite direction. Bigger, leaner, and more defined, Jackman could stand for an increasingly familiar male ideal.

Like him, many men spend hours in the gym trying to bulk up. They may carefully monitor their diets, timing their protein intake for maximum effect. They may indeed grow larger and more muscular; family, friends, and partners will notice. They may share images of weight-lifting transformations on social media, or even consider anabolic steroids or testosterone injections. Despite it all, some of these men will continue to see themselves as too small, too ill-defined, and not muscular enough.

This condition is known as muscle dysmorphia, a form of body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) characterized by an unrelenting preoccupation with the idea that one is not muscular or lean enough. Even when a man is objectively well built, he may perceive himself as physically inadequate. It’s not common, but it’s real: a 2025 study of nearly fifteen hundred boys and men between the ages of 15 and 35 (Ganson et al., 2025a) concluded that the disorder likely existed in approximately 2.8% of those surveyed — across demographic groups, not only among weightlifters or athletes. (Indeed, a 2024 study in the Journal of Eating Disorders found higher muscle dysmorphia symptom scores among men who wanted to increase their body weight than among those who wanted to reduce it.)

As I’ve suggested, while physical fitness is usually worth aspiring to, there’s a significant difference between ordinary, healthy fitness and muscle dysmorphia. Even when a man is objectively strong, “cut,” and full of muscles, he may look at himself in the mirror and see only weakness. This preoccupation can then lead to repeated, muscle-seeking behavior, such as checking himself in the mirror, researching new exercise routines, measuring body parts, or seeking new nutritional information. It can also involve excessive weightlifting, spending too much time at the gym, or taking on rigid dietary pursuits that can even become unhealthy or dangerous.

But as all that exercise produces results, it never seems like enough: getting bigger just feeds back into the perception that they still aren’t big enough. And when men with the disorder can’t soothe their anxiety by working out, they can feel real emotional distress, as well. The central characteristic of muscle dysmorphia is being absorbed by the perception that one is not muscular enough; it describes men whose daily routine and sense of self are dependent on the pursuit of muscularity, not those who just don’t like missing a workout (Cooper et al., 2020).

These days, muscle dysmorphia is particularly associated with male body-image concerns, although women can experience it as well. As we’ve seen in Hugh Jackman’s transformation, men in popular culture are expected to be physically strong and imposing, with muscles sharply defined and standing out through their clothing. (So many superheroes!) Women face body image concerns as well, of course, but the pressure on women often focuses on losing weight and appearing thin rather than strong. For men, being muscular can stand in for physical attractiveness, competence, or even dominance.

It’s easy to find examples of these masculine norms on social media nowadays; just as in the past, we’ve been treated to male movie stars whose bodies are designed to show off a great deal of time in the gym; adherence to these norms has been associated with the symptoms of muscle dysmorphia (Grunewald et al., 2022). Research by Ganson and colleagues (2025b) supported this, finding that greater exposure to muscle-oriented social media content was associated with higher levels of probable muscle dysmorphia.

Filters, strategic lighting, and increasingly sophisticated photo-editing tools can offer further enhancement to these already extreme physiques, and sometimes the men in these social-media posts use performance-enhancing drugs, which pushes them still further out of the realm of the realistic. Overall, the fitness content on social media can complicate the distinction between healthy exercise and unhealthy physical enhancement. Psychological vulnerabilities may also play a role: in a 2024 study of men attending gyms, Kandemir et al. found that perfectionism helped explain the relationship between vulnerable narcissism and the risk of muscle dysmorphia.

Muscle dysmorphia can be harmful in a number of ways. Training too hard, even through pain, can lead to injuries — some of which can be lasting. Restricting one’s diet can also have serious consequences for one’s health, such as hormonal disruption (meaning reduced production of testosterone, among other things), reduced bone density, and an increase in the risk of fractures or hypotension (Mountjoy et al., 2023).

The obsession with being muscular can take its toll on one’s social life, too, when men seem to prioritize their nutrition or their workout schedules over their partners’ needs. Daily work obligations can be affected when muscle dysmorphia affects one’s ability to concentrate (Cooper et al., 2020).

And of course there are the serious emotional consequences of feeling ashamed of oneself — like depression, anxiety, or self-hatred — because of body-image-related beliefs. Even when a man’s friends seem to admire their discipline or dedication, or comment in positive ways about the change in his physical condition, these would-be compliments can have a paradoxically damaging effect if they spur a man to try even harder to achieve his unrealistic muscular aspirations, or if they make his beliefs seem more reasonable than they are.

As I’ve noted before, men may be comparatively less likely to seek help for emotion-related problems than women; when the topic is body-image concerns, they may be less likely still. Some may not even believe they have a problem, as the effort to become (and look) strong seems to be valued by their peers and by the culture overall (Brown et al., 2023).

In therapy, however, it is possible to draw a subtle but meaningful distinction between a realistic, healthy body image and a source of compulsive striving. Irrational or distorted cognitions may be examined; other sources of self-worth can be identified. In other words, the effort to become “big” may also have an idiosyncratic meaning to each individual man, and this meaning stands a chance of being explored and identified in the context of the therapeutic relationship. (Medical intervention may become necessary as well, in cases involving potentially harmful medications or nutritional restriction.)

In the end, muscle dysmorphia creates a slippery slope: trying to be big seems to promise better self-esteem, but the effort only results in further, more extreme beliefs about how big one needs to be to achieve those benefits. The only real way out is to grow in a different direction — to learn that feeling good means accepting yourself as you are, and trying to live in a healthy way without wondering what you still need to change.

The 8 Best Miniseries of the Streaming Era, Ranked

We are living in the streaming era of television. Though Netflix launched streaming video-on-demand as far back as 2007, they didn't produce their first original series, Lilyhammer, until 2012; and it wouldn't be until 2013, with the release of shows like House of Cards and Organge Is the New Black, that streaming would truly gain cultural hegemony over the entire televisual medium. Gone was the era of network television, and in came the era of streaming.

Miniseries in particular find their roots in the British serials of the 1950s, but it was when American television adapted the format in the 1970s that it really became a cultural phenomenon. From classics like Roots to 21st-century masterpieces like Band of Brothers, the era of network TV delivered many a great miniseries. The streaming era of television has definitely not fallen behind, though, giving us in the likes of Adolescence and xxx some of the greatest miniseries of all time. Only streaming originals can qualify for this list.

After actor Stephen Graham read a news article about a young boy stabbing a girl to death, he proposed telling a story inspired by this wave of violent attacks by young boys against girls in Great Britain through the miniseries format. He and Jack Thorne then co-created Adolescence, a scathing critique of incel culture, male rage, toxic masculinity, and the "manosphere." It's an awfully timely yet sensitive topic, but the series treats it with precisely the care it deserved.

The result is one of the best miniseries of the last five years, composed of four episodes all shot in a single take, which lends the series an air of urgency and raw authenticity that elevates both its emotional and intellectual impacts tremendously. Bolstered by its exceptional cast (including Graham himself and a revelatory Emmy-winning turn by young Owen Cooper) and its excellent writing, it's one of the most important crime drama series of the 2020s.

Beth Macy's non-fiction masterpiece Dopesick: Dealers, Doctors, and the Drug Company that Addicted America deals with the origin and evolution of the opioid epidemic that began in the United States around 1996 with the release of OxyContin. In 2021, Hulu adapted that story in Dopesick, which just so happens to be one of the most perfect miniseries of the last 25 years. It's every bit as ambitious, expansive, and insightful as the book it's based on.

It's such a devastating investigative drama that it's most definitely not an easy watch, but boy, is it an important one. It's a 360-degree systemic portrait of absolutely every link in the chain of the opioid crisis, from the Purdue Pharma boardroom to the victims of the crisis. Elevated by its incredible cast (including the likes of Michael Keaton and Peter Sarsgaard) and its non-linear structure, it's about as urgent as streaming-era miniseries get.

Far more than just one of the best movies of the last 100 years, Francis Ford Coppola's The Godfather is one of those artistic masterpieces so great and so important that they straight-up transcend their medium. What really makes it such a special movie, however, is that the story behind it is every bit as interesting and compelling as the film itself; and that story is the subject of Paramount+'s The Offer.

Making-of dramas can either be so full of propulsive energy that they're irresistibly entertaining, or so cold in their approach that they feel like Wikipedia pages come to life. The Offer definitely belongs to the former group. The larger-than-life performances, far from just being impressions, are truly phenomenal; the writing turns Hollywood politics into a genuinely momentous thriller; and the show's approach to the story should be enough to make any cinephile melt in enjoyment.

Western television hadn't really been a particularly prominent part of the mainstream for several years now, but Taylor Sheridan apparently never got the memo, because with his Yellowstone universe, not only did he become one of the kings of streaming-era television, he also made Western shows mainstream again. Leaving behind the soap-opera tone of the franchise's flagship show and stripping the Western genre of its nostalgia and problematic myth-making, it's a series fully dedicated to showing just how unforgiving the Oregon Trail really was. Based on the eponymous 1983 novel by Walter Tevis, it rapidly became one of the biggest Netflix sensations of the COVID-19 pandemic lockdowns, launching Anya Taylor-Joy's mainstream popularity into the stratosphere.

If there’s one thing I’ve learned as I’ve gotten older, it’s that being active and being strong aren’t necessarily the same thing. For most of my life, I’d been recreationally active, and had loved kayaking, swimming, and taking my dog on long walks. I’d never done any kind of structured strength training, until right before my 75th birthday. One day, I was sitting on a curb outside my house, and when it was time to stand up, I couldn’t. I felt embarrassed, and the idea of falling and not being able to get up or being in a situation where I couldn’t take care of myself was scary.

I wanted everyday activities, like climbing stairs, carrying groceries, and getting up off the floor, to feel easier. I also wanted to trust my body more, and feel confident in my ability to handle whatever life threw my way. So, I joined Aspire Athletic Club, a gym near my home in Schenectady, New York, and began working out once a week with the owner, Chris Wertman. In the beginning, even simple movements were challenging, so I started with basic bodyweight exercises like the box squat, step-up, and bird dog. The focus was on learning proper form and getting comfortable with the movements.

After a few weeks, I gradually incorporated 5- to 8-lb dumbbells into the movements, and a month or two later, I noticed a real difference in my strength: I could do more repetitions, lift even heavier weights, and move with greater confidence. It was so encouraging to see the hard work pay off, and it motivated me to keep going. About a year into consistent training, I was feeling stronger and ready to take on more. I began training full-body twice a week and incorporated more challenging exercises like squat variations, the walking lunge, weighted glute bridge, and chest fly to build overall strength.

I’d never deadlifted before, so seeing someone my age lift with that kind of strength was incredibly inspiring. That decision marked the beginning of a new chapter in my strength training journey—I told my new trainer of about six months, Jack Tyree, that I wanted to lift heavier weights and try deadlifting. I even began training for my gym’s upcoming annual deadlift exhibition, which was in eight months.

Since I’d just been training with dumbbells, I tried working with a kettlebell while I got comfortable with the movement and proper technique. As I got stronger and more confident, I graduated to the barbell and continued adding more weight each week. Then, this past May, I deadlifted 132 lbs for a single rep at the exhibition, setting a personal record. I never imagined I’d be able to do something like that, but I was so proud of how strong I’d become—and I was excited to see how much further I could push myself.

Six months after the exhibition, I now strength train three days a week: one day focused on upper body, one on lower body, and one dedicated to mobility and balance. Jack still programs all my workouts, and every session includes six to eight exercises, with 3 or 4 sets of 6 to 8 reps each. I use a mix of free weights and weight machines, and some of my go-to moves include the farmer’s carry, curtsy lunge, lat pulldown, scapular pushup, squat, and bench press. Deadlifts are still my favorite; I can now do three reps at 132 lbs, which I’m really proud of.

Outside the gym, I love getting outside and staying active, so I walk a few miles with my dog every day for my cardiovascular health. In addition to mobility and balance work at the gym, I also try to stretch at home whenever I can to ease muscle soreness and improve my range of motion, especially in my shoulders and hips.

I know nutrition is an important part of recovery too. Although I don’t follow a strict nutrition plan or count macros, I try to eat protein at every meal to support muscle growth. Usually, it’s chicken, red meat, or a scoop of protein powder mixed into my morning oatmeal. I also prioritize whole foods and aim to incorporate a variety of fruits and vegetables throughout the day.

When I first started strength training, I didn’t know where to begin. So, having someone guide me through the movements, teach me proper form, and help me build strength safely made all the difference. My trainers have helped me recognize what I’m capable of, push beyond what I thought was possible, and progress at a pace that feels right. They also create workouts that challenge me while keeping my goals in mind. Having their support and encouragement has given me the confidence to try things I never imagined I could do. Whether it’s deadlifting heavy weights, bench pressing a barbell, or taking less rest between working sets, my trainers have taught me not to put a ceiling on what I think I can accomplish.

Before I strength trained, I felt weak and unsteady, but I didn’t give up and accept that I’d feel like that for the rest of my life. In some ways, I completely started over with exercise at 74 because I tried something brand new—and that’s affected my life outside of the gym.

Now, at 78, I feel stronger than ever; I can get up off the floor with ease, carry my own groceries into the house, shovel snow, garden, and play sports with my grandchildren. These are things I want to keep doing independently, and having the strength to do them confidently means the world to me. While I love lifting weights and hitting new PRs, the greatest reward is how much easier everyday life has become.

Learning to lift in my mid-70s wasn’t easy, and building that strength didn’t happen overnight, but I kept showing up, putting in the work, and gradually accomplishing what I once never thought was possible. Now, as I approach 80, I’m still getting stronger, and I want other women to know they’re never too old to get started, and they may be capable of far more than they realize. If I can go from struggling to get up from a curb to deadlifting 132 pounds, who knows what else is possible?

The current flagship television from Sony, the Bravia 9 II, is an RGB TV model (Sony calls it “True RGB”) that brings to life even the darkest scenes in Game of Thrones. I ended up preferring the overall picture quality of that LG model and some other OLED models, but—after my usual rigorous testing—I felt the Bravia 9 II came close to matching the high caliber of those other options. In fact, it’s just a notch or two lower than the absolute best television you might find.

The Sony Bravia 9 II is all black, sits on two feet, and includes four HDMI ports (only two are HDMI 2.1, though, which allows for higher-bandwidth streaming and sound at 48 Gbps). Setup was a breeze; Google TV was up and running in less than five minutes, which is an impressive feat, given that both Samsung and LG televisions typically add a few extra steps to the install process, such as authenticating your login (with Samsung) or relying on an app (with the LG ThinQ).

The premium Sony Bravia 9 II also offers exceptional sound. It includes two upfiring tweeters for more realistic Dolby Atmos than the Bravia 7 II, which lacks these extra channels. When I watched House of the Dragon, the room-filling audio made me feel like I was flying a dragon or piloting a ship.

Viewing angles on the Bravia 9 II are about the best I’ve seen on any television. I tested this element by camping out in a chair and playing video games off to the side, and there wasn’t a hint of screen glare. These perks are, again, a sign of its premium quality, verified through my usual benchmarks. Skin tones looked varied and vivid; one person had a little more red in her face than what I noticed on the LG Micro RGB Evo or the Samsung Micro RGB R95H.

In a demo-reel benchmark test I use for all televisions, I was shocked by the Bravia 9 II's stellar performance. I’ve watched the same short segment with a fence in front of a winter landscape countless times to test various models, but here, the green grass in the background looked supremely vivid and clear. It’s because the Bravia 9 II has such exceptional screen brightness, color accuracy, and absolutely no glare.

I prefer the bare-bones Samsung remote or the one included with an Apple TV 4K streaming device. Just give me a Netflix button and volume controls, and I’m good. Still, the layout of this remote mostly makes sense, and I like that it’s backlit.

The Bravia 9 II didn’t pass all of my tests for performance while I watched shows and movies, but it did allow me to tweak more settings than I’m used to. My main takeaway is that it's nice that you can tweak settings on the Bravia 9 II to great effect, thanks to an RGB panel that’s super-bright. The movies Awake and The Creator tested my patience a little in that regard. Knowing you can achieve those results is nice; actually doing it does take time.

I also noticed that the latency, at a 120-Hz refresh rate when I used one of the HDMI 2.1 ports, was almost nonexistent. That helped with every slight twitch when a seven-headed monster from the dark underworld of hell started attacking me.

Knowing you can tweak the settings for the results you want and that anyone in your household will be able to see America’s Got Talent from any angle in the room is a major plus. I want to see dragons at night and monsters in dark hallways, but for some animated shows, sports, and network TV, you might just want your device to work well, right out of the box, without worrying about cinematic quality. Yes, a flagship lets you do a lot more, but at a much steeper price.

The south coast derby is another fierce rivalry in the Championship, and it has already produced drama this season, with Saints beating Pompey on their home patch.

After years apart, the two sides met last season, and whilst the games weren’t the best for neutrals in terms of action, the atmosphere made them a gripping watch.

How I Had to Learn to Stop Saying “Yes” When I Meant “No”

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted November 19, 2026 | Reviewed by Tyler Woods

For years, I was a girl who would say “yes” when I meant “no.” It was only after I decided to stop drinking that I realized how much my “yes-girl” tendency had fueled my drinking cycle over the years.

Over the years, both through my own recovery and my work in helping others break their drinking patterns, I have come to see that most people who eventually come to question their relationship with alcohol don’t have a “drinking problem;” instead, they have a “drinking solution.” In my case, my inability and unwillingness to say “no” had created a lot of problems that I ended up turning to alcohol to solve.

I would say yes to a football weekend marathon when I didn’t even know what a quarterback was, and then turn to a 6-pack to make time pass faster. I would say “yes” to hosting the relative whom I couldn't stand, then try to push away my resentment with a bottle of wine. I would say “yes” to helping out at a neighbor’s garage sale after a long week and end up trying to recover from exhaustion with a few cocktails.

As I started to help others break their old drinking patterns, I realized I was far from the only person who got caught in this pattern. In fact, I came to nickname this particular drinking pattern “the Yes-Effect Drinking Loop.” It’s one of the 10 most common invisible drinking loops that keep an unwanted drinking pattern alive.

In most unhealthy drinking patterns, drinking is only what we see on the surface. The Invisible Drinking Loop is a term I created to help people understand the hidden forces that keep their drinking patterns alive. An invisible drinking loop is often driven by the need to solve a problem, fueled by the belief that alcohol can help, and maintained by the habit loop in which drinking has become the default routine to cope with or deal with certain situations or emotions.

In the “Yes-Effect Drinking Loop,” drinking has become the solution to deal with the side effects that arise when a person struggles to say no.

As is true with most drinking loops, simply stopping drinking doesn’t break the loop.

With alcohol out of my life, I found myself sitting through events that I had no interest in, battling the desire to reach for a drink to make time pass faster, hanging out with people whose company I didn’t enjoy, missing the quick mental escape alcohol could offer, and dealing with overwhelming cravings by the end of the day after I once again stretched myself too thin in the effort to please others instead of honoring my own limitations.

It took me a long time to realize that simply stopping drinking doesn’t solve the problems; instead, it exposes them. I needed to learn to find another way to address the problem that I once attempted to solve through drinking, and that meant learning how to say no.

Learning to say no after years of saying yes is not easy. When faced with a choice between disappointing others and disappointing myself, my default response was to choose the latter. Somewhere along the line, I had been conditioned to believe that fulfilling others’ expectations, even those of people whom I wasn't even that close to, was a way to keep myself safe, liked, and in good standing.

Yet, I failed to see the hidden cost of all the efforts to keep others happy; I kept myself unhappy. So much so that alcohol became the only way I knew how to push down my disappointment, exhaustion, and resentment.

Learning to say “no” started with a radical decision to honor my own preferences rather than automatically prioritize others’ expectations. Don’t get me wrong, it doesn’t mean becoming self-centered or never making compromises in life. But it does mean taking an intentional pause before giving in to my compulsive need to keep others happy. And when faced with the choice between disappointing others and disappointing myself, I wanted to make that choice consciously, rather than by default.

Of course, giving myself permission to say no was one thing. Actually knowing how to say it was another. After years of automatically saying yes, saying no felt like a skill I had not yet learned.

That's why Skill Expansion is one of the core pillars of my approach to breaking an old drinking pattern.
