How Revolut Cut Its AI Coding Bill 90% by Self-Hosting an Open-Weight Model

When British fintech Revolut rolled out AI coding assistants to roughly 2,000 engineers, the bill did what AI bills always do: it exploded. Some engineers were spending $1,000 — even $10,000 — a month on AI tokens with little measurable return.

So Revolut did something almost no company of its size has tried: it ditched the frontier APIs as its default and moved its engineers onto a self-hosted, open-weight model. The result, per Revolut's own numbers, was a roughly 90% cut in AI coding inference costs — plus coding that got about 60% faster.

Not Every Slow Query Needs a New Index

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

What a SQL review taught me about assumptions, AI-written queries, and common-sense tuning

A development team asked me to review the SQL behind a change-data publishing job before they moved it from once a day to once an hour. In QA, one full run took 10 to 12 minutes. After the review, the same run took under 30 seconds. In production, most of its queries now finish in under a millisecond.

Almost none of that came from adding indexes. It came from reading the queries, asking what the job actually needed, and checking each assumption against real data. This post walks through what we found, in the order we found it, and what I would tell any team (or any AI assistant) writing SQL against a busy production database.

The job polls a PostgreSQL read replica for rows that changed since its last run and publishes them as events. It is a classic checkpoint pattern:

I'll use a generic schema for the examples:

Every table has created_at and updated_at. The source system also maintains updated_at_num, a numeric YYYYMMDDHHMISS copy of updated_at, and that column is indexed on every table. Keep that last detail in mind.

Every change-window filter in the job looked like this:

The intent is reasonable: if a row was never updated, fall back to its creation time. But PostgreSQL can only use a B-tree index when the predicate matches the indexed expression. Wrap the column in COALESCE, and the index on updated_at (or updated_at_num) is no longer a candidate. Every window query became a sequential scan of the whole table.

The COALESCE also encoded an assumption: that updated_at can be null. So before changing anything, we checked:

Zero on every table. The source system sets both timestamps when a row is created, so the fallback could never fire. The fix was to drop the function and filter on the indexed column directly:

That one change turned every window query into an index range scan.

The COALESCE wasn't the only defensive habit. One query filtered on TRIM(a.account_type) = 'STAFF'. In QA that is harmless: small tables, fast scans, correct results. In production it does two quiet things:

No padded values, so the TRIM came out, and the queries became consistent with each other.

I want to be clear about where this comes from. The developers I work with write good SQL. But we are now in an era where a lot of SQL is drafted by AI assistants, and they are very good at producing queries that return the right answer. They are much less good at knowing your data. A model will happily add COALESCE, TRIM, LOWER or DISTINCT "just in case", because in the abstract each one is safer. On a 10,000-row QA table you'll never notice. On a 10-million-row production table, each one can turn an index lookup into a full scan.

The result being correct does not mean the assumptions behind it are. Before a query ships, check it against the data's real shape: null counts, value distributions, duplicates, and how skewed the busiest keys are. Or ask a database engineer to review it. It never hurts.

The partner-count query looked like this:

The outer query was narrowed to one organization and a one-hour window: 59 candidate rows. The subquery wasn't narrowed at all. PostgreSQL read 1.77 million membership rows, built a distinct list of 831 IDs, then matched that list against the 59 candidates. That was 31,395 buffers and 83 ms to return a count of zero.

A note on IN versus EXISTS: PostgreSQL already turns IN (subquery) into a semi-join, so the two often plan the same way. The DISTINCT adds nothing, since a semi-join never returns a row twice, and it can push the planner toward building the full list first. Switching to EXISTS helps. The bigger win is adding the filter. If the outer query is limited to something you also know about the subquery's rows, say so in the subquery too. Don't rely on the planner to infer it.

The slowest query in the job didn't return any data. It computed the run's ceiling:

It took 853 ms and about 1 million buffers to return one number. The plan showed three different behaviours:

PostgreSQL has a neat shortcut here. As the CREATE AGGREGATE documentation explains, a MIN- or MAX-like aggregate can be answered from an index, because SELECT max(col) FROM tab is equivalent to SELECT col FROM tab ORDER BY col DESC LIMIT 1. The planner walks the index backward and takes the first non-null value. It also works on partitioned tables and UNION ALL views, by merging each child's index. It does not work through a join. The planner source (planagg.c) limits the shortcut to queries that reference exactly one table, so the join branch had to build the whole join just to find its largest value.

That is where understanding the use case matters more than tuning. This value is an upper bound for the next change window. It doesn't have to be the exact newest staff row; it only has to be at least as high. Dropping the join and taking max(updated_at_num) over all of staff_links gives a bound that is never lower, and costs a single index probe. The join filter still applies in the query that reads the window.

The same review turned up a second easy win. The ceiling was global, but the job ran it once per scope, inside a loop, so it returned the same value several times per run. Computing it once per run cost nothing to change.

The password_changed_at branch I left alone. More on that in Lesson 6.

My first round of EXPLAIN ANALYZE used a one-hour window, the job's steady state. Most queries looked great: under a millisecond. Then I noticed how often the plans said (never executed). In that hour, no organization had changed, so every join after the first step was skipped. The plan was cheap because there was no work, not because the work was cheap.

Widening the window to 30 days told a different story. One query that ran in 0.7 ms on the hourly window chose a completely different plan:

The cause was a row estimate. The planner expected 188 primary memberships and got 15,368, about 80 times more. With 188, starting from memberships looks cheap. A likely reason is how PostgreSQL combines filters: by default it multiplies each condition's selectivity as if they were independent, so correlated filters (type, deleted flag, end date) can be badly underestimated. Check that statistics are fresh with ANALYZE first. If the columns really are correlated, CREATE STATISTICS can tell the planner how they relate.

So I now test every change-data query with three windows:

The first run is easy to forget. Here it was fine: page one of the full-history query took about 20 ms, because the planner could stop as soon as it had 500 rows. But you only know that by running it.

After the rewrites, I had a list of index candidates that would have made every remaining query near-instant: one on password_changed_at, a partial index for a rare account type, and a few more. Before building any of them, I added up what an hourly run actually cost without them:

The slowest single statement was under a second, far from any timeout. Every index you add costs writes, storage and maintenance forever, and here the database was a replication target that would pay for each one on every write. So most of the candidates were dropped. That is a fine outcome. Not every slow-looking query needs an index; sometimes it needs nothing.

One query was different, because its cost multiplied. It loaded full account details in chunks of 100 IDs, and each chunk joined org_members on account_id, a column with no index. Every chunk scanned a 9-million-row table: about 0.7 s.

That is the test I use now: don't ask whether a query is slow, ask whether its cost grows with the data or the backlog. Fixed costs can usually be tolerated. Costs that multiply need an index. We added exactly one: org_members (account_id).

The new index worked exactly as planned: the org_members step went from scanning 5.4 million rows to reading 2,217 buffers. Then I looked at the total for the chunk query. It had gone from 723 ms to 26.9 seconds.

With org_members suddenly cheap, the planner switched to a nested loop and probed memberships once per organization row through an index on org_id. That index holds every membership in the organization, one per member account. The accounts in this chunk belonged to very large organizations, so each probe walked about 14,000 entries to find the one primary membership it needed.

The planner expected roughly one row per probe. When it knows a value in advance, PostgreSQL can look it up in its most-common-values statistics. Here each probe's value came from the outer row at run time, so the planner estimated an average number of rows per organization. The handful of very large organizations didn't fit that average, and re-running ANALYZE doesn't change how the estimate is made.

The fix was another index, but a very small one: a partial index containing only the rows the query asks for.

It holds about 15,000 rows instead of 650,000, so it is small and cheap to maintain, and each probe now lands directly on the row it needs. PostgreSQL uses a partial index only when it can prove, at planning time, that the query's WHERE clause implies the index predicate. The docs point out that a bind parameter such as membership_type = $1 can't prove that. Because this query uses membership_type = 'PRIMARY' AND is_deleted = false as literal values, the planner can match the partial index without any code change.

The lesson isn't "avoid indexes". It's that an index changes the plan around it, not just the step it targets. Re-run the full EXPLAIN ANALYZE after every index, and read the total, not just the line you were fixing.

Both indexes went in with CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY, so writes kept flowing. A few things I check every time:

1. Look for old transactions first. Per the CREATE INDEX docs, a concurrent build waits before each table scan for transactions that have modified the table, and after the second scan for any transaction holding a snapshot older than that scan. My pre-check found 65 sessions from a replication tool sitting idle in transaction for 28 days. Before cancelling anything, check whether they actually matter:

Ours held no snapshot, no transaction ID and no locks on the target tables, so they couldn't block the build or hold back vacuum. That's a cleanup item, not a blocker.

2. Run with autocommit on. CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY cannot run inside a transaction block, and some SQL clients wrap scripts in one.

3. Decide on lock_timeout deliberately. It lets a build fail fast instead of queueing behind a long transaction, but a build that fails partway leaves an invalid index behind. I left it unset and watched the progress view instead.

4. Check for invalid indexes afterwards. CREATE INDEX ... IF NOT EXISTS sees an invalid leftover's name and skips it with only a NOTICE. Drop it with DROP INDEX CONCURRENTLY and build again, or rebuild it with REINDEX INDEX CONCURRENTLY.

5. Build smallest to largest, and watch replication lag during the big ones.

This is what I now run through for any SQL headed to a large production table, whether a person or an AI wrote it.

Looking back, the biggest gains came from questions, not configuration:

None of that needed database expertise the developers couldn't learn. It needed someone to explain how the planner reads a predicate, why a function hides an index, and why QA data isn't evidence for production. Once that was clear, the developer rewrote the queries himself, and the run time in QA went from 30–50 minutes to under 30 seconds.

Some tuning really is just common sense and basic troubleshooting: read the plan, check the data, and ask what the query is for. If your team writes SQL, especially with an AI assistant drafting it, get a database engineer to look at it before it meets production data. It never hurts, and sometimes it saves you an hour-long first run, or a 27-second query you didn't know you had.

Pricing freelance dev work: from hourly rate to a project quote

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

If you freelance as a developer, you'll probably be asked for an hourly rate, a sprint price, a monthly retainer and a fixed project quote, sometimes all from the same client. They can all come from one number. This post builds that number from your own costs, then turns it into each kind of price, with a short section on keeping scope creep from eating the margin.

All dollar amounts, hours and percentages below are made-up example figures, picked so the math is easy to follow. They aren't typical, average or recommended rates. Swap in your own. Tax and buffer percentages here are planning aids, not tax or accounting advice.

Start with what you want to take home in a year, then add what the business costs to run. For a developer that might be a hardware replacement fund, IDE and SaaS licences, hosting and domains for your own projects, insurance and accounting.

Example: take-home goal $67,200, plus expenses of $400/month × 12 = $4,800, so $72,000 in total.

Part of each invoice may need to go to tax, and a buffer can help with slow months and gaps between contracts.

Example: 25% tax set-aside + 15% buffer = 40%, which leaves 60% for income and expenses:

$72,000 ÷ 0.60 = $120,000 revenue needed (example figure)

Some of a freelancer's week probably can't be billed: sales calls, proposals, invoicing, keeping your own tooling up to date, learning a new framework for the next contract. Holidays and sick days come out too.

Example: 35 working hours a week, 10 of them non-billable, so 25 billable hours × 48 working weeks = 1,200 billable hours a year.

$120,000 ÷ 1,200 = $100 an hour (example figure)

That's your floor, not a target. Every other price below starts from it.

Some clients, especially teams already running Scrum, may find a per-sprint price easier to budget than an hourly one. Multiply the billable hours you'll commit per sprint by your rate.

Example: a 2-week sprint at 25 billable hours a week = 50 hours × $100 = $5,000 per sprint.

Say in writing what a sprint includes (planning, demo, a set number of hours) and what happens if the client adds work mid-sprint. For example, it waits for the next sprint, or it's priced as a change.

After a launch, a client may want someone on call for bugs, dependency updates and small fixes. A monthly retainer turns that into a fixed number of hours at your rate.

Example: 10 hours a month × $100 = $1,000 a month for maintenance.

Some clients may prefer a fixed price for a whole project. Break the work into tasks, estimate each in hours, then add a contingency for the unknowns: the third-party API with thin docs, the legacy code nobody has touched in years.

That's 64 hours. Add a 25% contingency and you get 80 hours × $100 = $8,000 (example figure).

To spread the risk, split payment into a deposit and milestones.

Example: a 25% deposit of $2,000 before work starts, then the remaining $6,000 in three milestone payments of $2,000 each (say, accounts done, API done, launch).

A fixed quote holds up best when the scope does. "Can we also add a CSV export?" sounds small, but a few requests like that can push the hours well past the estimate.

Example: if the $8,000 project ends up taking 100 hours instead of 80, the effective rate drops to $8,000 ÷ 100 = $80 an hour, below the $100 floor from step 3. That can be a signal to tighten scope, raise the contingency, or re-estimate next time.

Pen and paper or a spreadsheet can handle this math. Re-run the numbers whenever your costs, hours or goals change.

TL;DR: The Hong Kong Monetary Authority and the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology announced a post-quantum cryptography assessment toolkit on November 2. Post-quantum migration is not the same thing as making every blockchain or bank system instantly quantum-secure.

Good security planning is often unglamorous. Discovering where keys are issued, which partners rely on them and when equipment will be replaced is difficult work, but it makes future transitions possible. The banking sector has a reason to start early because data captured today can remain sensitive long after the original connection has ended.

The Hong Kong Monetary Authority and the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology announced a post-quantum cryptography assessment toolkit on November 2. The initiative is aimed at helping banks understand where current cryptographic systems might need upgrades as quantum capabilities develop. It is a preparation programme, not a declaration that quantum computers have broken banking or cryptocurrency encryption. The dated original disclosure is the starting point for the figures and product scope discussed here.

Large organisations use cryptography in certificates, customer authentication, interbank links, software updates and the storage of sensitive data. Migrating those systems can take years because algorithms, devices, vendors and legacy applications must remain interoperable. A toolkit can help identify dependencies, prioritise sensitive data and test readiness for future standards before a forced emergency transition.

The HKMA has pointed to gaps in sector readiness and ambitions to improve preparedness by 2030. The most immediate benefit may be governance: teams can assign ownership of the inventory and agree which systems require the longest lead time. That is more useful than debating a speculative date on which a general-purpose quantum computer could break a particular cryptographic scheme.

A crypto incident produces several separate timelines: containment, investigation, recovery and a return to reliable service. Those phases can overlap without finishing together. A traced wallet is not automatically frozen; a frozen balance is not automatically returned; and a reopened withdrawal route is not a completed security audit. Keeping those differences visible helps readers judge progress more accurately.

For additional perspective, it is worth reading our existing reporting on Binance’s earlier staged withdrawal restart and Bitcoin payment infrastructure and node software context. Those background reports help separate established facts from a new, still-developing announcement.

Post-quantum migration is not the same thing as making every blockchain or bank system instantly quantum-secure. Different primitives face different levels of risk, and some proposed methods affect key size, verification performance or compatibility. The toolkit’s output is an assessment input, not a substitute for independent engineering and formal testing.

Operational controls matter most when a business is under stress. Security announcements should identify the system affected, the customers who can act, the evidence supporting any recovery number and what remains uncertain. Clear scope protects users from false reassurance and from unnecessary panic. It also provides a sensible basis for follow-up accountability.

Watch whether participating institutions publish migration road maps, begin procurement changes and test post-quantum authentication with core systems. For crypto readers, it is a reminder to separate near-term operational security from longer-term cryptographic research. No credible reading of the announcement implies that Bitcoin private keys are currently being routinely cracked by quantum machines.

Anthropic is working on an open-source system to verify which AI model produced a given output, according to a blog post published Tuesday. The system, called FlashProve, is built to provide what researchers call “provable inference” – a cryptographic proof, essentially a mathematical guarantee, that a given output came from a specific model. It’s meant to let users confirm that the answer they received really came from the model they think they’re using.

Similar to cryptographic email signatures that let recipients confirm a message really came from its claimed sender, FlashProve would be robust against impostors attempting to disguise one model as another, even when the impostor is the lab that built and runs the model. In particular, the researchers note that the system could be used to prevent an AI company from secretly swapping in a cheaper model to cut costs, or a more expensive model to inflate its performance results.

FlashProve could also be used to verify claims by third parties that a specific task was done by an advanced model. “A developer might claim that their software library was scanned for vulnerabilities by a frontier cybersecurity model, or a company might claim that its business plan was given the go-ahead by an appropriate model,” the researchers write. “How can a user verify these claims?”

To work, the system requires AI labs to publish a “fingerprint” of each model they release that can later be matched to specific outputs and verified cryptographically. Crucially, the fingerprints don’t reveal a model’s weights, the internal numbers that define how it behaves and which closed-model makers like Anthropic and OpenAI keep private. That means labs could publish the fingerprints without giving up their most valuable asset, enabling the system to work with commercial models, too. The system is also designed to be lightweight, so verification could be performed on a local device.

As part of the announcement, researchers released a detailed technical specification and working code for various elements of the system, which is publicly available under an open-source license on GitHub. California fights "preposterous" Trump admin demand to scrap state broadband laws

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

State tries to obtain broadband funds despite rejecting Trump admin condition.

California is accepting $1.86 billion in broadband grant funding from the Trump administration, but the state is not accepting the Trump administration’s demand that it stop enforcing a net neutrality law and other telecom regulations.

California’s stand against the Trump administration could result in multiple legal battles involving the state, federal government, and Internet providers seeking government funds. Although California told the federal government it would accept the money, it is reserving the right to challenge the condition imposed by the US. The state could delay or cancel plans to distribute broadband grants depending on how the process unfolds.

The National Telecommunications and Information Administration (NTIA) decided last year that states must agree not to enforce any rate regulation or net neutrality rule on ISPs that receive money from the $42 billion Broadband Equity, Access, and Deployment (BEAD) program. California had to decide whether to accept its share of the fund despite that restriction.

State officials argue that California should be able to obtain the money and keep enforcing its laws because the Trump administration’s demand would violate the state’s constitutional rights. The California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) made its view clear in a carefully worded letter yesterday to Assistant Secretary of Commerce and NTIA Administrator Arielle Roth.

“The CPUC cannot be bound by any terms or conditions that are unconstitutional or not authorized by law and it expressly does not waive or concede any arguments or challenges concerning the legality, interpretation, or applicability of any term or condition,” CPUC President John Reynolds told Roth.

Reynolds’ letter also said the CPUC cannot prevent other California agencies from enforcing state laws, suggesting that the attorney general would maintain the right to punish misbehaving Internet providers.

Reynolds’ letter acknowledged that the NTIA may not agree with the CPUC’s stance and said the state thus will not distribute any funding to ISPs until after the state and federal agency sort out any differences. If the NTIA refuses to change its position, California could sue the Trump administration on the grounds that the condition is unconstitutional.

The NTIA does not appear likely to bend. “As noted in the Department’s response to CPUC, the terms and conditions of the award constitute the entire agreement between NTIA and CPUC. No part of the letter outlining CPUC’s concerns is incorporated into or made part of the award,” an NTIA spokesperson told Ars today.

Consumer advocacy group Public Knowledge supported CPUC’s position. The Trump administration’s demand to preempt state laws is “brazen and meritless,” the group said.

“This administration’s preposterous demand that industry be given a blanket waiver from affordability requirements, net neutrality and privacy protections, and any other commitments to connect and serve Americans has no basis in the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA), which established BEAD. And, as the commission points out, the NTIA has no authority to alter California state law or require California agencies to not enforce the law,” said Raza Panjwani, senior policy counsel at Public Knowledge.

The CPUC voted to ratify the funding plan in September. But Reynolds said in yesterday’s letter to the Trump administration that his agency’s acceptance of the funds is “based on the CPUC’s best understanding of the effect of Section 50” of the BEAD terms and conditions. Section 50 of the BEAD rules says each state:

shall commit that it will not enforce any law, regulation, order, contracting requirement, or other enforceable obligation that directly or indirectly regulates the rates, terms, and conditions of broadband Internet service… or imposes net neutrality rules, open access, or other utility-style rules on broadband Internet service, against a Subgrantee or its affiliates anywhere it provides service within the State (i.e., both BEAD and non-BEAD locations), while that Subgrantee has any subgrant that is still within its period of performance, extended period of performance, or federal interest period.

The BEAD rules specify that a net neutrality rule is one prohibiting ISPs from “blocking content, throttling speeds, imposing data caps, or engaging in paid prioritization.” ISPs obtaining BEAD funding could be exempt from such laws for up to 14 years.

Reynolds’ letter said the NTIA-imposed condition is “vague and ambiguous as to its scope and its relationship to Section 47 U.S.C. § 1702(g)(2)(A)(i) of the BEAD statute,” the US law that created the BEAD program. He wrote:

The CPUC does not interpret Section 50 to require the CPUC to waive, release, or otherwise forfeit any rights, claims, arguments, or defenses concerning potential implications of Section 50 that may violate applicable laws. Further, the CPUC’s acceptance of funds under this award should not be understood as agreement either to refrain from compliance with mandatory legal duties or obligations, or to constrain other State of California entities in the exercise of their authorities to enforce state law, which is outside the scope of the CPUC’s authority. Moreover, California’s net neutrality law expressly provides that “any waiver of the provisions of this title is contrary to public policy and shall be unenforceable and void.” Likewise, many of the CPUC’s own programs that touch on broadband Internet access service are mandated by the California Public Utilities Code, and are neither discretionary nor waivable.

The NTIA today updated its BEAD progress dashboard to reflect that California has signed its final agreement with the federal agency. California was the last state or territory to turn in its final agreement.

“All 56 states and territories have signed and returned their award agreement, finalizing the process,” the NTIA’s updated dashboard page said.

California could face legal problems if and when it distributes the money, which is to be given to Internet service providers in exchange for deploying broadband in unserved and underserved areas. ISPs could insist that they don’t have to follow state law and sue California, or simply decide to violate consumer protection laws and defend against any lawsuits the state attorney general might file against them.

Reynolds apparently hopes to avoid that outcome by holding discussions with the NTIA to resolve any disagreement over the enforceability of California state law.

“To the extent that NTIA and the CPUC disagree about the CPUC’s obligations under Section 50 and other terms and conditions, the CPUC will execute any subgrant agreements if and when the CPUC and NTIA are able to resolve such disagreements and any other applicable conditions are met,” Reynolds wrote. “To that end, we will direct our staff to work diligently and cooperatively with NTIA staff to find such a solution.”

Whether California’s strategy works “depends on what happens next,” Stanford Law professor Barbara van Schewick, a longtime proponent of net neutrality and other telecom regulations, told Ars today.

“The letter doesn’t change the terms of California’s award: Condition 50 is still in it, and only a court or NTIA agreeing in writing can take it out,” she said. “The letter puts California’s position on the record before any contracts with providers are signed and preserves California’s legal arguments against Condition 50.”

Attorney General Rob Bonta’s office declined to comment when contacted by Ars today. A spokesperson for California Gov. Gavin Newsom said California is not giving up its right to enforce state laws against Internet providers.

“California is accepting federal broadband funding to bring high-speed Internet to hundreds of thousands of homes and businesses, many of them in rural and underserved communities,” a governor’s spokesperson said in a statement provided to Ars. “We’re ready to get these projects built with our local and federal partners. Connecting these communities is the goal, and so is protecting them. California’s laws, including our net neutrality protections, still stand.”

California’s net neutrality law prohibits ISPs from blocking or throttling lawful traffic and says ISPs may not require fees from websites or online services to deliver or prioritize their traffic to Internet users. The first Trump administration lost an attempt to preempt state net neutrality laws, and California defeated ISPs that challenged the state law in court.

California also has a law that prohibits mobile providers from throttling first responders during emergencies. That law was passed in 2019, a year after Verizon throttled an “unlimited” data plan used by Santa Clara County firefighters during a wildfire. Local wildfire-preparedness groups warned that the BEAD condition could prevent California from enforcing the throttling law and requirements for battery backups on cell towers.

According to van Schewick, “California has strong arguments that Condition 50 is illegal. Congress designed BEAD to get broadband built while making sure the providers who take the money follow the law: the statute directs states to ensure funded providers can carry out their work ‘in compliance with all applicable Federal, State, and local laws.’”

But van Schewick said the letter is just an initial step. “The real test comes the first time a BEAD provider blocks a competitor’s app, throttles firefighters, or walks away from a price commitment,” she said. “If the state enforces its laws when that happens, the letter will have protected Californians. If the state looks the other way, Condition 50 will have worked exactly as the providers intended, without a single court ever ruling on it.”

For Lisa and Tom Cuchara, that includes the meadows and woodland around Little River in southern Maine. Chris McGinnis searches the rainforests of Costa Rica and Panama, while also expanding his macro photography portfolio in Maine and his own backyard.

Their photographs show the miniature world that can turn up on a rotting log or a garden stem. Baumgarten gets low on the forest floor to photograph mushrooms and the slugs that feed on them. The Cucharas and McGinnis have both made some of their favorite finds after dark, the Cucharas in a garden in the Northeast and McGinnis at a pond in Panama.

In one of Baumgarten’s favorite macro photographs, a garden slug stretches along rotting wood beneath a cluster of orange mushrooms, its head raised toward one of the caps.

“What excited me the most was the garden slug getting ready to chow down, which isn’t a common shot,” he says.

Baumgarten takes many of his macro shots at a park close to home. McLean’s Park has several kilometers of trails through mixed forest, which he uses for hiking, biking, cross-country skiing, snowshoeing, and photography.

“It is about a 15-minute drive from my home,” he says. “I go there several times a week to walk my dog. As a mature forest there are plenty of fallen trees and branches that make for the perfect environment for a bunch of different types of mushrooms.”

“What I love about the park is the variety of photographs I can take there,” he notes. “One day I might photograph a person walking their dog in autumn along a leaf-covered trail, with tall trees and yellow foliage all around. Another day, I might be down among the fallen logs and branches photographing mushrooms.”

Baumgarten likes to shoot most of his mushroom macro photos below eye level. “I love capturing them from underneath,” he explains. “For me, the gill structure is perhaps the most interesting and photogenic part of the mushroom. I want a soft background that doesn’t pull focus from my subject. By shooting from just below the mushroom I can usually include the more distant, lush greenery of the forest.”

“While mushrooms don’t move, when I’m photographing a slug in the low light of the forest, I have to be aware that even its slow movement can cause blur,” he continues. “Shooting handheld, I might use a shutter speed of around 1/100 second to keep it sharp, raising the ISO if I need to.”

Bumping up the ISO, the camera’s sensitivity to light, lets him shoot at a faster shutter speed when the light on the forest floor is dim. With a mushroom, he can usually keep the ISO low and slow the shutter down instead. The image stabilization in his OM SYSTEM OM-3 lets him handhold exposures as long as half a second, even while using in-camera focus stacking, which combines the sharp areas of photographs focused at different distances to keep more of a close-up subject in focus.

“I capture mushrooms in a very similar way to how I photograph wildlife,” Baumgarten says. “For many wildlife shots I prefer to use my long lens. This gives me great close-up images of my subject. But a great close-up is not always the best way to showcase the animal. Showing the animal in its environment helps me tell more of the story about where it lives. That’s when I turn to using a wider lens. I will often do the same when photographing mushrooms.”

Mushrooms can appear quickly and may not stay in good condition for long, so Baumgarten recommends watching the weather to help decide when to go looking.

“Mushrooms need warm, moist conditions, so I recommend exploring one or two days after a good rain,” he advises. “I have had great success from late spring to late autumn. I recommend avoiding bright sunny days. The dappled sunlight on the forest floor can work for some shots, but often it is more of a hindrance than a help.”

A subject that stays put gives photographers new to macro more time to experiment with focus and composition.

“Photographing mushrooms is a great way to get into macro shooting,” he adds. “I love the geometry of most mushrooms and the infinite patience they have. I’ve never had a mushroom run away from me as I sneak up on it.”

Those quiet searches are also part of why he keeps returning to the forest.

“I often turn to macro photography to get me out of a rut,” Baumgarten says. “I primarily shoot landscapes and wildlife and over the years there have been many stretches of time where I just couldn’t capture shots that I was happy with. It’s at these times, when I feel a bit down, that I grab my macro lens and head into the forest for a bit of exploration and de-stressing. I slow down, observe carefully, and invariably find something in the macro world to photograph. And the best part is, I don’t have to go far. Macro subjects are everywhere, and a local park or garden can be a great place to start looking. I’m lucky to have McLean’s Park just a short drive from home.”

For Lisa and Tom Cuchara, some of the strangest subjects turn up after dark.

“One evening I headed into the garden to look for tiny nighttime wonders,” Lisa Cuchara recalls. “Imagine my surprise when I found a bright green hornworm sporting what looked like dozens of tiny white marshmallows. Those little white blobs weren’t eggs or fungus, though. They were the silken cocoons of tiny parasitoid wasps. The wasp larvae had developed inside the living caterpillar before coming out to spin their cocoons. It was creepy and fascinating at the same time.”

Gardeners don’t like hornworms, since a hungry one can make a massive amount of tomato foliage disappear overnight. The wasps, most likely a Cotesia species, are another matter.

“This was a perfect example of nature’s own pest control,” Lisa explains. “Unlike regular parasites, which generally don’t kill their hosts, parasitoids kill the animal they develop in. These tiny wasps help keep hornworm numbers down, which makes them real allies for gardeners.”

“When I saw this scene, I knew that the challenge was getting the detail of both the caterpillar and those wonderfully wonky white cocoons while working handheld in the dark,” Lisa recalls. “Even at f/11, there just isn’t enough depth of field to get everything sharp in a single exposure.”

An aperture of f/11 is a small lens opening that would normally extend depth of field, the zone of a photograph that looks sharp. In-camera focus stacking, used with the Cucharas’ OM-1 Mark II and the M.Zuiko Digital ED 90mm F3.5 Macro IS PRO lens, got around the problem.

“I was able to shoot a focus-stacked image handheld, without setting up a tripod or disturbing my subject,” Lisa notes.

For insects, the Cucharas use a small flash fitted with a diffuser.

“A bare flash gives you harsh highlights and deep shadows, especially on an insect’s shiny exoskeleton,” the Cucharas explain. “Using a diffuser turns that small flash into a much larger, softer light compared to a tiny subject. The flash also helps freeze movement, whether it’s a slight breeze moving a leaf or an insect shifting position.”

“Without a flashlight and a willingness to explore the garden after sunset, I might have walked right past an extraordinary biological story unfolding just inches away,” Lisa adds.

On other outings, the Cucharas have photographed a moth laying eggs at night and a crab spider grabbing an unsuspecting bumblebee. They’ve also watched a caterpillar that had just shed its outer skin, known as a molt.

“A black swallowtail caterpillar will eat its own molt for energy,” they add. “Those spontaneous moments of seeing something so unique are just one reason we enjoy macro photography so much.”

The Cucharas have turned their own yard into a wildlife habitat, replacing most of the lawn with the goal of mainly native plants grown without pesticides or herbicides. They also lead macro and close-up photography retreats at Little River in North Berwick, Maine.

“We love places where you can move just a few feet and find a whole different world,” they say.

Insects are cold-blooded, so how active they are depends on the temperature and conditions around them.

“Pick a rainy, drizzly, or overcast day,” the Cucharas advise. “Insects like to hang out under the leaves and they’re less likely to move. And get there early, because a lot of insects are cold and slow in the early hours, which gives you more time to photograph them.”

“Slow down and look under every leaf,” they continue. “Don’t rush from flower to flower; pick a small area and explore it from a few angles. You start to see a jumping spider on a stalk, holes in a leaf from insects eating, or a curled leaf with a caterpillar inside.”

“If you’re just starting to practice macro photography, a stationary flower with some personality, pollen, or water droplets is a wonderful starting point,” the Cucharas note. “A flower past its prime, a crooked stem, a curled petal, or an oddly shaped mushroom can be a far more compelling story than a flawless blossom. And learn to notice the background, because it’s just as important as the subject.”

“At one of our retreats, six groups rotated through my session to photograph a swallowtail chrysalis on its host plant,” Lisa recalls. “It gave me a chance to talk about fennel and other host plants, and about how to photograph it without disturbing the spot or making it more obvious to predators. No photograph is worth possible harm to the subject, including insects and the plants they’re on.”

Lisa also says that it’s not necessary to have a large piece of land to start creating your own macro photography sanctuary. “I always recommend planting more native and host plants everywhere, even if it’s just a few planters at your condo. Native plants are what attract the insects that look fascinating in a photograph.”

Even with their own garden, the Cucharas still head out to other spots for macro photography. However, unlike static landscape overlooks, macro photography is always delivering something new. “We often visit the same places again and again, and we always come home with different photographs,” the Cucharas explain. “Flowers open and fade, insect species change from month to month, and the light and weather change. Macro photography might be the best genre for photographers who are always looking to shoot, and learn, something new.”

Chris McGinnis has captured some of his favorite macro photographs after dark, in the rainforests of Costa Rica and Panama.

McGinnis says that one of the most interesting subjects in the Central American rainforest is the red-eyed tree frog. “With these frogs, I am constantly surprised with the variety of moments I am able to capture them in, and maybe my favorite moment was on a trip to Panama with fellow OM SYSTEM ambassador Lee Hoy,” McGinnis remembers. “We had some workshop clients with us, and they faded one by one and went off to sleep, but Lee and I stayed out shooting. We were on this little pond, looking for frogs, and we’d found and photographed several. All of a sudden Lee says, ‘Red-eyed tree frogs in amplexus.’ I said, ‘What? What do you mean?’, not knowing what amplexus meant. Lee pointed to a branch and there were two red-eyed tree frogs with the male on top of the female. I’ve photographed hundreds of these frogs before, but I had never seen them do this. The shot was a unique addition to my portfolio, and I learned a new word in the process.”

According to McGinnis, red-eyed tree frogs spend the day higher up in the trees and come down toward the water as the sun goes down, which is why he looks for them at night. But whether it’s day or night, he often chooses to shoot macro photographs with a non-macro lens.

“That night with the frogs was a time when I could have used my M.Zuiko Digital ED 90mm F3.5 Macro IS PRO,” he recalls. “I wanted to be up really close, but I wanted a wider view so the story was the frogs in their environment, as that can make a more interesting shot.”

Instead, he had the M.Zuiko Digital ED 8-25mm F4.0 PRO on his camera that night. It’s a wide-angle zoom that can focus close enough to fill the frame with a frog while still showing the branch the pair was sitting on.

“I don’t think I could ever get tired of searching for red-eyed tree frogs,” he adds. “It’s an addiction for me. I find one, I photograph it until it jumps away or I’ve exhausted every angle I can get. Then I could go back to my room and go to sleep, or I could try to find another one. And I have a really hard time walking away, because that next one might be something I’ve never seen before, like capturing two red-eyed tree frogs in amplexus.”

“The biggest challenge of the rainforest at night is getting lost,” McGinnis warns. “So I always get a guide, as there is incredible value in going out with someone who knows their way around the rainforest. Not to mention the danger hiding on the ground and in the trees. Central America has lots of venomous snakes. You grab the wrong thing, you step in the wrong place, and you could be in trouble. A local guide knows how to keep you from accidentally running into one of them.”

Rain is a given in a rainforest. “Photographing in the rain is challenging because it can be harder to find subjects or keep your clothing dry, but luckily I never have to worry about my camera gear in the rain, which eliminates one of the challenges,” he notes. With IP53-rated weather sealing, his OM-1 Mark II can keep shooting through snow, dust, and rain. “I’ve been shooting days on end in pouring, torrential rain in Central America, and I’ve never had a problem with my gear.”

When McGinnis isn’t finding small subjects in the Central American rainforest, he also helps lead multi-day macro retreats with Lisa and Tom Cuchara and other OM SYSTEM ambassadors, on fellow ambassador Steve Morello’s property in southern Maine, as well as other locations. One of his favorite subjects on Morello’s property is dragonflies.

“If you find a dragonfly as the sun’s coming up, they’re not warm yet,” McGinnis advises. “They are cold and they might have dew on their wings, making it harder for them to fly. They’re almost like sitting ducks at that time, and much easier to photograph than in the heat of the day, when they’re busy hunting.”

He also searches his own backyard after dark, where wolf spiders and other hunting spiders give themselves away. Their eyes have a reflective layer that bounces a flashlight beam straight back, a glint known as eyeshine.

“In my own backyard, I love to photograph spiders and praying mantises after dark,” he continues. “Spiders hunt at night, and they are easier to find than most people realize. If you hold your flashlight up to the side of your head and scan the grass about 10 to 15 feet ahead of you, the eyeballs of a spider will flicker back at you. In the pitch black, all you’re looking for is those little diamonds in the grass. Having the flashlight right to the side of your eye keeps your line of sight in line with the beam of the flashlight.”

Some of McGinnis’s favorite discoveries don’t happen until he’s back at his computer. “I was trying to get this mantis to cooperate, and I ended up with tons of shots of it in different poses.”

“As I was reviewing them, I noticed a little something else in the shot that was invisible to my naked eye,” he continues. “That mite on its head is very, very tiny, just one or two millimeters. That’s actually happened to me many times, and finding those tiny surprises when I zoom in on the computer is one of the best things about macro photography.”

McGinnis says this is a great way for both beginners and seasoned macro photography veterans to practice and capture unique photos. “Go outside with a flashlight and scan the yard, and you’re bound to find something. No matter where you live, especially in the summertime, go out after dark with your camera and a light. On a nice, warm summer night, it’s just really fun. I’m exploring this small world, and through that exploration, new things reveal themselves. That’s what keeps me going back. Even if it’s my backyard, a local park, or a tropical rainforest, I’m always discovering new things and finding interesting stories in the small world that most other photographers walk by.”

Is Palantir Stock a Buy After its Latest Earnings Report?

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Revenue nearly doubled, but a slowdown in contract growth has investors on edge.

I've liked Palantir Technologies (PLTR -1.65%) stock for a long time and even predicted earlier this year that it would reach $220 before the end of 2026.

Unfortunately, after the company's third-quarter earnings report, it appears that prediction won't come true. But I'm still holding my shares.

Despite another quarter of outstanding revenue growth, Palantir shares fell more than 5% following the company's Q3 report and are now 16% below their all-time high. Investors appeared concerned about a slowdown in the company's total contract value (TCV), which increased just 6% year over year after jumping 49% in the second quarter.

Does that mean Palantir's remarkable growth story is starting to lose steam? Not necessarily.

Palantir's third-quarter revenue was $2.31 billion, up 96% from a year earlier. U.S. commercial revenue grew 148% to $984 million, while U.S. government revenue increased 96% to $953 million. For the first time, Palantir generated more revenue from its U.S. commercial customers than from its U.S. government business.

I've long argued that Palantir's Artificial Intelligence Platform (AIP) is revolutionary because it helps companies put AI to work in their actual operations. Rather than simply generating text or answering questions, AIP allows businesses to connect AI models with their existing data and use them to make decisions and improve efficiency.

And the results suggest that businesses are increasingly willing to pay for those capabilities. Palantir's U.S. commercial customer count increased 29% year over year to 686, while the company closed 212 deals worth at least $1 million during the quarter.

The company also raised its full-year revenue guidance to between $8.53 billion and $8.534 billion, representing approximately 91% growth.

Those aren't the numbers of a company whose business is losing momentum.

There's a legitimate reason investors are paying attention to Palantir's contract numbers. Total contract value measures the potential value of contracts signed during a period, including certain contract options. It's an important indicator of future business, although not all of that value represents guaranteed revenue.

Palantir's third-quarter TCV reached $2.92 billion, increasing just 6% year over year. That was a sharp slowdown from the 49% growth reported in the second quarter.

CFO Dave Glazer explained on the company's earnings call that Palantir signed several large contracts that were shorter than usual as customers began adopting its new Sovereign AI capabilities. The company expects those agreements to grow in size and duration as customers expand their use of the technology.

That makes sense, but it's still something investors should watch. Shorter contracts can reduce reported TCV even when customers are spending more on Palantir's software.

Meanwhile, Palantir's total remaining deal value increased 55% year over year to $13.4 billion, suggesting its existing business continues to expand.

Still, Palantir's premium valuation leaves little room for disappointment. The stock has a forward price-to-earnings ratio of 108 times and a forward price-to-sales multiple of 51 times. If contract growth remains sluggish, investors may begin questioning whether the company's extraordinary revenue growth can continue.

I've been bullish on Palantir for years, and its transition toward a larger commercial business is one of the biggest reasons.

Government contracts provide an important foundation, particularly as Palantir expands its work with the Pentagon through programs such as Maven. But the commercial market offers opportunities across industries, from manufacturing and healthcare to financial services. AIP gives Palantir a compelling way to pursue those opportunities.

My $220 price prediction for 2026 will probably miss the mark. I'm not thrilled about it, but I can accept it. But Palantir remains one of my cornerstone holdings because I believe the long-term opportunity is much larger than any single year's stock-price target.

A disappointing quarter for contract growth deserves attention. It doesn't erase the company's progress in turning AI into a practical business tool -- or my conviction in Palantir's long-term prospects.

NASA astronauts voted in the 2026 midterms from space. Here’s how.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Election Day reached beyond Earth this year.

Three NASA astronauts aboard the International Space Station cast ballots in the 2026 midterm elections, with astronaut Anil Menon sharing a look at his orbital voting setup on Tuesday, Nov. 3. His polling place? The same small compartment where he sleeps.

In a video posted to X, Menon showed how he used his computer to complete an electronic ballot and send it to Harris County, Texas. Fellow astronauts Jessica Watkins and Luke Delaney also voted from the station.

"We filled out an electronic ballot and sent it securely to the election officials," Menon wrote.

So how does a vote get from orbit to an election office? It takes a few more stops than a trip to the mailbox.

According to NASA, astronauts can request an absentee ballot using a Federal Post Card Application. Once completed aboard the station, their electronic ballot travels through the agency’s Near Space Network, which also carries other communications between the station and Earth.

The ballot passes through NASA's Tracking and Data Relay Satellite System to a ground antenna in New Mexico, then heads to Mission Control in Houston before reaching the appropriate county clerk. NASA says the ballot is encrypted and accessible only to the astronaut and the clerk.

This isn't a new Election Day workaround. Texas lawmakers cleared the way for astronauts to vote from orbit in 1997. That year, NASA astronaut David Wolf became the first American to do so while aboard Russia’s Mir space station.

Nearly three decades later, the space station's sleeping quarters are still pulling double duty for democracy.

Suno Says Jason Isbell Lawsuit Over Artist ‘Identities’ Must Be Dismissed: ‘Nothing Unlawful About That’

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The AI music company has filed its first major response to the novel lawsuit, which accuses it of violating musicians' likeness rights.

Suno is asking a judge to throw out a lawsuit claiming the AI music firm stole the artistic identities of Jason Isbell and other stars, arguing there’s “nothing unlawful” about seeking to replicate the “vibe” of a particular artist.

Isbell sued in August, accusing Suno of allowing users to “conjure up a beloved musician’s identity” by entering their name as a prompt in its AI song generator. It was a novel case centered on an artist’s likeness rights, unlike earlier litigation against AI labs over the use of copyrighted music to train models.

But in Suno’s first major response on Tuesday (Nov. 3), its lawyers say there is “nothing unlawful” about merely using an artist’s name to describe music, any more than it’s illegal for a human artist to say “let’s go for something here with more of a Jason Isbell vibe.”

“When people describe styles of music, they often use artists’ names as shorthand for the genre they perform in,” Suno’s lawyers write in a motion obtained and first reported by Billboard. “This case turns on the premise that the law somehow condemns that universal practice. In reality it does not; the First Amendment would not allow it to.”

Suno’s motion, which asks the judge to dismiss the case immediately, argues that Isbell’s lawsuit attacks “ubiquitous methods of communicating ideas” and would hurt the “creative and cultural ecosystem” if allowed to proceed.

An attorney for Isbell did not immediately return a request for comment on Tuesday.

Isbell and other artists sued in August, accusing Suno of violating artists’ likeness rights (also known as publicity rights) by encoding their entire musical personas as tokens in its AI model. The case was filed as a class action, seeking to potentially represent “millions” of other artists: “The platform is built to identify musicians by name and generate identity-evocative output on command. This is its product.”

Suno was already facing a slew of lawsuits from major music companies, who claim the company violated their federal copyrights by ingesting millions of songs to train their models. The new case was different, accusing Suno of breaching state laws in New York, California, Tennessee and elsewhere that protect a person’s name, image and likeness from commercial exploitation.

Suno says it doesn’t actually allow users to prompt its AI model with specific artist names. When users do enter artist names, the platform says it translates that request into “descriptive musical characteristics,” which can then be used to direct the model to generate a song. A prompt of “Duran Duran,” for example, might get redirected into one of “New wave, Synth-pop, Pop rock.”

Isbell’s lawsuit claims those safeguards are faulty and can be duped by merely using spaces, such as Taylor Swift with “t a y l o r s w i f t” or Bad Bunny with “b a d b u n n y.” Even when a name is translated into musical styles, Isbell says the model clearly still exploits an artist’s identity: “Suno built and trained a model to index musicians by name. It encoded musicians’ identities into its AI model to create its commercial product without consent.”

But in Tuesday’s response, Suno says those accusations are legally deeply flawed. Simply using the name of a real person as a “shorthand to convey information about the genre that they want to create in,” Suno’s attorneys argue, is not the kind of thing that has historically violated state-law likeness rights.

“The law does not prevent people from using names to find or describe information, nor does it prevent building a tool capable of recognizing those references and acting on the information they supply,” Suno’s lawyers write, arguing that a ruling to the contrary would violate the First Amendment’s freedom of speech.

Even if likeness rights worked that way, Suno says the case would still be doomed by federal “preemption,” a legal doctrine that bars state-level claims that interfere with existing federal laws. Allowing artists to sue to assert their state-level likeness rights in such a way, Suno says, would overlap too much with federal copyright law’s existing protections for creative works.

“Congress made the considered policy choice not to confer intellectual property protection on an artistic style, precisely to allow subsequent creators to build on the prior works of a genre,” Suno says. “[This lawsuit] would create a shadow state law IP regime … fundamentally conflicting with the federal regime that has been in place for more than two centuries.”

The Origins of ‘Elf’: How Will Ferrell Came to Replace Chris Farley in the Christmas Classic

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In an exclusive excerpt from his new oral history, comedian Andy Richter — who has a small part in the movie — retraces the unlikely birth of the 2003 holiday hit.

The following is excerpted from Elf Unwrapped: Behind the Scenes of a Modern Christmas Classic – Andy Richter‘s Oral History of the Making of Elf the Film That Became a Holiday Classic, by Andy Richter (who had a small a role in the hit 2003 comedy) with Aaron Kino.

The story of Elf begins like most Christmas tales do — with a Jewish kid from Philadelphia. In 1993, an aspiring screenwriter named David Berenbaum moved from Philly to L.A. with dreams of making it big. He had no connections, no experience, and for the most part, no clue how to make it in “the biz.” But what he did have was an overactive imagination and a brain brimming with fanciful ideas. Related Stories Beyond the Book Book to Scream: How Literature Has Fueled 35 Years of Universal Orlando's Halloween Horror Nights Movies Before 'Star Trek,' Patrick Stewart Proved His Sci-Fi Chops in 'Dune'

David was a cockeyed dreamer, as many of us are when we land in Hollywood, but David just felt completely out of his element. Compared to the East Coast, L.A. is just . . . weird. No one seems to have a real job here, but they all talk like they’re showbiz big shots. People act like they’re your friend but then look past you mid conversation, just in case someone better walks by. David was lonely, especially when the holidays rolled around — which are even weirder in L.A. Suddenly, it was an unholy eight- five degrees around Christmastime! To cope, he rented a bunch of Christmas movies on VHS so he could watch them over and over. He binged classics like It’s a Wonderful Life, Miracle on 34th Street, Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer — anything to feel a bit more Christmassy. Feeling inspired, he sat down to write his first Christmas film: a screwball romantic comedy called Christmas in New Jersey, which centered on two people having the worst days of their lives, who decide to completely start over as if they had never been born (very It’s a Wonderful Life coded).

Fortunately for him, that script actually got optioned — which is when a production company or studio pays you a little bit of money for the “option” of one day making the movie and then paying you a lot of money. Unfortunately, 99 percent of optioned projects never get made, and that was the case with Christmas in New Jersey. The good news, though, is that it opened up a ton of doors for David. Impressed by the quality of his writing, the film executives he met with kept asking him, “Okay, what’s next?” “Next??” David thought. “Damn, I better come up with something else!” Luckily, he was still in that Christmas frame of mind when he started working on his next big idea: the script that would eventually become Elf.

When David first started writing Elf in 1995, he was writing on spec. In Hollywood terms, “spec” means “speculative” — in other words, no one was paying him to write this script, so he was doing the work for free, hoping that he’d be able to sell it down the line. And though he loved a lot of the holiday movies he’d been bingeing, it was ultimately a 1964 Rankin Bass TV special that really inspired this particular project.

For some background: Rankin Bass was this quirky little production company that basically dominated Christmas TV programming for a couple of decades. If you’re a fan of Elf (and holiday movies in general), you’ll probably recognize their stuff right away. They specialized in stop-motion specials, the kind where the puppets move one frame at a time. Their most famous production, Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer, has been on TV every December since LBJ was in the White House. But they also made Frosty the Snowman, Santa Claus Is Comin’ to Town, and The Year Without a Santa Claus — basically all those herky-jerky yet very magical holiday staples that feel like fever dreams nowadays but absolutely shaped everybody’s idea of what Christmas looks and sounds like.

Rankin Bass’s rendition of Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer (adapted from a 1939 booklet published by the department store Montgomery Ward) follows the time-honored tale of Rudolph, a poor reindeer who gets mocked by all the other reindeer because of his glowing and very shiny red nose. After those mean bullies cast him out, he stumbles across a fellow misfit named Hermey, an elf who longs to be a dentist. Rejected by their peers at Santa’s workshop, they set out on an adventure, stumbling upon the Island of Misfit Toys and running into now-classic characters like the Abominable Snow Monster along the way. When they finally return home, Rudolph ends up saving Christmas by using his nose to guide Santa’s sleigh through a terrible snowstorm, proving that what made Rudolph different is exactly what made him special.

David knew what it felt like to be a misfit; after all, it wasn’t that long ago that he’d been working in a lingerie store, sweating in eighty-five-degree heat at Christmas. Essentially, he saw himself in Rudolph and wanted to create a character of his own that followed a similar sort of hero’s journey. Rudolph’s innocence also reminded David of Josh Baskin, Tom Hanks’s character in Big: a somewhat whimsical weirdo who doesn’t really fit in. Thanks to his wish being granted by a magical fortune-telling machine, Josh is quite literally bigger than all his peers, and this is also what sparked the idea of a giant (well, human-sized) elf named Buddy.

So, at this point, David has an idea with a great hook: a human who is raised as an elf in the North Pole. It’s a funny concept, it’s packed with potential for physical humor, and it also has this “fish out of water with a Christmas twist” angle that hasn’t really been done before. But as excited as he was in the moment, David knew he needed to flesh it out wayyyy more. He didn’t want it to just be a one-note gag. Plus, after watching all those Christmas movies, he realized the ones that really stuck around — the ones that reached the pantheon — had a little something extra.

In the original drafts of the script, when Buddy left the North Pole in search of his human family, both his mother and father were still alive. This presented a few problems — namely that it required a whole lot more explaining. It seems believable that a man might not know he had a kid, but what had Mom been doing for the past thirty years? Searching for her missing baby? Or just going on with her life, sipping margs by the pool and pretending nothing happened? Either way, it doesn’t exactly scream “jolly good fun.”

So David decided to follow in the footsteps of Cinderella and Snow White and pretty much every Disney movie and Grimm fairy tale ever — and killed off Buddy’s mom. Focusing the script around Buddy’s relationship with his dad made a lot of sense story-wise, but even more than that, it was a deeply personal choice for David. His dad had passed away when he was young, and this idea of Buddy searching for his father really resonated with him.

Ultimately, it was this emotional yet endearing through line that elevated Elf from a funny premise or silly sketch into a full-blown movie with the potential for real staying power. Before, the drafts felt a lot more jokey, but with that last piece in place, the script finally transformed into what he’d wanted it to be all along: a holiday comedy with a whole lot of heart.

DAVID BERENBAUM, SCREENWRITER: I just went back to what would be the most personal connection for me — my dad’s passing — so that’s why I made that choice. Once I found that, the writing became a whole lot easier and I had something that I thought was good. Plus, I thought, comedically, it’d be funny for Buddy to go, “I’m here, Dad!”

In 1996, after writing many (many, many, many) drafts of the script, David sold his screenplay for millions of dollars, and it was smooth sailing right into production.

Of course that’s not what happened. Instead, the film got stuck in “development hell,” the term for projects in Hollywood that languish with production companies and studios for years and never actually get the green light.

First, Elf was optioned by the Motion Picture Company of America (aka MPCA), a production company known for making critically acclaimed classics like the Academy Award–nominated Ulee’s Gold (starring Peter Fonda) and Beverly Hills Chihuahua 2 (starring George Lopez). It was while MPCA held the reins that the very first actor was attached (or in Hollywood speak, “verbally agreed”) to play Buddy: the one and only Chris Farley, fresh off Saturday Night Live. Once he was on board, David began refining the script with Chris in mind, and, at several points, was even scheduled to sit down with Chris himself so they could go over the new material. Alas, that meeting never materialized, and after a year, MPCA’s exclusive option period expired, relegating the what-if of Chris Farley starring in Elf to Hollywood legend.

Then, another production company optioned Elf, but after that endeavor didn’t work out either, the revised script earned David acceptance into the prestigious Disney Writing Program. This program brings in a couple of writers each year and has them rewrite whatever is in production at the time. It’s a trial by fire—Disney-style—but it’s also an incredible opportunity. David spent two years there, working on anything and everything they threw his way; in fact, one film he wrote there — The Haunted Mansion, starring Eddie Murphy—actually came out a few weeks after Elf. During this time, Disney also had the opportunity to make Elf, but alas, once again, for the innumerable and inscrutable reasons that most Hollywood projects die, it never came to fruition.

Luckily, the wait to bring Elf to life would soon be over, once a fairy godmother in the form of another SNL legend came along.

The people who eventually brought Elf to the big screen came from wildly different corners of Hollywood, from nobodies to legends, executives to improvisers, Comedy Central to Saturday Night Live. It was a jolly band of misfits, and the strange alchemy that united them — part design, part luck — would end up paying incredible dividends.

In 1999, a subversive sitcom called Strangers with Candy premiered on Comedy Central. Cocreated by a group of Chicago improv comedians (including Stephen Colbert and future Elf scene-stealer Amy Sedaris), the show was an offbeat, crass send-up of after-school specials — those preachy ’90s TV movies that aired right when kids got home from school and taught very obvious lessons about very serious issues. Strangers with Candy set out to offend audiences everywhere — and it did so brilliantly. There’s this truly unhinged episode in the third and final season in which Amy’s character discovers that she has Native American heritage and tries to learn more about her “roots.” I make a cameo in this episode, as does a tall, goofy up-and-coming SNL cast member named Will Ferrell. This was actually the first time Will and I worked on the same project. But more importantly (at least for the tale at hand), Strangers with Candy was produced by Comedy Central executive Kent Alterman, who would also go on to play a pivotal role in the Elf saga. So maybe the true reward of making weird television that doesn’t quite get its due is the friends you make along the way.

In 2000, David Berenbaum sat down with Jon Berg, a fresh-faced manager/producer who loved David’s Elf script — even if nobody wanted to make it, yet. Both Jon and David were nobodies at the time, but Jon was really impressed by David’s script and offered to become his manager. Before long, Jon and his producing partner Todd Komarnicki came on board as producers — essentially agreeing to hustle, unpaid, to try to get the movie made (and then get paid). Obviously, finding just the right Buddy would be crucial to getting this thing off the ground, and the two of them had a very specific person in mind for the role.

WILL FERRELL (BUDDY): The first time I heard about Elf was when I got a call from Julie Wixson Darmody in Jimmy Miller’s office. She was like, “Hey, there’s this script. The basic premise is a human raised at the North Pole who has to go to New York. It probably needs some work, but you should take a look.” I read it and I was like, “Oh yeah. I see what you mean. This could be something . . .”

Fresh European Fridge Attacks Put Cold-Chain Security in Focus

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Beyond food storage, the same combination of Internet connectivity, remote access, and inadequate security practices can put temperature-controlled infrastructure across other critical sectors at risk.

A second wave of attacks on supermarket refrigeration systems across Europe this week has heightened concerns about the broader "cold chain's" vulnerability to similar attacks, given that supermarkets aren't the only businesses that rely on cold storage.

As in mid-October, an unknown threat actor appears to have gained access to refrigeration controllers at multiple European supermarkets and switched the systems to defrost mode, raising temperatures to 50 degrees Celsius (122 degrees Fahrenheit) and causing food spoilage.

Reporting by the BBC, Reuters, CNBC, and other outlets suggest the latest attacks were smaller in scope compared to the first wave in October that impacted more than 700 retail and grocery stores primarily in France. A smaller number of stores in Belgium, Germany, the Netherlands, Portugal, Poland and Spain were also caught up in the October attacks.

BBC reported the new attacks as impacting "dozens of supermarkets" in seven European countries including Belgium, Luxembourg, Estonia, Ireland, Sweden and Finland. Spokespersons from several supermarket chains like France's Carrefour, Belgium's Ahold Delhaize and Colruyt Group, and Sweden's Coop chains confirmed varying but relatively limited impacts from the new attacks.

In a Nov. 2 statement, Carrefour said a cyberattack targeting its refrigeration systems had impacted 15 out of 700 stores in Belgium. In an undated statement, US-based Copeland, which manufactures technologies for regulating commercial refrigeration systems, alluded to a cybersecurity incident affecting refrigeration systems in France, which it said related to a discontinued generation of supervisory controllers.

No individual or group has claimed responsibility for either the first or the second wave of attacks, and the motivation behind it remains a mystery. European authorities are currently investigating the attacks.

Researchers, including those at France's CERT, have linked the hacks so far to Internet-connected refrigeration controllers made by Denmark-based Danfoss, Italy-based Dixell, and Copeland. Organizations using these controllers often connect them to the Internet to enable remote maintenance and support, but in doing so also potentially expose them to unauthorized access and remote attack by threat actors.

Risks include unpatched vulnerabilities in these technologies that attackers can exploit, like three in Danfoss' AK-SM 800A controllers that researchers at Claroty disclosed this August and another two they reported the same month in Copeland controllers. Weak configurations and poor security hygiene are also other major problems.

Related:AI Takes Control of Robotic Arm in 16 Minutes Flat

"Misconfigurations and systemic hygiene failures represent the vast majority of the threat surface," says John Gallagher, vice president at operational technology (OT) and Internet of Things (IoT) security vendor Viakoo. "While unpatched vulnerabilities exist in older firmware, they are rendered dangerous only because basic architectural hygiene was skipped, especially [when] making them Internet-facing."

Common mistakes include widespread use of default passwords, vendor-shared service codes, or static credentials across hundreds of locations makes brute-forcing trivial, Gallagher adds.

The weaknesses are not confined to supermarket refrigeration systems. The same combination of Internet connectivity, remote access, and inadequate security practices can put temperature-controlled infrastructure across other critical sectors at risk, according to security experts.

Sean Tufts, field chief technology officer (CTO) at Claroty, points out that "pharmaceutical storage and distribution, food distribution centers, and industrial facilities all rely on connected operational technology to maintain precise environmental conditions."

Related:EU Grocers Pay a Steep Price for Unpatched Fridge Firmware

The level of exposure will vary depending on how these systems are deployed, connected, and secured. But many organizations that rely on remotely accessible refrigeration or temperature-control equipment face similar challenges around asset visibility, third-party access, and securing legacy systems, Tufts says. "The same types of weaknesses can exist across different industries, even if the equipment and operational requirements differ."

The potential consequences of a compromise, says Tufts, can vary by sector.

"A disruption to pharmaceutical storage could compromise temperature-sensitive medicines, while an incident affecting food distribution could lead to spoilage and supply chain disruptions," he notes. "Industrial environments may face additional operational consequences depending on the materials and processes involved."

The supermarket attacks are a reminder that refrigeration and temperature-control systems are part of essential operations and not just back-of-house equipment, Tufts adds. Organizations should identify these assets and know how they connect to the Internet and third parties.

"Where remote access is necessary, use a VPN, strong authentication, or a centralized secure-access tool with session tracking." Changing default passwords, keeping firmware current, and segmenting are also recommended security practices, he says.

Gallagher says that while it's unclear how the attacks on the commercial refrigeration systems played out, the most straightforward theory is the threat actor likely compiled a target list by scanning the public Internet for specific controller fingerprints, such as those tied to Danfoss, Dixell, and Copeland. They then likely deployed an automated script across that target list to exploit known web vulnerabilities or push default credentials, triggering the defrost mode simultaneously.

Gallagher says the same attacks that worked on commercial refrigeration systems can potentially work across almost any form of OT and IoT systems. That's because these systems often are managed outside of IT, lack automated controls to keep firmware and passwords updated, and have porous network borders. "The growth over the last three years in operational ransomware and in cyberattacks causing physical damage shows that these attacks are on the rise and not specific to any one kind of system."

NASA is reviving discussions to rescue the Hubble Space Telescope, which is operating well but is on track to reenter Earth's atmosphere about six years from now.

The agency issued a request for information (RFI) on Oct. 29 for "in-space servicing" capabilities, which could include boosting Hubble's orbit (which is naturally degrading due to atmospheric drag), or even a mission to "enhance its capabilities" in some way.

Hubble, which launched in April 1990 in partnership with the European Space Agency, was serviced five times by astronauts on space shuttle missions, the most recent being in 2009. The observatory's groundbreaking achievements include helping to show that the expansion of the universe is accelerating — a discovery that won the Nobel Prize — as well as a swath of finds about planets, galaxies, stars and other parts of the universe.

Hubble also regularly teams up with other telescopes for science work, and its observations have led to the publication of at least 23,000 scientific papers, NASA figures suggest. And, despite its age, Hubble remains in high demand. "Astronomers request about seven times as much observing time as is available each year," the agency stated in September.

Hubble is also widely appreciated by the public. People have long enjoyed and celebrated its iconic images, such as the Eagle Nebula's "Pillars of Creation," a gorgeous shot that has been revisited several times over the decades. As such, this is not the first time in recent years that discussions have opened about keeping the famed observatory running for a while longer.

In 2022, for example, billionaire tech entrepreneur Jared Isaacman proposed using SpaceX hardware to mount a Hubble-servicing mission. (Isaacman also purchased and commanded two crewed SpaceX missions to Earth orbit, which flew in September 2021 and September 2024.) NASA declined, however, citing possible risks to the healthy observatory.

But things have changed, apparently. Isaacman, who in December 2025 became NASA administrator, said in an Oct. 30 agency statement that Hubble's science return means the agency should "be looking at every reasonable opportunity to keep it delivering for as long as possible" — especially if doing so would be a cost savings compared with launching another mission.

In an Oct. 30 post on X, Isaacman added that it's "still worth exploring the options" and noted that NASA had previously considered "a far lower-cost solution" to keep the observatory flying. "That was then. Still worth the effort — Hubble deserves it," he added.

The new RFI also allows for alternative scenarios, however, which could include a "safe, controlled reentry into Earth's atmosphere or orbital disposal at the end of Hubble's mission."

Hubble will naturally enter the atmosphere in 2033 or 2034 due to orbital drag, so any attempt to service it would have to be in 2032, the agency stated.

Earlier this year, NASA worked with Katalyst Space Technologies on a $30 million mission that aimed to boost the orbit of the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory, which is also falling back to Earth due to orbital drag.

That long-shot rescue mission — which was developed quickly to race against accelerated solar activity, which ballooned Earth's atmosphere and increased drag — launched successfully this summer. But control issues prevented Katalyst's LINK spacecraft from meeting up with Swift. Swift is expected to reenter Earth's atmosphere tomorrow (Nov. 4) after 22 years of operations — far past its two-year design lifetime.

What My Job Is Like as a Fact Checker at Bon Appétit

It takes a village to publish a magazine—and website and app and Youtube channel and more. Phew! Today we’re introducing you to Bon Appétit’s research director Ryan Harrington.

For the non-journalists out there: What does research and fact-checking actually mean?

At Bon Appétit, every story or recipe goes through multiple edits. After that, it’s eligible for research review. At that stage, there are a few major things me and my team looks out for:

Information accuracy: This includes checking the spelling of names and brands, making sure descriptions of restaurant menus and dishes are up to date, reviewing the credibility of the sources we point to, verifying portrayals of quoted individuals, and anything else to make sure that when you read a Bon Appétit story, you know it’s true.

Cultural sensitivity: For a long time, food media has been dominated by a certain privileged perspective. This perpetuated a system that sort of relived the Age of Explorers, with people “discovering” new foods and pigeonholing or exotifying other cultures. A big part of research is honoring different experiences—giving people a platform without making it a spectacle.

Legal concerns: The research department is a bridge between our actual legal team and the editorial team. We keep an eye out for things like libel issues, copyright infringement, food safety risks, protecting sources, or anything else that might need to be discussed with lawyers.

And for the aspiring journalists: How do you become a fact checker? What did you do before joining the Bon Appétit team?

I finished undergrad with a bachelor’s degree in chemistry and then spent six years managing operations of a thoracic surgery laboratory, studying forms of lung cancer. I reached a point in that career where I was asked to go back to school for a master’s or PhD. I realized if I were to invest all of my time in further education, I wanted to study something I felt really strongly about. And that was food media.

I entered the NYU Food Studies master’s program with the goal of studying the cultural impacts of food media. Early on, I got an opportunity to intern at Food & Wine. During my time there, I was asked to help with other projects beyond my internship, and that’s when I started thinking about food media not as just an academic subject but as a viable career. I eventually started working for other publications like Travel & Leisure and Lucky Peach (r.i.p.).

I also tried working at a brewery, though I had started there in February 2020. Dealing with the development of COVID was maybe not the best time to dip my toe in the industry, but I did get a better understanding of the pandemic’s impact.

When I saw the research director position at BA, I knew my previous experiences and education gave me a strong toolset to lead research efforts, and I am grateful for the opportunity. I’ve now been here five years.

You also teach food studies at NYU. How does that inform your work at Bon Appetit?

Whether I am building a lesson plan or reviewing a story for BA, I always try to understand things from the perspective of the recipient: the students or readers. Are we conveying the story clearly? What are they taking away from it? Guiding people to those lightbulb moments, from the classroom to the newsroom is why I love doing what I do.

It’s so cool that you started a research fellowship program here, so students can get real-world experience in media. Tell us more about that.

As mentioned, I got my start in the industry thanks to an internship. I never had formal journalism or media training, but it was thanks to that program that I could build relevant experience and make connections that opened up a chain of other opportunities. When I started at BA, it was my top priority in being able to pay it forward and help mentor a new generation of passionate food lovers.

I teach them the skills to be a great research editor and also try to give each fellow the freedom to pursue their own interests and network while here. I grew up watching Alton Brown’s Good Eats.
