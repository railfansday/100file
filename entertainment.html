10 Most Powerful Characters In Fantasy TV History

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The sky is the limit when it comes to fantasy television. Characters can be as powerful as the story allows, and, most of the time, that allowance is pretty darn extreme. It generally all comes down to the genre-required magic system of the individual series. While any fantasy must have such a system, the way it functions can differ drastically. The power scale in one world doesn't necessarily match that of another. Still, when looking at the fantasy landscape as a whole, there are some clear outliers.

Whether a fantasy TV show falls under the subgenres of high fantasy, urban fantasy, science fiction, or anything in between, there is generally a certain standard of power. Characters won't often be so powerful that they are entirely invulnerable, but it can get pretty close. The circumstances around these individuals typically have to be highly specific, since their very existence will often break the power scale. For the right character, however, it's entirely worth it.

The DC fantasy television series Lucifer ran from 2016 to 2021 and centered on the titular character, Lucifer Morningstar (Tom Ellis), as he abdicates his throne in Hell to live in Los Angeles. Lucifer isn't quite as powerful in the TV adaptation as he is in the original comic series, but he's still something pretty special.

Lucifer has superhuman strength, speed, and invulnerability, but even more jolting is his ability to make humans divulge their truest desires through eye contact alone. By the end of Lucifer, this character basically ascends to true god power. It doesn't get much bigger than that.

Because Supernatural is a monster-of-the-week TV series that managed to keep itself going for 15 excellent seasons, it should be no surprise that the power scale got completely out of control. Various characters and villains became unthinkably strong, but The Darkness, also known as Amara, really took the cake. She is the twin sister of God himself with near-infinite power and abilities. The Darkness can warp reality, snuff out other deities, and resist any spell or weapon.

There are few wizards as iconic as Merlin, and that's especially true in the British medieval fantasy-adventure drama that borrows the character's name. Merlin leaves behind the traditional image of an aged old man, instead diving into the story of a young, inexperienced Merlin of Camelot.

While Merlin doesn't quite have total control of his abilities in this version of the story, there is no question in Merlin that there is true, potentially terrible power here. His magic is raw, unfiltered, and rooted entirely in instinct. Merlin has elemental control and telekinesis, and can even sway the forces of life and death.

Netflix's Stranger Things is another TV series that took its power scale to a whole new level. This is most effectively represented by the character Eleven, who possesses god-level telekinesis that can achieve just about anything she desires. Eleven can throw heavy objects across the room or snap a man's neck with just her mind.

Additionally, Eleven can transfer her consciousness into a "void" that lets her eavesdrop on conversations around the world. Thankfully, Eleven is also full of empathy and shines in circumstances of great love. Otherwise, she would have been a horrifying force.

The long-running British television series Doctor Who is set entirely around the character known as the Doctor, though he (or they, really) comes in many different forms. The Doctor is a humanoid alien who travels across time and space in his sentient time machine called the TARDIS. This naturally gives the Doctor great access to the universe, but it goes far beyond just that.

Regeneration is the name of the game, and the Doctor can take on a brand-new physical form when the previous one is no longer livable. Add in that unyielding intelligence and strategic skills, and there isn't much that could ever stop the Doctor for long.

Prime Video's The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power dives deeper into the story of the Dark Lord Sauron, so we get to see more of his immense power than ever before. In the grand scope, Sauron isn't even the most powerful character in Tolkien's lore. However, in an age when greater villains like Morgoth have been contained, this new Dark Lord was free to execute his own, terrible will.

Game of Thrones gave us a lot of pretty powerful characters, and who could have thought that Bran Stark would end up above them all? The hit high-fantasy series spent a lot of time pushing the Starks to their limits, forcing them to grow in miraculous ways. Arya takes a second-place seat in some ways, thanks to her magical lethality. Still, no one quite compares to Bran, who can see anything and everything as it has and will be.

We wouldn't normally consider this classic character to be any more threatening than the Evil Queen or Maleficent. Once Upon a Time put quite a twist on things.

Rumpelstiltskin became a being known as The Dark One. The status granted him a seemingly unlimited reservoir of dark magic, allowing him to become a sort of puppetmaster of the wider world of fantasy. Rumpel was ultimately so overpowered that you never really knew in Once Upon a Time where he might turn up.

The Witcher is another fantasy TV show that is absolutely packed with powerful displays. Geralt of Rivia is quite a force, and we saw Yennefer cause absolute devastation on the battlefield. Ciri is a source of power in her own right and can use it to manipulate space and time. Though it hasn't played out on screen yet, she ultimately comes to be known as the Lady of Space and Time.

Loki was never a weakling, but the fact that he was repeatedly defeated and ultimately killed in the Avengers movies demonstrates that he wasn't anything too spectacular compared to other forces. This is no longer true for the particular Loki variant that serves as the protagonist in Disney+'s Loki. As the two-season series progresses, this God of Mischief undergoes a dramatic evolution.

Making sense of the cousins, fiancées, and adopted sons that keep us fascinated as viewers.

The Gilded Age is ultimately a story about society and class, so it’s unsurprising that family ties and marriages are central to the plot. That also means that it can be difficult to sort out who’s who and how they’re all related. As the fourth season continues to gather steam, here’s a guide to all the players in the Scott, van Rhijn, and Russell families.

Peggy grew up in a prosperous Black family but has a difficult relationship with her father, a pharmacist who is unhappy with her decision to become a writer. Peggy was previously married and had a son, Thomas, whom she believed was stillborn. She later learns that her father lied to her and that Thomas is alive and living with an adoptive family. She meets Marian Brook when she is traveling home to her family and begins living with the van Rhijn family. Peggy becomes a reporter for the prestigious Black newspaper, the Globe, and writes a novel. She becomes engaged to Dr. Kirkland, a physician from a prominent family who treats her when she is seriously ill.

Arthur is the father of Peggy and the husband of Dorothy. He was born into slavery but became the successful owner of a pharmacy in Brooklyn. He has a difficult relationship with his daughter.

Peggy’s mother, Dorothy, is a pianist from Maryland who meets her husband in New York City when he saves her from men who were following her. Peggy is her only child.

When Peggy learns that Thomas, the son she believed had died, is alive and living in Philadelphia with the Spring family that adopted him, he is 3 years old. Shortly before Peggy finds out about him, Thomas dies from scarlet fever along with his adoptive mother.

Dr. Kirkland is a physician whom Peggy’s parents bring to treat her when she has a serious respiratory illness. He knew Peggy’s father when he was a child, and Arthur’s pharmacy career inspired him to become a doctor. He and Peggy date and then become engaged at the end of season 3.

Mrs. Kirkland, Dr. Kirkland’s mother, is from a wealthy Newport family. She expects her son to marry someone from a more pedigreed family than Peggy, and when she meets Mr. and Mrs. Scott, she treats them poorly, emphasizing her family’s status and Arthur’s former enslavement. She doesn’t approve of Peggy’s political activism.

Dr. Kirkland’s father is a pastor in Newport who grew up in a wealthy and well-known family.

Agnes is a widow and mother to Oscar. She was married to Arnold van Rhijn, an investment banker from a wealthy, early New York family. She is an aunt to Marian and lives with her sister Ada in a beautiful home on East 61st Street.

Ada is a devoted sister to Agnes, whom she lives with, and aunt to Marian and Oscar. When she meets Reverend Luke Forte, they marry quickly, and Ada begins living with him in the church rectory, which upsets the balance between her and Agnes. When her husband dies, Ada unexpectedly inherits a fortune, making her now wealthier than her sister.

Marian is the niece of Ada and Agnes, the daughter of their estranged brother. After her father dies, Marian finds out she’s been left no money, and she comes to live with her aunts, from whom she had been estranged. She becomes engaged to the widower Dashiell, who is Agnes’s nephew by marriage and Oscar’s cousin from his father’s side, but she later ends it. She has an on-and-off relationship with Larry Russell, George and Bertha Russell’s son.

An Episcopal reverend, Luke meets Ada after moving to New York from Boston and assuming a position at the Episcopal Church of Saint Thomas. He dies shortly after marrying Ada, leaving her the textile fortune he inherited from his mother.

Aurora, who is a major figure on the society scene, is a cousin of Oscar and the niece of Agnes. She becomes close with Marian through their family connections. Aurora is devastated when her husband, Charles, asks for a divorce.

He proposes to Gladys Russell, but he loses interest when he believes her family might go broke. Oscar has a secret relationship with John Adams IV, a descendant of the political family. In season 4, he marries Enid, a widow with a fortune who shares his interest in social climbing.

Enid reappears in season 2 as the wife of Joshua Winterton, an older man, and inherits his money and his place in society when he dies. When Enid gets to know Oscar, they become close and eventually marry, making her the daughter-in-law of Agnes.

A Cavalier King Charles spaniel, Pumpkin is the constant companion of Ada. Larry Russell rescues Pumpkin from a scary run-in with a horse.

Deadpool 2's Rob Delaney Debunks Popular X-Men Fan Theory About His Character Peter

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

No, X-Men fans, Peter from "Deadpool 2" and "Deadpool & Wolverine" is not Peter Wisdom. Peter actor Rob Delaney confirmed this in a red carpet interview with ScreenRant, and we offer our deepest nerd condolences to anyone who may have been invested in this.

The presence of Delaney's character Peter, a supporting player in David Leitch's 2018 action/comedy "Deadpool 2," is a joke unto itself. Deadpool (Ryan Reynolds) is assembling a team of superheroes called the X-Force that he intends to employ for a skydiving-based rescue mission. Deadpool is such a non-credible deadbeat, though, that he can't afford to be too choosy about who joins his team. He recruits characters like Shatterstar (Lewis Tan), Domino (Zazie Beetz), and Bedlam (Terry Crews), but also deigns to take on Peter, a regular, kind of dorky middle-aged guy with no superpowers.

When the trailer for "Deadpool 2" first dropped, /Film theorized that Delaney could be playing Peter Wisdom, an X-Men character with deep ties to X-Force. The character was first introduced in an issue of the Britain-set X-Men comic "Excalibur," dated February of 1995, and, like all comic book characters, he underwent multiple dramatic life events in a very short amount of time. He dated Shadowcat, became the mentor for X-Force, seemingly died, returned, and became the head of a spy agency. In the comics, Pete Wisdom can absorb heat from the environment around him and channel it into magical "hot knives" that protrude from his fingers.

The character also starred in his own series of comics in 2006 called simply "Wisdom," and he likely has at least a few fans out there. For those who think he appeared in the "Deadpool" movies, though, that wasn't Peter Wisdom. That was just Peter.

The issue of Peter's identity may be a little confusing, as sources like Wikipedia list Rob Delaney as definitely playing Pete Wisdom in both "Deadpool 2" and the 2024 follow-up "Deadpool & Wolverine." On IMDb, however, his proper credit of just "Peter" is retained. In the red carpet interview, Delaney was 100% unambiguous on the matter, saying:

"Peter Wisdom is a guy from the comics. My character is not Peter Wisdom. He's just a guy named Peter. And I think maybe they arbitrarily gave him the last initial 'W,' which explains the confusion. But in fact, [he's] just Peter, created from whole cloth by the writers."

Sometimes actors in Marvel Comics-based movies have to be coy about spoilers and plot details, but it seems that Delaney is not being coy in this case. He seems to genuinely be saying he isn't playing Pete Wisdom.

The arc of Delaney's actual character is pretty hilarious unto itself. Peter is recruited into X-Force, meets a grisly fate, is saved through the miracle of time travel, and then proves to be deeply beloved across the multiverse in "Deadpool & Wolverine." Peter turns up in that film's climax, which involves hundreds of parallel-universe Deadpools pouring through an interdimensional portal to fight each other. When Peter arrives on the scene, however, every Deadpool pauses to marvel at his presence. It seems that all Deadpools have a Peter in their home dimension, and that they all hold him in very high esteem. The hundreds of Deadpools embrace Peter, and he enjoys the attention.

With a universe as long-lived and complex as Marvel Comics, of course, some fans may happily chime in with an optimistic "never say die" attitude about Rob Delaney playing Pete Wisdom. Indeed, as "Deadpool & Wolverine" proved, every character in the Marvel Cinematic Universe has an untold number of parallel doppelgängers from all across the multiverse. Even if the original Pete Wisdom is a suave British James Bond-type with heat knife powers, and the movie Peter is a dippy American everyman, there's no reason why those characters can't be one and the same across the multiverse, right?

And, heck, "Deadpool 2" made a lot of vital changes to known comic book characters, including the aforementioned Shatterstar, a.k.a Rusty. In the comics, Shatterstar is an interdimensional traveler from Mojoworld, something he does explain in "Deadpool 2." But in the movie, he doesn't become a vital member of the team. Indeed, he has an unfortunate run-in with a helicopter. Deadpool, thereafter, quips that no one is going to miss Shatterstar. Nonetheless, the character returned for a very brief cameo in "Deadpool & Wolverine."

So why not do something like that for Pete Wisdom? As of this writing, /Film has been gathering information about the upcoming X-Men film from Marvel Studios, citing a familiar line-up and keeping track of pieces of casting. (Inde Navarrette from "Obsession" has been cast as the character of Rogue, for instance.) Time will tell if the makers of the new X-Men will have enough of a sense of humor to cast Rob Delaney as Pete Wisdom.

Before Captain America, Chris Evans Starred In An Underrated Non-Marvel Superhero Movie

Chris Evans is one of the most recognizable faces in superhero cinema, thanks largely to his portrayal of Steve Rogers in the Marvel Cinematic Universe. For nearly a decade, Evans helped bring the First Avenger to life, becoming a central figure in one of the biggest movie franchises of all time. However, his journey into the world of comic book-inspired adventures began long before he picked up Captain America's shield.

In 2005, Evans played Johnny Storm / The Human Torch, in Tim Story's Fantastic Four. He reprised the role in 2007's Fantastic Four: Rise of the Silver Surfer, bringing plenty of charisma and humor to the hot-headed member of Marvel's First Family. Nearly 20 years later, Evans stepped into the shoes of Johnny Storm again with a memorable cameo in Deadpool & Wolverine.

Between his appearances as the Human Torch and his eventual transformation into Captain America, however, Evans headlined another superhero adventure that deserves considerably more attention than it reserves. Released at a time when Hollywood was becoming increasingly fascinated with comic book stories about people with extraordinary abilities, this ambitious science-fiction thriller offered a decidedly different take on the genre.

It featured an intriguing mythology, an international setting, and a collection of characters whose powers made them anything but traditional superheroes. Unfortunately, the film never became the franchise starter its creators might have hoped for.

Push is a 2009 science-fiction action thriller directed by Paul McGuigan and written by David Bourla. Released in theaters on February 6, 2009, the film stars Evans alongside Dakota Fanning, Camilla Belle, and Djimon Hounsou. Rather than adapting an established comic book, Push creates an entirely original world populated by people with extraordinary psychic abilities. Its premise revolves around a secret government organization called Division, which captures and experiments on individuals with special powers in an effort to turn them into living weapons.

Evans plays Nick Gant, a young man with telekinetic abilities who has spent much of his life hiding from Division. His circumstances change when he encounters Cassie Holmes (Fanning), a teenage clairvoyant who can see glimpses of the future. Together, they search for Kira (Belle), a woman with the ability to manipulate people's thoughts and memories, while attempting to stay one step ahead of Division and its dangerous agents. As their mission develops, the characters must use their individual abilities to outsmart an organization that knows almost everything about their capabilities.

What makes Push particularly interesting is how it approaches the superhero genre. Instead of relying on familiar costumes, secret identities, and spectacular displays of power, the movie builds its mythology around different categories of psychic abilities. Telekinetics, clairvoyants, and mind controllers operate within a shared world, and their powers come with limitations that make confrontations more about strategy than brute strength.

The film’s Hong Kong setting also gives it a distinctive visual identity, combining neon-lit streets, cramped apartments, and elaborate action sequences. The result is closer to a supernatural espionage thriller than a conventional superhero blockbuster.

Despite its ambitious concept and talented cast, Push struggled to win over critics when it arrived in theaters. Its complicated mythology and purportedly convoluted storytelling became major points of criticism, with some reviewers arguing that the movie introduced too many ideas without giving them enough room to develop. Rotten Tomatoes currently lists the film with a 23% critics' score, reflecting the largely unfavorable response it received.

Nevertheless, dismissing Push entirely overlooks some of its most appealing qualities. Evans delivers a charismatic leading performance, while Fanning brings an intriguing combination of confidence and vulnerability to Cassie. The film's visual style, creative approach to psychic abilities, and willingness to experiment with familiar superhero conventions give it a personality that separates it from many other genre releases of its era. It also arrived during a particularly interesting period for superhero entertainment, when films like Iron Man and The Dark Knight were helping reshape audience expectations.

Push might not be a masterpiece, but it remains a fascinating chapter in Evans' expansive superhero career. Its original mythology and unconventional approach make it worth revisiting, particularly for viewers interested in seeing the actor outside his familiar Marvel roles. Before he became the face of Captain America, Chris Evans was already leading an ambitious superhero adventure that deserved a little more recognition.

The team with the second-best point differential in the NFL is the 49ers, at +58. The Jaguars’ point differential is 50 points better than the next-best team.

At 6-2, the Jaguars do not have the best record in the league, as the Bills, 49ers and Vikings are all 6-1. The 49ers are favored to beat the Raiders and improve to 7-1, and the Bills and Vikings play each other on Monday night, so the winner will be 7-1.

But at the midway point of the season, point differential is a better indication of a team’s quality, and a better predictor of a team’s future success, than win-loss record. That’s because with as small a sample size as half a season, a few plays in a few close games can skew the won-loss record, but a few plays don’t skew point differential. Historically, the teams that prove to be the best over the course of a season are the teams that win some blowouts, even if they also lose some close games.

Winning blowouts and losing close games is exactly what the Jaguars do. Both of the Jaguars’ losses were one-possession games that were tied in the fourth quarter, while the Jaguars’ wins have included some massive blowouts: The Jaguars’ 35-point win over the Colts was the biggest blowout of this NFL season, their 29-point win over the Patriots was the fourth-biggest blowout in the NFL this season, their 24-point win over the Browns was the seventh-biggest blowout in the NFL this season, and Thursday night’s 23-point win over the Ravens was the 11th-biggest blowout in the NFL this season.

The Jaguars have won four games by more than 21 points this season; no other team has more than one win by more than 21 points.

Jacksonville has a 3.5-game lead in the AFC South, and six of the Jaguars’ nine remaining games are against opponents with a losing record. For all that sci-fi television has become much more experimental narrative formulas in the age of streaming, one corner of the genre remains surprisingly attached to tradition: superhero shows. Even the series that deliberately lean into strangeness for a selling point do so with the promise that everything will eventually click into place and the true heroes and villains of the story will meet for a final showdown no matter how morally ambiguous they’d been up until that point.

That makes an often overlooked 2017-2019 FX series is deserving of much more recognition. Before Disney+ explored superhero surrealism through MCU shows like WandaVision and VisionQuest and Prime Video lambasted the entire genre with The Boys and Invincible, Legion was already dismantling expectations. Creator Noah Hawley was tasked with making a Marvel adaptation, and instead delivered a series that treats every scene as a chance to experiment. The show’s 27 episodes are dripping with confusion, discomfort, and unpredictability, and it makes for some thoroughly entertaining viewing.

In terms of the show’s premise, Legion follows David Haller (Dan Stevens), a psychiatric patient who discovers his mental health struggles are linked to extraordinary mutant abilities. While the series is loosely connected to Fox’s X-Men movies and draws from Marvel Comics mythology, Legion’s plot stands on its own, and Noah Hawley used that independence to turn what could have been a standard superhero TV show into something truly unique.

From the moment it begins, literally nothing about Legion plays into expectations surrounding its plot and narrative structure. For example, most superhero shows spend time establishing a status quo for reality before introducing something that threatens it. Legion makes reality itself the problem. Memories can be manipulated, seemingly ordinary spaces become mental prisons, and it’s always unclear whether a scene is actually happening or just playing out inside someone’s head. Because David’s mental state means he can’t trust his own experience, the same applies for viewers too.

This combination of atmospheric surrealism and an incredibly unreliable narrator defines how Legion’s story is told. The show’s 27 episodes include some incredibly avant-garde installments. Season 1's “Chapter 7” turns escape from a mental institution into a black-and-white silent film sequence, while “Chapter 10” in season 2 depicts the confrontation between David and central antagonist The Shadow King using dance. This psychedelic approach gives Legion’s psychic conflict its own unique style, making the show’s superpowered struggles more unsettling than a simple exchange of energy blasts or city-leveling fist fight.

It’s not only the scene-by-scene storytelling in Legion that flips expectations on its head. The wider narrative also dismantles a core theme of almost all superhero shows: discovering immense power means inheriting a heroic destiny. David eventually comes to see himself as burdened with glorious purpose, but his self-perception as a savior and the reality of his place in the world couldn’t be more different. His certainty that he deserves love and redemption increasingly justifies the harm he causes others, inverting the traditional message that power, heroism, and responsibility all go hand-in-hand.

Several superhero shows that arrived after Legion have also embraced unreliable perspectives, unconventional formats, and morally compromised "heroes". Few, however, are able to sustain the sense that even their storytelling methods can unexpectedly change at any moment across three whole seasons. Legion may not be the most accessible series out there, but its experimental style shapes the entire experience. Its underlying uncertainty is inseparable from its explorations of what ideas like “truth” and “reality” even mean when everyone’s perception is subjective, and this thematic interest in turn means it breaks almost every narrative rule.

Calling Legion a superhero show is accurate as a description of its source material. The show incorporates X-Men characters such as David himself, the Shadow King, and even Professor Charles Xavier. As a description of actually watching it, however, the “superhero TV show” label is incredibly misleading. Sure, there are genre staples like mutants, secretive organizations, and potentially catastrophic powers, but the show itself has little interest in using these elements to construct a good vs. evil superhero narrative about saving the day.

If one were to assess Legion in a vacuum without considering its X-Men roots, psychological horror is a much more appropriate genre label. David’s mind is a thoroughly unsafe place where childhood memories conceal terrifying intrusions from outsiders, and apparent understanding is often just a trick to disguise more secrets. Alongside psychological horror, Legion also works as an experimental sci-fi series, using concepts like psychic spaces and, eventually, time travel to explore identity, responsibility, and the desire to rewrite painful experiences.

There’s also romance to be found, but once again, this isn’t the kind of love story usually found in superhero shows. David’s relationship with Syd Barrett (Rachel Keller) initially seems like it will be the source of a glimmer of hope, that it’s going to be a tale of two isolated people finally finding connection. However, like everything else in Legion, the love story soon gives way to some unsettling explorations of themes like consent, bodily autonomy, and manipulation. Since David and other characters can violate boundaries in ways ordinary people physically cannot, this leads to some truly disturbing moments.

That makes Legion the perfect superhero TV show for anyone suffering from superhero fatigue. It’s not a superhero story: it’s a deep and complex drama that just so happens to center on characters who first appeared in comic books. Its three-season arc is a psychedelic tragedy that uses its character’s spectacular abilities as tools to explore some truly dark ideas. Legion may technically be a superhero series, but it’s one that feels ideal for viewers who openly balk at them.

The 10 Greatest Horror Thriller Opening Scenes, Ranked

Horror thrillers are a special kind of horror that needs to grip you in the plot right from the very beginning. It is essential to set up the story, build the suspense, and attract the audience's attention right from the opening scene in all genres of cinema. Horror particularly needs this, resulting in some of our favorite opening moments of all time.

The opening scene, when done right, can fill the viewers in on what they can expect and enjoy the absolute wonder they're about to experience for the rest of the story. Sometimes, the opening surpasses the rest of the film even. Horror has no short list of the most compelling opening scenes, but the best ones truly come from when horror meets thriller. No better genres mix suspense, atmosphere, and bone-chilling dread than these two when combined. They've resulted in some of the best openings horror has to offer. From one nail-biting sequence to the next, here are the ten best first scenes in horror-thriller history.

Ari Aster established himself as the king of thematic horror thanks to his 2019 cult classic Midsommar. This eerie slow-burner is the modern equivalent of Robin Hardy's The Wicker Man, combining slow-moving tension and shocking rug pullers. But unlike its iconic inspiration, Midsommar hits the ground running with one hell of a brutal opening tragedy.

The film starts with the protagonist, Dani (Florence Pugh), beginning her spiraling descent into grief and madness when her mentally struggling sister kills her parents and herself via carbon monoxide poisoning. It's a gun-punching opener highlighted by a slow, quiet first few moments followed by an intense transition to a graphic mass murder, accompanied by haunting music. This scene perfectly sets up the unexpected brutality of Aster's unique film.

The pinnacle of horror in the late 2010s, Jordan Peele's remarkable filmmaking debut, Get Out, is the modern masterpiece that so many films since have continued to reach for greatness. A thriller that constantly keeps you on the edge of your seat, its greatest strengths come from its ambiguity and unconventional storytelling. Hardly anywhere does this shine more than in its eerie and rather strange opener.

Instead of a gruesome kill or frightening introduction of an antagonist, it opens with an ordinary Black man (Lakeith Stanfield) walking down a dark street when he's kidnapped by a mysterious man in a knight's helmet. It doesn't establish the plot, and we don't even know who this young man is; we just witness a bizarre kidnapping that we don't get an answer for until much later in the story. Though this might seem unusual, it perfectly grips you into a one-of-a-kind psychological story that will surely leave you speechless by the end.

Horror saw an unbelievable change at the turn of the 21st century with James Wong's Final Destination. Easily one of the most paranoia-inducing horror films in history, this incredible nail-biter changed the game with its unique premise and unrelenting suspense, making viewers scared that anything terrible could happen at any moment. And it all starts with a highly tense opening scene, which has become cemented in horror history.

When high schooler Alex Browning (Devon Sawa) experiences a premonition of a horrific disaster before his class trip's plane takes off, he freaks out and escapes the departure along with a lucky few shortly before the plane actually explodes. This opener has become synonymous with tension building. It's perfectly set up through the use of subtle ominous foreshadowing and eerie leitmotifs, and the terrifying punch comes when Alex's vision comes true. It's a well-crafted, successful opener that jump-started this iconic horror franchise with a legendary boost.

Before reinventing the DC Universe, Zack Snyder dipped his hands in horror, and boy did he give it a considerable contribution when revitalizing the popularity of one of the greatest zombie movies of all time, Dawn of the Dead. His 2004 remake of the late George Romero's masterpiece is certainly more fast-paced, action-packed and intense than its 1978 original. The wild, chaotic madness of its thrilling opener certainly affirms it.

In perhaps the greatest opening scene in zombie history, our lead heroine, Ana (Sarah Polley), awakens to a world rapidly descending into chaos as the dead are quickly rising. This opener doesn't hold back, dropping you right into the confusing mayhem and keeping you feeling completely on edge. While arguably the rest of the film doesn't match the intensity of this iconic introductory sequence, it's still a perfect starter that hardly any other zombie film can top.

John Krasinski's A Quiet Place is the thrilling sci-fi chiller that continues to define horror in the 21st century. With a unique, frightening concept, endless suspense, and a constant sense of impending doom, this 2018 masterpiece knows how to scare us out of our wits, and it first achieved this with a suddenly tragic opener that continues to give us nightmares.

In a world devastated by an invading hostile alien species known as "The Death Angels," the Abbott family, led by their struggling patriarch Lee (Krasinski), are forced to adapt to this new way of living while trying not to make too much noise, or else attract the attention of these predators. Unfortunately, one crucial mistake leads their poor youngest, Beau (Cade Woodward), to suffer a gruesome fate right in front of them. This opening kill hits viewers like a truck, instantly establishing the severity of what happens when these characters make a sound. It's an unflinching and sudden gut-puncher that perfectly sets this story off on a high note.

Hailed as one of the scariest monster thrillers of all time, 2014's It Follows takes the cake for mounting dread and suspense. It is a masterclass in tension, telling a fresh, original, and horrifying story of an unstoppable entity slowly but surely walking towards its victim. Even its very first scene quickly establishes just how inescapable and bone-chillingly unsettling this idea is.

It starts with a young woman, Annie (Bailey Spry), chaotically rushing out of her house and across her neighborhood alone while looking around like something is walking straight towards her. The audience doesn't see through her perspective, nor is there an indication that something actually is following her. By the end of this scene, it cuts to a shocking image of Annie's mangled corpse on a beach. This startling scene quickly sets up the story but doesn't give anything away. It's a brilliant approach that instantly hooks the viewer into figuring out what did this to that poor girl.

The film that changed slashers forever, John Carpenter's indie horror masterpiece Halloween reshaped this legendary subgenre into the dominant force it is today. Featuring iconic moments and characters that have become synonymous with horror, Halloween gave audiences an unforgettably eerie thriller that kept them on the edge of their seats right off the bat.

In perhaps one of the most famous twists in all of cinema, the film starts from the perspective of an unknown killer as they slowly stalk and murder poor teenager Judith Myers (Sandy Johnson) in her home, before stepping outside to be unmasked by his parents as six-year-old Michael Myers. From Carpenter's eerie score to the unsettling tone and uninterrupted suspense, this perfect opener set up what made Halloween such a cherished icon of horror. It's a masterclass in using mood, cinematography, and music to create the best kind of terror.

Jason is relentless, but he is also predictable — and that is the gap you would exploit.

Michael watches before he moves. He is patient, methodical, and almost impossible to detect — until it's too late for anyone who isn't paying close enough attention.

‘Hinotama: Ball of Fire’ Review: Gentle, Handsomely Shot Domestic Drama Is a Promising Debut for Yaza Takenoshin

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The fresh-out-of-college director's first feature is inspired by the work of Ryusuke Hamaguchi, whose sometime DP Yoshio Kitagawa lends his expertise to proceedings.

The early moments of “Hinotama: Ball of Fire,” the debut feature from young Japanese director Yaza Takenoshin (who graduated from Harvard this past summer), almost move like clockwork, finding a pleasing rhythm in everyday mundanities — matching the repetition of numbers being called out for test papers to a mother having groceries counted at the checkout.

Showing in the Nippon Cinema Now selection — a space for new and upcoming Japanese filmmakers — at the Tokyo Film Festival, the film follows young Takeshi (Riku Onishi) as he watches his parents’ marriage crumble. It’s a slow but clearly inevitable process, the warning signs understated but clear to see. Takeshi is getting worn down by glimpses of his parents’ flaws, and becomes fascinated with the “ball of fire” that his old friend Akari (Himena Irei) tells him about — perhaps in the hope that seeing it will restore his childlike wonder about the world. Yaza has spoken of his admiration for filmmakers such as Ryusuke Hamaguchi, which is evident in his film’s neat and steady (but never inert or emotionless) camerawork and its patient observation of characters as they navigate fracturing relationships. The inspiration is rather direct: “Himotama” is indeed shot by Hamaguchi’s sometime cinematographer Yoshio Kitagawa, who offered to shoot the film when the student reached out for advice. Often, Kitagawa’s cinematography embraces symmetry, emphasizing the clean geometric lines of Takeshi’s home, picking an object or person to place at the centre of the frame. A lot of these orderly compositions soon give off the impression of a cage — if Mari Kusu’s performance as Takeshi’s mother, bristling with frustration, didn’t already imply as much.

Some terse dialogue soon cuts through that visual neatness, as Takeshi’s father hints at his dissatisfaction or frustration in a small aside — which then turns into a bigger fight, not long after as he “demands respect” and laments his children never thanking him. It all comes back to that earlier rhythm, a discontent from living as though on a factory line, where everything progresses with a subtle sense of automation and no feeling or passion. Takeshi drifts through all of these scenes without giving away his own dissatisfaction, until the dam bursts; destroying a pristine pair of sneakers by jumping in a muddy puddle, the moment all the more startling because of the stillness of the camera, and the length of Yaza’s takes to this point.

Takeshi’s father is a petty and proud man, in ways continually expanded upon throughout the film’s runtime, though how he’s actually framed never truly changes. Rather, Yaza patiently accumulates small details about him. He isn’t plainly a terrible, angry person, at least not right away anyway: The beginning of a propagandist campaign to his children for the eventual split comes with the leveraging of memories clearly meaningful to them. Yaza seeks subdued performances from his actors in general, particularly in the case of Onishi as the increasingly insular but troubled Takeshi, whose frustrations with his parents mostly manifest in questions about that titular ball of fire, when he’s not gazing into space.

Sometimes that muted approach can become a little overbearing — as in the shots of children standing in an orderly line as they watch Takeshi’s younger brother Kento (Koe Odani) walking to a new house. In moments, it feels a little unnatural, even compared to the deliberately uncomfortable family dynamic built in the film’s first third. A sudden jump forward to three months later destabilises the film to a greater extent, but there are still moments of pathos to be found, particularly as Kento is forced to grow up fast, taking initiative to try and alleviate the suffering of his family.

“Hinotama” eventually ends up taking one step further into a more allegorical tone, which doesn’t fit with the film’s stronger elements — notably, its gradual observation of the increasingly visible cracks in a traditional family unit. Still, it’s an incredibly promising first feature.

25 Years Later, Gandalf's Wisest Lord Of The Rings Quote Has No Equal In Fantasy Movie History

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

25 years later, Gandalf the Grey's words of wisdom in The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring still hold true. If Samwise Gamgee is the beating heart of the Fellowship, and Frodo Baggins its soul, then Gandalf is its mind, the leader who understands that knowledge is just as great a power as any magical ability he possesses, and perhaps even more so.

There are a lot of great Gandalf lines from throughout the three Lord of the Rings movies (and, of course, J.R.R. Tolkien's books long before them), which help to make him one of the most beloved characters. "A wizard is never late..." is iconic, and his lines in the scene with the Balrog - "You shall not pass!" and "Fly, you fools," - have been endlessly quoted ever since The Fellowship of the Ring hit theaters in 2001.

But as memorable as they are, there is one that stands out from the rest as being the greatest words of wisdom imparted by the wizard, which not only shapes the entire narrative, but applies directly to all of our own lives, too.

The Gandalf quote in question comes when Frodo laments the heavy burden the One Ring has placed upon him, wishing it had not come to him, and that none of it had happened. To this, the grey wizard says:

"So do all who live to see such times, but that is not for them to decide. All we have to decide is what to do with the time that is given to us."

The line works on multiple levels. To Frodo, it is exactly what he needs to hear at that moment, even if it's not necessarily what he might want to hear. Gandalf offers him a sense of comfort, in that the Hobbit is not alone in his thoughts, validating his wishes. But at the same time, he doesn't pander to him and tell him that it's going to be easy, but does state his own sense of agency in these events.

This speaks to the Fellowship at large, and Middle-earth as a whole. It's a perfect way of distilling down the ideas of free will vs. what's already divined that are at play in The Lord of the Rings, which we also get a sense of in the same scene, as Gandalf tells Frodo there are other forces at play, and that: "Bilbo was meant to find the ring. In which case, you also were meant to have it."

That juxtaposition of those quotes might suggest a conflict between free will and fate or luck in this story, but that's not actually the case. We do know that fate exists in Middle-earth, via the creator of Arda, Erū Ilúvatar, but they also granted people free will. And we see time and again throughout the story that, while fate and luck play their hands, it's the choices people make that ultimately define the story.

This is clear with all the Fellowship of the Ring members, each choosing to follow Frodo ("my sword... my bow... my ax..."), and all being tested at various turns, and the outcomes are entirely dependent on which path they choose. Fate may have played a part in Frodo getting the ring, but it was, as Gandalf says, deciding what to do, such as Sam being by his side, or Frodo not killing Gollum, that all led to the One Ring being destroyed.

All of this is enough to make Gandalf's quote one of the most powerful in fantasy history, but it's just how relevant and relatable it is — whether it's 1954, 2001, or 2026 — that makes it even better. Because what Gandalf is really getting at is a core tenet of humanity and how we live our lives. None of us could choose when we were born, nor the surrounding circumstances, but all we can do is decide what to do with the time given to us. It's a simple, beautiful message that continues to resonate far beyond The Lord of the Rings.

That Gandalf line alone is incredible, but what's just as impressive is that we get another bit of profound wisdom in the same scene. When Frodo talks about wishing Bilbo had killed Gollum when he had the chance, the wizard responds:

"Many that live deserve death, and some that die deserve life. Can you give it to them, Frodo? Do not be too eager to deal out death in judgment. Even the very wise cannot see all ends."

Again, it's a quite simple yet elegant and fitting way of boiling down something that is so fundamentally human. Believe It Or Not, Mr. Ripley Gets Meta in 'A Talent for Murder' Origin | Review

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

You're likely much more familiar with Tom Ripley — the sociopathic social climber who murdered his way across high society in a series of novels, films and shows including 1999's The Talented Mr. Ripley and Netflix's recent Ripley — than you are with his creator, cantankerous crime novelist Patricia Highsmith. But in the new meta-thriller A Talent for Murder, director Anton Corbijn and screenwriter Joanna Murray-Smith make a bet that the author herself is just as fascinating and shocking as her most famous character. And they might be right — at least, when Helen Mirren is playing the aggressive, provocative and famously bigoted Highsmith, in a swaggering performance that lets the typically elegant actress cut loose.

Mirren's Highsmith is the heart of a story that's one-half eccentric literary biopic, one-half pulpy mystery: what if Highsmith, in her final days, was approached by a mysterious young editor begging her for one final book? The tale that unspools from there never quite becomes the sleek cat-and-mouse thriller it would like to be. But it is a lot of fun, and a risky third act twist transforms the film from a typical mystery into a commentary about the nature of life as an artist — one that will leave viewers either elated or enraged.

In early 1995, 74-year-old Patricia Highsmith (Mirren) is living in solitude at her isolated Swiss country home, indulging in her favorite pastimes: guzzling booze, reminiscing on her professional triumphs, terrorizing the townsfolk with her bad temper, and enjoying the company of her large collection of knives, poisons, and guns. Enter Edward Ridgeway (Alden Ehrenreich), a young book editor who has been sent all the way from New York City with a single mission: get Highsmith to sign the contract for a sixth Tom Ripley novel.

Ridgeway is a Highsmith fanatic, and knows that getting her back to work would make his career. But he's also not quite comfortable with the situation he's been dropped into — or the mysterious fate of a previous editor, who had also been sent to extract a new book from Highsmith, but soon vanished off the face of the earth. Without an easy way back home, he tries to make his case. Of course, the irascible author tries to send him packing, using colorful language and some threats to his life. But even as the two clash, Highsmith is soon seduced by the idea that there's still demand for her work, that a final book could cement her literary immortality, and that Ridgeway might be the one to pull her out of the writer's block that has swallowed her final years.

From there, the story of Highsmith and Ridgeway's relationship weaves in and out of the fictional tale they spin, as they begin work together on her next Ripley novel. In scenes illustrating their story ideas, Ripley (also played by Ehrenreich) is determined to marry a young American named Clementine (Olivia Cooke) — an heiress who happens to share a name, face, and life story with an actual young woman Ridgeway met on his way to Switzerland. As real and imagined storylines get increasingly tangled, it becomes difficult to tell what's true and what's just a story. What is Ridgeway's real interest in Clementine? What is Ridgeway's real interest in Highsmith, for that matter? Who's actually manipulating who? And what's so damned important about Tom Ripley?

A Talent for Murder's greatest shock isn't any of the crimes that occur on screen — it's watching Mirren, an actor known for maintaining an air of poise even in her more villainous roles, play a character who is rude, sloppy and purposely indelicate. Sporting a head of barely-brushed hair and a confrontational sneer, Highsmith is delighted by her own crassness, whether she's holding forth on the mating habits of snails, speculating on Ridgeway's own sexual prowess, or just casually fondling some items from her firearm collection. Highsmith is a nightmare, but an engrossing one — and through monologues that reveal more than she intends about her past, we learn exactly how she developed such a rough shell, and how those difficult experiences also led her to create her charming criminal mastermind. Mirren is clearly having a ball playing a complete asshole, which makes her antics a joy to watch — and reveals that even at 81, there are still sides we have yet to see of the actress.

Ehrenreich doesn't undergo quite as big a transformation, but viewers who are only familiar with him from his time as Han Solo will be shocked by his turn as the nebbishy Ridgeway, who swings between playing the innocent and revealing hints of a more threatening core. While he isn't always compelling on his own, he and Mirren fight beautifully; his seemingly inexpert attempts to manipulate her and her over-the-top diatribes about him bring to mind a platonic version of Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf? Their blackly comedic attempts to take each other down allow Murray-Smith to slip in grand statements about art, creativity, and the nature of good and evil in a way that's genuinely entertaining — you'll be so tickled watching Mirren suggest that she poisoned Ehrenreich's eggs, you won't even notice when the conversation pivots to the value of art or the greater meaning of a painful childhood.

Ehrenreich's scenes as Ripley, which mostly feature narration from Highsmith and Ridgeway in place of dialogue, see the actor take the killer to a lighter, campier place than Matt Damon, Andrew Scott, or others who have tackled the role; he glories in the character's external sleaze but skips the inner torment, a decision that makes these sections pure fun. This is especially true when he plays off Cooke, who makes her character such a wide-eyed cartoon of naïveté that you don't really feel much sorrow over her constant misfortune; instead, you just get caught up in the enjoyable nastiness of Ripley's scheming.

However, as A Talent for Murder enters its final act, you may begin to wonder why exactly you're watching these two plot threads play out. It's lovely to be out there getting up to no good in the snow-covered Alps or sunny Roman piazzas (which are captured in luscious cinematography by Robbie Ryan), but what is all this plotting and stalking and mysterious phone call-making adding up to?? A few moments of confusion towards the film's end culminate in an audacious climax that viewers will either love or hate — a reaction that honestly feels much more in line with Highsmith's legacy as a person and writer than any traditional mystery movie denouement. The film's decision to make a big statement on the nature of art and artists feels slightly out of left field when it arrives, but it's also far more meaningful than any possible whodunnit reveal. You'll likely be thinking about it days later — though whether that's because you loved it or because you want your money back is impossible to predict.

A Talent for Murder began as a stage play (also written by Murray-Smith), a pedigree that's fairly obvious in the way it plays out — there are rarely more than two characters on screen at any given time, and they are prone to occasionally making grand philosophical declarations in the way only theater characters seem to.

Its beginnings as a play are also clear in the way the film values character over plot. Though some of the twists and turns don't quite work out, the emotional battle between the protagonists always feels right. Highsmith and Ridgeway's fights are funny, frightening, and strange, but more than anything, they reveal that the things we scorn and the things we need are often more similar than we'd like to admit. Of course, noting that there's a thin line between love and hate isn't exactly a revolutionary idea. But I can almost guarantee, you've never seen anyone illustrate it the way A Talent for Murder does.

An unconventional biopic that isn't afraid to go to strange places.

Memento became a defining thriller because of its groundbreaking reverse-chronology structure, deep psychological immersion, and subversion of the traditional revenge formula.

The film's brilliance comes from Nolan’s narrative mechanic. Nolan splits the film into two distinct timelines: in color, every time a new scene starts, you are thrown into the middle of a moment without knowing what came before; in black and white, the story progresses chronologically until the two timelines meet at the film's conclusion. 5 Best Movies Like The Love Hypothesis

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

There was never any doubt that Lili Reinhart's rom-com "The Love Hypothesis" would become a big hit for Prime Video. Streaming platforms have struck gold by taking romance books and turning them into comforting crowd-pleasers for the small screen. Sometimes this results in films as vapid as "My Fault: London," but often it results in charming movies that take allow us to indulge romantic fantasies via two hot people falling in love.

That's exactly what we got when "The Love Hypothesis" transformed from "Star Wars" fan fiction, of all things, into a novel and then a streaming movie. Ali Hazelwood's book about a biology PhD student who pretends to date her professor, only for the two to fall deeply in love became a hit, and was promptly given a film adaptation in which Reinhart plays the student, Olive Smith, while British actor Tom Bateman plays the ornery professor, Dr. Adam Carlsen.

There were two main tropes to both the book and the film: mismatched lovers who cross a boundary to be together, and the good old fake relationship that becomes very real. Of course, this is far from the first time these tropes have been used in romance movies, which is why it's actually quite easy to recommend films just like "The Love Hypothesis." While there are plenty of golden-age romance movies that used these plot devices (1941's "Come Live with Me" saw James Stewart and Hedy Lamarr fake a marriage only to fall head over heels), we're betting that fans of Reinhart's movie will want more modern examples. With that in mind, here's a list of the best movies like "The Love Hypothesis."

"My Oxford Year" may have a controversial ending but it's got everything you could want from a modern-day streaming flick based on a book. Melodrama and fairytale romance combine in this Netflix movie, which stars Sofia Carson (who's fast becoming the streamer's go-to romantic drama leading lady) as American exchange student Anna De La Vega. Before descending into a life of corporate drudgery, Anna decides to study Victorian poetry at the University of Oxford for a year. Wouldn't you know it, once there she meets an unreasonably charming and handsome English DPhil student and tutor named Jamie Davenport (Corey Mylchreest).

"My Oxford Year" shares much in common with "The Love Hypothesis." Not only are both based on books (though the former actually began as a screenplay before it was adapted into a novel), they both feature a student/teacher relationship that gets off to a rough start only to flourish into a modern-day fairytale. Both also feature strapping English leading men (though Bateman plays an American in "The Love Hypothesis") who start out as jerks only to reveal a tender side as things progress.

It is worth noting that there's a tragic element to "My Oxford Year," so if you're looking for something that wraps up as neatly as "The Love Hypothesis," this might not be the ideal watch. But otherwise fans of Lily Reinhart's movie should fall madly in love with Carson's alternative.

These streaming movies are all well and good but there has to be at least one undisputed classic on this list. There's no better example of the fake relationship trope than this 1990 romantic comedy in which we got to see prime-era Richard Gere and Julia Roberts fall for one another. The movie did what "The Love Hypothesis" and its fellow streaming-age rom-coms did decades before Netflix was even a thing, bringing together impossibly beautiful stars via a fake relationship that blossoms into the real thing.

If you don't know the plot of what might just be Julia Roberts' best movie, I'm not sure what to tell ya. He's a corporate raider, she's a sex worker he asks for directions. So begins a love affair that sees Gere's Edward Lewis paying Roberts' Vivian Ward to spend a week with him and act as his girlfriend. Needless to say, this confected relationship becomes a real one. Ward melts Lewis' cold corporate heart and Lewis draws out the real Ward from behind a wall of dissociation built up over her time spent as a sex worker.

If you're somehow yet to see the film, and are worried the tale of a millionaire corporate raider falling for a sex worker might not have the wholesome charm of "The Love Hypothesis," don't worry. There's a reason Roger Ebert called the film "the sweetest and most openhearted love fable since 'The Princess Bride.'" "Pretty Woman" is as romantic a comedy as they come. But beyond that, it somehow manages to feel grounded thanks to its multi dimensional characters, without compromising the fairytale romance aspect.

Sofia Carson will soon have a monopoly on streaming romance movies. Her 2025 romantic drama "The Life List" dominated Netflix just prior to the similarly successful release of "My Oxford Year." But before either of these movies, Carson made her Netflix romance debut with the 2022 film "Purple Hearts." Guess what — the whole thing was based on a book, Tess Wakefield's 2017 novel of the same name.

In "Purple Hearts" we get a modern day version of James Stewart's "Come Live with Me." This Carson-led love story sees the former Disney Channel star play diabetic waitress and musician Cassie Salazar, who's working herself ragged just to afford insulin. Her salvation arrives in the form of hunky Marine Luke Morrow (Nicholas Galitzine), who agrees to marry Cassie so she can gain health coverage and he can benefit from a pay bump. You can see where this is going.

Much like with "The Love Hypothesis," this is a story about a couple who agree to act as though they're together only to develop real feelings along the way. There's plenty of BookTok-style melodrama on display here, too, which should please anyone who enjoyed the tension of the third act fall-out in "The Love Hypothesis."

It's the film that made Glen Powell a star and proved Sydney Sweeney could make popular stuff outside of "Euphoria." If your favorite thing about "The Love Hypothesis" was watching two attractive people make out, then "Anyone But You" should be right up your alley. In fact, "Anyone But You" is arguably the apotheosis of the modern day two-hot-actors-fall-in-love rom-com. But there's a stronger connection between this film and "The Love Hypothesis."

Lily Reinhart's Olive Smith and Tom Bateman's Dr. Adam Carlsen agreed to fake a relationship for mutually beneficial reasons, and the same happens with Sweeney's Beatrice Messina and Powell's Ben. The duo pretend to be a couple so that Bea's parents will stop trying to repair her relationship with her ex, and Ben can make his own ex jealous. We all knew where it was going from the very beginning but that didn't make it any less enjoyable to watch.

Unlike with "The Love Hypothesis," the guy in this romantic comedy isn't a complete jerk. Full of himself, maybe, but not an anti-social hermit like Bateman's "Dr. Evil." Still, he and Sweeney's law student don't exactly get along, especially after their first meeting ended on awkward terms. That, alongside the fake relationship trope, gives Powell and Sweeney's couple a similar dynamic to that of Olive and Adam in "The Love Hypothesis." So if you somehow missed the unlikely box office smash that was "Anyone But You" back in 2024, now's the time to remedy that.

In 2018, "To All the Boys I've Loved Before" became the new gold standard for teen rom-coms. It also happens to be a nice little companion to "The Love Hypothesis." Based on a book? Check. Fake relationship trope? Check. Academic setting? Sort of check. Rather than college, this tale of teen love, adapted from Jenny Han's YA novel, plays out in high school, where Lara Jean Covey (Lana Condor) writes secret letters to her crushes. Of course, she never intends for any of them to read her missives, but guess what — that's exactly what happens.

Lara Jean's biggest crush is Josh Sanderson (Israel Broussard), and when she sees him approaching with his letter in hand, she kisses another one of the letter recipients, Peter Kavinsky (Noah Centineo) in an attempt to throw Josh off. Soon, Peter and Lara Jean are fake dating. This allows the former to distract from the embarrassment of Josh receiving a letter, and the latter to make his ex-girlfriend Gen (Emilija Baranac) jealous. But you know how this goes. Real romance develops, melodrama ensues, feelings get hurt.

"To All the Boys I've Loved Before" is, however, more than your standard BookTook phenomenon turned Netflix romance movie. The film is surprisingly well shot, retains a heartfelt sensibility throughout, and isn't afraid to embrace its classic fake relationship rom-com influences. It's a touching little high school melodrama that's well worth a watch for anyone who even remotely enjoyed "The Love Hypothesis."

What does it mean to truly own the red carpet? It means showing out the way that stars have at the biggest events on the planet for generations now: In the swankiest, swerviest, most spotlight-stealing fashion possible.

Maybe that’s stunting in a disco-tinged jumpsuit like a ruffled John Malkovich at the 2026 Venice Film Festival, suiting up in sumptuous red velvet like Gwenyth Paltrow in 1999, keeping things simmeringly subtle like Frank Ocean’s Prada look at the 2019 Met Gala, or popping out the big lapels on a suede tux like Tom Holland at GQ’s Men of the Year 2021. Whatever your interpretation, the best red-carpet looks need to feel unexpected, look unforgettable, and radiate confidence.

To help jumpstart your own legendary party ensembles, here are 77 of the greatest red-carpet moments ever—and what you can learn from each.

Don’t be afraid to lean into your sex appeal.

As one wise Twitter user once put it, “Colman Domingo always dresses like he's stepped out of a GQ Mag shoot.”

This lush Gucci velvet suit became so instantly legendary that Gwenyth herself recreated the look more than 20 years later.

Frank caused some consternation (though not around these parts) when he showed up to 2019’s camp-themed Met Gala in an understated, decidedly un-camp look. Four years on, though, his outfit remains a great reminder that sticking to the classics—in this case, black-and-white Prada from head to toe—is never a poor choice.

Ruffles have ridges, and so does this suit.

Leaning retro can sometimes go awry on the red carpet, but Stanfield managed to pull the ‘70s look off with funky ease when he pulled this giant-collared, razor-sharp Saint Laurent jumpsuit out for the Oscars.

This outfit looks like it comes with its own walk-in music, and it’s loud.

The right to rock bare-chested suit isn’t something you demand—it’s something you earn.

Keanu is no stranger to high-low tailoring—he’s been known to rock work boots with sharp suits and graphic hoodies with sport coats. But he’s never pulled that trick off better than he did here in ‘93, layering a badass leather motorcycle vest under a roomy three-button number.

Julia Effin’ Roberts in a Big Effin’ Suit. That’s it. That’s the caption.

Wearing Prada, like Wright at the 2023 Golden Globes, is a surefire way to get a lot of compliments. The brand makes you look stylish and smart. Don’t you just want to ask Wright which local art gallery to visit?

Gerber’s disco-ball gown is stunning, but it’s Butler’s silky, sumptuous, pussy-bow blouse—designed by Saint Laurent, as was his exquisitely tailored tux—that utterly steals the show.
