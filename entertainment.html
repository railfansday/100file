The Chevy El Camino Launched In 1959, And This Is The Ute's Most Sought-After Model Year

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

No, the Chevy El Camino wasn't the first to combine a two-door car with an open bed truck. The first production vehicle to do that was a Ford, but possibly not the one you're thinking of. A design engineer named Lew Bandt, then with Ford of Australia, developed what's often considered the original factory-made ute — the 1934 Ford Model 40-A Light Delivery. Depending on exactly how you define a ute, some folks point to the 1927 Volvo OV4 as well.

Of course, it was the 1957 Ford Ranchero that truly inspired the El Camino. The Ranchero was built on the Blue Oval's full-size platform of the time, and it became both relatively popular and relatively profitable for the automaker. In fact, it was successful enough that Chevy was inspired to get in on the ute action with its full-size 1959 El Camino. Originally a short-lived model only in production for that year and 1960, the El Camino would get a second chance at life in 1964. That's when the Bowtie brand revived the name and brought it back in slightly downsized form on the same platform as the intermediate-class Chevrolet Chevelle.

This is where things start to get interesting for gearheads. In 1970, Chevrolet introduced a 454-cubic-inch LS6 V8 that was reserved for both the Chevelle and its platform mate. With that mill standing out as the most powerful unit of the year, the former became a muscle-car legend and one of Dom Toretto's rides in the epic 10-movie "Fast and Furious" saga. Meanwhile, the latter turned into perhaps the most sought-after El Camino of them all, the 1970 El Camino SS LS6 454. Keep in mind that this was a whole different engine than the LS6 in the 200-mph 2027 Chevrolet Corvette Stingray.

There are two key reasons that enthusiasts crave this machine. Right off the bat, the 1970 El Camino SS 454 was a one-year wonder — its exact powerplant was the aggressive LS6 V8 that would be dropped from the El Camino for 1971. True, Chevy sold a 1971 El Camino SS 454, but that mill was the less-powerful LS5. As far as actual production numbers for the 1970 model, those are hard to come by. Hemmings says 4,475 Chevrolets in total were sold with the LS6 for that year, but they were spread out among both Chevelles and El Caminos.

The second major selling point here is the outrageous driving experience offered by these rides, and for that, credit also goes to their incredible engine. Factory rated at 450 horsepower with a whopping 500 pound-feet of torque, this motor featured a cast-iron block and heads along with an 11.25:1 compression ratio. Also adding to the muscle are a four-bolt main block, a four-barrel Holley carburetor, and a high-performance camshaft. The outcome was that both the El Camino and Chevelle could serve up more horses than not just the 1970 Corvette, but all other production cars, too.

As further proof of just how hard people are jonesing for the 1970 El Camino SS LS6 454, an example may hold the record for the highest auction price for any El Camino — the car went for $275,000 at a Barrett-Jackson sale in 2023. Just remember auction values can change quickly: one 1970 Chevelle LS6 lost its owner a million dollars.

5 Brilliant Books To Read Before Kicking the Bucket

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Five stories that are bound to make a vivid impression

On principle, any list of “books you must read before you die” should be unending. Literary staples aside, the number of must-read books in existence is constantly expanding and evolving. Older books undergo revaluation, no-brainer entries are viewed through a fresh lens, and contemporary titles join an already massive library of must-reads.

Also, tastes are subjective. Not everyone might jive with Great Expectations or Les Misérables despite their objective artistic merit. The definition of “must-read” has drastically changed over time. Keeping this in mind, Polygon has chosen five brilliant, indisputable books to read before kicking the bucket. Some are obvious picks, while others, not so much.

Fyodor Dostoevsky’s body of work is often classified as essential reads for good reason, and The Brothers Karamazov is undoubtedly one such entry. The author’s final novel was written under dire personal circumstances, which included failing health and profound emotional grief. The novel is deeply philosophical in nature, raising pertinent questions about faith, morality, and skepticism. From a certain perspective, it's also about human evil, and how it manifests as mundane emotions like greed or hedonism.

The novel opens with wealthy landowner Fyodor Karamazov neglecting all three of his sons — Dmitri, Ivan, and Alyosha. Dostoevsky goes into intricate detail to flesh out the characters in this dysfunctional household, which is caught up in an inheritance dispute and subsequent scandal. After a murder takes place, The Brothers Karamazov turns into a tense courtroom drama. Each Karamazov brother represents a human archetype, which Dostoevsky uses to debate the nature of free will. While this book isn’t as relentless and claustrophobic as Crime and Punishment, it is sweeping and dramatic in its exploration of character motivations and human behavior.

A milestone in African literature, Chinua Achebe’s Things Fall Apart is about Okonkwo, an influential leader of the fictional Igbo clan. Okonkwo is an accomplished warrior and champion who vows to be nothing like his alcoholic, flute-playing father. After he takes a young boy named Ikemefuna as his ward, the oracle of his village declares the child must be killed. This incident haunts Okonkwo, who goes on to oppose rampant colonialism and reclaim the lost dignity of his people. Achebe presents an essential viewpoint, as most colonial literature portrayed Igbo culture in biased and problematic ways.

Things Fall Apart provides rich complexity to the tribes portrayed, granting an authentic look into local customs, laws, and traditions. Okonkwo is a deeply flawed but heroic protagonist who tries to hold onto tradition in the light of aggressive socio-political change. Few novels dissect colonialism like Achebe’s debut story, which explains how colonizers quietly infiltrate local cultures and turn close-knit communities against one another. This leads to a fractured sense of shared history and heritage that requires an enormous amount of time to acclimatize to.

Arundhati Roy’s most famous work, The God of Small Things, is an unflinching look at vulnerability in the face of rigid societal rules. The novel follows fraternal twins Estha and Rahel, who grow up amid the arrival of their British cousin in India and intense familial tensions. Roy uses a disjointed narrative to tell this moving tale about social discrimination, colonialism, and local politics that inevitably affect the personal lives of our protagonists. The prose is vivid and sensory, filtered through the perspective of children who view the world in a more tactile way than adults do.

The God of Small Things pits massive historical upheavals against unforgettable personal events, including the fleeting beauty of growing up and the wonder of young love. While focusing on these tender moments, Roy also critiques casteist social practices that prevent the romantic union between a divorced woman and a carpenter. The brutality of British rule goes hand in hand with these quiet tragedies, which Roy explores by weaving in and out of time to showcase how our memories work. It is a haunting book that radically reinvents language to convey the hyperspecific feeling of losing childhood innocence in a postcolonial world.

The book that established the dark academia subgenre, Donna Tartt’s The Secret History is a riveting campus novel that everybody should experience. Set in an elite liberal arts college in Vermont, the book follows Richard Papen, who joins an exclusive Classics and Greek class in a bid to fit in. The other members of the class are all wealthy and hand-picked by the professor, and revel in their social isolation, which they consider proof of their intellectual superiority. After one of them is murdered, The Secret History turns into an inverted detective story that dwells on Richard’s perspective of the other students, and the effect that this death has on everybody.

Tartt doesn’t spring this death on the reader. Instead, the book opens with the declaration of the murder. The tension lies in slowly unraveling why this happened, and how the others are responsible for such a ghastly event. Richard is also an unreliable narrator, being a self-confessed liar and someone who blatantly alters objective truth due to his biases. Tartt uses layered, evocative prose to explore morality via the lens of Greek ideals, penning a cautionary tale against the romanticization of intellectual elitism. The bubble that surrounds these students bursts, pushing them towards real-world culpability they’re not equipped to handle.

Humans share a complicated relationship with their sense of smell, detailed vividly in Patrick Süskind’s Perfume: The Story of a Murderer. The story follows Jean-Baptiste Grenouille, an orphan in 18th-century France who is born with an exceptional nose that can differentiate between subtle scents around him. While this gift initially pulls Grenouille towards becoming a perfumer, things take a horrific turn after he meets a young girl with a scent that’s impossible to forget. What starts off as intense fascination turns into a morbid obsession with human scent, which he hunts in order to make the most exquisite perfume.

Perfume paints Grenouille as a fascinating antagonist with no scent of his own. Süskind uses his journey as a metaphor for human morality and desire, and how easy it is to be manipulated by factors like profound alienation. A person with no empathetic roots in the world will treat humans like fodder, as Grenouille has never been loved as a child and never grew to discover the sentiment. This is a chilling portrait of a monster whose story ends in a bizarre, haunting way that will leave you stunned.

Lotus L. Kang wins Canada’s top art prize, the Sobey Art Award

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Lotus L. Kang, winner of the 2026 Sobey Art Award Photo: National Gallery of Canada, Ottawa

The National Gallery of Canada (NGC) and the Sobey Art Foundation named the artist Lotus L. Kang asthe winner of the 2026 Sobey Art Award during a ceremony on Saturday (14 November). The Toronto-born, New York-based photographer, sculptor and installation artist won the 23rd edition of the annual award, the most high-profile and lucrative prize devoted to Canadian contemporary artists, and will receive C$100,000 ($70,000).

The other five shortlisted artists—Melaw Nakehk'o (from the Circumpolar region), Samuel Roy-Bois (Pacific), Audie Murray (Prairies), Caroline Monnet (Québec) and Shane Perley-Dutcher (Atlantic)—will each receive C$25,000 ($17,500), while the 24 longlisted artists each received C$10,000 ($7,000), bringing the total value of the award to C$465,000 ($325,800).

In a statement, Jonathan Shaughnessy, the chair of the jury for this year’s Sobey Art Award, praised Kang for “the commitment and rigour demonstrated in her practice for well over a decade”. He continued: “Through installations using unfixed photographic film, cast and found objects, and fabricated armatures, her practice resists the stabilising impulses traditionally associated with photography. Reoccurring motifs including lotus root, flowers, soju bottles, birds, anchovies, are not fixed objects, but repositories of place, longing and desire.”

James Bond Finds Its Perfect Replacement in This 6-Episode Miniseries

Espionage stories have fascinated audiences for generations, from the cool precision of classic John le Carré adaptations and the legacy of James Bond to modern hits like The Americans and Slow Horses. Even when they lean into action or stylized intrigue, there’s a certain comfort in knowing the danger is usually a fictional, heightened version of spycraft built for entertainment. But real-world espionage carries a very different kind of weight and may leave you questioning even your closest allies.

That’s exactly what makes A Spy Among Friends resonate. Based on true events and adapted from Ben Macintyre’s acclaimed book about British intelligence officer Kim Philby, the six-episode miniseries leans into the inner workings and psychological pressure of espionage rather than the slick, explosive elements the genre often relies on. Instead of battling enemies across borders, the series reveals that the most devastating betrayals come from within in one of the biggest intelligence scandals of the 20th century.

A Spy Among Friends tells the story of the decades-long relationship between Nicholas Elliott (Damian Lewis), a well-respected MI6 officer, and his closest friend Kim Philby (Guy Pearce), who is eventually unmasked as one of the most notorious double agents in history. The series frames their friendship as both a source of deep loyalty and a blind spot, which allowed Philby to operate as a Soviet mole for years without arousing Elliott’s suspicion. When Philby finally defects to Moscow, the British government launches an inquiry to determine whether Elliott was simply deceived or whether he might have been helping his friend all along.

The series begins in the aftermath of Philby’s exposure, sending Elliott to Beirut with the mission of extracting a confession from a man he'd known intimately for over 30 years. Over the course of four tense days and 34 hours of taped conversations, the truth slips further out of reach, culminating in Philby’s defection to the USSR. Back in London, Elliott faces his own interrogation at the hands of MI5 officer Lily Thomas (Anna Maxwell Martin), one of the show’s few fictional additions. Through her relentless questioning, the series unravels Elliott’s complicated mix of loyalty, denial, and self-preservation.

What ultimately sets A Spy Among Friends apart is its refusal to treat Philby’s treachery as a standard espionage mystery filled with high-octane twists and turns. Instead, it focuses on the emotional wreckage left behind when a deeply personal relationship becomes the site of catastrophic betrayal. Pearce and Lewis play off each other with remarkable precision, their performances grounding the series in the uncomfortable truth that the deepest betrayals rarely come from enemies abroad, but from the people we think we know the best.

As compelling as the story is on its own, A Spy Among Friends reaches another level thanks to the exceptional performances at its center. Damian Lewis came into prominence with his Emmy-winning role in Homeland, playing a man constantly walking the line between loyalty and deception, and later carried that same complexity into Billions. His portrayal of Nicholas Elliott taps into a similar tension. While Elliott is clearly devastated by Philby’s betrayal, there’s always an undercurrent suggesting he may have let his personal affection cloud his professional judgment. Lewis plays him with a quiet, tightly coiled restraint that makes Elliot’s internal crisis feel both painfully raw and deeply contained.

Guy Pearce, meanwhile, is mesmerizing as Kim Philby. His villainous turns have always had a distinctive nuance, from the cold, calculating betrayal he brought to The Count of Monte Cristo to the wounded arrogance of Iron Man 3. But here, he delivers something far more unsettling. Pearce resists the temptation to portray Philby as a conventional villain, instead capturing the strange magnetism that allowed Philby to manipulate everyone around him. The series keeps Philby’s motivations intentionally ambiguous, and Pearce leans into that uncertainty, playing him as someone who may genuinely believe his own lies. It’s the kind of performance that makes it entirely plausible that this man deceived governments — and his closest friends — for decades.

The writing and structure of the series amplify these performances. Each episode peels back another layer, shifting across timelines while gradually revealing which memories are reliable and which have been warped by guilt, ego, or denial. Showrunner Alexander Cary, who wrote all six episodes, uses this fractured narrative to recreate the emotional fog that defined Elliott and Philby’s friendship. And while true stories can sometimes slip into the dryness of a history lesson, A Spy Among Friends never does. Its character-first writing and dialogue-driven scenes give Lewis and Pearce the room to turn every exchange into a captivating, high-stakes moment, grounding real events in raw, intimate human drama rather than procedural detail.

A Spy Among Friends isn’t the kind of spy thriller that relies on spectacle, and that’s exactly why it works so well. It’s a reminder that real espionage is far more personal than what movies like James Bond often make it out to be. Anchored by magnetic performances from Lewis and Pearce, the miniseries offers a darker, more realistic portrait of spycraft, one where friendship becomes a liability and trust is never guaranteed. For fans of character-driven espionage rooted in real history, A Spy Among Friends is definitely worth watching.

Stephen King Named This Alfred Hitchcock Thriller As One Of His Favorite Scary Movies

Back in 2017, IndieWire amassed a list of Stephen King's favorite scary movies based on the titles he had either previously recommended on social media or written about in his 1981 non-fiction book "Danse Macabre." Among the many, many films included were Ridley Scott's "Alien," Mario Bava's "Black Sunday," Brian De Palma's "Carrie" (itself based on King's own litany of feminine rage), and George A. Romero's "Dawn of the Dead."

Somewhat surprisingly, Alfred Hitchcock's 1972 serial killer "Frenzy" made the cut as well. "Frenzy" was Hitchcock's penultimate film and is famously the only one of Hitchcock's movies to receive an R rating in the United States. Indeed, one might be startled to see actual on-screen nudity in a film from The Master of Suspense. The murders in "Frenzy" are notably brutal, too, and the movie, quite shockingly, contains scenes of sexual assault. This was clearly an attempt by Hitchcock to push the envelope, and one can only speculate if he was drawn to the film's twisted serial killer mentality, the sweatiness of the overall picture, or its sheer amount of on-screen violence.

"Frenzy" tells the story of Richard Blaney (John Finch), a sad-sack and impoverished London-based former military man who is accused of sexually assaulting his ex-wife, Brenda (Barbara Leigh-Hunt), and using a necktie to strangle her to death. However, the necktie is connected to a string of other murders that have been committed all over the city, as the real killer is Richard's creepy friend Bob Rusk (Barry Foster).

As with so many other Alfred Hitchcock movies, the main character in "Frenzy" becomes a falsely accused man on the run (see also: "The Wrong Man," "Stage Fright," "The 39 Steps," "Young and Innocent," "North By Northwest," "Saboteur," "I Confess"), and he has to elude capture to clear his name. Along the way, Richard ends up finding an ally and lover in his former co-worker Babs (Anna Massey) ... although, with Rusk still engaged in his murderous shenanigans, that doesn't bode well for her.

Hitchcock films excel at making audiences unwittingly root for the bad guy (see "Dial M for Murder" and "Psycho" for two better-known examples), and "Frenzy" includes a sequence where Rusk has to retrieve a personal item of his from a victim whose body he has already disposed of. Because of this, viewers are forced to watch as he struggles with the tension of having to retrieve the incriminating evidence, and while we might not feel sympathy for Rusk, we do become involved in his plight. (It's one of the most noteworthy scenes in Hitchcock's filmography.) You can see, then, why Richard turns to Rusk for help at one point, not knowing that his friend is the murderer.

The final act of "Frenzy" gets a little involved and maybe spins a little too far off with its trials and prison sentences, but know that the ultimate conclusion is very satisfying. It's a tonally unique movie as well, feeling very loose and 1970s-y. And where many of Hitchcock's previous films felt mannered and polite despite involving murder and fetishes, "Frenzy" is comparatively casual, focusing more on Rusk's overcooked murderous mindset and the sexual element of his killing spree.

What many cineastes saw as the sexual subtext of Alfred Hitchcock's previous movies becomes text in "Frenzy." By the 1970s, the filmmaker could afford to be a little more frank. The murder scenes, more so than in any other Hitchcock movie, "Psycho" included, are brutal and unsettling. There's even one particularly memorable shot in "Frenzy" of one of Rusk's victims, freshly dead, with her eyes wide and her tongue lolling out, that will remain in your nightmares for quite some time. This is not the balletic, aesthetic, obfuscated violence of Hitchcock's earlier movies. This is disturbing.

Many critics responded very positively to "Frenzy" at the time. Roger Ebert gave the film four stars (out of four), writing that it was both a departure for Hitchcock and a return to form. As he put it:

"This is the kind of thriller Hitchcock was making in the 1940s, filled with macabre details, incongruous humor, and the desperation of a man convicted of a crime he didn't commit. The only 1970s details are the violence and the nudity (both approached with a certain grisly abandon that has us imagining 'Psycho' without the shower curtain). It's almost as if Hitchcock, at seventy-three, was consciously attempting to do once again what he did better than anyone else."

Ebert added that "Frenzy" possesses a nice macabre sense of humor, another feature Hitchcock's fans appreciate from the Master. Vincent Canby, writing for the New York Times, listed "Frenzy" as one of the 10 best films of its year, and it was a hit at the box office to boot. (There's a reason we here at /Film once listed it as one of Hitchcock's 20 best movies.) Clearly, Hitchcock hadn't lost his talent for snagging audiences.

Agatha Christie is among the most frequently adapted mystery authors, yet not every adaptation captures the essence of what makes Christie's writing resonate. While some adaptations lean more toward the "cozy" aspect of detection, and others to the "stylish" aspect, Sarah Phelps's 2015 BBC adaptation of And Then There Were None takes an entirely different approach and, in my view, proves far more successful: it was courageous enough to take risks with darkness.

Through the course of three sufficiently tight and increasingly oppressive episodes, the adaptation strips away all the "cozy" puzzle box intrigue typically associated with Christie's works; what remains is a much colder and more disturbing form—namely, a psychological thriller that illustrates and reinforces the belief that the central mystery never limited the true hook of the source material, but rather the manner in which the central mystery is used to illustrate the guilt that permeates every character in the story's dramatic landscape. The commitment the adaptation has to emphasize the dark elements of the original story is also why it still holds up as one of the best adaptations of Christie's work more than a decade after it first aired.

In principle, the story has all the components of a true Agatha Christie book. A group of people accepts an invitation from their hosts to visit a secluded mansion on Soldier Island, off Devon's coast, and each arrives for different reasons. When the guests arrive at the mansion, they find that their hosts are not at home. Household staff Thomas and Ethel Rogers serve dinner before a gramophone recording accuses everyone present of past murders.

From there, the structure tightens with brutal efficiency. One by one, the guests begin to die, their fates eerily tied to a children’s rhyme displayed throughout the house. With no detective figure swooping in to restore order — a notable departure from many Christie adaptations — paranoia fills the vacuum.

What makes this version so effective is how quickly it abandons the cozy whodunit energy. Instead of feeling like a puzzle waiting to be solved, the story plays like a slow moral reckoning. The island becomes a trap, the weather closes in, and the characters start to realize they may not be innocent victims after all.

The success of a mystery will often depend on its characters. In this miniseries, the creators have stacked the cast with talent. Charles Dance’s portrayal of Justice Wargrave is both terrifying and accurate, while Sam Neill’s character, General MacArthur, is full of nostalgia, hidden regret, and longing.

Aidan Turner portrays Philip Lombard, a very charismatic character. Maeve Dermody’s portrayal of Vera Claythorne in this adaptation is the most emotionally resonant and grounded character in the entire production. The remainder of the supporting cast consists of Miranda Richardson, Burn Gorman, and Anna Maxwell Martin, who all exhibit the same level of dedication to both the character and to the moral decay of this story.

Fortunately for the viewer, none of the performances ask us to like these people. All of these performances embrace discomfort, frustration, and suspicion through lingering stares, tension-filled moments, and the slow accumulation of tension that eventually boils over into a not-so-happy ending for each character.

Earlier adaptations of Agatha Christie's 1939 novel often favored a happier ending, following the precedent Christie herself set in her stage version. The BBC adaptation instead embraces the novel’s lack of mercy.

And Then There Were None does a great job staying true to the original material and showing the reader or viewer how much we all have to answer for what we do every day. There is no super sleuth at the end of this story, and no sudden savior at the last minute; every character shows what they are truly made of under pressure.

Since its debut on BBC One, And Then There Were None has quietly become one of the most respected Christie adaptations of the modern era. That reputation isn’t built on flashy reinvention. It comes from doing the hard thing: trusting the source material’s bleakest instincts and following them all the way through.

Viewers looking for cozy crime comfort may want to look elsewhere. But for anyone in the mood for a tightly wound, beautifully acted descent into paranoia — one that actually sticks its landing — this three-part thriller remains an easy recommendation.

Born April 24, 1943, in Camden, New Jersey, Sterban began singing at six in Sunday school as a soprano. He sang tenor in his seventh-grade glee club, and upon his eighth grade, his voice dropped to bass.

Sterban was then offered the opportunity to join the Oak Ridge Boys after the departure of Noel Fox, which he accepted in 1972. Bonsall joined the group one year later, with Duane Allen, William Lee Golden and Sterban already in the group, which carried on as the faces of the band for five decades.

They performed with Johnny Cash, who helped them sign with Columbia Records in 1973, though it was after opening for Roy Clark in 1975 when they met his manger, Jim Halsey, who would later sign with the group.

They reached success with “Y’all Come Back Saloon,” which reached No. 3 on the country chart in 1977, and led to a string of hits. “I’ll Be True to You” became their first No. 1 song, though 1981’s “Elvira” would become one of their largest successes.

Originally recorded by Dallas Frazier, the Oak Ridge Boys’ version of the track went to No. 1 on the country chart and No. 5 on the pop chart, sold more than two million copies and won a Grammy. Sterban’s bass refrain was a major touchpoint on the song.

The Oak Ridge Boys went on to also become members of the Grand Ole Opry, the Gospel Music Hall of Fame and the Country Music Hall of Fame.

Sterban released his memoir From Elvis to Elvira: My Life on Stage, written with Steven Robinson, in 2012.

Netflix's 8-Part Female John Wick Series Is One Of Its Best Binges

It’s rare for any thriller to live up to the John Wick franchise, let alone a small-screen show, but one Netflix series with a single season managed this feat. It is tough to think of an action thriller movie from the last 15 years that was more influential than 2014’s John Wick. In terms of pure plot, the Keanu Reeves vehicle was the simple story of a retired assassin killing every criminal involved in his dog’s death.

However, in visual and aesthetic terms, John Wick spelled the death knell for the shaky cam style that dominated action thrillers in the 2000s and early 2010s thanks to the popularity of director Paul Greengrass’s groundbreaking Bourne sequels. After John Wick’s release, the movie’s sequels popularized a hyper-smooth, slick action filmmaking style that prioritized gliding, unbroken Steadicam shots over fast-cutting.

Soon enough, Atomic Blonde, Bullet Train, Extraction, the Nobody movies, Kate, Gunpowder Milkshake, Pretty Lethal, Violent Night, and almost every other action thriller movie had embraced this CGI-assisted filmmaking style. Few of these efforts could match the John Wick sequels, and even John Wick’s own streaming spinoff The Continental ended up feeling like a knock off rather than a legitimate franchise entry. This is why it is so impressive that Netflix’s under-seen South Korean thriller series My Name recaptured John Wick’s entire appeal perfectly.

As an action thriller about revenge, My Name sounds straightforwardly similar to John Wick in terms of plot. However, the complicated lore of the show’s story makes My Name closer in story terms to the Internal Affairs movies or their Americanized re-imagining, The Departed. Han So-hee’s Yoon Ji-woo is the daughter of a powerful mob member who joins the city's police so she can act as a mole and pass on information to the Dongcheon drug cartel.

Yoon Ji-woo’s boss, the kingpin Choi Mu-jin, encourages her to spy on the police, meaning the show’s heroine soon lands a spot on the very task force that is investigating the cartel. However, once there, she learns from her new detective partner that Choi Mu-jin is suspected to have killed her father. With split loyalties, Yoon Ji-woo has no one to trust and every reason to sharpen her combat skills. While My Name’s plot is as complex as any crime drama like Love/Hate, this show is no slow burn, realistic character study.

Instead, Netflix’s hyper-stylized My Name is a neon-drenched thrill ride, as Yoon Ji-woo battles her way through the city’s criminal underworld while trying to solve the mystery of her father’s sudden death. Gritty, dark, and fast-paced, the action show is a must-watch for fans of revenge thrillers.

Every action show needs to find the right balance between constant fights, chases, and gun battles, but My Name’s tense premise gives the story an incredible head start in this regard. My Name’s twisty plot and the complicated machinations of its heroine’s plan mean that even the show’s quiet dialogue scenes crackle with constant tension.

There is always a risk that Yoon Ji-woo will be exposed as a mole in either organization, which ramps up tension during quiet scenes and makes the action all the more hard hitting. Even compared to dark crime thrillers like This City Is Ours, My Name’s one-season story is a masterclass in tension building.

On that note, the show’s one-season status actually works for, rather than against, My Name as a series on the whole. While it would have been hard to drag My Name’s story out for more than one season, Netflix’s killer John Wick replacement wisely limited its story to just one perfectly paced outing instead.

10 Classic Rock Albums To Listen to Before Kicking the Bucket

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

​​​​​​Joining the choir invisible. Shuffling off this mortal coil. Ceasing to be. Biting the dust. Wearing wooden pajamas. Vacating the earthly meat prison. No matter how it's said, it all means the same thing: death. Death is the great equalizer, the one thing that is inevitable regardless of one's race, religion, income or social status. Every day brings us closer to hopping the twig.

But hey, you're still on this side of the grass, right? Unless you're a well-read zombie, but that seems unlikely (but not impossible... discuss). So you have a choice to make: sit idly by, paralyzed by the thought you may have had your last Slurpee; or make the most of the time you have left. And if — hopefully — you've selected the latter, before kicking the bucket you've got things you want — nay, need — to do, see, and hear. Thankfully, your friends here at Collider have compiled a list that speaks to the latter: 10 rock albums you need to listen to before going belly up, ones that are not only awesome but offer guidance as you prepare for the inevitable. We're dying, literally, to share those with you now.

There comes a point in everyone's life where it's time to drop the charades and get real. That means raw, emotional honesty with others and yourself, and there is no better album that wears emotional honesty on every track like Fleetwood Mac's 1977 classic Rumours. The story behind the album is legendary at this point, recorded as the interpersonal drama among the group was akin to a soap opera, with separations, outright spite and heartbreak, making the atmosphere in the studio, shall we say, tense. A lesser band would have buried that under upbeat pop/rock songs, recording in separate sessions if needed. Not Fleetwood Mac: they owned that energy and put it all on vinyl, creating a timeless masterpiece that is relatable, real, and, in the case of "Don't Stop," even hopeful.

Getting real with ourselves potentially opens up a proverbial Pandora's Box of questions about life, death, time, money, or even one's mental health. While it may not provide any answers to these questions, Pink Floyd, with their iconic album Dark Side of the Moon, sure as all hell wax philosophical about those same themes. Like Rumours, the album has an emotional honesty that resonates only with the fluid cohesion that comes from being a concept album. As a result, Dark Side of the Moon is less a record than it is an experience, one that should be made before going up to the "Great Gig in the Sky."

Speaking of experiences, seeing KISS in concert is one that is an absolute must. Or at least was, given the band gave their last performance at Madison Square Garden on December 2nd, 2023. So if that's on your bucket list, you now only have two options: wait for the KISS virtual avatars to hit town, or listen to their 1975 album Alive! Opt for the latter. Their breakthrough album, consisting of cuts from their first three albums (KISS, Hotter Than Hell and Dressed to Kill), achieves something that other live albums have mostly failed to accomplish: the feeling of being there. Alive! captures the bombastic, take-no-prisoners energy of a KISS live show and puts it on vinyl. Twice, that is, with that raw power requiring containment on a double album. Good luck replicating that, avatars.

The "when" of kicking the bucket is assured. The "how," on the other hand, is another story. There are millions of ways one could go, from horribly tragic to an eternally looping end as a Darwin Award winner. You could, as alluded to in the aforementioned Rumours, even "Go Your Own Way." And if that's the case, as Van Halen cheerfully suggests on their 1984 album, you "might as well jump" (but in all seriousness, please don't — help is always available). The last album with long-time frontman David Lee Roth (until 2012's A Different Kind of Truth) is also, arguably, their best, with "Panama," "Hot for Teacher," "Drop Dead Legs" and "Jump" the standouts. Really, though, the album is so good it doesn't really matter where you "jump" in.

It's ironically fitting that the last album the Beatles recorded before their demise, one that includes "The End," would show up on a list like this, no? As Collider explains, Abbey Road is arguably the greatest record of the 20th century. It's high praise, and even if you may disagree — you shouldn't, it's pretty damn good — then it still stands tall amongst the crowd. Noted as much for its iconic album cover as its tracks, Abbey Road has contributions from all four of the Beatles, with George Harrison and Ringo Starr each providing their strongest entries ("Something" and "Here Comes the Sun" for Harrison, "Octopus's Garden" for Starr). Even the much-maligned "Maxwell's Silver Hammer" is far better than its reputation. The album has a blend of genres that work as a whole, serving as a snapshot of the band at the apex of their creativity.

The Beatles fell apart after recording Abbey Road, while Australian rockers AC/DC could have done the same after the death of original frontman Bon Scott, and even toyed with the idea of disbanding. Instead, they stayed together, with Scott's father encouraging them to do so. They recruited Brian Johnson and set out to record what would be the definitive AC/DC album: Back in Black. It was created as a tribute to Scott, with the all-black cover a sign of mourning, while simultaneously launching a new era for the band. That new era came with a commitment to elemental, arena-ready guitar riffs and straight-forward rock, with a frontman who brought his own energy instead of being a Scott-lite. To say it worked out pretty well is an understatement: Back in Black hit the top of the UK album chart, became the best-selling album that never reached number one in the US charts, and is currently the second best-selling album of all time in music history. There is legitimately not a single track that sucks, from opener "Hells Bells" (which, hopefully, aren't what you hear on the other side) to "Rock and Roll Ain't Noise Pollution."

These classic rock albums are masterpieces from beginning to end.

10 Greatest Cat-and-Mouse Thriller Movies Of All Time, Ranked

The world of thrillers hinges around cat-and-mouse stories, with plenty of the best thriller movies there are working as well as they do because of the characters they put up against one other in their narrative. Though just about every genre relies on its heroes and villains, thrillers oftentimes tend to do so slightly more, relying on an evenly balanced protagonist and antagonist to make its surprises as potent as possible.

There's something mythic about seeing two people who seem equally capable of taking one another down going toe-to-toe, as the smallest shift in things can thus bring major results in a story, and the matter of who comes out on top is usually not as simple as it might initially appear.

Indeed, cat-and-mouse thrillers have a penchant for throwing all the conventional rules of film stories out of the window, with villains winning, heroes being corrupted and never redeemed, and nobody being safe from a brutal demise. These are story approaches that carry some major risks with them, but plenty of cat-and-mouse thrillers pull all this and more off well, as these ten movies prove.

One of the highest-rated movies in the generally staggeringly well-received roster of films created by the Coen brothers, 1984's Blood Simple is a neo-noir crime thriller that keeps you guessing right up until its end.

Centered around protagonist Ray - who is having an affair with Abby, his boss' wife, leading said boss to hire someone to kill them both - Blood Simple is able to make a seemingly straightforward premise full of suspense. Everyone in Blood Simple seems to be in crosshairs as a result, with the movie's many twists regarding the deaths and seeming deaths of its central cast keeping you on your toes in a major way.

Unlike most of the other major cat-and-mouse movies, The Prestige doesn't focus on the story of a law enforcement officer and a criminal but rather two stage magicians, whose competition to outdo one another becomes increasingly fraught due to the bad blood and history between them.

The Prestige keeps you guessing in more ways than one with this side of its story, as the movie delivers its narrative in a non-linear form. This allows the film to provide you with information exactly as and when it chooses, and means certain narrative events aren't clear far longer than they would be otherwise, which works to leave a lot of questions lingering in the plot.

With that said, the final plot twists of The Prestige are borderline impossible to predict beforehand, and are sold decidedly well by leading stars Hugh Jackman and Christian Bale, allowing the more decidedly larger-than-life elements of the story to take you fully by surprise.

One of the darker cat and mouse movies out there, I Saw The Devil is an action thriller that veers into outright horror movie territory at times. This is due to the bleak way in which it explores its story of a South Korean National Intelligence Service member hunting down a depraved serial killer, and how the matter marks the protagonist increasingly as the story progresses.

Agent Kim Soo-hyeon's investment in taking down Jang Kyung-chul is made personal by the killer being responsible for the brutal death of his fiancé, Jang Joo-yun. This tees up Soo-hyeon to be willing to stop at nothing to find his target, with it being suggested as time goes on that justice, to him, is making Kyung-chul suffer, rather than simply ending his life.

This makes the conflict that the events of I Saw The Devil hinge around all the more tense, as we're left to question what the endgame of this matter will look like, and whether Soo-hyeon's concept of vengeance will come at the cost of his remaning humanity by the time the film is through.

Despite releasing over 30 years ago, Silence of the Lambs is still one of the first films people tend to go to when they think of talented actors such as Anthony Hopkins or Jodie Foster, with a core strength of the movie being how all its central actors deliver their a-game throughout the story.

Of course, the twisting, turning nature of the investigation to catch serial killer Buffalo Bill certainly helps keep things engaging, and doubly so after Foster's Clarice Starling turns to imprisoned cannibal Hannibal Lecter for guidance on how to find him. Thus begins a mystery not unlike if a Sherlock Holmes movie and Saw were combined, with Lecter providing Starling clues regarding the case, forcing the FBI agent to test her training in every sense before she finds her target.

Those who enjoy Silence of the Lambs also have a subsequent range of other solid works related to the film to enjoy if this core premise works for them, from other films in the Hannibal movie roster, to the Hannibal show.

In one of Harrison Ford's most compelling performances, The Fugitive follows the story of Dr. Richard Kimble, who finds himself hunted by Deputy Samuel Gerard as Kimble seeks to clear his own name regarding the murder of his wife Helen.

Kimble's escape from a prison bus does put him on the wrong side of the law, but the circumstances around his escape leave you rooting for him. At the same time, Kimble somewhat lucking out into being able to escape leaves the viewer also painfully aware that the vascular surgeon isn't exactly a hardened criminal, and sets him up to struggle with staying out of Gerard's crosshairs in a way that many other cat-and-mouse protagonists otherwise don't.

With Dr. Kimble being kind-hearted to the point of risking his own capture to save other lives with his medical know-how, The Fugitive is engaging in that it has both Kimble and Gerard working out who may really be behind things in their own ways, as Gerard gets increasing evidence that the doctor may not be the person he initially anticipated.

The fame and esteem Dirty Harry has as a movie hinges upon the fascinating dynamic between Clint Eastwood's titular protagonist, Inspector Harry Callahan, and the unhinged sniper he spends the movie hunting down known as Scorpio.

Dirty Harry is a classic tale of a hero who doesn't play by the rules and ends up being the only person who can catch a blood-chilling killer, though this places the two characters in a complex cat-and-mouse game wherein both figures play both cat and mouse at different points in the story, leaving you genuinely unsure at points as to whether Scorpio may have bested Harry for good, since the villain is a truly wily one.

As a result, Dirty Harry is one of the most lastingly significant crime thriller movies out there still today, and a film that other releases are still constantly being inspired by, making it even more fascinating to go back to now.

No Country For Old Men forgoes many of the conventional directions that other movies might take such a story in, creating a grittier overall result, and one that feels far more realistic even in its most dramatic moments, much to its advantage.

Heat centers around De Niro's LAPD detective Vincent Hanna, and his efforts to detain Pacino's professional thief Neil McCauley, as well as the gang he recruits to work with him. Unbeknownst to McCauley, said roster includes a far more sinister kind of criminal, meaning the story sees both characters confront their own morals as they strive to outsmart one another, and deal with the impact this situation has on their private lives.

Catch Me If You Can is in many ways the archetypal cat-and-mouse movie, starring Leonardo DiCaprio and Tom Hanks as a talented con artist and the FBI agent painstakingly tracking him down respectively.

Elizabeth I is a two-part co-production between HBO and Channel 4, directed by Tom Hooper of The King's Speech fame, and starring Helen Mirren as the titular Queen Elizabeth I of Britain, who ruled from 1558 to 1603. The series focuses first on her relationship with the Earl of Leicester (Jeremy Irons), and the second on her relationship with the Earl of Essex (Hugh Dancy). Elizabeth I is totally Mirren's show. Karin Slaughter Said “Absolutely Not” to Adapting Her Novel ‘The Good Daughter.’ Then She Wrote the Whole Series

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The bestselling novelist initially turned down Bruna Papandrea, wrote all six episodes of the Peacock series on her own and only then pitched it: "I wanted to make sure I could land the plane."

Some stories refuse to stay on the page. The Hollywood Reporter’s Beyond the Book column explores what happens when books make the leap to screen and beyond — unpacking what stayed, changed, how it was done and why it matters with the creatives who made it.

Karin Slaughter never planned to adapt The Good Daughter.

When producer Bruna Papandrea asked whether she’d be interested in bringing one of her novels to the screen herself, the author’s answer was immediate. “I said, ‘Absolutely not,’” Slaughter tells The Hollywood Reporter. “I don’t have time.” Related Stories TV 'The Good Daughter' Review: Rose Byrne and Meghann Fahy Elevate Peacock's Overly Familiar Southern-Fried Thriller General News Foundation Books Unveils First Titles in Macmillan Partnership; John Goodman to Narrate Animated Short Adaptation (Exclusive)

She’d already watched two of her books become hits in other hands: Netflix’s Pieces of Her, adapted by Charlotte Stoudt, and ABC’s Will Trent, run by Liz Heldens. Though she declined, Slaughter couldn‘t move on, and the idea that she might do it herself stuck. So she started writing the scripts, and she told no one.

“I wrote them completely on my own because I didn’t want to tell anybody if I’d done a bad job,” she tells THR. When she finally told her agent, she made one request: Don’t tell Papandrea — or anyone for that matter — yet. “Because she’d want an option. I just wanted to do this in secret.”

The secrecy also gave her time to take on the creative challenge. “I know from my novel writing that the beginning of a story is important, but just as important is the end,” she says. “People can read a book they think is mediocre, and that’s fine. But if it starts out really great and craps out at the end, they’re furious. So I wanted to make sure I could land the plane.”

With the scripts done, Slaughter went back to Papandrea. “I said to Bruna, ‘This is the show. If you’re interested, that’s great. If not, obviously I’ll take notes, but I haven’t wasted your time and money on an option.’ She read it overnight and said, ‘Let’s go, let’s do this.’”

The result is The Good Daughter, Peacock’s six-episode adaptation of Slaughter’s 2017 bestseller, which drops on the streamer in full on Nov. 12. Slaughter is the sole writer on every episode and serves as showrunner and executive producer.

The series stars Rose Byrne and Meghann Fahy as Samantha and Charlotte Quinn, sisters still living with the aftermath of a single event of violence during their childhood, with Brendan Gleeson as their father, Rusty. The cast also includes Shayna Benardo, Olivia Williams, Harper Steele, Jake McDorman and Michael Dorman. Made Up Stories, Papandrea’s banner, produces with Fifth Season.

When the team took the project to streamers, Peacock “just got it,” Slaughter says. “They had thoughtful questions, and they seemed like people who would be good partners.”

Though an author taking on the showrunner role for the first time, Slaughter‘s résumé shows why she could afford to skip the usual option-and-develop process. Will Trent was renewed by ABC this spring for a fifth season and had 10 million cross-platform viewers last season, making Slaughter’s catalog one of the more reliable book-to-TV pipelines. The Good Daughter, though, is the first time she’s been in charge of that pipeline herself, and she set ground rules early. Drawing on her long relationship with her book editor, she made a request of the network about notes.

“I said, ‘Obviously there are going to be notes, but I need you to speak with one voice,’” she recalls. “You can have all the meetings in the world and discuss it, but I don’t want a note that Kevin from accounting suggested and you don’t agree with.”

She also wanted the reasoning behind each note on the table, so she could interrogate it. “I’m not a ‘note behind the note’ person — I want to know the note. So let’s have a conversation so I can figure out what you want, how to do it and how to make sure we’re both happy with the outcome,” she says.

As a first-time showrunner, the biggest adjustment, she says, was losing a novelist’s room to explain things. “With a book, I get 500 pages to tell you what people are thinking,” she says. “So it was figuring out how to convey information without making it clunky. I never wanted anything to feel written. As a novelist, dialogue is probably one of my strengths, so it felt natural.”

To keep the mystery working and span across six hours, she made clue sheets as her way “to figure out how to make the puzzle work and give all the pieces.” When a note came in on a script, she could show exactly where a detail paid off. “I’d say, ‘We need this piece of information because it pays off in [episode] five or six,’” she says. “After a while, you develop a certain level of trust.”

She also kept the season concise. “I learned very quickly that as much plot as a novel has, there’s not enough for eight episodes. I made the decision early on: This is only going to be six episodes, because I’m not going to do a filler episode,” she says. “Pacing is a hallmark of my writing. I wanted you to be emotionally invested and on the edge of your seat. There’s the tension you get from the plot, but this is a story that relies on emotional tension, and I needed those emotions to hit at the right times.”

She also pushed for Peacock to release every episode at once. “I was scared they’d say no and parcel it out.”

The reasoning for the immediate rollout is because Slaughter thought of the series as “a six-hour movie.” That interpretation also shaped the choice of director Steph Green (The Americans), who directs all six episodes. “She understood that it was a movie and not standalone episodes,” Slaughter says. Each installment has its own genre — one is a legal drama, another a family drama then a murder mystery — but Green kept a “symmetry of place, setting and atmosphere” across all of them, Slaughter explains.

“I think the first thing I said to her was, ‘I’m so tired of thrillers where everything is dark, even in the daytime. Does anyone have a lamp in this house? I want the bad things to happen in daylight,’” Slaughter says. “That’s what we focused on, and our cinematographer, Zoë White, totally got it.”

Slaughter also fought to film in Georgia. “They made us scout in Toronto, and I was such a butt the whole time,” she says laughing. Once the team reached North Georgia, she recalls showing them the red clay — red is a key color in the story — and the kudzu. “Because we’re writing about things that happen to women and girls every single day, we needed to ground this in a naturalistic environment,” she says.

A Georgia native herself, Slaughter took the story‘s Southern backdrop seriously. “I was very clear: This is not Southern Gothic. Those words have meaning. This is not grotesque, it is not horror. It’s a story set in the South, but it’s not a Gothic story. Steph put together this beautiful look book, and it felt like a perfect fit because she got what I wanted to do and how I wanted it to look.”

The same thinking shaped the score. Composer Jonathan Sanford grew up in Maryland’s Prince George’s County but has family roots in Georgia, and he understood, Slaughter says, “that there’s a way to tell Southern stories that’s just such a trope,” with slide guitar and banjo.

“I said, ‘I don’t want to hear any of that. I want a new, crisp Southern musical language developed for this,’” she says. “As a Southerner, it was very important to me to represent the South for what it is, not all these stereotypes.”

Slaughter teases that Fahy even sings a track that will be featured on the show‘s soundtrack. “I knew she was a singer — she has a beautiful voice — and from the moment we started shooting I was working on everybody: ‘We’ve got to get Meghann to do something,’” she says. “I think they did it just to shut me up. But she did amazingly well. It’s a beautiful cover.”

Fahy and Bryne may star as the leading protagonists, but Jessica Biel was originally attached to star and executive produce the series, which scored a straight-to-series order at Peacock in March 2024. She eventually left the project in September of that year. Slaughter holds no grudge. Biel, instead, went on to star in The Better Sister, based on the novel by Slaughter’s friend and fellow author Alafair Burke. “I loved watching it. What do I have to be angry about?” she says.

Slaughter credits Bryne and Fahy for evolving her characters with Byrne’s casting as Sam inspiring a change in the character. “Sam in the book is a lot colder than Sam in the show, and that’s Rose, because she has this natural warmth,” Slaughter says. After watching dailies, she reworked lines to “lean into and support what she was doing.”

Fahy’s Charlie called for rewrites, too, particularly in her romance with Ben, played by Dorman. “In the book, Charlie is much more thirsty for Ben — she’s chasing him. Meghann Fahy is not thirsty,” Slaughter says. “So I had to find ways to express that longing for Ben in a way that was believable.”

And because Fahy handled Slaughter’s Southern rhythms so easily, Slaughter kept writing her more lines. “I’m going to write the hell out of this scene,” she remembers thinking. “It made more work for her.”

Slaughter has been candid about using violence in her books to open up a dialogue. With The Good Daughter, she hopes the series grounds the conversation in the fact that violent events happen to “real people” and that “there are real people living their lives after these things happen.”

She points to Bryne‘s Sam, who lives with lasting injuries from the shooting she experienced at a young age, including impaired vision and walking with a cane. “I watch a lot of television, and I can’t think of many shows where you see a survivor of gun violence and what their life is like,” she says. “We always hear that horrible statistic — ‘this many people died and this many survived‘ — and we think, ‘Thank God they survived.’ But we don’t think about what that recovery looks like. I wanted to show that.”

Byrne, she says, did “so much work with a physical therapist, figuring out how to move in a body like that. She has such physicality, and to distill all of that into this person recovering from a gunshot wound shows the range of her abilities. The fact that she could elevate that performance is remarkable.”

Despite taking on the showrunner title, Slaughter’s work as a novelist never stopped. After finishing the show’s scripts, she went back to work on her most recently published book, We Are All Guilty Here, and noticed the show has already changed how she writes, no longer keeping characters in one place for long stretches.

“When I write my Will Trent novels, I can have a whole scene in a car, and it’s a chapter,” she says. “But with Guilty, I was moving people around more.”

The reason was a day on the Good Daughter set. “You don’t know guilt until you’ve put Rose Byrne and Meghann Fahy in a car on a trailer in South Georgia, 10,000 degrees in August, and the air-conditioning’s not on,” she says laughing. “I was like, ‘Oh my God, why couldn’t I have set this in an office? Why are they sweating?’”

Whether she’ll take another book to the screen herself, Slaughter isn’t saying yet. For now, she’s finishing her next novel and focused on getting viewers to The Good Daughter. “It’s exactly the kind of show I’d love to watch,” she says. “I’m so proud of it!“

‘El Mal Padre,’ Inspired by ‘a Fear of Facing Fatherhood,’ Unpacked by Director Roberto Bueso

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Bowing to praise in San Sebastián Competition and sold by Film Factory, ‘El Mal Padre’ stars Eduard Fernández, a National Cinematography Award winner in Spain

Roberto Bueso‘s “El Mal Padre” (“The Bad Father”), a San Sebastian main competition contender sold by Film Factory, marks a tragicomic take on the mistakes of parenthood, and one son’s struggle not to repeat them.

Eduard Fernández (“Marco,” 2024), a four-time Goya winner and recent recipient of Spain’s National Cinematography Award, plays Santiago Balmes, a celebrated writer and academic who, in the face of a terminal diagnosis, calls together his estranged adult children with the hope of healing. What emerges is a portrait of a stubborn father trying to make amends, and a quiet reckoning with how futile that effort can be when it comes too late. Fernández leads a cast that includes Adam Jezierski (“Vota Juan,” 2019), Francesco Carril (“The New Years,” 2024), and Meritxell Calvo (“Burning Body,” 2023).

Bueso co-wrote the film with Óscar Díaz Cruz. It is his third feature after “La Banda” (2019) and “Llenos de Gracia” (2022), both of which played Spain’s Málaga Festival, and marked his first time in San Sebastián‘s main competition.

“El Mal Padre” is produced by Fernando Bovaira‘s MOD Producciones and MOD Pictures alongside Misent Producciones and Atresmedia Cine, with the participation of Atresmedia, Netflix and Valencian public broadcaster À Punt, and financing from Spain’s ICAA.

Alex Catalán served as cinematographer. Caramel Films will release the film in Spanish theaters on Nov. 20.

Variety spoke with Bueso about “El Mal Padre.”

This film was born out of losing your father and becoming one yourself. How did making the film help you process these difficult life changes?

It has been a very positive therapeutic process. Starting from my own experience gives you a solid foundation to work with conviction and honesty. Óscar Díaz Cruz, my writing partner, also became a father for the first time while we were looking for the story. We realized that the questions we were asking ourselves had to do with the fear of facing fatherhood. Our generation has reflected on and come to realize that many of the ideas held by the previous generation about the role of men, and therefore of fathers, can no longer be applied to ours. We wanted to try to bring those ideas into dialogue.

Eduard Fernández has talked about how hard it was to play someone this unlikeable. What did you two decide was the line for the character?

We never talked about specific red lines. We wanted the character to be very emotionally clumsy. He had made a lot of mistakes on an emotional level, causing a great deal of pain around him, but he still tried to justify himself. We didn’t want to judge the character. For Eduard and me, it was important to maintain the character’s harshness, while also giving him charisma and a sense of humor. We wanted to show his mistakes and contradictions, but also give him the opportunity to show his vulnerability and honestly reflect on the damage he had caused the people around him. It was a beautiful process, searching for that balance together with Eduard.

In terms of scale, “El Mal Padre” is a more intimate film than your first two. How have you found the shift in production?

This film has ended up being closer to me. I’m very fond of my first two films, but I think that with this third one I’ve been able to go deeper into my own vision and sensibility. Every project is shaped by the circumstances in which it comes to life. With this film, I’ve dealt with themes that were closer to me and that involved greater maturity. In any case, I always try to be honest about what I’m telling.

The film was shot entirely in the Comunidad Valenciana. Why was filming there important to the tone and story?

I’m from Valencia, and I know my homeland very well. Its landscapes are part of my emotional memory. I remember my childhood summers with a particular kind of light, with the presence of the sea and the rocks. For me, it has been a privilege to share that childhood Arcadia with the characters in the film, especially the children. I wanted them to return to the place where their family was still together before everything fell apart, and for it to be precisely there that they would have the opportunity to recreate a similar image, even if only for a moment.

The four-part Portugal-Brazil co-production, directed by Vieira and Rodrigo Rebouças, marks Volf Entertainment’s first true-crime series

Prime Video and Portuguese public broadcaster RTP have acquired rights in Portugal to “Gisberta – Life and Death of a Trans Woman,” a four-part true-crime docuseries from Leonel Vieira, the director-producer behind box office hit “O Pátio das Cantigas” and HBO Max thriller “O Grito.”

The four 45-minute episodes are in post-production at Lisbon-based Volf Entertainment, which is producing with Brazil’s Ultravioleta. Prime Video has SVOD rights in Portugal; RTP holds free-to-air and AVOD rights. Brazilian filmmaker Rodrigo Rebouças (“Meninos ao Mar”) created the project and co-directs with Vieira, who also serves as showrunner.

“Gisberta” revisits the 2006 murder of Gisberta Salce Júnior, a Brazilian transgender woman killed in Porto after being tortured by 14 minors from the Catholic-run Oficina de São José. The teenagers were acquitted of homicide, although some received lesser penalties or educational measures. The case sparked outrage in Portugal and Brazil.

Drawing on archive footage, court records, Gisberta’s letters, interviews and dramatized reconstructions, the series traces her life from Brazil to Europe, the killing and its judicial aftermath. It also examines allegations of sexual abuse and mistreatment of children at the institution, which closed in 2010. Interviewees include relatives, friends, lawyers, journalists and people connected to the case.

The project was presented at Conecta Fiction & Entertainment in Toledo in 2024, where it won two awards: Acorde music-licensing support and an invitation to Chile’s Sanfic Industria Series Lab and pitching sessions.

“The Conecta awards gave the project a good boost: they were the springboard for us to pitch it to Amazon Prime,” Vieira told Variety. “With most of the financing secured between Amazon and the Portuguese cash rebate, we then approached the public broadcaster RTP, which also decided to come on board.”

Vieira is also looking to French distributor About Premium Content (APC), involved in financing the project during development, for international sales.

“APC is an excellent international distributor for this project, and we’re confident they will do a great job with it,” he said.

Founded in 2022 and backed by an investment fund managed by Quadrantis Capital, Volf has built its slate around Vieira’s extensive film and television career. “Gisberta” marks the company’s first move into true crime.

“Yes, it is Volf’s first true-crime series and, I believe, the first to be produced for a streaming platform in Portugal,” Vieira said. “We hope it does well, because we have another powerful story that we could also tell.”

Volf’s credits also span the Spain-Portugal thriller “Favàritx,” released on Max and RTP. Vieira is in post-production on “A Noite” (“The Night”), his José Saramago adaptation co-produced with Spain’s Spal Films and starring Nuno Lopes, Adriano Luz and Enrique Arce.

‘The Empress’ Delivers a Satisfying Ending for One of Netflix’s Best Hidden Gems | Review

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

German period drama The Empress is one of Netflix’s most underrated gems, a historical series that dramatizes the story of Elisabeth I of Austria (Devrim Lingnau Islamoğlu), the daughter of a Bavarian duke who married an emperor for love and became famous the world over for her beauty and independent spirit. Set not in Tudor or Regency England—as so many of these sorts of series often are— but in 19th-century Austria, the story blends fairytale romance with political conflict and social intrigue as Elisabeth and her husband, the Emperor Franz Joseph (Philip Froissant), attempt to navigate both threats to their throne and the stifling responsibilities of power.

Much like the historical period it’s set in, the show’s third and final season takes on a darker and increasingly tense tone. Struggles for independence in various crown lands spark war, political upheaval, and questions about what the future of the Habsburg Empire can and should look like. The fate of one marriage is perhaps a small thing next to the intensity of international geopolitics, but it is Franz and Elisabeth’s relationship that remains the heart of The Empress until its final frames.

The final season picks up a few months after the dramatic conclusion of Season 2, which saw Franz leave to command his army on the front lines of the Sardinian War. Elisabeth, who has finally managed to give birth to the male heir that secures the Habsburg family succession, is left behind in Vienna, alongside her difficult mother-in-law, the Archduchess Sophie (Melika Foroutan). But while Franz ultimately makes it back from the fighting safely, the emperor who returns from the front is a very different man from the one who left.

Traumatized by all the death he has both witnessed and been responsible for, he’s rude and standoffish, repeatedly pushing away his family and rejecting the comforting advances of his wife. Froissant does some of his best work in these early episodes focused on Franz’s return, where it's not only clear how much he’s hurting, but how ill-equipped he is to deal with the mental and emotional fallout from a war he chose to fight.

Only through his affair with an unassuming woman named Terese (Marlene Burow) does Franz begin to find something like peace again, though that decision ultimately costs him in other areas of his life. It’s a dark period for both the Austrian royals: the discovery of her husband’s infidelity, along with a debilitating health scare, sends Elisabeth out of Vienna—and, more importantly, away from her husband and his mother—for the first time since her marriage.

The real Elisabeth loved to travel, and this shift is an important part of the character’s understanding of both herself and her relationship to the larger world. Lingnau is luminous as a young woman restored to the freedom that has long been denied her, and she effortlessly conveys determination to do right by the people who have entrusted her with power over them. On her journey home from the picturesque island of Madeira, she meets the dashing Count Andrássy (Dejan Bućin), a mysterious Hungarian revolutionary. Their intense connection happens almost laughably quickly–-over the course of what is maybe two conversations—but his charismatic personality draws her swiftly into his country’s political fight for self-determination, even though it frequently puts her at odds with Franz and Sophie’s ideas about Austria’s future.

Still, despite all the political wrangling between empires, vassal states looking for the chance to determine their own destinies, and Habsburg sibling Maximilian’s (Johannes Nussbaum) ascending to the throne of Mexico, the chemistry between Lingnau and Froissant remains the emotional glue that holds this series together. Sure, Franz and Elisabeth struggle with both marital and political problems, but no matter what happens, they still can’t seem to give up on one another.

Behind the scenes of 'The Empress', Netflix’s popular historical drama - from the sets and filming locations to costumes, stunts, and the approach the cast took to portraying this fascinating chapter in history.

Foroutan pulls double duty this season as both Archduchess Sophie and her twin sister, Princess Maria Anna of Bavaria, who appears just long enough to ruin a key political negotiation with Kaiser Wilhelm II (Sebastian Blomberg). And Nussbaum gets to play Maximillian at both his most idealistic and utterly defeated. While his character has long been notable for being... let’s just call it performatively over the top, this is perhaps the most human version of him we’ve ever seen. Also, it should be noted that costume designer Gabriela Reumer once again knocks it out of the park, crafting a stunning series of sumptuous gowns and lavish uniforms that serve as various forms of armor for almost all of the show’s characters.

In keeping with its overall fairytale feel, The Empress’ ending blends both fiction and fact. The show simply doesn’t mention some of the more harrowing events of Elisabeth’s later life and reign, glossing over the eating disorder-like obsession with her own weight and leaving out the tragic loss of her only son Rudolf, who was found dead with his mistress in what was suspected to be a murder-suicide. Her assassination at the hands of an Italian anarchist is reduced to a footnote that appears onscreen before the finale’s closing credits.

Instead, The Empress turns the establishment of the Austro-Hungarian Monarchy into a turning point for many things — a new dawn for independence in Europe and a fresh start for Franz and Elisabeth’s relationship, grounded in an understanding that this particular empress needs the freedom to be herself. Is it an ending that's particularly truthful about the events of these people’s lives and their relationships with one another? Not really. But is it a satisfying (if poignant) conclusion to the story we’ve spent three seasons watching? Absolutely. In a season festooned with so much darkness, it offers a welcome burst of hope that maybe, sometimes, love really can conquer all.

Almost 20 years ago, Baltimore’s Trapped Under Ice released their first demo — a record that would sow the seeds, in many ways, of modern hardcore. After all, it launched the career of one of the genre’s most dynamic frontmen, Justice Tripp of Angel Du$t, and later featured Turnstile‘s Brendan Yates on drums.

Now, the story behind the creation of that demo has finally been told — via the upcoming book “Intent to Distribute: Twenty Years of Flatspot Records,” out Feb. 5, 2027. “When you spend so much time doing something, you’re usually focused on what’s next. The Breakdown and Outburst demos in ’87. Beyond’s Dew it! and Turning Point’s demo in ’88. Merauder’s ’93 demo. What follows is the story of Trapped Under Ice’s early days and everything that came with it, from rodent-infested homes to cocaine bricks to fights with tasers.

Trapped Under Ice was created from the ashes of Nick Fury. What was the genesis of the band? Nick Fury’s big creative impression was that we were a teenage straight edge band, but we were all reaching the age of 20 and our interests shifted. As far as influence, I hear classic NYHC like Breakdown, Killing Time, Crown of Thornz…Breakdown and Crown of Thornz, yeah. Madball was a subtle influence, so I would say maybe some of that Killing Time is through their influence on Madball. We always get Outburst, too, but I think when people think of them, they’re hearing Breakdown or the similarities between the two. I don’t think I even heard Outburst until we put out the TUI demo.

How did you arrive at the band name? Does it come from the classic Metallica song?It was Sam’s idea and at the time, it wasn’t consciously because of a Metallica song. We didn’t know that was the source of inspiration until the first time we saw it on a flyer and thought, “Makes sense, Sam’s a Metallica freak.” Also, I was writing lyrics before we had a name and there was a theme of “this is cold” and “that’s cold.” So when the name came about, it was like, “Oh, this fits the lyrics. We’re gonna run with that.”

Did “Stay Cold” exist before the name Trapped Under Ice?“Stay Cold” was an early song we wrote and actually, I do believe that was before we had named the band. We hadn’t recorded the song yet, but we had that song written for a while before we recorded it for the EP that came out later.

So you did the demo before you started playing shows?Yeah, we wanted to make sure we had music out before actually doing it live.

What were the details of recording the demo?  We recorded the demo in our friend’s basement — Joe Mitra’s Wired Wrists Studio. I remember we recorded and the snare drum sounded kind of crazy. So he was like, “I wanna try this,” and we’re all young and learning stuff. We didn’t really know what we were doing and were all experimenting in our own ways, and Joe ended up tweaking the snare drum. At the time, it didn’t bother me too much — I was like, “Oh, it’s weird.” I didn’t really know or care or think too much — it was just “is there a mosh part that makes me feel something?” After the vinyl came out, a friend of mine said, “I’m really sorry about that snare.” He thought I was upset about the drums and I was like, “Oh man, is something wrong with it?” As time goes on, you become more familiar with what things are supposed to sound like, but I think the snare is one of those things that makes it special. It has this alien sound where people are like, “Wow, these guys are from another planet.”

So you guys are recording the demo — clearly, you’re very young. How did you pay for the recording?We were definitely bad kids at that time and he probably charged us like $500 bucks to record the demo— probably $100 a song or whatever. We didn’t have that at the time, so we would just do shit that kids did — rob people and stuff. That’s not cool. I feel like at that time, we got into going to parties and if the vibe was cool, we were cool and then if it wasn’t, we were robbing people that were mean to us. And we basically funded recording that way: stole stuff from somebody’s house and the things we stole paid for our demo. You know, I’m surprised we never got locked up or killed or something.

How did “Soul Vice” come about? Was it originally an intro?  So when I wrote that song and riff, I remember thinking I wanted it to be an intro, so it’s structurally not like a verse and a chorus. I just wanted a cool riff that sounds like Breakdown, that fucking hits you with the mosh part.

Lyrically, I wanted to be kind of vague, but I think it’s like a cool summary of my family life, which was a little crazy, and I had just moved out — I was actually 17. I was learning to deal with living on my own and adulthood and having anxiety problems and then I was kind of thrown into a world where I didn’t have a support system. It was just me and my friends. I didn’t feel like I had anybody I could depend on. I was learning about loss, people dying around you, and just trying to process all these feelings at once. And now all these adult things are being thrown at me and I can’t deal with them all at once.

“Reality Unfolds” is such a good example of a certain thing that TUI does musically where you basically just tell Sam to write something fun. He always does this — overnight he’s like, “Hey, I wrote this last night,” and it’s what “Reality Unfolds” became.

Did Sam come up with some of the vocal cadences or did you do that?So, I do a lot of that but that’s one thing I like about Sam. He’s very producer-minded. A lot of times he can come up with a whole song musically and then I’ll riff on it but he’ll say, “I like when you did this,” or “Can you do that here? I don’t like this.” And he’ll give me fun challenges like, “I like this word, what if you put this word in the song somewhere?” I think he’s a really aware person.

I met Sam at a young age, and he’s from a different area and could observe me really well. He was really conscious of my voice, my accent and the way I talk. I don’t think I talk like a lot of people, especially before I started touring a lot — hearing my voice and accent in a microphone and being like, “Oh, I sound like Baltimore trash.” So he would play off of that and be like, “Hey man, can you say this word in the song?” I’d be like, “Why do you want me to say that word so bad?” And then you hear it — it’s because he thought it was funny I say it that way. He would always play off my accent and I think that’s a funny early TUI thing.

If you think about how those songs are structured, there are many parts where the music drops out and there will be a short vocal solo — it’s a very pop song approach. TUI’s got a lot of that. Where do you think that came from? Yeah, I love that, man. Those are moments that we were obsessed with, and at first we were like, “We should do that in every song.” Biohazard has a couple of those moments. A great example is on “Punishment” where he’s like, “I question, not me…” — that whole section. Another one is “March of the Damned” by Integrity where Dwid says, “Father! Take me back!”

That’s another thing that producer-minded Sam really emphasized — people love relating to music through the vocals. It’s what we all sing along to. I think there’s a more direct reference for that one: “Big Kiss Goodnight.” I showed him this riff I wanted to use and just like with “Reality Unfolds,” Sam came in the next day with “Big Kiss Goodnight” entirely written. My part was the simplest, dumbest riff on the whole record and he just made this crazy song. That includes the part where everything rings out and “you just say some cool-ass shit.”

How did the original cover for the demo come about? Was that originally intended to be a promo photo? Kind of? We knew we wanted a promo photo and there’s these bands we loved that were big scary guys. I think we went into it knowing that we weren’t big and scary. We couldn’t compete with the Fury of Five promo photo or the Box Cutter promo photo or Madball. We weren’t intimidating people and we took the photo as seriously as possible because we were referencing the stuff that we love. But the idea that we were acting like that was funny. It’s weird because I remember people were really upset like, “These guys think they’re so tough.” That’s the whole point — we’re not and that’s why it’s cool.

We just wanted it to be a cool statement of “here’s the promo photo, that’s the cover.” I remember Sam making the logo for that and I think it has icicles coming off it. We knew that was stupid — we’re not hiding around it. Self-aware is a good way to put it.

There were holes in the floor, so Baltimore-sized rats lived with us. The homies would come over and crash or go out and be debaucherous and that was where you all met after the fight breaks out or after the police get called. And then he did it, it looked cool and I was like, “That’s fucking sick — nobody else has that.”

Klipa was a local guy who grew up in a white trash neighborhood and just did white trash shit. The only hardcore he was aware of was Hatebreed and Icepick, so we were like, “We can convince him to do this by telling him it sounds like Icepick.” Every day with Klipa is like 20 stories. One day we went to practice and we called him and he said, “I moved to Florida.” He got kicked out of Florida for stealing purses, so the next week when it was time to practice, he was back in Maryland so he practiced with us. He’s a character, like out of a movie.

You talked about debaucherous evenings that started on Clinton Street. Give me an idea of what one of those might entail. There was a group of people that we were feuding with and I remember one night we found out that they were all squadded up somewhere, so we called all the boys. When Klipa got to my house, he walked across the street to the El Salvadoran family that we’re friends with and spoke to them in fluent Spanish, which none of us knew he could do. They gave him some beers and five bucks. He told them that he was really broke and needed gas to get somewhere to fight these guys, so they gave it to him. We drove to go fight these guys, found them and fought. So when we came back to the crib, the neighbors were like, “What happened? Did you beat their ass?” They were really excited about it.

Things definitely got a little out of hand and went further than anybody anticipated a couple times. Sometimes weapons got used, and at the time our weapon of choice was tasers. Everybody had tasers because we thought it was funny — if you tase somebody, sometimes they would pee themselves. And that was funny to be like, “Hey, we made this guy piss his pants and that’s how we won the fight.”

What were some of the early shows for TUI?  So I think the first show TUI played was a Ruiner show. Van Damage was a band in Pennsylvania and at the time, we would play with them a bit. Down to Nothing would invite us to play with them. And then a lot of local shows, like one a week for a long time. We were thinking we’re just gonna do a Baltimore show or two and then we kept getting asked to play out of the state, which was really exciting. When you’re a kid, you don’t have any intentions of leaving the state. I think Lemoyne, Pennsylvania, would have been our first out-of-state show. Brockton, Massachusetts, was also an early adopter of Trapped Under Ice.

It’s kind of also the time when Title Fight was becoming a real band. So we played a show in Wilkes-Barre with Title Fight. It was crazy. I remember thinking, people really like this band — they’re gonna do really well. They obviously exceeded whatever I thought they would do. Later, I became aware of a new band called Bad Seed that was just the members of Title Fight with the exception of another friend or two. And it was very much similar in influence to Trapped Under Ice. They’re all a little younger than us and from what they told me, they were like, ”Oh, you can make music like that and people are into it?” So they started Bad Seed. They were a band that we would play with a lot in the beginning, too.

For those early shows, did you have enough material to play in addition to the demo? Did you have any covers that you’d play? We had a cover of “Last Breath” by Hatebreed that we would do sometimes. We’d also do a little Metallica medley, “Street Fight” by Breakdown. I think that’s it. Our set was songs from the demo and then one of those.

Let’s talk about the Baltimore and D.C. scenes around the time of TUI’s ascension. What was it like then? Definitely much smaller, I’d imagine, but there were several bands from the area like Gut Instinct and Next Step Up. Between Virginia and Maryland, D.C., and Baltimore, there were so many different waves that were happening. Desperate Measures was really important to our friend circle. They were one of those bands that made it feel cool to be from Maryland, which wasn’t the coolest thing. I always say Slumlords was really cool because they were one of those first bands to be a Baltimore band. There were a couple bands from Baltimore, but nobody was wearing it like that. And I think that was big with TUI — we wanted to be a Baltimore hardcore band. We’re gonna put Baltimore on our merchandise and make it a cool thing because it really never was cool.

Honestly, I think Stout is the most important band in Baltimore hardcore history. So you got Gut Instinct, which is a little more traditional hardcore and influenced a lot of early hardcore bands. And then there was Next Step Up, which is more of the groovy thing that really influenced bands like Hatebreed. And then Stout was like, the next level and evolution of heavy music where it just sounds demonic and experimental on some level. But it’s like, hardcore dudes who really like Pentagram and are trying to see how hard they can make music. Definitely a big thing that TUI took from.

That’s just who he is and you can’t turn it off. When I was a kid I was like, “I wanna be a unique individual,” and he’s one of the first people to turn me on to individuality in the context of heavy music. He doesn’t follow any of the rules.

I feel like there were two bands who were singing about their feelings, Crown of Thornz and American Nightmare — two bands from different generations. That’s one of the things people really loved about American Nightmare and made them a unique and very artistic band. I think neither of those bands were particularly focused on being heavy in the way that TUI was. I think our unique angle was to be like, “I got my feelings hurt by this girl“ or “I’m feeling abandoned by my father” on a song with a riff that sounds like Madball. But the ultimate lesson as a frontman in rock & roll music is Iggy Pop. That’s my number one. There are more obvious examples too, like Prince is really important to me.

When we first started playing, I remember Sam asking me about the way I moved onstage, shaking my hips and stuff. He was like, “I don’t know if that fits the vibe,” and I said, “That’s why it’s cool.” And to his credit, Sam might not always understand what I’m doing, but I’d explain myself and he’d be like, “Let’s run with it.” I’ve always done that — I always wanna get on stage and be flamboyant and a spectacle. I wanna move in a way that makes you uncomfortable. That’s not just hardcore music, that’s rock & roll music. I wanna engage and challenge people. Ebenezer Review: Johnny Depp Neither Helps Nor Hurts This Passionless Retelling

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Charles Dickens' 1843 novella "A Christmas Carol" is one of the greatest stories ever written. It's a tale that is both very much of its time as well as timeless, speaking to systemic issues of cold-hearted greed and inequality as well as the ability to change one's ways for the better. It's a malleable text that can be calibrated for all ages, just as it can be told with everything from a lighthearted touch to a more dour and sinister horror-centric one.

This versatility has led it to become one of the most commonly adapted stories of all time. To put it mildly, we are not lacking for versions of Dickens' "Christmas Carol." That doesn't mean further adaptations shouldn't be made, but rather that the artists behind them should have a unique take on the material; like Shakespeare, it's well known enough to not have to play completely straight.

Yet playing it straight seems to be the entire ethos for "Ebenezer," the new adaptation from director Ti West and starring Johnny Depp as Scrooge. If a "Christmas Carol" adaptation isn't taking a big swerve with either a singing Will Ferrell or Muppets, for example, the general assumption is that some renowned actor will give their take on Scrooge, as luminaries like Alastair Sim, Albert Finney, and George C. Scott have done. That seems to be what West and company are assuming with Depp in the role, and it's likely why the rest of the film around him isn't taking too many big swings. Yet it leaves "Ebenezer" feeling a little too plain on the one hand, and potentially unsavory where Depp's personal status is considered. It's a movie that seems to exist on Depp's shoulders for better or worse, a choice that leaves it being technically competent but ultimately confused.

If you already know "A Christmas Carol," then "Ebenezer" will not hold many surprises for you. Ti West and screenwriter Nathaniel Halpern treat Charles Dickens' novella in much the same way the Royal Shakespeare Company might treat "Hamlet," which is to say that they retain the bulk of Dickens' dialogue and structure (the film is even separated into five staves, as in the novella) while coloring outside the lines ever so often. After an original opening sequence detailing the literal end of the partnership between Marley (Ian McKellen) and Ebenezer Scrooge (Johnny Depp), the film chronicles the usual beats of the tale, from establishing Scrooge's miserly, calloused attitude to the gradual thawing of his soul via his haunting by three Christmas ghosts.

Although Paramount Pictures' ghastly marketing campaign promised that "Ebenezer" would present a Scrooge we'd never seen before and go places other adaptations hadn't dared, the true extent of any changes or embellishments to the Dickens tale lies in the way that certain very small supporting characters who purport to either be in service of the needy or in need themselves are revealed to be greedy con artists.

The choice does add a nice little layer of moral complexity to what is typically a straightforward story, yet West and company don't do all that much with it in the end. Given his extensive indie horror background, West does lend the proceedings a welcome macabre aesthetic. This is certainly the spookiest "Christmas Carol" since Robert Zemeckis' bizarre 2006 version. The three ghosts are especially ghastly and eerie, and the whole film has a Victorian London by way of '70s British films feel to it, like if Ronald Neame's "Scrooge" was mashed up with West's "The Innkeepers."

While much of "Ebenezer" is solid and skillfully crafted, it's all clearly in service of the character and the performer at its center. To say that Johnny Depp brings some baggage to the role is to make an understatement, for even setting aside the considerable issue of his offscreen life, his onscreen persona would be its own monolith to reckon with. Especially in this material, as it blatantly conjures up his past successes with director Tim Burton.

What made the Depp/Burton duo special was how the director was able to draw out some truly bizarre character choices from the actor while making sure the film around him fit the outrageous, heightened tone of the performance. Here in "Ebenezer," Depp appears to be shooting more for respectable prestige than crowd-pleasing outrageousness. He's not trying to pull a Jack Sparrow, in other words, and instead is letting the prosthetic makeup do much of the transformation for him.

With the exception of a small moment here and there, Depp's Scrooge is largely solid yet unremarkable. Which begs the question: why bother in the first place? It seems like the entirety of "Ebenezer" was arranged to allow Depp to chew scenery and showboat around, which the actor perversely refuses to do. It's possible that Depp now considers such an approach something he's no longer interested in, and it's not like he hasn't given grounded performances before, like his turn as John Dillinger in "Public Enemies," for example. Depp's performance as Scrooge simply doesn't feel fully realized. It's fine on paper, and even has flashes of depth, yet it ultimately is more calibrated than inspired.

I have a deep affection for "A Christmas Carol," and generally find something to like in all of its many adaptations. In this way, "Ebenezer" is no different. Ti West, Nathaniel Halpern, Depp and the rest haven't twisted the story beyond recognition, nor have they sullied it. Yet in playing things so generally safe, "Ebenezer" becomes a muted film.

West throws in a flourish here and there, like the great work with the three ghost characters or a moment where Scrooge is abducted through his bed that deliberately recalls Depp's cinematic debut in "A Nightmare on Elm Street." There's even an extended denouement to the tale which stands in contrast to most versions of "A Christmas Carol," where instead of rushing through Scrooge's change of heart, the film takes its time as the man makes his reparations.

Yet even that choice feels calculated in a meta sense to better present Johnny Depp, Movie Star, back to the public after his cancellation. After all, this story is famously one about a loathsome man who learns contrition and seeks to make amends, and while the film itself never breaks the fourth wall with this idea, it comes damn close. Even with that held back, it feels like "Ebenezer" is more a proving ground than impassioned adaptation, with West demonstrating he can do more than just indie horror, and Depp showing more of his restraint as a performer. What will happen to Depp the person is not for me to say, but he's going to have to work harder and take more risks than "Ebenezer" if he wants to give his screen career a second chance.

When the Lions meet the Patriots in Munich on Sunday, the fans in Germany are likely to see both teams go for it on fourth down.

That’s because Lions head coach Dan Campbell has long been the league’s most aggressive coach on fourth downs, and this year, Patriots coach Mike Vrabel is No. 2.

Coaches’ fourth down decisions are tracked for the Aggressiveness Index at FTN Fantasy, looking at how often coaches go for it compared to other coaches in the same situation. By that measurement, Campbell is first with a score of 1.45 (meaning he goes for it 45 percent more than an average NFL coach) and Vrabel is second with a score of 1.22.

The Aggressiveness Index is a more detailed way of tracking a coach’s fourth-down decision making than simply looking at the number of times a team has gone for it on fourth down. Some teams go for it often on fourth down not because the coach is aggressive, but because they happen to face a lot of fourth-and-shorts while trailing late in the fourth quarter. That’s not a particularly aggressive time to go for it.

What gives Campbell such a high score on the Aggressiveness Index is decisions like going for it on fourth down at his own 24-yard line with the score tied late in the fourth quarter against the Packers. That’s the kind of call that Campbell makes and other coaches don’t, which is why his Aggressiveness Index is so much higher than any other coach’s.

Campbell is at or near the top of the Aggressiveness Index every year. Last year he was second only to Mike Kafka, who was very aggressive on fourth downs during his seven-game stint as the Giants’ interim head coach. Perhaps unsurprisingly, after Kafka was not retained by the Giants, Campbell hired him to be the Lions’ passing game coordinator.

Vrabel has not been as aggressive as Campbell on fourth downs, but he has been more aggressive than the average NFL coach, both during his time with the Patriots and before that as head coach of the Titans. Both Vrabel and Campbell are former players with reputations for a blend of old-school toughness and a new-school understanding of the strategic advantages of aggressive fourth-down decision making. It’s a winning combination.
