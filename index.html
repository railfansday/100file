What is easy for humans to skim past in code, agents see as a call to action. They do not know better, latching on to that which is littered across the files read during the planning and research phase of the coding journey.

As agents' agency through tools and skills grows the ability for side-quests scales proportionally. Obsolete specs, long resolved bugs, and lagging documentation no longer rest in long forgotten corners of the codebase, but rather are unintentionally ingested as context indirecting and misdirecting the next wave of coders - the agents. This indirection is not free: in the best case it only costs tokens, steps, and time - in the worst case it distracts and grounds the agent on falsehoods leading to incorrect logic and decisions during implementation.

Measuring how often agents fall into these traps across millions of monthly sessions is impossible with manual human review - we need an objective signal derivable from the code itself. Inline TODO(bug/...) comments provide the perfect proxy. TODOs tend to be left behind long after the underlying bug is resolved or abandoned, while the linked bug ticket acts as the canonical record. By cross-referencing every TODO an agent encounters against the TODO's bug status and the user's prompt, we can automatically separate valid work from wasted detours at scale.

That step alone however was not enough to determine if the TODOs in the codebase were load-bearing or not. Paramount’s 4-Part Action Fantasy Epic Has One of the Best Seasons in TV History

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

With one of the best villains of all time

Few things feel more grown-up than dealing with the consequences of your actions. Whether you stay up too late and still have to get up the next day or make a mistake at work and have to answer for it, adulthood means no one’s coming to fix things. For the most part, it’s all up to you.

The Legend of Korra has been the subject of much criticism over the years, but it’s always been a show about its young hero Korra (Janet Varney) growing from a feisty teen into a relatively less feisty adult. It’s not until season 3 that she experiences her biggest change, dealing with the consequences of one of her biggest decisions: leaving the spirit world portals open.

It’s one of the best decisions Korra makes, but it also creates her most thought-provoking villain.

Season 3 is appropriately titled “Book 3: Change,” and it picks up after the Harmonic Convergence, the planetary alignment that flooded the world with spiritual energy. After a great battle (literally, Korra becomes a kaiju via astral projection), Korra leaves the portals open, believing that people and the spirits should live in harmony together. It’s a decision with vast ramifications, from spirits adorably popping up throughout Republic City to the awakening of air-bending skills in random people, reviving a culture that had been nearly lost.

One of these new benders is Zaheer (Henry Rollins), a member of the Red Lotus, which is a secret anarchist organization whose mission is to disrupt world order and eliminate the Avatar cycle. Zaheer uses his airbending to escape his high-security prison and rescue his comrades, who are some of the coolest villains in animation. These include the lavabender Ghazan (Peter Giles), waterbender Ming Hua (Grey DeLisle), and combustion bender P’li (Kristy Wu).

Meanwhile, Korra and her team travel the world, slowly collecting new airbenders in the hopes of rebuilding the Air Nation.

“Change” is a blockbuster of a third season, taking full advantage of Studio Mir’s animation capabilities. It’s the one season the studio animated from start to finish, and it shows. While The Legend of Korra always had incredible fights, in this season, the bending takes center stage. Ming Hua offers a new flavor of waterbending; she doesn’t have arms, so she fashions several out of water, looking like a terrifying spider. P’Li’s combustion is accompanied by a high-pitched whine, with the energy launching like a rocket through her third eye. Her hair is braided, and she’s huge. On a design level, these are the types of characters you only need to see once to remember forever.

Increasing the sense of peril is the fact that the Red Lotus feels so adult and experienced when compared to Korra and her friends. Zaheer and P’Li are devoted partners. Together, these four are close comrades, with their experiences and crimes weathered on their skin. It results in some of the franchise’s best showdowns, often involving characters that were previously sidelined, like Tenzin (J.K. Simmons), who has a standout battle in the Air Temple, and Korra’s father Tonraq (James Remar), who comes to her aid and prevents her from being kidnapped by the Red Lotus.

Then there’s Zaheer himself. He is a blank slate, the quietest and least charming member of a gang of incredibly cool villains. He’s so detached from the world and so at home with his ideologies that he feels inaccessible — no one’s convincing this guy of anything. His one tether to the world is P’Li, a relationship that the season cashes in on in spectacular fashion.

In arguably one of the coldest moments in kids' animation, Zaheer kills the Earth Queen by taking the air out of her lungs. Her death throws Ba Sing Se into chaos. And while the series never backs away from his extremism, like every great villain, Zaheer makes a lot of sense. His beliefs cause Korra to wonder if maybe the world would be better off without corrupt leaders like the Earth Queen, who lives surrounded by obscene wealth and imprisons her subjects for her own gain while the people in her kingdom suffer.

When it comes to Korra, there’s so much breathing room when the show finally drops her love triangle with Mako (David Faustino) and Asami (Seychelle Gabriel). It’s a development that allows Korra to focus on other things, like how bad she is at talking to bureaucrats or her having to hold back when speaking with people that are incredibly annoying.

Revenge is a dish best served 12 years later

Korra gets to explore her dynamics with other characters, including Suyin (Anne Heche) and Lin Beifong (Mindy Sterling), two standouts in the franchise. She’s also frequently paired with Asami, with the two finally moving past the fact that they dated the same guy (even if they’ll end up falling in love with each other the following season).

“I think Korra was more likable in Book 3 because — but that was actually part of her arc that we’d set out to do — is that she was becoming more selfless, and it wasn’t just about her own stuff,” co-creator Bryan Konietzko told The Mary Sue in 2014.

By the end of the “Change,” Korra has saved the world once more, but is more depleted than ever. After being poisoned by the Red Lotus, she’s in a wheelchair, watching her past self’s civilization restored as the Air Nomads rise once more. Another adult lesson Korra learns? Sometimes life’s greatest moments are bittersweet.

I Gave My Accounts-Payable Agent Hindsight Memory. It Stopped Asking.

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Every month, an invoice from a steel distributor arrives priced 3% above the purchase order, and every month someone in accounts payable stops, opens the contract, finds clause 7.2 (monthly steel-index adjustment, capped at 5%), and approves it. The matching software flags it perfectly. It just has no idea that this exact question was answered last month, and the month before.

That is the gap I built Ledgerwise for: an agent that sits after the 3-way match and resolves invoice exceptions the way your desk resolved them last time — and knows when that history isn't enough.

An AP desk matches three documents: the purchase order (what we agreed to buy), the goods receipt (what actually arrived), and the invoice (what the vendor wants paid). When they disagree you get an exception: a price variance, a short shipment, a GST rate that doesn't match the contract, a surcharge line nobody ordered, a re-sent duplicate, or a vendor letter saying "we've changed our bank account."

Detecting those is a solved problem, and in Ledgerwise it's deliberately boring: matching.py is deterministic Python with no model and no memory. It finds exceptions and measures them — a percentage for price, rupees for a surcharge, units for a short shipment.

The interesting part is deciding. That's agent.py, and it runs one loop per exception:

The memory layer is Hindsight, an open-source agent memory system from Vectorize. I picked it over rolling my own vector store because AP history isn't a pile of documents to search — it's a set of decisions with actors, amounts and dates, and I wanted something that extracts and organises those facts rather than just embedding paragraphs.

The obvious design is: recall similar past decisions, and if the top result says APPROVE, approve. Run that against the replay data and it looks great for a few weeks — until a logistics vendor sends a ₹6,800 fuel surcharge. Every earlier surcharge from that vendor was approved, but they were ₹1,200–₹2,400, and the agreed cap is ₹2,500. Similarity says "same vendor, same exception, approved." The money says otherwise.

That case reshaped the whole design. The question stopped being "what does memory say?" and became "is memory enough here?"

Each human decision is retained as one string with two parts: a prose sentence that Hindsight's retain pipeline extracts facts and entities from, and a structured line my policy can parse back out.

document_id keeps a re-decision on the same invoice from becoming a duplicate memory, and timestamp uses the invoice date rather than "now", so temporal questions work.

The magnitude matters as much as the decision. "Approved a 2.8% variance" and "approved a 4.2% variance" are different precedents.

I replay eight weeks of invoices from six vendors, each with one realistic recurring quirk, and play the AP lead whenever the agent escalates.

Week 1, Kaveri invoices 18% GST on a 12% line. No memory, so it comes to me. I reject and type: "HSN 2839 is contracted at 12% GST; ask Kaveri for a credit note. Never pay the 18% version."

Week 3, same quirk. Recall returns two human rejections. The agent rejects on its own, cites both invoice numbers, and drafts the credit-note email. Nobody touches it.

Week 5, steel at +3.1%. Humans had approved up to +2.8%. The agent escalates with "leaning approve" instead of extrapolating. I approve, and the learned range widens.

Week 6, Meridian Electricals sends a "we've moved banks" letter. Memory has six clean Meridian invoices. It escalates anyway.

The "Brief" button calls Hindsight's reflect for a vendor and returns what a new joiner should know before the payment run: fuel surcharges up to ₹2,500 are pre-agreed, one at ₹6,800 was rejected. That's the institutional knowledge that usually leaves when the AP lead goes on leave.

1. The reason field is the product. I made the UI refuse to save a decision without a typed reason. Those sentences are what escalations show, what reflect summarises, and what makes the memory auditable. "Approved" is a log line; "approved under clause 7.2, index sheet attached" is knowledge.

2. Store the magnitude, not just the outcome. Similarity search will happily match "same vendor, same exception" and ignore that this one is three times bigger. Every precedent carries its number, and the policy compares numbers.

3. Decide what memory is allowed to override before you write any recall code. Bank changes, unknown POs and anything outside a learned range are human-only by design. Writing those guardrails first made the rest of the policy much easier to reason about.

4. Don't let the agent cite itself. Retaining the agent's own decisions is useful for audit and reflection. Counting them as precedent is how a system drifts.

5. Make memory configurable, not magical. A skeptical, literal bank disposition (skepticism 5, literalism 4) fit an AP desk better than the defaults. One approval of a 3% variance should not become permission for "price variances" in general.

The honest limitation: Ledgerwise is only as good as the reasons people type. A desk that clicks "approve" with "ok" gets an agent that can count but can't explain.

Nearly 20 years later, Evans stepped into the shoes of Johnny Storm again with a memorable cameo in Deadpool & Wolverine.

Between his appearances as the Human Torch and his eventual transformation into Captain America, however, Evans headlined another superhero adventure that deserves considerably more attention than it reserves. Released at a time when Hollywood was becoming increasingly fascinated with comic book stories about people with extraordinary abilities, this ambitious science-fiction thriller offered a decidedly different take on the genre.

It featured an intriguing mythology, an international setting, and a collection of characters whose powers made them anything but traditional superheroes. Unfortunately, the film never became the franchise starter its creators might have hoped for.

Push is a 2009 science-fiction action thriller directed by Paul McGuigan and written by David Bourla. Released in theaters on February 6, 2009, the film stars Evans alongside Dakota Fanning, Camilla Belle, and Djimon Hounsou. Rather than adapting an established comic book, Push creates an entirely original world populated by people with extraordinary psychic abilities. Its premise revolves around a secret government organization called Division, which captures and experiments on individuals with special powers in an effort to turn them into living weapons.

Evans plays Nick Gant, a young man with telekinetic abilities who has spent much of his life hiding from Division. His circumstances change when he encounters Cassie Holmes (Fanning), a teenage clairvoyant who can see glimpses of the future. Together, they search for Kira (Belle), a woman with the ability to manipulate people's thoughts and memories, while attempting to stay one step ahead of Division and its dangerous agents. As their mission develops, the characters must use their individual abilities to outsmart an organization that knows almost everything about their capabilities.

What makes Push particularly interesting is how it approaches the superhero genre. Instead of relying on familiar costumes, secret identities, and spectacular displays of power, the movie builds its mythology around different categories of psychic abilities. Telekinetics, clairvoyants, and mind controllers operate within a shared world, and their powers come with limitations that make confrontations more about strategy than brute strength.

The film’s Hong Kong setting also gives it a distinctive visual identity, combining neon-lit streets, cramped apartments, and elaborate action sequences. The result is closer to a supernatural espionage thriller than a conventional superhero blockbuster.

Despite its ambitious concept and talented cast, Push struggled to win over critics when it arrived in theaters. Its complicated mythology and purportedly convoluted storytelling became major points of criticism, with some reviewers arguing that the movie introduced too many ideas without giving them enough room to develop. Rotten Tomatoes currently lists the film with a 23% critics' score, reflecting the largely unfavorable response it received.

Nevertheless, dismissing Push entirely overlooks some of its most appealing qualities. Evans delivers a charismatic leading performance, while Fanning brings an intriguing combination of confidence and vulnerability to Cassie. The film's visual style, creative approach to psychic abilities, and willingness to experiment with familiar superhero conventions give it a personality that separates it from many other genre releases of its era. It also arrived during a particularly interesting period for superhero entertainment, when films like Iron Man and The Dark Knight were helping reshape audience expectations.

Push might not be a masterpiece, but it remains a fascinating chapter in Evans' expansive superhero career. Its original mythology and unconventional approach make it worth revisiting, particularly for viewers interested in seeing the actor outside his familiar Marvel roles. Before he became the face of Captain America, Chris Evans was already leading an ambitious superhero adventure that deserved a little more recognition.

If an adaptation attempts to adapt a story faithfully but makes a handful of changes, it will often be torn apart by the fanbase as they pick apart how it lost sight of what made the series great in the first place. Alternatively, if the story feels so drastically different that it fails to even evoke a sense of what the original story inspired, it begs the question of why it was considered an adaptation in the first place.

So, when Prime Video's The Boys franchise took its lead from the comics as imagined by Garth Ennis and Darick Robertson, it was exciting and refreshing to see where the show changed course to make a story that would be engaging and surprising for longstanding fans of the franchise and newcomers to the series.

While The Boys comics serve as the foundation for the TV show and its spin-offs, Gen V and soon the prequel series Vought Rising, they are fundamentally different to the Prime Video series. As a result, the series benefits massively as it draws in the established fanbase alongside fresh eyes, and delivers something that genuinely feels like it belongs in the world of the comics, but does not remain constrained by what came before. History Says Energy Led All Sectors in the Last Bear Market. Here's What's Different Now.

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Energy stocks have delivered exceptional gains in 2026, but a surprising shift in the oil market could change the outlook for investors.

Energy stocks were the clear winners during the 2022 bear market. The S&P 500 energy stocks delivered a total return of 65.7%, while the broader S&P 500 (^GSPC +0.31%) lost 18.1%, including dividends.

Energy stocks are leading again in 2026, but without a broader bear market. S&P 500 energy stocks have gained 42% so far this year, compared with a 12% gain for the broader S&P 500 index, excluding dividends.

In 2022, rising oil prices boosted energy companies' earnings, while inflation and aggressive interest rate hikes weighed on the broader market. The 2026 rally has been supported by oil supply disruptions in the Middle East, with unusually high refining margins. But a recovery in global oil supplies could put pressure on energy profits and stock valuations.

ExxonMobil (XOM +0.34%) reported earnings of $55.7 billion in 2022, compared with $23 billion in 2021. Chevron's (CVX -0.15%) earnings also more than doubled to $35.5 billion in 2022. Both companies returned billions to shareholders through dividends and share repurchases.

However, April 2025 offered a different picture. President Donald Trump's tariff announcements triggered a sharp market sell-off. The S&P 500 fell 21.3% from its February 2025 peak during intraday trading, but did not officially enter a bear market based on closing prices. But S&P 500 energy stocks fell 13.7% in April 2025, making energy the worst-performing sector.

Rising concerns about slowing economic growth and weaker oil demand negatively impacted energy stocks in April 2025. This also demonstrated that energy stocks do not outperform during every market downturn.

The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) estimated that 4.8 million barrels per day of Middle East crude oil production remained shut down in September 2026, compared with a peak of 10.9 million barrels per day in May. But damaged pipelines and risks to oil tankers continued to affect shipments.

However, EIA expects global liquid-fuels production to fall short of consumption by around 1.3 million barrels per day in 2026. The production is then projected to exceed consumption by approximately 5 million barrels per day in 2027. This represents a potential swing of nearly 6.3 million barrels per day in the global liquid-fuel supply-demand balance.

This forecast assumes that supply routes will reopen and production will also recover. If that happens, rising inventories could reduce oil prices even without a recession in the U.S. The EIA forecasts annual average Brent to decline from $96 per barrel in 2026 to $84 per barrel in 2027. However, renewed geopolitical tensions or slower infrastructure repairs could delay the recovery.

Oil markets are already responding to expectations of easing supply disruptions. Brent crude prices dropped below $100 this week. Hopes of peace agreements involving Iran and Ukraine contributed to the decline, even though the underlying supply disruptions had not been fully resolved.

In the third quarter of 2026, ExxonMobil's Energy Products segment, which includes refining and fuels, reported earnings close to $7.2 billion. Chevron's downstream business earnings also more than quadrupled to $4.9 billion in the third quarter of 2026.

However, these strong downstream earnings may not persist. Disruptions to refineries in the Middle East and Russia have constrained gasoline and diesel supplies. This has pushed their selling prices higher relative to crude oil. However, as damaged refineries resume production and fuel supplies recover, the difference between refined product and crude oil prices could narrow.

Lower crude prices could also hurt earnings from oil production. ExxonMobil estimates that every $1 per barrel change in Brent crude prices would affect its annual upstream after-tax earnings by approximately $700 million, based on expected 2026 operations. Applying this sensitivity to the EIA's projected $12-per-barrel decline in annual average Brent crude oil prices suggests a potential $8.4 billion annual headwind for Exxon's upstream earnings. This estimate assumes that Exxon's production volumes, costs, and other operating conditions remain largely unchanged.

According to FactSet Research Systems' Oct. 30 report, S&P 500 energy stocks traded at 13.2 times expected earnings over the next 12 months, significantly lower than S&P 500's forward 12-month price-to-earnings ratio of 18.7x. However, the valuation of S&P 500 energy stocks was above its own five-year average forward 12-month PE of 12.6.

According to analyst estimates compiled by FactSet, S&P 500 energy sector earnings are expected to decline 7.8% in 2027, compared with projected earnings growth of 16.3% for the broader S&P 500. This suggests that energy's lower valuation relative to the broader market may reflect expectations of weaker future earnings, rather than necessarily making the sector a bargain.

I would avoid aggressively buying energy stocks after their substantial gains in 2026. A recovery in oil supplies could weaken earnings, even if the broader stock market remains strong.

Against a backdrop of repeated industry challenges in court, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) is locking in the drug price negotiation framework established under the Biden-era Inflation Reduction Act (IRA).

In turn, the process under which CMS selects certain high-spend Medicare Part B or Part D drugs and negotiates lower prices with manufacturers will carry on into 2029 and beyond. The rule codifies and expands policies previously established through guidance for the initial 2026-28 period.

The final rule is slated to go into effect Dec. 5, CMS said in a fact sheet accompanying Thursday’s announcement. By Feb. 1 of next year, CMS will select up to 20 drugs covered under Medicare Part B or Part D for price negotiations taking effect in 2029, with the potential to also select medicines included in previous rounds for renegotiation.

That upcoming group of drugs will make up the fourth cycle of pricing negotiations, with negotiated prices taking effect in 2029. CMS asserted that the rule gives both patients and drugmakers “a clear path forward as the agency builds on the program’s results and continues negotiating prices for high-cost drugs.”

The initial rounds of price negotiations have already included a slew of prominent brands, including Novo's semaglutide franchise of Wegovy and Ozempic and cancer meds like Bristol Myers Squibb’s Pomalyst, Pfizer’s Ibrance and AstraZeneca’s Calquence.

Meanwhile, the latest tranche of drugs selected earlier this year for negotiation, whose new prices would go into effect in 2028, includes notable entrants like Gilead’s HIV treatment Biktarvy, AbbVie’s Botox and Eli Lilly’s diabetes drug Trulicity, to name just a few.

Beyond codifying the framework for price negotiations in 2029 and subsequent years, CMS has also taken steps to fine-tune several elements of the program.

Namely, the rule finalizes a policy on how CMS classifies certain fixed-combination drugs, with the aim of addressing “potential program integrity concerns” that could encourage drugmakers to evade negotiated prices by steering patients toward new product formulations.

The rule also includes a provision establishing a temporary “small biotech floor” that prevents CMS from offering or accepting a counteroffer below a specified price floor for certain eligible small biotech drugs in 2029 and 2030. It also codifies two policies related to Medicare Part D intended to ensure plan coverage of medicines with a maximum fair price in effect and limit the prices plans pay pharmacies and other dispensing entities.

The IRA and the Medicare price negotiations it empowered have been frequent targets of the biopharma industry, which has broadly alleged that the policy is unconstitutional and that the pricing talks aren’t as voluntary as advertised. But attempts by major drugmakers and powerful trade organizations like the Pharmaceutical Research and Manufacturers of America (PhRMA) to push back on Medicare price negotiations have repeatedly stalled out.

In the interim, the U.S. government has made a separate and more recent push to rein in high prescription drug costs by leaning on President Donald Trump’s “most favored nation” (MFN) strategy.

“President Trump directed us to lower drug costs for Americans using every tool at our disposal,” CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz, M.D., said in a statement announcing the new rule. “This rule provides additional tools to help CMS negotiate better deals, achieve greater savings for taxpayers and beneficiaries, and improve health outcomes for seniors who rely on the Medicare Prescription Drug Program.”

MFN, for its part, has yielded at times, controversial deals with more than two dozen drugmakers, generally involving commitments to price new medicines in line with prices in high-income comparator countries and offer MFN pricing on certain existing drugs through Medicaid.

Through those deals, which have also included investment commitments in the U.S., drugmakers have been able to secure temporary exemptions from the Trump administration’s threatened pharmaceutical import tariffs.

Around this week’s midterm elections, several Big Pharma executives have signaled that they expect little immediate change to their thinking around MFN. Nevertheless, the lack of transparency surrounding the deals has already drawn scrutiny from Democratic lawmakers. With Democrats set to control both chambers of Congress beginning in January, the party may soon press harder to uncover the details of those agreements.

A Backdoor Sat in the Firmware Partition of 1.3 Million Set-Top Boxes

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 6 • Originally published at blog.neticslabs.com

Capture of the Google Cloud Threat Intelligence post “Shutting the Door on the XCPE Botnet”, taken 3 November 2026 from cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/shutting-the-door-on-the-xcpe-botnet.

On 2 November 2026 Google Threat Intelligence Group published Shutting the Door on the XCPE Botnet, with the disclosure that Google and industry partners had dismantled a backdoor enrolled on more than 1.3 million set-top boxes. The attribution is the part that changes how the story should be read: the researchers assess with high confidence that the malware was developed, deployed and operated for financial gain by Videostrong Technology, an Android original equipment and original design manufacturer.

The malicious software arrived preinstalled, across many device models and brands, built directly into firmware over multiple years. Google found dedicated infrastructure, evolving configurations and tools for managing affiliates, and assessed with high confidence that Videostrong intentionally developed, deployed and operated the backdoor and botnet for financial gain. Its response was to cut ties with Videostrong and ban the company from making devices with preloaded Google apps.

Google's own account of how the code got past certification is worth reading twice. Certified Android devices pass a suite of compatibility and security tests, and the vendor used obfuscation to exploit a gap in those tests, loading malicious code after the device's first boot. Review processes changed as a result. For anyone who buys devices on the strength of a certification mark, this is the uncomfortable lesson: certification covers the tested state, and the vendor retains control over code loaded after first boot.

Netics editorial visual: the scale Google reported for the enrolled device population.

Three stages are documented, and the first one explains why removal fails. A stub package, shipped as x.cpe in some builds and com.oemservice in others, sits in the system partition and runs at android.uid.system privilege. On boot it checks the oem/ directory, installs the corresponding x.cpe package into /data/app/ and reinstalls itself after any user uninstall, which makes the device's own settings screen an unreliable place to look for the truth.

Stage two offloads the work. The backdoor acts mainly as the command-and-control communicator and passes module execution to com.abc.nexus, a file that carries native anti-analysis utilities to defeat sandbox detection and configures the monetisation modules. Since August 2026 that loader has been phased out in favour of tailored droppers that install a single module, delivered through the same command channel. Stage three is the modules themselves, arriving as native ELF daemons, as SDKs compiled into the loader's bytecode, or as secondary APKs fetched remotely. Google says a significant majority of the staging files—modules, shell scripts and legitimate applications—were hosted as GitHub releases on operator-owned GitHub pages.

Google Threat Intelligence Group figure, 2 November 2026: the XCPE ecosystem, from the infected set-top box and its backdoor through staging infrastructure to command servers and modules. Source: cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/shutting-the-door-on-the-xcpe-botnet.

The same bootstrap returns geolocation and STUN parameters so the device can find its public address and traverse NAT, a file-upload URL for logs and diagnostic dumps, and switches for individual advertising modules. All samples analysed to that point used a single broker hostname, mqtt.szbboys[.]com. Command traffic that looks like ordinary telemetry and a broker reachable over the public internet describe a detection problem that lives in network logs, where most small organisations look last.

The economic design is a rented shop window. Videostrong sold backdoor access to criminal affiliates, and the loaded modules turn the box into a residential proxy exit node, an ad-fraud engine, or a host for further backdoors. The named SDKs are specific: Linkfog, AppSalt and NetNut among the proxy networks, CBOXTV and HiddenView among the ad-fraud engines, Moyu and Spirit among the backdoors that load more modules. AppSalt is the residential proxy SDK owned by Honeygain, which is owned by Oxylabs, a detail that shows how ordinary the corporate chains behind these modules look.

Greed shows in the stacking. A single device can run several modules with the same function, and the researchers report as many as three residential proxy SDKs installed on one device. A compromised box becomes a metered egress point for someone else's traffic, with the household or office IP address carrying the reputation damage.

The partner list behind the disruption reads like a cross-section of the industry: Japan's National Police Agency, Lumen, HUMAN Security, Plume, Synthient and TrendAI.

This story turns firmware provenance into an inventory problem. A new image can fix a kernel or container flaw on every host that rebuilds. This backdoor was compiled into the firmware partition of devices already deployed in living rooms; the vendor is barred from certification and the hardware remains in the field.

Google's disruption made three moves worth noting for any operator. It took legal action against the domains and servers used to control the botnet and to install modules. It took legal action against command domains used by more than a dozen affiliate malware families, which the researchers assess severs access to at least 10 million additional compromised devices, since those affiliates distribute through other channels too. And it changed how firmware integrity is verified on certified Android TV devices.

The operator's version of that work starts with a list. Which Android boxes, signage players, conference-room dongles and kiosk displays are on the network, who bought them, which vendor built the firmware and whether that vendor still exists in a position to ship updates. Most small estates cannot answer the last question, and that gap is the exposure this campaign exploits.

Google cut off command infrastructure and tightened firmware verification. Google took legal action to take down the domains, affiliate command servers are down, a manufacturer is out of the certified programme, and Google says it tightened firmware verification. A box already installed retains its backdoor. The researchers are explicit that the illicit market is resilient, with operators pivoting to new loaders or rival SDKs when a component is disrupted.

For a small organisation, the practical controls are unglamorous and cheap. Segment devices that need outbound internet from the rest of the network, deny egress to hosts nobody recognises, and watch for long-lived sessions to message brokers, which almost no television box needs. Prefer hardware whose firmware updates you can verify, and treat a bargain Android box from an unknown ODM as an untrusted endpoint with a screen attached. Prebuilt images carry their own provenance and inventory requirements. The earlier analysis of the Copy Fail container flaw argued from the software side; a full security audit and hardening is where an estate turns that reasoning into a list of devices it can defend.

“Backdoor operators, ad-fraud rings, and residential proxy providers operate as an interconnected supply chain.” The sentence closes the report, and it also describes the buying decision in front of any organisation refreshing its screens this quarter. The supply chain runs through the firmware partition, and the receipt is the only document most buyers keep.

InfoQ Homepage News GitHub Actions Lockfiles Reach GA to Secure Workflow Dependencies

Nov 06, 2026 3 min read

GitHub has made workflow dependency lockfiles for GitHub Actions generally available on October 28, 2026, two weeks after the feature entered public preview. Workflows can maintain clear tag and branch references. The runner executes the specific commits listed in the .github/workflows/actions.lock file, even if those references change later. The feature is available on GitHub Enterprise Cloud, Enterprise Cloud with data residency, Team, and Free plans. The GA release includes the capabilities from the preview.

The change targets a long-standing weakness in how Actions resolves dependencies. A reference like actions/checkout@v4 or a branch name can change. This means a hacked action can add bad code to a pipeline without altering the repository using it. Teams can pin to full commit SHAs, but this reduces readability. It also leaves transitive dependencies, like those from composite actions, unpinned. Lockfiles address both gaps by recording commits for direct and transitive dependencies.

Tooling comes through the gh actions-lock extension for the GitHub CLI, hosted in the gh-actions-lock repository, which generates and maintains the lockfile. Adoption follows the workflow. Once a workflow is onboarded, every dependency needs a lockfile entry. The Actions runtime then runs the recorded commit, no matter where the tag or branch points. This lets teams migrate incrementally rather than converting an entire organization at once. The extension has a verification mode. It checks if the lockfile is up to date without changing any files. This way, it can act as a CI gate to spot differences between workflow files and the lockfile.

Dependabot integration addresses the usual cost of pinning, which is stale pins. When Dependabot opens a version update for an action in an onboarded workflow, it also updates the matching lockfile entry. Both changes appear in the same pull request, so the workflow reference and locked commit stay in sync. GitHub says this works without additional configuration. For governance, enterprise, organization, and repository admins can enable a "Require lockfile" policy. This policy helps enforce adoption at the relevant scope.

GitHub is explicit about the limits of the mechanism. A lockfile guarantees that runs use the commits you locked, but it does not determine whether those commits are safe, and the announcement advises reviewing dependencies before locking them. The security value therefore depends on the review that happens when an entry is created or updated. In practice, the Dependabot pull request becomes the review point, and teams that auto-merge those updates will carry any malicious change straight into the lockfile.

The release arrives as GitHub tightens other Actions defaults. A November 2 changelog entry announced that pull_request_target will be blocked by default starting December 2, another change aimed at limiting how untrusted code reaches privileged workflow contexts.

For teams evaluating adoption, setup instructions are in the GitHub Docs page on workflow dependency lockfiles. A reasonable starting point is to onboard a few high-privilege workflows, such as release or deployment pipelines, add the staleness check to CI, and confirm that Dependabot's combined pull requests fit existing review practices before enabling the enterprise-wide policy.

Competing platforms have not announced a native equivalent so far. GitLab's pipeline security guidance advises users to manually pin includes and components to commit SHAs. Users should also pin images to digests. After the March 2026 supply chain incidents, GitLab introduced open-source pipeline execution policies. These policies focus on blocking known attack patterns instead of creating a dependency manifest. Microsoft's Zero Trust guidance for Azure Pipelines also suggests using SHA pinning. It advises limiting Marketplace extensions too.

CircleCI orbs use a central semantic-versioning registry instead of commit hashes. This approach keeps the registry immutable. Teams on these platforms often use cross-platform tools. For example, they might use pinner to pin tags to SHAs and add a CI verification step. They may also rely on scanners like ActionSieve to flag unpinned references.

These tools pin the reference written in the pipeline file but do not resolve transitive dependencies, which is the gap GitHub's lockfile design addresses. As with GitHub's feature, none of them judges whether a pinned dependency is trustworthy, so the review step that precedes locking remains the control that matters.

InfoQ Homepage News Vault Fork OpenBao Adds External Keys and Quantum-Ready TLS

Nov 06, 2026 4 min read

OpenBao has released version 2.7, adding read scaling for PostgreSQL-backed deployments, support for external hardware security module (HSM) and key management service (KMS) keys, and post-quantum cryptographic algorithms. The release also introduces control groups for human review of sensitive operations and new headers intended to keep requests consistent across load-balanced OpenBao nodes.

OpenBao began as a community fork of HashiCorp Vault after HashiCorp changed Vault's licence from the Mozilla Public License to the Business Source License in 2023. The project is now governed under the Linux Foundation's Open Source Security Foundation and is distributed under MPL 2.0, an Open Source Initiative-approved licence. Its stated aim is to provide a secrets manager under open governance rather than control by a single vendor.

The fork was both ideological and commercial. Some users objected to the loss of an OSI-approved licence and to the possibility that a vendor could change the terms governing infrastructure they had already adopted. Others saw a practical opportunity: Vault had placed capabilities such as namespaces, HSM integration and advanced scalability behind enterprise licences, while an open fork could make comparable functionality available without those restrictions. The move followed the wider pattern that produced OpenTofu from Terraform and Valkey from Redis.

OpenBao has developed well beyond a simple continuation of Vault Community Edition. Since the fork, the project has added namespaces for multi-tenant isolation, plugin-based auto-unseal integrations, and read scalability for its integrated Raft storage backend. Version 2.7 extends that scaling work to PostgreSQL, allowing standby OpenBao instances using that backend to serve reads in a similar way to Raft-based deployments.

Alex Scheel announced the release for the OpenBao project, writing that it adds "PostgreSQL horizontal scalability, External HSM and KMS-Backed Keys support, and Post-Quantum Cryptography". He also noted that the release arrived after a shorter development cycle than the previous one and included "many substantial features and several major security patches".

The external keys feature lets the PKI and Transit secrets engines use key material held in an HSM or KMS rather than storing it inside OpenBao. Support is delivered through third-party plugins, building on the auto-unseal plugin work in the previous release. This should appeal to organisations that must keep root certification authority keys or other high-value material under hardware control.

The post-quantum work adds ML-DSA and ML-KEM to TLS listeners, certificate authentication, PKI and Transit. Operators can therefore use post-quantum signatures and key exchange in relevant parts of the platform. The change does not by itself make a deployment quantum-safe: clients, certificate authorities and surrounding systems must also support the chosen algorithms. It does, however, give OpenBao users a route to begin testing hybrid and post-quantum configurations.

Control groups add an approval workflow to OpenBao. A new control_group stanza in an ACL policy can require a second party to review access to specified paths. This is aimed at high-risk operations where a single authenticated identity should not be able to act alone.

The release also includes breaking changes. The separate HSM distribution has been discontinued because the PKCS#11 plugin is now externalised. Vendor-specific built-in auto-unseal mechanisms for Alibaba Cloud, AWS, Azure, Google Cloud and Oracle Cloud have also moved to plugins. Kerberos, LDAP and RADIUS authentication, along with the LDAP secrets engine, have moved into the openbao-plugins repository. The file storage backend has been removed; the project had never recommended it for production because it lacks high availability and transactions.

Luca Berton, who interviewed Scheel at Open Source Summit Europe, wrote that external keys allow operators to "run your PKI root backed by an HSM" with key material comparable to Vault Enterprise managed keys. He also highlighted the importance of post-quantum support before government transitions expected around 2030, citing the risk that encrypted traffic recorded today could be decrypted in the future.

Commercial support is also becoming part of the OpenBao ecosystem. ControlPlane launched ControlPlane Enterprise for OpenBao in March, offering security hardening, supply-chain assurance, rapid CVE remediation, optional FIPS 140-3 compliance support and 24/7 technical support. That offering illustrates how an open-source project can remain freely licensed while vendors compete on operational assurance. ControlPlane positions the product as a supported path for organisations that want OpenBao's licence and governance model but still need enterprise-style operational commitments. Its materials also contrast OpenBao's MPL 2.0 licence with Vault's BSL 1.1 licence and note that OpenBao retains API compatibility with Vault for many use cases.

Secrets management being a foundational dependency has been covered in InfoQ recently. We previously reported on Vault 2.0 and IBM's support model following its acquisition of HashiCorp, as well as on the broader pattern of community forks preserving open standards after vendor licence changes. OpenBao's progress suggests that the fork is becoming a credible alternative rather than merely a protest project.

OpenBao version 2.8 is expected to focus more on security and stability, with likely improvements to authentication. For teams evaluating a migration, the immediate questions are less about individual features than about operational maturity: plugin lifecycle management, PostgreSQL high-availability configuration, post-quantum client compatibility and the availability of support for production incidents.

InfoQ Homepage Articles Identity as an Architectural Concern: Enterprise IAM Beyond Authentication

Nov 06, 2026 12 min read

For a single application, identity is mostly a vendor decision. You pick an identity provider, wire up SSO, validate tokens at the edge, and move on with the features. For one application and one team, that's the right call. It stops being enough as the organization grows. Login keeps working fine. Everything after that gets difficult: who approved a given access and whether it should still exist when an auditor asks about it months later.

This article draws on building and operating an identity and access management platform in regulated financial services, where a wrong entitlement becomes an audit finding. My argument is that at enterprise scale, identity has to be treated as part of the architecture. Teams that add it late tend to find it conflicts with decisions they've already locked in. I'll assume you have experience shipping OAuth 2.0 or SSO. What follows covers the design problems that appear once many services, and many owners, are involved.

Identity belongs in architecture discussions because it constrains decisions that look unrelated. The data model is where this shows up first. Once you have to explain and audit access, "who can touch this resource" needs to be a relationship you can query with history, because auditors ask what was true six months ago as well as what is true today. Service boundaries and event flows raise their own problems; each gets its own section below.

Regulation adds another constraint. Here's a useful test: Could you answer "Who had access to this system on 14 March?" from your data model alone, without grepping logs across several tools? If not, an audit wasn't part of the design.

A security review can catch a missing check or a misconfigured scope, but it can't retrofit a schema that was never built to answer that question. The cheapest way to pass that test is to record every grant, revocation, and attestation as an event with a timestamp and an actor from the start. Then the auditor's question is one query against the event log.

The Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) model is used by default and is usually sufficient. It strains when your questions shift from "Does this user have this role?" to how things relate to each other. Our entities are people, the service accounts they own, and the accesses those accounts hold. The questions we kept asking were: "Who owns this account?", "Is this access still valid?", and "What does this person control?"

All three are relationship questions. Ownership is a relationship. An access has a validity window and an attestation history attached to it. A person who leaves or changes roles pulls a set of owned accounts along behind them. In a role table, you rebuild each of those links with joins every time you ask. In a graph, each link is an edge and the query walks them.

That's why our backend models these relationships in Neo4j, queried through GraphQL. GraphQL fits because a query's nesting mirrors the traversal: this account, then its owner, and then its access. A caller asks for exactly the slice of the graph it needs, from an account down to its valid accesses. Nobody handwrites traversal logic or over-fetches a large object and filters it in memory. Figure 1. Ownership-and-access graph modeled in Neo4j, showing how flat RBAC breaks down when access needs expiration and nuance. (Image source: created by the author.)

Before the query, here is the model it runs against. There are three kinds of nodes: Person, ServiceAccount, and Resource. There are two associations between them. First, a person OWNS a service account, while second, a service account HAS_ACCESS to a resource. The association, HAS_ACCESS, possesses some properties such as expiration, the date for when the access will be no longer valid, and lastAttested, which displays the last date when the access was attested by the owner. Validity is an attribute of the edge rather than of any node since it refers to the particular combination of the account and resource nodes.

This query, as shown below, poses a question we frequently ask ourselves: Who is the owner of this account and which of its accesses are currently valid? Looking through the query from the top, the first MATCH gets the account based on its ID and follows the OWNS relationship until it reaches the owner. The next OPTIONAL MATCH follows the HAS_ACCESS relationship towards each of the resources, and the WHERE clause appended to it filters out any access where the expiry date has expired. Because it is an OPTIONAL MATCH, an account having no accesses left will yield its owner anyway. Finally, the RETURN clause yields the owner’s name along with all the accesses left with their resources, expiry dates, and last attested date in a single array.

Even here, it's easy to get it wrong. Put the expiry check in the WHERE clause instead of the join, and accounts with no valid grants vanish from the result, owner and all. The query works, but it still leaves three problems out of view. Ownership is a single join in this example, but ownership can be delegated, and once it can, that join becomes a walk up a chain of unknown length. In the graph, the same chain is a variable-length path in one pattern. The expiry check also has to be repeated in every query that touches grants. Any query that leaves it out returns access that should have been removed. In the graph, the check sits inside the traversal, so a consumer can't skip it by accident. Finally, when a person leaves, the accounts they own aren't in front of you, and you have to go looking for them. In the graph they're one hop away, because "owns" is an edge. Both queries are simplified from what we run in production.

But Neo4j has inherent costs. It's one more datastore to operate, and traversal performance depends heavily on how you model things. A badly shaped graph can be slower than the join it replaced. It was still worth it for us, because ownership and validity are things we traverse constantly. The flat version pushes the hard part onto whoever writes the next query.

Comparing everything in the platform, the service accounts took the most governance work. Every service account is owned by a real person: Roughly eighty thousand people review, rotate, and vault the accounts they own. An account’s safety depends mostly on that owner: whether they're still engaged and whether they've changed teams or left the company. An account whose owner has moved on is exactly the stale, over-privileged credential that nobody notices until it's misused.

Before the rebuild, the three things an owner had to do (attestation, rotation, and vaulting) lived in three separate tools. An owner had to remember which tool did what. That's a small annoyance for someone who does it daily. For someone who does it twice a year, or who inherited an account when a colleague left, it's a real failure mode.

The rebuild put all three owner requirements into a single workflow, so an owner sees one screen with what they own and what's due. Getting there took more than wiring screens together. Each tool had been built with its own assumptions about schedules and ownership, and once they shared a workflow, those assumptions had to be reconciled. Figure 2 shows the revocation workflow before and after the consolidation: Three separate tools, with revocation taking about three days, became one unified workflow that revokes in under a day.

Figure 2. Revocation workflow before and after tool consolidation. (Image source: created by author.)

In enterprise identity work, the risky integrations are usually the ones where your system depends on a system you don't govern. For us, that was vaulting. We already handled attestation and rotation well before the rebuild. We had written that code ourselves, so we knew how it failed. Vaulting ran in a separate tool, owned by another team, on infrastructure we couldn't change. Folding it into our workflow meant a critical control now depended on their release schedule and their outages.

The hard part was deciding what our workflow should believe when their API didn't answer, or answered slowly, or answered after our call had already timed out. Those are not edge cases when you're doing this for tens of thousands of owners. They happen every day, somewhere. So we made the integration "fail closed". If the vault was unreachable, the workflow didn't report the step as complete, and an owner was never told a credential was vaulted when it wasn't. That sounds obvious on paper. In practice it meant our side couldn't treat "we sent the request" as success. We had to confirm the vault's own state, reconcile it against ours, and treat any gap between the two as something our side resolves.

If every service makes its own access decisions, authorization logic ends up scattered across dozens of codebases, where nobody can audit it. When a rule changes, you hunt for it through every service that has implemented its own version, and you can miss one. What works is letting identity context travel across boundaries while the decision stays in a dedicated policy decision point. Identity propagates through scoped claims using OAuth 2.0 token exchange, so a service acting for a caller gets a narrowed token instead of reusing a broad one.

That's a sensible design for a distributed system and it indicates revocation is eventually consistent. Access stops once consumers process the event and invalidate any cached decisions, which happens some time after the revocation is recorded. That gap should be measured and capped before anyone describes revocation to a security stakeholder.

Events can be lost, so the cache also has a time limit. A cached decision is served only while it's younger than a maximum age. After that, it's re-resolved from the source of truth whether or not an event arrived. That maximum age is the worst case scenario for a dropped event. Before we relied on the design, I simulated the consumer and cache to confirm that a missed event never kept access live past it.

Choosing the maximum age involves a real trade-off. A short maximum age causes stale access to expire quickly, which is what security wants, but every expiry sends another check back to the source of truth. At our volume that load isn't free. A long maximum age is easy on the system and worse for revocation, because a dropped event has longer to matter. You pick a number you can live with on both sides, then state it plainly: A lost event leaves access live for at most that long. That's a sentence a security team can plan around.

The cache window was the small part, though. The larger delay was procedural. Before the rebuild, fully revoking access took around three days, most of the time being spent on steps a person had to complete in separate tools. With a single workflow, access revocation takes under a day.

For a single app with a few roles and one team, you can skip all of this. Flat RBAC in your primary database is the right choice, and Neo4j, Kafka, and a policy layer would be over-engineering you'll regret maintaining. The threshold is organizational: many services making access decisions, service accounts that outnumber and outlive the people minding them, ownership that has to be re-verified, and an audit obligation with teeth. Authorization is moving out of individual services and into policy engines that can be tested on their own. For service accounts, the zero-trust version of this is continuous verification: an account re-proves its entitlements on a schedule instead of keeping them by default. Our service account rebuild was a manual version of the same idea, because attestation is reverification with a human in the loop. The interesting question is how much of that human step can be made automatic without losing the accountability that a named owner provides.

On 26 March 2026 Uber, Verne and Pony.ai announced a three-way deal to bring robotaxis to Europe. Pony.ai supplies the autonomous driving, Croatian company Verne buys the cars and keeps them on the road, Uber brings the network. That same announcement already stated that rides would be bookable "via Uber as well as through Verne's app", and that Uber "intends to invest an undisclosed amount into Verne".

Two weeks later, on 8 April, Europe's first commercial robotaxi service started in Zagreb: Verne's app, about ninety square kilometres of the city including the airport, from seven in the morning to nine at night.

On 19 August the rides also appeared on Uber's app, with a licensed operator on board behind the wheel, Verne acting as fleet owner and service operator. On 10 September the first rides with nobody at the wheel began, on the twenty-two kilometres to the airport: test rides for invited passengers, while the commercial service kept the operator on board. On 7 October the investment was confirmed by Verne's chief executive, Marko Pejković: "I can confirm, yes, Uber has invested in Verne." The size of the stake was not disclosed.

The press covered the machine. For anyone coordinating a fleet, the thing to look at is different, and it is not an ambush: it is a choice. Europe's most advanced operator, the one that actually owns the driverless cars and has its own app, decided from day one to also go through the aggregator's app, and to let the aggregator into its capital.

If that is what the company with the robotaxis does, it is worth asking what a dispatch company will do: one with the shifts, the licences and thirty years of customers, but without a list to restart from.

You do not need to wait for robotaxis to see how this ends. It has already happened twice, with human drivers.

August 2020: Uber buys Autocab, the British company that supplies booking and dispatch software to private hire operators, which is to say to its own competitors. On 29 March 2021 the UK competition authority closed its inquiry finding "no competition concerns", and the deal completed in April.

It is worth reading why it closed. The authority had considered exactly the data question, whether operators might be pushed into handing data to Uber, and set it aside by observing that there are "other credible suppliers" those operators "could switch" to if the service worsened or if data were demanded. In other words: in the authority's reasoning, the protection is not a promise, it is being able to leave.

The operators' main question at the time was the same one: what happens to our data. Autocab answered that it would handle them as always, without sharing. No abuse has emerged since, and that is not the point. The point is that the answer was a statement and not a clause, while the real guarantee had already been named by the regulator: the ability to walk away.

April 2025: Lyft buys FreeNow for 175 million euros. It was not buying cars. Ninety per cent of the gross bookings on that platform in 2024 came from taxis, which is to say from other people's vehicles and licences.

In both cases whoever was driving stayed at the wheel. What changed is who stands on the other side: in the first the supplier became the competitor, in the second the app changed owner.

In Italy the instinctive answer is that none of this applies yet, since the road code defines a vehicle as a machine "driven by a human" and a car that drives itself does not fit. Testing is nonetheless governed by the 2018 Smart Road decree.

But those rules defend the licence, not the relationship with the person getting into the car. No law obliges a passenger to call one number rather than another. Customers go where booking is easier, and they do not ask anyone's permission.

The Biggest Stock Winners and Losers From the Midterm Elections

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The market historically performs well after midterm elections, but some stocks appear to be clear winners and losers following the results.

The midterm elections shifted the political landscape in the U.S., and, regardless of anyone's politics, that will impact some industries and stocks in a meaningful way going forward. Stocks have historically performed well following the midterm elections, regardless of which party wins. The S&P 500 (^GSPC -4.36%) has climbed from November to November 95% of the time since 1938, so now should still be a good time to buy stocks in general.

However, let's look at some stocks set to benefit from the election results and others that could be hurt.

Tariffs have been a bane to the retail sector, and the election results give retailers hope that there could be a future shift in policy. This isn't likely to happen right away and may not take hold until after the 2028 elections. However, the market tends to be forward-looking and already anticipating better days for retailers. Here are three retail-oriented stocks that could benefit.

Amazon (AMZN -2.19%) is the largest e-commerce company in both the U.S. and the world. The company's logistics network gives it a significant advantage, and it has been seeing strong operating leverage from its investments in AI and robotics. Meanwhile, any removal of tariffs should be a big boost to its retail operations, as it would increase consumer demand and help expand margins on the goods it sells.

Interestingly, Amazon's AWS unit is also viewed as a winner. While the elections were seen as a backlash against the AI data center build-out, this is expected to hurt neoclouds and benefit the big cloud providers. The reason is that the big cloud computing companies are generally better positioned to navigate regulatory hurdles, given their established relationships with utilities, regulators, and local governments, as well as their better financial resources and ability to secure long-term power agreements.

Shopify (SHOP -6.06%) has become the e-commerce software company retailers are increasingly turning to in an effort to take on Amazon. The stronger its retail customers are and the better they are performing, the more it benefits Shopify, which gets a revenue cut through its Shopify Payments solution.

The company also appears to be one of the biggest potential AI winners. This is a top stock to own if tariff pressures ease and consumer spending strengthens.

e.l.f. Beauty (ELF -3.33%) was one of the hardest-hit brands from tariffs, given most of its products are manufactured in China. Meanwhile, a $1 price increase for its namesake brand impacted its sales growth. As such, a rollback in tariffs could be a huge benefit to the company.

Meanwhile, the company's acquisition of Hailey Bieber's Rhode brand is proving to be a huge winner. The company is expanding Rhode's product assortment and distribution, which is leading to strong growth. A tariff-reduction tailwind would only add to its growth story.

Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX -9.05%) has become one of the largest neoclouds, with aggressive goals to expand its computing power capacity. Tougher permitting could put a real dent in its ability to grow as quickly as it would like, with permitting delays leading to lost revenue. Texas governor-elect Gina Hinojosa, for instance, specifically ran on limiting data center growth.

Meanwhile, it could also hamper SpaceX's ability to launch another U.S. mobile provider and complicate a potential merger with Tesla.

On the upside, if SpaceX can overcome technical hurdles and costs come down, its orbital data center ambitions could become a real advantage in a tougher terrestrial data center permitting environment. Unfortunately, the same can't be said for other neoclouds like CoreWeave (CRWV -9.83%), Iren (IREN -16.95%), and Nebius (NBIS -14.95%).

Palantir Technologies (PLTR -9.73%) has become one of the most important AI software-as-a-service (SaaS) companies on the planet, with its solution akin to an AI operating system that makes AI more useful in the real world. Its growth has been outstanding, driven by U.S. commercial customers, but the U.S. government remains its largest client.

The company's U.S. government growth has been strong, but a potential shift in administration could slow it. Given the stock's hefty valuation, any slowdown in growth could hurt the stock. However, I'd likely view any significant dip as a buying opportunity.

Diamonds are forever - phoenix club seek FA Cup upset

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The notion of your football club ceasing to exist is unthinkable for many fans, but for supporters of Rushden & Diamonds it became a sad reality in 2011.

The Northamptonshire-based side, backed by the late Max Griggs, rocketed through non-league in the 1990s and in 2003 reached the third tier of English football before financial issues after he stepped away ultimately saw them fold.

A community lost its focal point, and within years their Nene Park stadium had been razed to the ground.

"It was heartbreaking - we knew we'd been robbed." reflected Rob Usher, the chairman of phoenix side AFC Rushden & Diamonds, who will make their FA Cup first-round debut this weekend.

"I think it was that anger that probably fuelled the fans saying 'no, we don't accept it. We're not gone, we're not done and we want our club back."

Some 2,000 travelling fans will make their way to see the now eighth-tier Diamonds take on League One side Burton Albion on Saturday - a bittersweet reminder of days the supporters of the original club could have once taken for granted.

Usher, like many of those involved with the Northern Premier League Division One Midlands side, has followed both incarnations of the club - firstly as a fan, but also as a groundsman and turnstile operator before becoming chairman in 2022.

"My first game was an FA Cup tie against Shrewsbury Town in 1998. It was the year we ended up playing Leeds United in the third round." he said.

"Rushden & Diamonds gave us some great times. Winning leagues, the Duane Darby's, the Onandi Lowe's of our past. But I tell everyone I've never traded for what we have with AFC Rushden & Diamonds.

"This is a community, this is an army of people who turn up and give their time and their money, their care, their devotion... not because they want the club to exist, but because they want the club to thrive. That's why it's so special".

Diamonds chairman Rob Usher said the club was at the heart of its community [James Burridge/BBC]

Diamonds, who now play their home games at Hayden Road, started their journey to the first round proper with a 5-0 win at Kimberley Miners Welfare in the extra preliminary round on 8 August.

They have also beaten Long Eaton United, Desborough Town, Peterborough Sports, Braintree Town and Morecambe to reach this stage.

Along with Stafford Rangers, who host Grimsby Town on Sunday, they are the only side left in the competition to have played from the earliest round and have so far earned £23,194 from the FA's prize fund.

The winners of the tie at Burton's Pirelli Stadium will collect £47,750, with the losers taking £15,800.

Joining AFC Rushden & Diamonds' attempt to make more history will be Billy Sharp, the Championship's all-time record goalscorer, who has reversed his recent decision to retire.

The 40-year-old, best known for his spells with Sheffield United and Doncaster Rovers, scored his first nine professional goals while on loan from the Blades to the original Diamonds in 2005.

"They [Rushden & Diamonds] made me as a player," said Sharp, who will be targeting a landmark 300th goal while filling in for forward James Clifton who is serving a suspension for accumulated yellow cards.

Brown was involved in the phoenix club's inaugural season and has made 350 appearances across two spells.

"It's been a rollercoaster," he said. "It's a big journey and I'm glad to have been a part of it.

"The club is owned by the fans so it needs all the fans to pull together to be able to grow. They turn up every Saturday no matter where we are and it's nice to have a chance for them to enjoy a different kind of environment on Saturday."

This is AFC Rushden & Diamonds' first appearance in the FA Cup proper. Their predecessors, Rushden & Diamonds, last featured in 2010-11, losing 3-0 to Yeovil Town in the first round [Shaun Frankham]

While the travelling supporters will hope to see an upset against Burton, for many, the experience of bringing their community together for such an occasion will provide the most lasting memories.

Diamonds say they have put on six coaches "at cost price" to help transport fans to the game in east Staffordshire.

Many are part owners and volunteers, including husband and wife Teresa and David Hales who help look after the club's kit.

"It's brilliant, absolutely brilliant." said Teresa. "For a little club like us it's absolutely amazing. I'm just so proud of everybody. Players, volunteers, everybody.

"There have been times over the years where I've sat with my head in my hands thinking, 'oh God, what's going to happen next?' But we're on the way up. We're really on the way up."

Teresa (left) and David (centre) Hales are part owners of the club and volunteer as kit managers [James Burridge/BBC]

"It would be the shock of shocks," said club chairman Usher. "I've seen this squad this year pull off unbelievable things when the odds are against us, so I don't count us out.

"I've seen this club come back from death quite literally in 2011 and once again four years ago [when the phoenix club faced financial troubles].

"Diamonds are never done. Diamonds are forever."

Nene Park, the home of the original Rushden & Diamonds, was demolished in 2017 [Shutterstock]

Rushden & Diamonds were formed in 1992 following the merger of Rushden Town and Irthlingborough Diamonds.

The merger was the brainchild of Max Griggs, the owner of Northamptonshire-based Dr. Martens, with the aim of getting the single, larger club into the Football League.

The club reached the Conference, now known as the National League, in 1996 and realised Griggs' dream of EFL football in 2001 under the management of Brian Talbot.

In 2003, Diamonds were promoted as champions to Division Two, now known as League One, but Griggs' retirement as chairman signalled the beginning of their ultimate demise.

By 2006 they had been relegated back to non-league and mounting financial pressures saw them kicked out the Conference in 2011 before ultimately folding.

Rivals Kettering Town moved into the club's Nene Park stadium at Irthlingborough for a brief and ill-fated period between 2011 and 2012, before the ground was demolished in 2017.

The team with the second-best point differential in the NFL is the 49ers, at +58. The Jaguars’ point differential is 50 points better than the next-best team.

At 6-2, the Jaguars do not have the best record in the league, as the Bills, 49ers and Vikings are all 6-1. The 49ers are favored to beat the Raiders and improve to 7-1, and the Bills and Vikings play each other on Monday night, so the winner will be 7-1.

But at the midway point of the season, point differential is a better indication of a team’s quality, and a better predictor of a team’s future success, than win-loss record. That’s because with as small a sample size as half a season, a few plays in a few close games can skew the won-loss record, but a few plays don’t skew point differential. Historically, the teams that prove to be the best over the course of a season are the teams that win some blowouts, even if they also lose some close games.

Winning blowouts and losing close games is exactly what the Jaguars do. Both of the Jaguars’ losses were one-possession games that were tied in the fourth quarter, while the Jaguars’ wins have included some massive blowouts: The Jaguars’ 35-point win over the Colts was the biggest blowout of this NFL season, their 29-point win over the Patriots was the fourth-biggest blowout in the NFL this season, their 24-point win over the Browns was the seventh-biggest blowout in the NFL this season, and Thursday night’s 23-point win over the Ravens was the 11th-biggest blowout in the NFL this season.

The Jaguars have won four games by more than 21 points this season; no other team has more than one win by more than 21 points.

Jacksonville has a 3.5-game lead in the AFC South, and six of the Jaguars’ nine remaining games are against opponents with a losing record.
