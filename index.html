Cutting 64 of 256 Experts from a GGUF by Byte Copy: What the Headers Say About GLM-5.3-SLIM on One B200

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 11 • Originally published at ginigen-ai-edge-ai-news.static.hf.space

Scantist's GLM-5.3-SLIM-GGUF is a 192-expert version of Unsloth's 256-expert UD-IQ2_M file, and it is built by copying bytes, not by requantizing. We read both sets of GGUF headers on November 11, 2026. The file drops from 238.58 GB to 179.86 GB, a 24.6% cut, and the header math says 93.4% of that cut is the expert slice, 6.4% is a removed next-token-prediction (MTP) layer and 0.2% is router rows. Every one of the 225 routed-expert tensors in the new file is exactly 0.75 times the size of its source tensor, in the same quant type, and all 1,407 other non-router tensors keep their names, shapes and types.

The practical point for anyone sizing hardware: the cut buys fit, not speed. Both files read about 21.5 GB of weights per decoded token, because top-8 routing is unchanged. On one B200 the smaller file leaves roughly 11.6 GiB for everything else, which is the difference between "does not load" and "about 100K tokens of shared KV cache".

The repository Scantist/GLM-5.3-SLIM-GGUF was created on November 2, 2026. When we read it on November 11 it showed 1,303 likes and 11,066 downloads. It holds six shards of one model plus an importance-matrix file and three evaluation scripts. The base is unsloth/GLM-5.3-GGUF, folder UD-IQ2_M, which also has six shards.

The average bits per weight goes up slightly even though nothing was requantized. That is not a contradiction. The experts are stored at about 2.42 bits and most other tensors at 4 to 8 bits, so when you remove a quarter of the experts, the high-precision tensors weigh a little more in the average.

Cutting 64 of 256 experts sounds like a clean 25%. The headers show why it lands a bit lower on the file and higher on the experts. We split the 58,716,322,560 bytes of tensor savings into three pieces.

The three rows add up to the byte. The expert slice is exactly 25% of the routed-expert bytes in those 75 layers (54.84 of 219.37 GB), but those experts are only 92% of the original file, because attention, shared experts, embeddings and the output matrix do not shrink. A quarter of 92% is 23.0 points. The MTP layer then adds 1.57 points by disappearing altogether, which is how the total reaches 24.6%.

In GGUF the expert axis is the last, slowest-varying dimension of each routed-expert tensor. The header for blk.20.ffn_down_exps.weight lists dimensions [2048, 6144, 256] in the source and [2048, 6144, 192] in the new file. One expert is therefore one contiguous byte range of the same length in both files, and keeping experts means copying ranges.

A byte-slice like this one is cheap, reversible and lossless with respect to the source file, but it is a lossy change to the model, and the evidence for its cost is thin here. Separate three questions that are easy to blur: does the file fit, how much context is left, and how fast does a token come out. Pruning experts moves the first two. Batching and kernels move the third.

Does removing experts make GLM-5.3 faster? Not by itself. Top-8 routing reads the same number of expert bytes per token, about 6.86 GB in both files, and the whole weight read is about 21.5 GB per token. The gain is that the model fits on one 192 GB-class GPU.

Why is the saving 24.6% and not 25%? The expert slice removes exactly 25% of the routed-expert bytes in 75 layers, which is 23.0 points of the file because attention, embeddings and shared tensors stay. The removed MTP layer adds 1.57 points. The three pieces sum to 58,716,322,560 bytes.

Is the 192-expert file requantized? The header evidence says no. All 225 routed-expert tensors keep their quant type and are exactly 0.75 times the size of their source, and the other tensors keep their names, shapes and types.

How much KV cache does one token cost? The MLA part is 87.75 KiB at f16 by our arithmetic from the config (576 values, 2 bytes, 78 layers). The card lists 93 KiB with the DSA indexer, and 26.2 KiB at q4_0.

Does the pruned model score well? Scantist reports 84.8% on GPQA-Diamond and 92.7% on HumanEval, but did not run the 256-expert baseline on the same harness, so the cost of the pruning is not measured.

The Allowlist Guarded the Destination, Not the Route: A Proxies Parameter Leaked Credentials From an Agent HTTP Tool

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Agent HTTP tools have a problem that ordinary HTTP clients do not: the caller is a language model that reads untrusted text. When an agent fetches a web page, a ticket or a README, whatever is in that content can try to steer the next tool call. That is indirect prompt injection, and it is the threat the advisory describes.

The Strands http_request tool let operators attach secrets to requests safely, in theory. You listed an environment variable and the hostnames it may be sent to, like this (the example comes from the tool's own docstring):

The model then passed auth_env_var="GITHUB_TOKEN" and a URL. The tool refused any URL whose hostname was not in the list. That control is sound for what it covers: the model cannot ask for the GitHub token to be sent to evil.example.

The gap was a second input the model could also set. The PR description puts it plainly: the proxies parameter "is included in the LLM-controllable input schema, allowing an LLM influenced by indirect prompt injection to route credentialed requests through actor-controlled proxy infrastructure, bypassing the HTTP_REQUEST_TOKEN_CONFIG hostname allowlist." The AWS bulletin adds the consequence: "The credential is attached, and the request is routed through the actor's proxy on the first hop, disclosing the credential in cleartext."

The GitHub advisory rates it Moderate with CVSS 6.9 and classifies it as CWE-863, incorrect authorization. At least one vulnerability aggregator lists 7.4. Scorers disagree, so read the vector and your own exposure, not the number. The advisory also notes a second weakness in the same neighborhood: GET requests bypassed the consent gate. I confirmed that in the v0.8.1 source, where the confirmation dialog applies only to POST, PUT, PATCH and DELETE. A credentialed GET to a hostile route therefore needed no human in the loop.

Because it guarded the destination, and a credential's journey has more than one position. Before the request reaches api.github.com, it passes through whatever the client was told to use as a proxy. That first hop sees the request, and the allowlist had no say over it.

This is the same shape as the lesson from the symlink and trailing-dot bugs I covered earlier: a check is only as strong as the set of inputs it actually constrains. Here the check constrained the URL's hostname, while the egress route, the scheme and the port were all outside it.

Here is the check in v0.8.1, trimmed to the part that matters:

.hostname drops the scheme and the port. So http://api.github.com/user and https://api.github.com:8443/user both pass a list containing api.github.com. Whether that matters depends on what the credential rides on, so I measured it rather than guessing.

I wrote a short script that starts throwaway listeners on 127.0.0.1 and uses the requests library (2.34.2) with a fake token. Nothing leaves the machine. The relevant part:

The listener for case 1 received this, secret included:

The listener for case 2 received only CONNECT api.github.com:443 HTTP/1.1 and Host: api.github.com:443. The secret was not in it, because with certificate verification on, the headers travel inside the TLS tunnel.

That second result refines the headline, it does not contradict it. The bulletin's "cleartext" statement holds when the approved hostname is reached over plain HTTP, and a hostname-only check does not stop that. The tool also gates disabling certificate verification behind a separate environment variable (STRANDS_HTTP_ALLOW_INSECURE_SSL), which is why the TLS case stayed closed in my run. I did not test every deployment shape, and I did not run the attack against a real Strands agent. Treat the experiment as a demonstration of the mechanism, and the advisory as the authority on the product.

I also checked one more egress knob that remained after the patch: redirects. The tool exposes allow_redirects, and requests follows them by default. Against a second local listener on a different port, the Authorization header was stripped on the cross-origin hop, but a custom X-API-Key header was forwarded. The tool supports auth_type="api_key", which sets exactly that header. This is general requests behavior, not a reported Strands flaw, and exploiting it would need an open redirect on an approved host. I include it because it shows how many places "where does this credential go" can be decided.

Small, which is the instructive part. PR #513 changed two files, with 14 additions and 26 deletions. It deleted the proxies property from the tool's JSON schema, deleted the doc example that showed a proxy, and stopped forwarding tool_input.get("proxies") to requests. The test that asserted "the proxy was passed through" now asserts the opposite: an LLM-supplied proxies field must not reach requests.

The point is that after a fix like this, the right question is the one the patch author implicitly asked: which knobs on this tool should a model ever hold? The patch's own comment answers it for proxies: "Proxies are an operator/deployment concern, not something the model should choose."

Here is a runnable model of three gate designs against five credential-leaking requests and two legitimate ones. It is illustrative, not a benchmark of any product, and gate C is my design, not what Strands shipped (they removed the knob).

Three observations. Gate A, the hostname check, stops only the case everybody thinks of. Gate B closes three more by comparing the whole origin, and it costs nothing on legitimate traffic. Gate C closes the last one, the route, and it does so by removing the model's say over routing rather than by inspecting the route.

That last design principle is the one to carry away. You can try to validate a model-supplied proxy URL, but the validation has to know every host you consider safe, every scheme, every redirect, and every environment variable the HTTP library reads. Taking the parameter out of the schema is a smaller attack surface than any validator, and it is the option the maintainers chose.

When the test fails, a person has to decide whether the model should hold the new knob. That one review step would have caught proxies the day it was added.

Across v0.8.9 to v0.8.12 (September 15 to November 2), the release notes list 34 tools by name as deprecated, including python_repl, mcp_client, browser, code_interpreter, use_computer, file_write, editor and use_aws. The notes do not give a reason for the deprecations, and I will not guess one. The v0.9.0 notes say the same capabilities now ship as built-in tools in the Strands Harness SDK, with a migration guide linked from the release.

Practical consequences for anyone running it:

What is CVE-2026-18394? An incorrect-authorization flaw (CWE-863) in the http_request tool of strands-agents-tools before 0.8.2. The model could set a proxies parameter, so an indirect prompt injection could route a credentialed request through an attacker-controlled proxy and bypass the hostname allowlist for HTTP_REQUEST_TOKEN_CONFIG.

Which versions are affected and what is the fix? Versions before 0.8.2 are affected. Version 0.8.2 removes proxies from the model-visible input. Proxies are now configured only through HTTP_PROXY and HTTPS_PROXY in the environment, or in code. The advisory also recommends rotating credentials configured on affected versions.

Would HTTPS have protected the token? In my local test, a proxy on an https:// request saw only the CONNECT line and not the Authorization header, because certificate verification was on. The bulletin's cleartext scenario applies when the approved hostname is requested over plain HTTP, which a hostname-only allowlist permits. Do not rely on TLS to rescue a gate that ignores the scheme.

Is an allowlist enough for credentialed tools? Only if it covers the origin and the route. A hostname list alone does not constrain scheme, port, proxy or redirects. Pin the exact origin, keep routing out of the model's hands, and add consent for credentialed requests regardless of HTTP method.

Should I upgrade to v0.9.0? Not as a security fix. The release deprecates the package and turns every tool into an import error. Stay on a patched version only as a bridge, and plan the migration to the replacement tools, reviewing their parameters before you give them credentials.

Aquí está el cálculo que casi nunca se hace. Si un benchmark tiene n preguntas, un acierto más sube la nota 100 / n puntos. En GPQA Diamond son 0,505 puntos por pregunta. Un 94,44 equivale exactamente a 187 de 198, y un 93,43 a 185 de 198: dos preguntas de diferencia.

Además, 94,2 no corresponde a un número entero de aciertos (daría 186,5 de 198), así que no es una sola corrida contada pregunta a pregunta, y su ficha no trae nota de protocolo. No es un reproche: simplemente no se puede saber, y esa es la cuestión.

Para estimar el ruido de muestreo uso el intervalo de Wilson al 95 %, que se comporta bien con proporciones cercanas al 100 % y con n pequeño (el intervalo normal clásico se sale del rango). Para el 94,44 de GPQA da de 90,3 a 96,9. Para el 100 de AIME 2026 con 30 problemas, de 88,6 a 100: un 100 sobre 30 problemas es compatible con un modelo que falla uno de cada nueve.

La regla práctica que sale de la gráfica: cuando el margen entre el 1.º y el 2.º es menor que el ancho del intervalo, no hay base estadística para ordenarlos. Eso pasa en GPQA, AIME 2026, HMMT y SWE-bench Verified. En cambio, en MMLU-Pro (1,63 puntos con 12.032 preguntas) y en HLE (6,1 puntos con 2.500) el margen sí supera el ruido de muestreo. Con la salvedad de que esta es una aproximación conservadora: al comparar dos modelos sobre las mismas preguntas, un test pareado es más sensible que mirar intervalos por separado. Pero necesita las respuestas por pregunta, que la tabla no publica.

Este es el caso donde la nota importa más, y lo vemos en nuestros propios resultados. Las notas de Darwin-180B-RSI en AIME 2026 y HMMT febrero 2026 dicen que el valor del ranking es el voto mayoritario de 16 muestras (maj@16) y, en la misma nota, dan la exactitud media de esas 16 muestras: 98,75 en AIME y 96,59 en HMMT, frente al 100 de la tabla en ambos.

(El eje vertical de esta gráfica empieza en 90 para que se vea la diferencia; no leas las alturas como proporciones.)

La media dice qué te toca en una sola llamada. Para un producto con latencia y coste por token, la segunda es la más cercana a tu experiencia. La tabla muestra la primera. TensorRT-LLM 1.4 Makes trtllm-serve Run 8 HTTP Frontends by Default: RAM Cost, Connection Balance, and What Still Differs

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

TensorRT-LLM 1.4.0rc3 (GitHub release 2026-11-06) flips one default that changes the shape of your deployment: trtllm-serve now starts 8 HTTP frontend processes instead of 1. The release notes mark it BREAKING (PR #19379). I read that PR, the earlier prototype PR #16523, the metrics fix #19394, the current docs on main, and the release notes, then ran two small simulations of my own. I did not run a GPU workload.

The CLI default for --num_serve_frontends on the trtllm-serve serve subcommand went from 1 to 8. The launcher process owns the engine. The other 7 frontends re-exec the command line, attach to the same executor, and bind the same port with SO_REUSEPORT, so clients still see one URL.

Three details from the PR are easy to miss:

The same release candidate carries two other BREAKING entries (#21036 removes unused LlmRequest fields, #21041 removes obsolete TensorRT-backend compatibility surfaces). Those are API removals. The frontend default is the one that changes what a plain launch does on your host.

Every request on a single trtllm-serve process goes through one asyncio event loop: json.loads, pydantic validation, and then every streamed chunk written back. The prototype PR (#16523, merged 2026-07-22) reported this on a DeepSeek V4 disaggregated generation worker:

Doubling concurrency from 512 to 1,024 roughly doubled the delay. Doubling it again multiplied it by about 14, while decode step time stayed flat. The PR author called this an M/M/1-style blow-up, and I agree with the shape. In a single-server queue the mean wait grows like utilization divided by one minus utilization, so it looks harmless until the loop approaches saturation and then explodes. Adding GPU capacity does nothing for it, because the GPU is not the queue.

That is why eight processes help: the same parsing and streaming work is spread over eight event loops and eight GILs. The PR says the setting was already the production configuration in the authors' agentic disaggregated setups, with 8 on context workers and 4 on generation workers.

The kernel assigns each new connection to one of the listening sockets, effectively by hashing the connection tuple. The docs say it plainly: each request is handled entirely by the frontend that accepted its connection. Once a client holds a keep-alive connection, all of its requests land on the same frontend.

So the question that matters is how many long-lived connections your clients open. I modeled it with the standard library only. The assumptions are mine and deliberately simple: connections are assigned uniformly at random to 8 frontends, every connection carries equal load, and the total offered load equals 3 single-process event loops (so one frontend alone would be hopelessly saturated). Perfect balance would put each frontend at 0.375 utilization, a mean M/M/1 wait of 0.6 service times.

I ran 20,000 draws per connection count with a fixed seed:

With 1,024 connections you are within 13% of perfect balance. With 16 you are at a median utilization of 0.75, a mean wait of 3.0 service times against the balanced 0.6, and about one draw in thirteen saturates a frontend. This is an illustration, not a measurement of TensorRT-LLM. Real traffic is lumpier than equal-weight connections (a long streaming response weighs more than a short one), which I expect makes the tail worse, but I have not measured that. If your traffic comes through a router or gateway with a small connection pool, count its connections to this server before assuming 8 frontends means 8x headroom.

The PR's verification section is the only measurement I found, so I use it as is. One container with 1x GB300, Qwen3-0.6B, launched the way that cluster runs production:

Resident memory was about 1.99 GB per attached frontend and about 2.5 GB for the launcher. Every extra frontend is a full Python process that imports TensorRT-LLM and loads the tokenizer, which is what the docs warn about. The arithmetic for the default is 2.5 + 7 x 1.99 = 16.43 GB, about 13.9 GB more than a single frontend.

Two things to keep in mind. First, this is one run on a tiny model, so treat 1.99 GB as an order of magnitude, since a larger tokenizer or extra imports will move it. Second, a container memory limit sized around a single serving process will now get OOM-killed during startup. Attached frontends hold no GPU memory: in the PR, nvidia-smi listed only the worker (58.7 GiB including KV cache) and the launcher (748 MiB), identical with 1 or 8 frontends.

This is the part I almost got wrong. The body of #19379 lists a caveat: /metrics, /prometheus/metrics and /health are answered by whichever frontend accepted the connection, so scrapes see one frontend's share and counters appear to reset. It cites a measurement of trtllm_request_success_total showing 5 distinct values (6, 7, 8 and 10 among them) for 64 requests across 32 scrapes.

But #19394, "make multi-frontend metrics, profiling and liveness launcher-owned", was merged on 2026-10-28, two days before the default flip, and I confirmed with the compare API that both land first in rc3 (neither is in rc2). It reports the same 32 scrapes returning one identical value (64) afterwards. The mechanism: the launcher sets one shared PROMETHEUS_MULTIPROC_DIR before it spawns children, iteration-derived series come from the launcher only, and attached frontends forward GET /metrics to the launcher over a Unix socket. The current docs on main match that: /metrics is forwarded and /prometheus/metrics covers every frontend.

Why does this matter to you? Because Prometheus treats any decrease in a counter as a reset. To show what the sharded behavior would have done to a dashboard, I simulated 64 requests landing on 8 per-process counters, scraped at random every second request:

With per-frontend counters the 5th to 95th percentile spread is 47 to 86 (minimum 23, maximum 111), and 38% of runs were off by more than 10. Illustrative again, but it is why a build without #19394 and with 8 frontends is a bad combination for anything alerting on request rates.

AI training helped some women more job interviews, but not as many as their rivals got

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In a hiring test, AI training lifted interview calls for underrepresented women from 11 to 14 percent, while others got 25 percent without it.

Learning a popular skill like AI is supposed to help you get a job. But a new study found that for women who already get fewer interviews, having an AI certificate only helped a little bit.

In a London hiring experiment, it lifted their rate of interview invitations from about 11 to 14 percent.

Prospective employers invited comparable women outside those groups, with no certificate at all, to interview 25 percent of the time. The certificate narrowed the gap, but most of the gap was still there.

Nick Drydakis, an economist at Anglia Ruskin University (ARU) in Cambridge, England, ran the experiment in 2025. He directs the university’s Centre for Inclusive Societies and Economies.

Governments, employers and training providers have been promoting AI training as a route into work. Most research on AI and hiring, though, has looked at bias inside screening software.

Drydakis asked what employers do when the applicant is the one with AI skills.

Between January and June, his team answered 1,016 real job ads in London, sending three matched applications to each one. That came to more than 3,000 applications in all.

Every applicant was a fictional woman applying for a full-time private-sector job that didn’t require a degree. Each held a Level 3 vocational qualification, a practical course usually started between ages 16 and 18, plus a one-year apprenticeship.

The study ran as four separate experiments. In each, one application came from a woman in the majority group, and two came from a woman in an underrepresented group.

The underrepresented groups were Black British women, women aged 50 instead of 28, lesbian women, and women who disclosed being on the autism spectrum.

Sexual orientation showed up as a partner’s name, Helen rather than George. Autism showed up as a short line such as, “I am on the autism spectrum and known for my strong focus and attention to detail.”

One of the two applications from the underrepresented group also listed a six-month AI training certificate.

Its description covered using AI tools to automate office paperwork and handle data, aimed at the routine digital work these jobs involve.

Before any applications went out, 71 people, including HR staff, employers, students and hiring researchers, checked that each signal read clearly and that the matched applications looked equally strong otherwise.

Every application went out within a day of the ad appearing, and the team saw no sign that any employer noticed the matched sets.

Across all four experiments, prospective employers invited women from the majority groups to interview 25 percent of the time. Women from the underrepresented groups, with matching qualifications and no certificate, were invited 11 percent of the time.

The gap appeared in every experiment. Because the applications were matched, Drydakis concluded that employers were responding differently at the screening stage.

With the AI certificate, the underrepresented women’s rate rose to 14 percent. That’s about 28 percent more invitations than the same women got without it, yet still far below the majority group’s 25 percent.

“AI training can help open doors, but it should not be viewed as a substitute for fair recruitment practices,” Drydakis said.

The boost wasn’t the same everywhere. Women who disclosed autism were invited about 7 percent of the time without the certificate and 11 percent with it, against 24 percent for women who didn’t disclose.

Lesbian women’s invitation rate rose from about 13 to 18 percent, against 26 percent for heterosexual women. Both of those gains held up in the statistical tests.

For women aged 50, the rise from about 10 to 12 percent was smaller, and its statistical support depended on the test used.

The invitation rate for Black British women showed a small change, from 15 to 17 percent. Because of this small change, the experiment cannot determine whether the certificate provided any benefit at all.

The interview invitations also differed in what the jobs paid. Underrepresented women without the certificate were more often invited by employers advertising lower wages, about 7 percent lower per hour on average.

With the certificate, that pay gap shrank to about 4 percent. It didn’t disappear.

So the certificate helped in two ways, more calls and better-paid ones. However, neither brought the women level with the majority group.

Drydakis has argued in earlier work that AI skills act as a form of AI capital, something workers can build that pays off in the job market. This experiment fits that idea, up to a point.

“If we want technological change to create opportunities for everyone, investment in digital skills must be matched by strong efforts to tackle discrimination and ensure qualifications are assessed consistently across all applicant groups,” he said.

No majority-group applicant ever carried the AI certificate. That means the study can’t say whether the training would help every applicant just as much. If it did, the gap between groups wouldn’t shrink at all.

The study also stopped at the interview invitation. It didn’t follow job offers, actual pay, or how anyone was treated at work. Nor can it tell whether employers acted on prejudice or on guesses about who would do the job well.

All the applicants were women seeking jobs without a degree requirement in one city, so the results may not hold for graduate jobs, men, or other places.

A certificate that stood out in 2025 may also become routine as AI training spreads, and repeating the experiment in a few years would show whether its value holds up.

The full study was published in the journal Industrial Relations: A Journal of Economy and Society.

Does the President's Removal Power Extend to the Voice of America?

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

The DC Circuit affirms an injunction barring the removal of VOA head Michael Abramowicz. Will the justices see the case the same way?

Trump v. Slaughter affirmed the President's presumptive power to remove principal officers (other than members of the Federal Reserve Board). But does this power also extend to inferior officers? And is it constitutional for Congress to effectively insulate some inferior officers from presidential removal?

Today, in Abramowitz v. Lake, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit affirmed a district court injunction barring the removal of Michael Abramowitz, the head of the Voice of America. In the process, it rejected the Department of Justice's arguments that the district court lacked jurisdiction to consider the case and held Congress may (as a practical matter) insulate inferior officers by presidential removal by vesting removal authority in the hands of an agency head that requires Senate confirmation.

Judge Pillard wrote for the panel, and was joined by Judges Srinivasan and Pan. Here is how she summarized the opinion.

Seeking to preserve the journalistic integrity of the news broadcasters within the U.S. Agency for Global Media, Congress empowered a separate agency, the International Broadcasting Advisory Board, with final authority over the hiring and firing of the broadcasters' heads-of-network. The leaders of the Agency for Global Media ignored that statute when they attempted unilaterally to fire Michael Abramowitz, the head of Voice of America. Abramowitz sued, and the district court permanently enjoined the Agency for Global Media from removing Abramowitz without the International Broadcasting Advisory Board's approval.

We affirm the injunction. The district court had jurisdiction to enforce the statutory requirements governing the Voice of America directorship. Those statutory requirements are consistent with Article II. And the district court had equitable power to enjoin defendants from effectuating the illegal termination.

While acknowledging that, as a general matter, Congress has channeled litigation concerning allegedly improper removal of government employees to the Merit Systems Protection Board under the Civil Service Reform Act, the panel concluded that this general rule does not apply to the VOA as "Congress gave no hint" that it sought to divest district courts of jurisdiction to hear cases such as this when it revised the statute governing VOA in 2021. The removal protection of the VOA head "is not a standard employment law," according to the D.C. Circuit, "but a durable barrier against any breach of the firewall protecting the journalistic integrity of Voice of America." Writes Judge Pillard: "Congress had not reason to channel Abramowitz's claim through the CSRA, and every reason not to."

On the merits, the panel concluded Congress lawfully insulated the head of the VOA--an inferior officer--from removal by the President by vesting authority to appoint and remove the head of VOA in the International Broadcasting Advisory Board (IBAB).

IBAB is a seven-member "separate independent agency," the members of which must be bipartisan and are subject to Senate confirmation. IBAB is the relevant department head, even though it does not directly supervise VOA (as that job is performed by the U.S. Agency for Global Media-- an arrangement the panel concluded is lawful under Morrison v. Olson.

The Department of Justice argued this unconstitutionally constrains the President's removal power, but the court was unconvinced.

So long as the President has adequate control over the department head, and the department head can control the inferior officer (as is the case here), the President can transitively control the inferior officer. The department head's supervision "preserv[es] the chain of command in Article II." Braidwood, 606 U.S. at 754.

Yet practical effect of the court's ruling is that there is nothing the President can do to replace the head of the VOA. Six of the seven IBAB seats are currently open (having been removed by President Trump in 2025). Thus the Board is inquorate, and lacks the ability to remove the head of the VOA (or do much else). And, should Congress refuse to confirm new IBAB members, the current VOA head will remain in place. The DC Circuit may conclude this arrangement formally leaves the President with "adequate control" over the Board, but what good is control over a department head that is incapable of acting?

I suspect the Administration will seek further review, ultimately in the Supreme Court.  The question is whether it files a petition for certiorari right away, or first seeks rehearing en banc. While there is no likelihood the full D.C. Circuit will disturb the panel ruling, a petition for rehearing en banc might generate a dissent that could help attract the justices' attention. This case would also give the justices the opportunity to revisit Morrison v. Olson and say more about the extent to which inferior officers may (or may not) be insulated from presidential removal.

8 Movies To Watch if You Love Ken Russell's 'The Devils'

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

In 17th-century France, Loudun became the site of the Loudun possessions, a gruesome case of alleged mass demonic possession that is now understood as a landmark study in mass hysteria. The "possessions" were blamed on Urbain Grandier, a charismatic but womanizing local priest who was then executed for political purposes. This event became the inspiration for Aldous Huxley's 1952 non-fiction book The Devils of Loudun, and then John Whiting's 1960 stage play The Devils. In 1971, Ken Russell took both of these works as inspiration and made The Devils.

One of the most notoriously heavily-censored horror movies in film history, The Devils was massively controversial upon release for its graphic sexual imagery and blasphemous content, but has since come to be recognized as one of the greatest films of its time. With the recent release of the 4K restoration of the film's 114-minute director's cut, now's about as perfect a time as any to watch the eight most noteworthy films that feel remarkably similar to The Devils for one reason or another.

As far as deliberately and calculatedly blasphemous films go, it doesn't get much more iconic than Viridiana, made by one of the most notoriously anti-clerical auteurs in film history, Luis Buñuel. Arguably the greatest of Buñuel's Mexican movies, Viridiana is one of the greatest movie classics of 1961, following a devout novice nun who abandons her vows to shelter local vagrants after the suicide of her fetishistic uncle.

Its surrealistic imagery is about as brilliant as you'd expect from any Buñuel film.

A must-see for all those whose favorite part of The Devils is the scathing study of The Profane as a philosophical concept, Viridiana is an unforgiving critique of religious dogma and naive idealism. Its surrealistic imagery is about as brilliant as you'd expect from any Buñuel film, and Silvia Pinal's lead performance is a tour de force. It's definitely more overtly sacrilegious than The Devils, but it's every bit as much of a hyper-influential must-see.

Before The Wizard of Oz came around to revolutionize fantasy cinema forever, there were many fantasy films that are also still remembered among the greatest of all time, including the horror essay film Häxan. This silent Swedish masterpiece is a semi-documentary blends scholarly literature and phantasmagoric horror, charting the history of witchcraft and Satanism from the Middle Ages to the 20th century.

It's one of the clearest and strongest cinematic ancestors to Ken Russell's magnum opus, and one of the most important documentaries in the history of cinema. Insightful without ever feeling didactic and atmospheric without ever sacrificing its scholarly dimensions, Häxan laid the foundations for horror cinema to explore themes of religious hysteria, state control through religion, and institutional persecution. Both through its visuals and its mere premise, it was far ahead of its time.

Robin Hardy's The Wicker Man is one of the greatest and most influential British horror movies of the 20th century, as well as one of the most important folk horror films of all time. It's based on David Pinner's horror novel Ritual, and it follows a devout Christian cop who travels to a remote Scottish island to investigate a young girl's disappearance, only to discover that the local pagan community has lured him there as a human sacrifice for a failing harvest.

Much like The Devils, The Wicker Man explores how sexual repression can be weaponized and exploited by oppressive forces; and much like The Devils, it forces the sacred and the profane to clash visually, narratively, and thematically throughout. It's one of those movies unlike anything you've ever seen before, a genre-defining masterpiece that shows how dogmatic self-righteousness can be little more than a self-destructive trap.

What better recommendation for people who loved Ken Russell's The Devils than the rest of Russell's impeccable filmography? As it happens, after making one of the best horror movies of the 1970s, he made one of the best hard sci-fi movies of the 1980s: Altered States, based on Paddy Chayefsky's 1978 novel. In it, a Harvard psychophysiologist uses sensory deprivation tanks and powerful tribal hallucinogens to explore alternate states of consciousness, unwittingly triggering a dangerous devolution into a primitive ape-man.

Brilliantly experimental and surrealistic from beginning to end, Altered States is anchored by unparalleled visuals and sound design, creating a true aesthetic supernova further bolstered by John Corigliano's avant-garde score. Like all auteurs worth their salt, Russell had a collection of themes that were most interesting to him over the course of his career, and Altered States thus explores many ideas similar to those found in The Devils. Instead of 17th-century religion and politics, this film explores 20th-century scientific hubris, exploring ideas of repressed biological impulses through intentionally grotesque images.

Chilean-French avant-garde auteur Alejandro Jodorowsky is one of the biggest and most influential names in the history of surrealist cinema, and there are many who would point to The Holy Mountain as his magnum opus. After the underground success of the acid Western El Topo, Beatles manager Allen Klein agreed to produce Holy Mountain, and John Lennon and Yoko Ono also contributed financially. The result is one of the most delectably strange and fascinatingly impenetrable Mexican movies of all time, about seven people led up a sacred mountain by a powerful alchemist so they can steal the secret of immortality.

The Holy Mountain is certainly not for those who prefer their fantasy movies straightforward, and it's probably not the most accessible introduction to cinematic surrealism for those unfamiliar; but it's a must-see masterpiece nonetheless, especially for those who love the provocative blasphemy and hyper-stylized colors of The Devils. One of the best international masterpieces of all time, The Holy Mountain is just as fiercely anti-authoritarian as Russell's masterpiece, rejecting institutional corruption and repressive state control through a bunch of deliberately shocking moments.

There is perhaps no better Ken Russell film for people to watch after seeing (and loving) The Devils than Women in Love, which is also anchored by a powerhouse performance by Oliver Reed. This is not a horror movie, though it is a period piece, this one a romantic drama following the relationships between two sisters and two men in a mining town in post-World War I England. Based on the 1920 novel of the same title by D. H. Lawrence, the film obtained an impressive four Academy Award nominations, including Glenda Jackson's victory in the Best Actress category.

This was the movie that established Russell as a major force in world cinema, and it was its huge commercial and critical success that gave Warner the confidence to give Russell a blank check to make The Devils. These two films couldn't possibly be more different in terms of tone, with Women in Love being an uncompromisingly dense and philosophically profound adaptation of the source material. However, since Women in Love directly preceded The Devils, you can see many of the blueprints for Russell's next film there: eroticism, theatricality, and characters trapped between society's expectations and their own physical and psychological impulses.

Directed by Pier Paolo Pasolini and loosely adapted from the 1785 novel The 120 Days of Sodom by the Marquis de Sade, Salò, or the 120 Days of Sodom is perhaps one of the most controversial major films in the history of cinema. Taking the basis of the Marquis de Sade's story from 18th-century France to World War II Italy, it's about four wealthy fascists who kidnap a group of teenagers and subject them to 120 days of systemic mental, physical, and sexual torture inside a secluded villa. For its unapologetic approach to topics that continue to be taboo today, the film has been widely banned and censored across the world throughout history.

It isn't the least bit hard to see why. Much like The Devils, this is one of the heaviest movies of the 20th century, an absolutely unrelenting political allegory of fascism's cold cruelty shot with high-art anti-sensationalism that nonetheless doesn't make the film any easier to stomach. It's a remarkable feat that Pasolini was able to take such an inflammatory premise and manage to treat it with such care that it doesn't feel exploitative at all, a compliment that can also be directed at The Devils.

There are only two major feature films directly depicting the Loudun possessions, and the first one came out a decade before The Devils. It is, of course, the Polish horror art film Mother Joan of the Angels, directed by Jerzy Kawalerowicz and based on a novella of the same title by Jarosław Iwaszkiewicz. Winner of the Special Jury Prize at the 1961 Cannes Film Festival, Mother Joan of the Angels is one of those near-perfect horror movies that nobody remembers anymore, but which should be essential viewing for anyone even slightly interested in the story and context of the Loudun possessions.

Most would agree that the film isn't quite as masterful, timeless, or hard-hitting as The Devils, but it's still very much worthy of praise. With some incredibly striking black-and-white cinematography that stands in stark contrast to the vibrancy of Russell's masterpiece, Lucyna Winnicka's transcendental performance as Mother Joan, and a tremendous level of intellectual and existential depth, Mother Joan of the Angels is to the '60s what The Devils is to the '70s. Both are among the greatest "demonic possession" movies in the history of horror cinema.

I didn’t just start thinking about how to end sexual violence when I sat down to write this. I’ve been thinking about it for most of my life, certainly since adolescence, when people began confiding in me about the assaults that devastated them. One friend described what happened to them as a slow murder of their soul.

I think about everyone who stayed silent out of shame or fear. I think about those who did speak out and were blamed or not believed by their communities, their families, and their friends, the very people who were supposed to support and advocate for them. Many described this secondary assault as more painful than the first.

So when I read about the alleged gang rape at Cornell, I felt deep sorrow for Jane Doe and shock at the response of the men and women who spoke out against her. We must not forget that sexual assault is never the victim’s fault. Responsibility always belongs to the person who assaults them. It does not matter what she wore, what her relationship was with the person or people who assaulted her, or whether she said yes and then changed her mind. And if she was under the influence of substances, even by her own choice, that does not lessen anyone else’s responsibility. I am speaking as a clinician and as a human, not as a lawyer.

So how do we teach our children, especially our sons, not to sexually assault another person?

That question keeps haunting me. I don’t know the men involved in the Cornell case, or the other countless men who have committed sexual assault. I do know that what was taught, or not taught, to them before the assaults contributed to their behavior — for instance, when they ignored a person telling them “No,” either verbally or by their body language; when belonging to a group mattered more than doing what was right; when they disregarded a person’s boundary; when they did not stop when a person was impaired; or when they did not protect a person in danger, and all of this was seen as acceptable.

I couldn’t help but look back at my own children’s childhoods. Some of my proudest moments as a mother happened at parent-teacher conferences when I learned that my son had noticed a classmate sitting alone and invited him to join his lunch table. Another time, I learned that my daughter had stood up for a child who was being bullied, knowing it might cost her friendships.

We begin shaping our children the moment we hold them as infants and respond to their needs with love and compassion. Babies learn empathy by receiving it when we respond to their cries and name their feelings. They learn that their feelings matter, as do the feelings of others. How we treat our children and others becomes how they treat other people as well.

We teach toddlers about boundaries by teaching them that their bodies belong to them. That means not forcing them to hug relatives, and making sure that when a sibling says “stop,” they stop. And we teach them that behavior has consequences: When you hurt someone, you take responsibility for what you did and do what you can to make the situation better.

Children learn respect, compassion, and right from wrong through what their parents (or primary caregivers) say, how they behave, and what they overhear. If they hear the adults around them say a girl “asked for it,” that is where victim-blaming is learned. Instead, we can model kindness, empathy, respect for boundaries, respect for others, self-respect, respect for rules, and the critical thinking it takes to resist groupthink.

We can’t control every influence. Children learn from everything around them: school, friends, their friends’ parents, television, and social media. That is why parents’ influence is so important. Their words and actions matter. Parenting happens through both messaging and modeling. Children notice more than most parents realize: the subtle cues and the conversations they assume their children weren’t paying attention to. And when a parent does realize that they sent the wrong message through words or behavior, they can tell their children they made a mistake and explain what they want them to learn instead.

Through our own actions, we show children how to respect other people’s boundaries and needs. We teach them to read one another’s facial expressions and body language. We teach them that if someone has been drinking or using other substances and is not able to make decisions for themselves, they cannot take advantage of that person. We teach them that wrongdoing has consequences. And we teach them that if they are part of a group that is doing things they are uncomfortable with, or that they know are wrong, they need to separate from the group and even get help if needed. Being different from a group is hard, but doing what’s right matters more.

Teaching empathy, compassion, boundaries, respect, courage, ethics, consequences, and critical thinking is no guarantee against abuse, but it’s certainly a great start. And I hope that raising children with empathy means raising adults who believe survivors and provide support.

A great old-timey game-programming hack (2013)

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A long time ago, when I was a college undergrad, I spent some time working on computer video games. This was in the 8-bit PC era, when the gaming hardware was almost impossibly slow by today’s standards.

It might not surprise you, then, to learn that game programmers of yore did all sorts of crazy things to make their games run at playable speeds. Crazy, crazy things.

This is a story about one of those things.

While I’ve done my best to recall the important details, I may have gotten some things wrong. If I have, please forgive me. It was a long time ago.

A friend of mine, a gifted programmer, was nearly finished with a new game. Somehow, he had squeezed into a 1980’s-era PC, pretty faithfully, what was at the time a graphically impressive coin-op game popular at the arcades.

The only problem was that his version of the game was unplayable. It was too slow, and the choppy motion broke the player’s immersion. It was after all a side-scroller.

My friend, who had been working on the game while also taking a full load of college courses, was starting to seem a little frazzled. Fearing that he had missed some easy optimization, he asked me to take a look at the code.

I did. But there was no easy optimization to be had.

The code was tight. Everywhere I looked, he had already done all the things I could think of. Loops had been unrolled. Unneeded draws had been eliminated. All evidence of waste had vanished.

And, with them, our hopes of an easy fix.

Before I get to the crazy stuff, however, I should back up and explain how graphics hardware worked back then.

Here’s the quick version: The graphics hardware didn’t do jack squat.

On the PC in question, if you wanted to draw something onto the screen, you had to do that yourself, byte by byte. No texture mappers, no blitters. Just bytes. Bytes that you had to move yourself.

My friend’s game spent most of its time redrawing the background. (Again: side-scroller.) For every frame, it had to draw nearly a screen’s worth of background tiles, shifted for the player’s position.

If my memory serves me, each tile was 28 by 28 pixels. Each pixel was one of 16 colors, taking half a byte to represent. Therefore, tiles were represented in memory as 28 contiguous rows of 14 bytes each. The first 14 bytes represented the first row, the second 14 bytes the second row, and so on.

The screen, however, was 320 pixels wide by 240 pixels high. In memory, then, a screen buffer was laid out as 240 contiguous rows of 160 bytes each.

So when copying a tile at address X onto a screen buffer location starting at address Y, you had to copy 28 rows. To copy each row, you had to copy 14 bytes. Fortunately, the processor for this game was a 6809, which had a few 16-bit index registers and delightful “auto-increment” addressing modes (sort of like applying the postfix ++ operator to pointers in C). That meant you could copy 4 pixels at a time while advancing both the X and Y registers in passing:

That’s one. To copy a full tile, you need to do the same thing 28 times. So, in turn, you sandwich that code within a 28-count loop.

But this isn’t your first time at the keyboard. You know what to do. Unroll that loop!

So when my friend showed me his code and asked if I could make it faster, I really wanted to help him. I really wanted to be able to answer yes.

But I had to answer no. I hated giving that answer. But, studying the code, I couldn’t see any way to speed it up.

Later, I couldn’t get the problem out of my head. Maybe I had missed something. I had grown up on Apple II computers and their 6502 processors. My friend’s code, however, was for the 6809. Maybe it afforded optimizations that I didn’t know about.

With renewing optimism, I dialed into the university’s library to access the card catalog. (These were the days before the World Wide Web.) Through a VT220 terminal emulator, I searched for books about the 6809.

There was one. A 6809 microprocessor manual. It was in the engineering library. Luckily, I was an engineering student and had sign-out privileges.

Just like that, I was off to the library.

When I got to the engineering library, I found the book right where it was supposed to be, looking a little beat up:

The MC6809-MC6809E Microprocessor Programming Manual.

Not bothering to find a chair, I flipped through the pages standing, looking for some oddball, 6809-specific instruction that might throw a lot of bytes and throw them fast. Page after page, though, turned up nothing.

Then I came upon PSHS. “Push registers on the hardware stack.”

On my beloved 6502, if you wanted to save registers on the stack, you had to do it one register at a time and, even then, by transferring them through the accumulator. It was slow and expensive. So I had learned to avoid the stack when speed mattered.

But on the 6809 you could save all of the registers – or any subset of them – with a single instruction. And, amazingly, it cost a mere 5 cycles, plus 1 cycle more per byte pushed.

Since the processor had three 16-bit general-purpose registers – D, X, and Y – I could load them up and then use a single PSHS instruction to write 6 bytes in just 11 cycles. The corresponding pull instruction, PULS, had the same low cost.

Further, the 6809 had two stack registers, S and U. I could use one as a source pointer and the other as a destination pointer. In theory, with a single PULS/PSHU pair, I could copy 6 bytes in 22 cycles.

Excitement mounting, I headed for the front desk and reached for my student ID. I was going to sign out that wonderful little book for further study.

Walking back to the dorms, I formed my plan.

I would save the S and U registers somewhere, and then point S at the background tile and U at the screen buffer. Then I would pull from S and push to U, copying 6 bytes at a time, using D, X, and Y as go-betweens. To copy the 14 bytes that make up a row would require three such iterations, which unrolled would be about 60 cycles.

When I reached my room, I found a piece of paper and sketched it out:

For comparison, the naive row-copy loop that we looked at earlier took 157 cycles to do the same thing. And my friend’s optimized code took 98. Already, this crazy idea was looking like a big win.

Still, that last PULS/PSHU pair! What a clunker. It was needed to handle the final two bytes per row, since the rows were 28 pixels = 14 bytes wide, and 6 doesn’t divide 14 evenly.

If only the game had used 24-by-24 tiles instead… But it hadn’t, so I pored through the manual, looking for a way to lower the cost of that inevitable last pair.

And then, by surprise, I struck gold! It was another 6809 oddity, the DP register.

On the 6502 and most 8-bit processors of the era, the lowest 256 bytes of memory was called the zero page. The zero page was special because its memory locations had single-byte addresses and could be accessed with shorter and usually faster instructions.

The 6809’s designers took this idea one step further. They let you use the DP register to designate any page as the zero page, which they called the “direct page.”

But none of my byte-slinging instructions needed to use the direct page. That meant I could use the DP register as an additional one-byte go-between. Now I could copy 7 bytes per pull-push pair!

With this change, I could copy a whole 28-pixel row and advance to the next in just 5 instructions:

That bit of code made me feel just awesome. I had managed to harness every single available register on the machine for byte-slinging! D, X, Y, U, S, and even the oddball DP – they were all fully engaged.

If you’re acquainted with stacks, you may have noticed a subtle flaw in my brilliant plan:

Pushes and pulls work in opposite directions.

See, I lied about that little block of code I just showed you. It didn’t actually copy one row from a background tile onto the screen. What it actually did was break the row into two 7-byte blocks – I’ll call them “septets” – and then draw them onto the screen swapped.

Recall that tile rows were laid out sensibly in memory as 14 contiguous bytes, like this:

Further, if you ran the row-copy code multiple times, the vertical stack of rows you copied would end up being upside down. That’s because the code uses pushes to write the rows onto the screen. Pushes move the stack pointer toward lower addresses, and lower addresses correspond to screen lines higher up on the display.

But this problem, too, had an elegant solution!

The breakthrough was to see “upside down” and “swapped” as two instances of the same thing. Reversal.

Reversal has this handy property: it’s an involution. Reverse something twice, and you get back the original. With this in mind, I reasoned that if the tiles were pre-processed to reverse their rows and then the septets within the rows, the tiles would end up looking fine when they hit the screen. The mangling that occurred during copying and pre-processing would, in effect, undo each other.

Working it out on paper, I also discovered that the pre-processing step could ignore the rows and just treat the tiles as one long, linear sequence of septets.1 To see why this is so, let’s consider a small tile of 2 rows of 2 septets each:

Knowing that the mangling problem could be solved, I was feeling good about my overall idea. I couldn’t see any other problems, so I sketched out a new copy-tile subroutine to present to my friend. Since the row-copy logic was now just 5 instructions, I used it 4 times, unrolling the sole remaining loop a bit. Now, instead of copying 28 single rows, I copied 7 quad-rows.

Adding up the cycles, the total was just 1689. I had shaved almost 1200 cycles off of my friend’s code. That’s a 70 percent speed-up!

When I caught up with my friend and said I’d figured out how to make the copy-tile routine 70% faster, his face lit up. When I explained about the whole septets-and-mangling thing, though, his face unlit, doubts rising. But when I showed him the code, he got it. It clicked.

In about a half hour’s work, he had the code integrated into the game. After a rebuild and reboot, the game was loading.

The game seemed amazingly fast. In truth, it was probably only a third to a half faster, but that was enough. Some important perceptual threshold had been crossed. The game-play was now smooth, natural. You could feel the difference.

We just sat there, playing the game and grinning. It was a good day.

A few days after the speed problem was behind us – or so we thought – the game’s finishing touches were applied. One of those finishing touches was sampled sound effects. This required feeding byte-sized morsels to the audio-output DAC a few thousand times each second. To schedule the feedings, my friend had turned on a hardware-clocked interrupt.

Worked like a charm. The sounds sounded great, and everything was perfect.

After playing into the game one night, we noticed that some of the tiles were getting corrupted. The more we played, the more corruption we saw.

What happens if an interrupt goes off during the copy-tile routine?

The processor, to prepare for calling the interrupt handler, would push its current state onto the system stack. But, during the copy-tile routine, there was no system stack. The system-stack register S had been commandeered. And where was it pointing? Right into the memory buffer that held the reference tiles!

We slumped. After all that effort, to think that our all-important speed-up was causing memory corruption…

Needing a break, we walked to an all-night diner near campus to eat and think. Over pancakes and bacon, scrapple and grilled stickies, we talked through the problem.

There were only two stack registers, and without commandeering both of them, the copy-tile routine wouldn’t be nearly as fast. There was no way, then, to return the S register to the system without losing our hard-won speed. But there was also no way that we could get reliable sound without using interrupts.

One way or another, then, interrupts were going to be triggered. And, when they were, if copy-tile was running, whatever S was pointing to was going to get hammered.

“How can we prevent the corruption?” I asked.

We sat there, ignoring our food, the question hanging in the air.

Suddenly, my friend slapped the table. He had it.

“Don’t prevent the corruption,” he explained. “Let it happen. Just not to the reference tiles.”

“Just swap S and U in the copy-tile routine. U will point at the reference tiles, and S at the screen buffer. If an interrupt goes off, the corruption will happen where S is pointing – on the screen. The corruption will last only until we redraw the next frame.”

Eager to try his solution, we quickly finished our meal.

Walking back to the dorms, though, it bothered us that players might see screen glitches, even if for just a frame. We both felt that there had to be some way to make the hack perfect, if only we could find it.

Industrial automation startup Path Robotics raises $300M in funding

Path Robotics Inc., a startup working to automate industrial welding workflows, today announced that it has raised $300 million in funding.

Georgian led the Series E round. It was joined by a consortium that included the venture capital arms of Yamaha Motor Co. and Navier Corp., one of South Korea’s largest tech firms. The investment gives Path Robotics a post-money valuation of $2.45 billion.

Welding tasks were historically difficult to automate because of a phenomenon called heat-induced metal distortion. It occurs when the heat produced by a welding torch warps the metal parts being joined together. Furthermore, the parts change shape again when they cool down. Those distortions are often unpredictable, which means they can throw off a robot arm that follows a static, pre-programmed welding plan.

There are also other sources of complexity. Mass produced metal parts that are based on the same blueprint often differ slightly because of manufacturing inconsistencies. In many cases, those variations make it necessary to adjust the way welding is performed. That isn’t always possible with traditional automation software.

Path Robotics has developed an AI model that addresses those challenges. Obsidian, as it’s called, is specifically designed to power industrial robots equipped with welding torches. The company says that the model can observe how metal parts change shape during the welding process and adjust its workflow accordingly. Furthermore, it automatically accounts for unexpected part variations.

Obsidian monitors the welding workflow using four lasers attached to the torch that it controls. The emitters illuminate the parts being welded together and the reflected light is captured by a pair of cameras. According to Path Robotics, Obsidian collects data not only during the welding process but also before and after each run.

The data powers an AI model that the company refers to as a weld world model. Path Robotics’ researchers use it to generate simulated welding projects. Those simulations, in turn, power Obsidian’s training workflow.

Path Robotics originally developed Obsidian for large robotic workstations called welding cells. In April, it introduced a four-legged robot called Rove that uses the model to automate shipbuilding tasks. The company says its software can also be used to make parts for vehicles, power generators, utility poles and other heavy equipment.

“We’re seeing accelerating demand from manufacturers that need to expand production capacity but can’t get there with traditional automation,” said Path Robotics co-founder and Chief Executive Officer Andy Lonsberry. “This capital allows us to scale our welding business alongside our customers while investing further in the data, compute, and model development needed to take on every task before and after welding.”

The best thing about a great trilogy is that it gets three chances to break a heart and somehow never wastes one. No middle chapter that feels like a detour, no final entry that leaves a viewer wishing the story had stopped earlier. Just three films that keep deepening the world they have made, until even a coal-strewn path or an empty dance floor starts to feel like a real place.

The six below are trilogies to hand over without a word. Some are funny in the way people get when they are afraid to be sincere, while others leave the room completely silent after the credits. Ranging from intimate black-and-white memories to a nine-hour march through war, each entry brings its own distinct weather and rhythm. They earn the rarest praise of all. Finish the last film, and the urge to restart is immediate. Allow me to give you near-perfect trilogies.

The people in Whit Stillman’s Metropolitan, Barcelona, and The Last Days of Disco don’t storm out of a party so much as spend the ride home explaining why they were right to stay. Tom Townsend (Edward Clements) wanders into Manhattan debutante culture, Barcelona sends talkative young Americans abroad, The Last Days of Disco follows Alice (Chloë Sevigny) and Charlotte (Kate Beckinsale) beneath a glittering ceiling, trying to look at ease while the music changes around them.

Researchers Discover New Lace Bug Species on Japan’s Remote Ogasawara Islands

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

DNA uncovered a hidden species among Japan’s lace bugs and hints that a tree introduced by humans could help give rise to another.

Lace bugs living on two groups of Japan’s remote Ogasawara Islands had been classified as a single species, despite differences in their diets and coloring. Comparisons of their DNA have now revealed that they belong to two species, a distinction supported by subtle physical differences.

The archipelago lies roughly 900 to 1,000 kilometers (560 to 620 miles) south of Tokyo and is a UNESCO World Natural Heritage site. Its land snails and plants are celebrated for including species found nowhere else. About 440 insect species are also known to be unique to the islands, but their evolution has received far less attention.

Among those insects is Omoplax, a genus of lace bugs thought to have diversified rapidly across the islands. Jun Souma, of Hirosaki University’s Shirakami Research Center for Environmental Sciences, investigated these insects with Naoyuki Nakahama, of the Graduate School of Agriculture at Osaka Metropolitan University.

“The reason I took a particular interest in Omoplax desecta is that it feeds on both the native plant Rhaphiolepis indica var. tashiroi and the introduced plant Calophyllum inophyllum in the Chichijima island group,” Souma said. “This is quite unusual for lace bugs, which typically exhibit high host specificity. I was also intrigued by the fact that it is distributed across multiple island groups, which is different from other Omoplax species.”

To investigate relationships among the insects, the researchers analyzed 52 specimens using MIG-seq, a method that provides DNA data from across the genome. Populations classified as Omoplax desecta on the Chichijima and Hahajima island groups were genetically distinct. The study is published in the open-access journal ZooKeys.

The genetic evidence helped explain differences Souma had noticed in the Hahajima insects.

“I had wondered why it fed exclusively on Rhaphiolepis indica var. tashiroi and why there were no individuals with dark brown to black pronotum, which are sometimes seen in the Chichijima island group,” Souma explained. The pronotum is the plate just behind an insect’s head. “After conducting population genetic and phylogenetic analyses, I began to suspect that this population might be a distinct species. Upon closely observing their morphological characteristics, I was certain of this.”

Subtle differences in male genitalia confirmed the distinction. The Hahajima population is now described as Omoplax sandoru, named after the local name of its host plant.

For Omoplax desecta in the Chichijima group, the introduced tree offered another clue to how these insects might diversify. The DNA data indicate that its shift to feeding on Calophyllum inophyllum happened once, and recently. Whether that change will eventually produce another species remains a possibility rather than an established outcome.

“I also find it interesting that the introduction of Calophyllum inophyllum by humans may have led to a budding of future speciation in Omoplax desecta,” Souma added.

The researchers also reconstructed an evolutionary tree to trace relationships among the insects. That allowed them to test whether species with similar appearances and food preferences were one another’s closest relatives.

“The most surprising finding of my research was that species feeding on the same plant and appearing very similar are not necessarily sister groups,” said Souma, referring to groups that are each other’s closest evolutionary relatives. “Adaptive radiation among insects on oceanic islands may be more complex than comparisons of morphological characteristics and life histories would suggest at first glance.”

Adaptive radiation is the diversification of related organisms into species adapted to different ecological roles. Here, the findings point to both geographic isolation and shifts between host plants as drivers of species formation.

The researchers could not obtain DNA from two rare species, leaving gaps in the evolutionary picture. Further sampling will be needed to trace how these insects reached the islands and diversified.

This '60s Japanese Thriller Is One Of The Best Movies Streaming On HBO Max

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Ben Affleck's new kidnapping thriller "Animals" made a splash on Netflix this year — at least in terms of viewership. Critics dismissed the picture, and justifiably so, given how shaky the "Animals" script is. Yours truly clocked that "Animals" is basically an unauthorized remake of Akira Kurosawa's "High and Low," and I was not the only one.

In both movies, a rich man who has just invested all his money in a venture (a business takeover in "High and Low," a mayoral campaign in "Animals") receives a phone call that his son has been kidnapped. Except, the ransomers mistakenly took one of his much poorer employee's sons — so, does he still bankrupt himself to save a kid that isn't his?

I would highly suggest turning off Netflix and flipping over to HBO Max (where "High and Low" is currently streaming) to watch Akira Kurosawa show you how it's done. Several of the filmmaker's classics are currently streaming on there, including "Seven Samurai," "Rashomon," "Throne of Blood," and "Yojimbo." Like with "High and Low," all of those movies also star Toshiro Mifune, Kurosawa's go-to leading man at his peak. But "High and Low" is my personal favorite of the bunch and Kurosawa's magnum opus.

Kurosawa is distinguished for his samurai movies or Jidaigeki (Japanese historical dramas). He often adapted William Shakespeare's plays, too, moving their stories from Europe to to the east: "Throne of Blood" is literally just "Macbeth" in feudal Japan, and it's one of the best "Macbeth" movies to boot.

"High and Low" stands out in that collection for its contemporary setting and source material. (It's adapted from a procedural novel, "King's Ransom," by American author Ed McBain.) While he may not have had the Bard to draw on this time, Kurosawa still made some pure cinematic brilliance.

If I were ever tasked with assembling a list of "old" and/or international movies to convince people that black and white or non-English-language movies can be great, "High and Low" would be on there, 100%. That's not just because it's great (it is), but it's great popular entertainment: a taut suspense thriller and investigative procedural. It's not at all "pretentious," as untrained viewers might call it.

"Someone wants a ransom for the wrong kid, but only you can pay it" is also a great moral dilemma for a thriller and one that slots neatly into anywhere in the world with class disparity. That's why "High and Low" has been remade in many different settings. We know what a hero would do in this situation, but we're not sure if Kingo Gondo (Toshiro Mifune) is a hero from the beginning.

Now, the movie wisely stacks the deck in Gondo's favor so he doesn't come off as an aloof rich guy. He wasn't born rich, he worked his way up, so he knows what losing everything is like, and that makes the sacrifice extra painful. And while he works in shoe manufacturing, he's got a creative spirit. He's planning a leveraged buyout because he wants to make high-quality shoes his way instead of saving money on a cheaper product.

Would you sacrifice all your savings and life's work to save an innocent child? You probably just answered "yes," but it's still an abstract question for you. "High and Low" portrays how someone would act when the choice and its consequences become real. The most impressive balancing act in the movie is that it both does and doesn't feel like a foregone conclusion when he agrees to pay the ransom.

"High and Low" doesn't end when the ransom gets paid. The kidnapper escapes, setting off the second half, where Gondo is largely absent and the police characters take over to try and find the kidnapper. "High and Low" is as bifurcated as its title, with the two halves divided by setting: Gondo's hilltop mansion in the first half, the town below in the second, with the train sequence where Gondo delivers the ransom as a fitting bridge. (It's worth noting that a more literal translation of the original Japanese title, "Tengoku to Jigoku," is "Heaven and Hell.")

During that first half, Kurosawa uses Gondo's living room like a theater stage. Even confined to a single house, the picture looks huge. The screen is wide enough that you can keep track of each character's movements, and the film blocks no frame carelessly. The contained setting, and immense pressure placed on Gondo, still give the first half a claustrophobic feeling. The second half entering the town below is a pressure release, but your attention doesn't go slack because there's still a mystery to solve. The ending of "High and Low," where Gondo and the kidnapper finally come face-to-face, is a triumphant denouement.

Many great American filmmakers have looked to Akira Kurosawa, like "The Godfather" director Francis Ford Coppola. Watching "High and Low," it's easy to understand why.

Scientists discover a giant 66-pound rodent that lived 34 million years ago

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

An ancient rodent of extraordinary size has been identified from fossils uncovered in a mine in southern Thailand. The discovery, made by an international team of paleontologists that includes scientists from McGill University, provides new evidence that giant rodents appeared much earlier and in more parts of the world than previously believed.

The researchers found "remarkably large" fossilized teeth belonging to a previously unknown species called Yakamys sirindhornae. This prehistoric rodent lived approximately 34 million years ago, during the middle of the Paleogene period. Its remains, consisting of a lower second molar and a lower incisor, were recovered from the Bang Mark Pit at the Krabi lignite mine in Thailand's Krabi Province.

Although only two teeth have been identified, they offer valuable clues about rodent gigantism, the evolutionary process through which rodents develop unusually large bodies. The discovery suggests that Southeast Asia played a more significant role in the early evolution of giant rodents than scientists had recognized.

Giant Rodents Have a Much Older History Than Expected

"Today, the largest living rodent is the capybara, but very large rodents are not restricted to recent ecosystems," said Virginie Millien, a biology professor at McGill University and co-author of the study. "Giant rodents evolved repeatedly during the later history of the group, mostly in South America. This discovery suggests that Southeast Asia also played an important role in the early evolutionary history of large-bodied rodents."

The newly identified animal's name reflects both its impressive size and its connection to Thailand. Yakamys combines "yak," the Thai word for giant, with "mys," the Greek word for mouse or rat.

The second part of its scientific name recognizes an important supporter of scientific research in Thailand.

"The species name, sirindhornae, honors Princess Maha Chakri Sirindhorn, in recognition of her longstanding support for science and paleontological research in Thailand," Millien said.

How Big Was This 34-Million-Year-Old Rodent?

To estimate the animal's size, the research team applied seven established methods that use tooth measurements to calculate body mass. Their results suggest that Yakamys sirindhornae weighed approximately 30 kilograms, making it about twice as heavy as other known rodents from the same period, including Hulgana.

That would have made the newly discovered species exceptionally large compared with its contemporaries. However, the researchers emphasize that determining an extinct animal's weight from a small number of fossilized teeth involves considerable uncertainty.

"This is only a ballpark figure, as there is substantial uncertainty associated with fossil body-mass estimation," Millien said.

The limited fossil evidence also makes it difficult to reconstruct how Yakamys evolved or what its life was like. Additional discoveries will be needed to better understand its physical characteristics, ecological role, and evolutionary relationships.

International Research Uncovers Thailand's Prehistoric Past

The fossils were identified through an ongoing international collaboration investigating ancient mammals in Thailand's Krabi Basin. The project brings together scientists from McGill University, Thailand's Department of Mineral Resources, and French research organizations, including CNRS and Université de Poitiers.

By revealing evidence of an unusually large rodent in Southeast Asia 34 million years ago, the discovery expands scientists' understanding of where and when rodent gigantism emerged. Future fossil finds could help establish how these ancient giants lived and how their evolution relates to the enormous rodents that appeared elsewhere in the world.

The research received support from CNRS, Université de Poitiers, Université de Montpellier, McGill University, and the Department of Mineral Resources of Thailand.
