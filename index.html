In our previous posts - Easy FunctionGemma finetuning with Tunix on Google TPUs and Autonomous LLM post-training with Tunix on TPUs - we showed how Google Tunix streamlines Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and single-turn Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) on Cloud TPUs. Real-world AI agents, however, rarely finish their job in a single turn. They have to reason over partial observations, invoke tools, observe environment feedback, and adapt their strategy across multiple steps. So in this post, we will walk through how to train a multi-turn, tool-using LLM agent using Tunix's agentic reinforcement learning pipeline on Cloud TPU v6e-8. You can find the complete code in this GitHub repository.

We will revisit the Plane Strike board game from an earlier TensorFlow/JAX reinforcement learning blog, replacing the custom MLP policy with Gemma 4 E2B in thinking mode on a single Cloud TPU v6e-8 host. Plane Strike is played on a 6x6 grid (A1 to F6) where one 8-cell plane is hidden in one of 4 orientations. Each turn, the agent sees the result of its previous strike and the raw visible board (. untried, X hit, O miss), thinks through candidate placements, and calls the strike(cell="...") tool to strike one cell. The game ends when all 8 plane cells are hit, or after 23 strikes.

One Ansible task, one Azure task. Install and start a service on three servers, then put MySQL on a VM and prove a PHP page can reach it. The tasks come from the KodeKloud Engineer platform.

The task: a playbook that installs vsftpd on all three app servers and starts it.

enabled: yes alongside state: started is two separate claims, and the verification shows both:

enabled in the first line means it comes back after a reboot. active (running) in the second means it is up now. Day 23 made the same point about systemctl enable --now.

ansible.builtin.yum's documentation page now says one thing: "This is a redirect to the ansible.builtin.dnf module."

So what executes is dnf. The task name says yum, the playbook says yum, and the module doing the work is the dnf module. Which is correct for these servers, because Day 71 established the app servers are CentOS Stream 9, where dnf is the package manager and yum is a compatibility name.

Three things follow, and they pull in different directions.

The playbook is right as written. The redirect handles it, and nothing needs changing.

It is misleading to read. Anyone debugging this is reading dnf's documentation whether they know it or not, and the two modules' parameters have not always matched.

And it is not portable. Both yum and dnf commit the playbook to the RHEL family. ansible.builtin.package dispatches to whatever the target actually uses, so the same playbook runs on Debian hosts too.

For a lab that names yum, yum is the right answer. For anything meant to outlive one distribution, package is the module that does not need rewriting later.

One other thing worth recording, and it is mildly annoying. The inventory for this task has ansible_ssh_pass back in it, for all three hosts, in plain text. Day 82's whole task was removing exactly those, and Ansible's documentation says of ansible_password, "never store this variable in plain text; always use a vault."

Each lab starts fresh, so the plain-text passwords come back with it. The fix has to be redone every time, which is a reasonable description of security work generally.

Service Worker Keeps Stopping? That's the Contract, Not a Bug

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The message in the console is not an error. Service worker registration successful followed by silence is the designed behavior of Manifest V3, and the sooner that stops feeling like a crash, the sooner the bugs get easier.

These are the questions I answer most often, in the order they usually arrive.

Chrome terminates an idle background service worker after roughly 30 seconds without extension events. A single event may run for up to five minutes, but a pending setTimeout does not count as activity. The worker is a request handler, not a daemon.

Usually not. Open chrome://serviceworker-internals, find your extension, and read the lifecycle entries. A normal stop says the worker became idle and was terminated; a crash includes an error and a stack. If you see no exception, you are looking at the lifecycle doing its job.

Each worker incarnation starts with a fresh console. If you only log to console.log, the previous incarnation's output is gone when the new one starts.

Now the popup, or a debug page, can show how many times the worker restarted and when. That single field explains most "it randomly resets" reports.

The popup is a separate context. While it is open, messages and port connections can keep the worker alive, but closing the popup ends that. A long-running job that only worked while the popup was open was never a background job.

There is no supported way. Keepalive loops — a message every 20 seconds, an offscreen document created for no reason, a silent audio element — are detectable, burn battery, and are exactly what the platform was designed to prevent. They postpone termination; they do not survive a browser restart or a suspension.

Events that wake the worker include runtime messages, action clicks, alarms, web navigation, storage changes from other contexts and install/startup events. A timer callback is not an event; it is a callback in a heap that no longer exists. Alarms are the bridge between "later" and "an event".

Use a boot id and a sequence number per job. If the job record shows attempts: 2 and the boot id changed between them, you have proof of a restart. Without that, you are guessing from timestamps.

Deadlines, job identity, status and attempt counts. Not remaining seconds, not interval handles, not "isRunning" booleans without a lock timeout. The full model is a small record, and it is cheaper than every workaround combined.

Alarms are approximate and have a 30-second floor, so a feature that needs exact sub-minute scheduling without an open UI has no good answer in MV3. That is a real product constraint, not a misunderstanding.

vite-mv3-sw-compile-persist softens the migration: long timers become alarm-backed wrappers and marked state is restored before handlers run. It does not make the worker persistent, does not support destructured state, and cannot recover anonymous callbacks that captured locals. Anyone promising otherwise is selling a keepalive hack with better branding.

Keep the record small; a large blob read on every wake costs more than the bug it prevents.

Your "49 KB" file is actually 50.5 KB. Blame the kilobyte.

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A user picks a file. Their computer says 49 KB. Your form says "max 50 KB." The upload gets rejected anyway, and now there's a support ticket that says your site is broken.

It isn't broken, exactly. You and the user just disagree about what a kilobyte is. And honestly? Both of you are right.

There are two definitions in everyday use:

The IEC prefixes (KiB, MiB, GiB) exist precisely to separate the two. Almost nobody uses them in a UI, though. So "KB" on a screen could mean either one, and you usually can't tell which by looking.

Windows Explorer uses 1,024 and calls it KB. Raymond Chen explained why on Microsoft's own blog years ago: everybody else said KB for 1,024 too, so Explorer followed existing practice. That's still the behaviour.

macOS went decimal. Apple's support page on storage capacity says OS X Leopard and earlier reported sizes in binary, which means everything from Snow Leopard (10.6) on counts 1 KB as 1,000 bytes. Same file, different number than on Windows.

Linux / GNU coreutils gives you both. ls -lh, du -h and df -h use powers of 1,024. Add --si and you get powers of 1,000. Here's a 51,000-byte file with coreutils 9.7:

Notice the capital K versus lowercase k. That's the only hint you get. Also notice -h printed 50K for something that's 49.8 KiB, so it rounded up. Rounding is its own little trap on top of everything else.

On a machine doing 1,024 math, that looks like a "49 KB" file. If your server rejects anything over 50,000 bytes, it fails. The user isn't lying and your code isn't buggy. You're just using different rulers.

The 51,000-byte file from above works the same way: 51.0 KB in decimal, 49.8 KiB in binary.

HTML can't enforce this for you. supports accept, capture and multiple, and that's it. There's no size attribute. Any size limit lives in JavaScript or on the server, and the server is the one that counts.

PHP: upload_max_filesize and post_max_size accept shorthand. The PHP manual says plainly that 1K = 1,024 bytes and 1M = 1,048,576 bytes, and even adds a note that the IEC would call this a kibibyte. So upload_max_filesize = 50K means 51,200 bytes.

nginx: client_max_body_size 50k also means 51,200 bytes. The docs list k/K as kilobytes without saying how many bytes that is, but the parser in nginx's source (ngx_parse_size) multiplies by 1,024. Go over and you get a 413.

So here's the fun bit. A PHP or nginx config that says "50K" allows 51,200 bytes, while a hand-written check like if (size > 50000) allows 50,000. Two different limits, both called "50 KB." I'd bet plenty of apps have exactly this mismatch between their help text and their config.

One more wrinkle. nginx's client_max_body_size applies to the whole request body, not the file. PHP's post_max_size covers all the POST data too. A multipart/form-data body wraps each file in boundary lines plus Content-Disposition and Content-Type headers (RFC 7578 describes the format).

How much does that add? Sending that 51,000-byte file with curl -F produced a 51,199-byte body. About 200 bytes of overhead, which grows with longer field names, filenames and extra fields. Against a 51,200-byte nginx limit, that upload squeaks through by one byte. A file a couple hundred bytes bigger, still "under 50K" by any reading, gets a 413.

That check is for the user's benefit only. Keep the server check, since anyone can skip your JavaScript.

The main thing is to stop writing "KB" alone anywhere a limit gets enforced. Bytes are boring. Boring doesn't generate tickets.

Ravi Agrawal: Let’s start with the U.S. point of view on the Iran war. The consensus has been that Trump has three options. He could escalate militarily, but that has very uncertain outcomes. He could spin a victory and get out, but the spin is . Or he could focus on diplomacy and try to return to the memorandum of understanding (MOU) we had last summer, but that is a lot of hard work. What do you think the White House strategy is now that the midterms are over?

Vali Nasr: I think the president is actually pursuing all three strategies at the same time. Leaning in on diplomacy does not mean that he’s setting aside war plans, and asking for war plans does not mean that he is doing less diplomacy. He doesn’t have any good choices.

Ideally, Trump would end this war through a negotiated settlement, much like what happened in June, but he has to meet the Iranian price for doing that, which is actually quite steep for him. Currently, the positions of the two are too far apart. The Iranians want him to first go back to the MOU and implement it before they talk about anything else, such as the nuclear issue. Trump, on the other hand, needs the nuclear issue to be at the forefront in order to justify why he went to war for nine months.

War is also not really an option, because in order for war to be meaningful, Trump has to do something big. But something big opens the door to Iranian retaliation, particularly in the Persian Gulf, which could devastate the global economy in larger ways. I’m sure Trump is hearing from Chinese President Xi Jinping and Arab, Chinese, and Russian leaders that nobody wants that kind of damage to the global economy. So he’s settling for the third option, which is to let Iran sit under the blockade and do nothing. In my opinion, that’s not a strategy. That’s twiddling his thumbs.

The blockade is not foolproof. Iran is getting oil out and making money. At the same time, the Strait of Hormuz is still under a lot of pressure. The Iranians have actually increased the number of attacks they’re making there. Furthermore, I don’t think it’s sustainable, financially or otherwise, for the U.S. Navy to maintain the blockade long term. It is quite financially and labor intensive for the Navy to do so, and it doesn’t have any bases in the Persian Gulf that could provide relief. If the Iranians are able to sustain themselves with the amount of holes that already exist, then, ultimately, this is not sustainable.

In fact, if the blockade starts working in the way in which the U.S. treasury secretary and U.S. secretary of state say it will, then Iran is likely to escalate. So ultimately, this strategy that everybody thinks is settled on is not tenable in the long term. Either it will collapse under its own weight, or one or the other party is bound to disturb it, which might bring us back to war.

RA: I imagine that you could have said a lot of this two weeks ago. The big change that has come in the last couple of weeks is, of course, the U.S. midterm election results. Come January, the Democrats will have power in both the House of Representatives and the Senate. They feel that part of the mandate from the American people is trying to find a way to end the war. What is your sense of how Congress might impact how Trump tries to prosecute the end of this war, or dilly-dally, as you said?

VN: One outcome of these elections is a much more visible push within the United States against the war. It’s not just that the American people have spoken. If there was any shadow of a doubt that they really, really disliked this war, it has been dashed, particularly within the Republican Party. They might now think that if the Republican Party is to have any chance in 2028, this war should end now.

So there is a sense that there is more momentum for diplomacy after the election than there was before. In fact, when U.S. Vice President J.D. Vance wrote to Iran’s leadership, everybody said, “That’s exactly what we thought the consequence would be of the drubbing the administration got.”

But Trump is unpredictable. He has his own view of what’s in his interest as well as what’s in America’s interest. He may go back to a conflict with Iran, so we’re not out of the woods. But the Iranians know that the economic cost of the war is having a tangible political impact on America, as well as France and Germany, even if it is not being reflected in the stock market on a daily basis. Ultimately, these countries are likely to decide at some point that this war is not worth the political blowback it’s causing.

RA: I was going to come to how Tehran is gaming out the post-midterms scenario. You make the point that Iran may sense that it is getting some of what it wants—it is imposing costs. The flip side of that could be that the costs have been imposed, and Trump has now seen the worst of it. He seems to be doubling down. With nothing to lose, he may now be impervious to electoral pressure, meaning the Iranians may not have much leverage with him.

VN: That’s very true. This is a gamble. It doesn’t mean that because the elections happened, the Iranians can declare victory and say, “OK, we humiliated President Trump, now let’s sit down and talk.” Yes, they had a big win in terms of the midterm results, but now they have to deal with Trump.

I think the way they see it is that the decision to go to war is still uncomfortable for President Trump. It’s true that he’s not looking at another election, but the Republicans are looking at 2028. And ultimately, the longer this war goes on, the more the pressure it brings about, particularly if the next phase of the war is more destructive. The Iranians are broadcasting that loud and clear as a form of deterrence. They’re saying they’re going to wreak havoc on the region and the global economy, no holds barred.

If that’s the case, then President Trump still has to think about his legacy. He has to think about the future of the Republican Party. And he has to consider that if he enters into a wider war with Iran and he still isn’t able to defeat it, the scale of his failure will be even greater than it is now.

Until now, the most important factor on the American side hasn’t been some kind of a calculated, rational, technocratic national interest. It has been the psychology of one man from the get-go of deciding to go to war, to Trump’s various permutations.

Nobody—whether it’s the United States’ allies or Iran or China—can say with any certainty how Trump might actually act. So I think the Iranians are taking this one step at a time, reading the tea leaves and applying pressure where they can with Congress—particularly the new Congress coming in—to see whether they can avoid a big war and get a settlement that would benefit them.

The reasons they point to are that the amount of Gulf crude going through Hormuz has actually dropped significantly, from 83 percent to 60 percent, because Gulf states are using pipelines more and relying less and less on Hormuz. The rial is down to 1.7 million to the dollar. Recent U.S. sanctions have targeted the regime explicitly, as opposed to targeting the Iranian people. They’ve shut down parts of Mahan Air, which the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) relied on, as well as a United Arab Emirates branch of Banque Misr, which served the IRGC.

There’s a sense in the White House—or at least, it’s their narrative—that these measures must really hurt the people in power in Iran, and at some point the pain will be so great that the regime will be forced to make different choices. Do you buy any of that?

VN: No, I don’t. This is largely self-serving, anecdotal rhetoric, which has gotten us to this point. I don’t see a sign that the degree of pressure that this administration thinks it is imposing on Iran is actually being felt there and changing attitudes. When Iran sent its diplomats to the United Nations, the proposal they presented to the United States was an expansion of the Islamabad-brokered MOU, changed in ways that benefited Iran more. The Iranians referred to it as “Islamabad-Plus.”

Let’s also consider the fact that when Mecca defense alliance members and Pakistan’s army chief went to Tehran in October, Iran’s response to Vice President Vance’s letter was that Iran was sticking to its demands that Washington must fulfill all seven preconditions to resuming talks and reopening Hormuz. The United States’ pressure tactics are not working.

This administration doesn’t want to go to war or make a deal with Iran. They need to sell this “do nothing” strategy as a viable, working strategy, so they’re repackaging the blockade and saying it’s going to work this time. Two months ago, U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent was promising that there would be a run on the Iranian banks. That hasn’t happened. So I’m suspicious of these kinds of claims.

Iran is under a tremendous amount of pressure, but I don’t think the administration has a gauge of where the tipping point for them is. So it’s throwing a Hail Mary pass with more sanctions, hoping that they will do the trick, and then we get a lot of rhetoric trying to sell the current holding pattern as an actual strategy.

RA: So in response to everything you’re saying, Tehran’s strategy might be to wait out the next two years.

VN: If they have to, yes. Although, I don’t know. Iran’s supreme leader said in his last major statement that this “no war, no peace” situation is not sustainable or acceptable to Iran.

When and how will Iran disturb this status quo? We don’t know. We can already see that they are escalating pressure on shipping. In the past week, more of the shuttle ships that the UAE has been using to ferry oil from inside tankers in the Persian Gulf to tankers in the Arabian Sea are being hit. The number of mines installed in the Strait of Hormuz has also increased. More oil might go out on some days than others. So the Iranians are making the situation more unstable.

There is a difference between the way the West and Iran look at this situation. The U.S. and many observers think a move from Iran has to be really big to be meaningful. For instance, if they can’t bring the cost of oil to $200 a barrel, then they’ve lost.

But the Iranians actually do not look at things this way. Very early in the war, they understood that the price of oil is not 100 percent in their hands. What they believe works is sustained instability in the energy market. The costs of oil, natural gas, jet fuel, diesel, fertilizer, and so on are far above where they were. None of these measures by themselves will break the global economy, but if you put them together for long enough, they’re very effective.

Iran sees the result of the U.S. elections as proof of this concept. Their strategy is to boil the frog gradually. I think sometimes in the West we look at this and say, “Well, the frog is just sitting there and nothing’s happening to it, so the Iranians have failed.” So I think this is a race between whether Iran runs out of gas first or its strategy works.

Right now, I detect a certain degree of confidence from Iran. I don’t see the trepidation that observers on the outside are assuming the regime has. So either Iran has a good poker face, or it’s seeing the picture very differently than Washington does.

RA: It’s fascinating, and it’s always so hard to know whom or what to believe.

I’m curious what you make of these reports that claim to be from U.S. intelligence suggesting that Iran is developing plans to detonate a dirty bomb in Washington, D.C. These are just reports. Tehran, of course, denies that any of this is true, but I’m curious how you are reading that story and reacting to it.

VN: We’re in the midst of a war, and there’s a lot of disinformation. There are people in the United States or in Israel who want the United States to go back to war with Iran, to “finish the job,” so to speak. After the elections, which made it very obvious that the American public is firmly opposed to this war, you could only change that public opinion in favor of war if there was this kind of threat against America, or if you could posit that Iran is a deranged and crazy regime that must be taken out. So it could possibly be disinformation.

On the other hand, Iranians have also been taking certain actions as a form of deterrence. I don’t know whether Iran actually launched a cyberattack on refrigerators in France—French intelligence claims they did, though the Iranians deny it—but it’s a message. “If you attack us, then we have cyber capabilities that could paralyze your cities.”

It’s a kind of deterrence tactic. It’s a similar case with Iran issuing warnings to air bases in the U.K., which ultimately got the United States to move its airplanes from two different air bases. Some of this benefits Iran, because it’s a way of saying, “Do not assume that the next war would be cost-free. Our reach is much farther than you assume.”

It also suggests to me that Iran doesn’t want to go back to war. Rather, its game plan is to try to get the United States to come to the table to accept their terms.

New Homes Offer More Affordability for Buyers: Report

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

With affordability remaining an issue at large in housing, new homes continue to represent a tempting alternative in a challenging market.

The latest data from the National Association of Home Builders (NAHB) found that affordability for new homes actually improved more than that of existing homes in Q3, focused on both home prices and rates.

The Q3 NAHB/Wells Fargo Cost of Housing Index (CHI) found that families earning the national median income of $106,800 needed 33% of their income to buy a median-priced new home, compared to 36% of their income for a median-priced existing home.

Low-income families (those who earn only 50% of the national median income) need 66% of their income to buy a new home, compared to 73% for an existing home.

NAHB Chairman Bill Owens noted that this was the “widest affordability advantage (for new homes) since the CHI began,” as builders “are working hard to offset rising costs.”

The report also noted that this is due in part to the difference in price between new homes ($400,400 median price) and existing homes ($416,700 median price), called out as the widest national median price gap since the CHI began.

The other attribution for the new home “affordability advantage” is the incentives and deals offered by builders.

Owens called out NAHB’s recent data on builder confidence, which saw 43% of builders offer price cuts in October—the highest share since 2020. Additionally, 65% of builders offered sales incentives.

A recent Realtor.com® report also found that many buyers purchasing new homes are seeing lower mortgage rates due to builders offering buy downs or other deals with mortgage lenders. The average starting mortgage rate of a new-home buyer was 5.01% in Q3, compared with 6.15% for existing-home buyers.

While new homes are offering better affordability than existing homes, overall housing affordability is still very much challenged.

For both new and existing homes, families earning the national median income remain fairly cost-burdened (paying more than 30% of their income for housing) or severely cost-burdened (paying more than 50% of their income for housing).

Of the 175 metro areas tracked in the report, 87 are cost-burdened by housing, and eight are severely cost-burdened. Leading the way among the eight severely cost-burdened markets is San Jose, California, where families use 79% of their income on housing.

Owens outlined that in order to move housing affordability in the right direction, “policymakers must do more to reduce red tape, address labor shortages and fix supply chains so builders can expand the nation’s housing supply.”

“As builders contend with rising construction costs, tighter lending standards and persistent labor shortages, policymakers should remove regulatory obstacles, enact permitting reform and create a stronger business environment that enables builders to produce more attainable, affordable homes,” added NAHB Chief Economist Robert Dietz.

Newborn stars are hiding in the red threads of a neighboring galaxy

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Webb peers into N159, where dusty filaments shelter some of the youngest stars in a neighboring galaxy.

Today’s Image of the Day takes us inside a cloud where stars are being born in a neighboring galaxy.

In this view from the James Webb Space Telescope, pale blue gas fills broad cavities, orange pillars jut from their edges, and dark red threads wind across the middle.

Some of the youngest stars in the scene are still tucked inside those threads.

The cloud, known as N159, lies about 161,000 light-years away in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a satellite galaxy of the Milky Way.

Webb’s image spans about 230 light-years. Across that distance, some stars are still gathering the material they need to grow, while others already shine fiercely enough to change the shape of the cloud around them.

Start with the pale blue areas. They trace hydrogen gas exposed to intense ultraviolet light from massive stars.

That radiation can knock electrons loose from hydrogen atoms. As the electrons rejoin hydrogen nuclei, the gas gives off light of its own.

A bright patch can therefore reveal where massive stars are affecting their surroundings, even if the glow itself comes from the gas.

Those stars also send streams of particles into the cloud. Over time, these stellar winds can help hollow out cavities and sculpt the denser material along their edges.

Look closely at where the blue meets the orange. Small pillars extend into the open spaces, marking places where pockets of dense gas and dust have persisted as material around them has been cleared away.

The bright knot on the left is the Papillon Nebula, an unusually compact pocket of glowing hydrogen that the Hubble Space Telescope first resolved in 1999.

In Webb’s wider view, it takes up only a small part of the frame. Its intense glow draws the eye, but the quieter areas around it hold some of the best clues to where stars are forming now.

One of the best places to zoom in is just above the blue cavity on the right. Thin red strands cross this part of the frame.

They trace molecular hydrogen, a form of the gas in which two hydrogen atoms are bound together.

Deep inside dense strands like these, gas and dust can shield developing stars from the radiation and winds of their more powerful neighbors.

Webb’s Near-Infrared Camera records light at wavelengths beyond what our eyes can see.

Some of that light gets through dust that blocks visible starlight, revealing points of light buried in the cloud.

Image processors assign visible colors to observations made through different filters.

Here, those colors distinguish the red threads of molecular gas from the orange glow of carbon-based molecules and the pale blue of ionized hydrogen.

Astronomer Nolan Habel and colleagues compared the brightness of more than 300,000 points of light across ten infrared filters.

Some gave off more infrared light than expected from a star alone. That extra light can come from surrounding dust, which absorbs energy from the star and radiates it back into space.

Many of the sources the researchers identified as possible young stars lined up with dusty strands and clouds of molecular gas.

In a preprint describing the observations, the team reported that the eastern part of N159 contains more developed groups of stars, while the western part shows signs of more recent star formation.

Some groups may be less than half a million years old. That difference suggests that star formation unfolded in stages across the cloud.

The Large Magellanic Cloud has a smaller proportion of elements heavier than helium than the Milky Way does.

The early universe was also poorer in those elements, which built up over generations of stars.

Studying a region like N159 gives astronomers a chance to see how stars form with a mix of ingredients closer in some respects to what was available long ago.

The image also captures a change that is easy to miss at first glance. In one part of the cloud, stars have already opened clearings around themselves.

In another, dense gas still shelters stars that are just getting started. Given time, much of that gas will disperse, leaving the stars exposed.

For now, the thin red strands mark places where some of the newest stars in this corner of a neighboring galaxy are still taking shape.

The celestial saga that is Frank Herbert’s Dune was practically made for cinema. And yet, for so long, adapting the breathtakingly expansive space opera seemed like something of a curse. David Lynch called his turn at the franchise “a huge, gigantic sadness” in his life, and for Alejandro Jodorowsky, his psychedelically expensive vision for Dune never escaped development hell.

On December 18, 2026, Denis Villeneuve’s Dune: Part Three arrives, overcoming the hurdles that had tripped up its predecessors and capping off the decade-in-the-making trilogy in an explosive finale. Key to the creation of the trilogy’s universe was keeping it as grounded as possible in ours, with otherworldly planets raised from the volcanic shores of Iceland’s beaches and the banded dunes of the United Arab Emirates’ Liwa Desert. By this point, fans of the first two installments are well-acquainted with settings like the desert planet of Arrakis, but Villeneuve's latest offering delves deeper than ever into their intricate textures. Condé Nast Traveler spoke with production designer Patrice Vermette, location manager Duncan Broadfoot, and director of photography Linus Sandgren to learn how this much-anticipated conclusion was crafted.

Dune: Part Three opens with Fremen forces landing on Enfeil, a wetland planet that stuns the soldiers—used to desert and drought—with its rain. After the battle concludes, the Fremen forces run to the black sand shores of the planet and wade into the sea. This sequence was filmed in Iceland, specifically at Ingólfshöfði Beach, a nearly 250-foot-tall cape and nature reserve. “Denis [Villeneuve] always envisioned a black surface going on for forever, a super flat surface with those low clouds and a band of light, just the sun piercing through the horizon," said Vermette, “Because of the black sand, we thought, obviously, Iceland. We also looked to see if it was possible to shoot in the Canary Islands.” At the end of the day, Iceland won the booking, leading the team to spearhead an unprecedented film shoot. “When we scouted Iceland, there were multiple options,” said Broadfoot, “but this particular beach that we ended up on is part of a nature reserve, and actually during the summer, you can book puffin tours, but it's never been shot on before, so that was also an appealing aspect.”

Twenty different locations across the UAE's Liwa Desert were stitched together to comprise Arrakis. Across all three films, the Liwa Desert has been used as a stand-in for the desert planet at the center of it all. “There's a reward to travel to the Liwa Desert, which is close to the border of Saudi Arabia, and the dunes there are like the kind you've seen in Lawrence of Arabia,” said Sandgren, “it's magical just to be there; they're beautiful and contain copper sand and beige sand, and the mix of the two creates these incredible patterns of ripples from the wind each night, and every morning, you see new patterns.” While Sandgren arrived at the desert for the first time to film for Part Three, for Broadfoot and Vermette, returning to the region was like a homecoming for the crew. “Denis [Villenueve] and Patrice [Vermette] are always very keen to find something new that we haven't filmed on before, so that was always the challenge," said Broadfoot, “specifically for Part Three, we had to go deeper and further into the desert than we had done in Part Two.” Filming in the ever-shifting landscape with temperatures peaking at a boiling 120 degrees Fahrenheit posed significant challenges to the shoot, especially as the crew based themselves even deeper into the desert, but getting comfortable was almost counter to the brief. “We love making movies because we thrive on challenges, you know?," said Vermette, "If it was easy, I don't think we'd want to make them.”

While nearly all of the Arrakis scenes were shot in the Liwa Desert, select sequences were set in Al Ain, the UAE's “Garden City.” Though scenes shot in Al Ain didn't take up as much screen time as those shot in the desert, they span a considerable range as the area served as the setting for Alia Atreides's personal Golgotha—the site where Paul's sadistic younger sister crucifies traitors to the regime—as well as where Chani plays with her young children. “It's an incredible area that is still very unknown,” said Vermette. “We shot on a mountain called Jebel Hafeet, the only real mountain in the area,” said Broadfoot, “we scouted the entire mountain and found a plateau up high, which gave you this sort of scale and rocky, craggy environment that's very different than the desert which we were filming in. It was a blank canvas for Patrice [Vermette] to take over and create his beautiful set.”

To enhance all the footage that was shot on location, several landscapes from Part Three were recreated using a combination of practical and digital effects across four facilities in Budapest: NFI Studios, HungExpo Budapest Congress and Exhibition Centre, Korda Studios, and Waberer. Much of the battle scene on Enfeil was shot in Budapest. “Most of it was in the back lot of Waberer,” said Vermette, “the amazingly technical thing achieved by Denis [Villeneuve] and Linus [Sandgren] was that they erected a 65-foot-tall by 470-something-foot-long LED screen wall, which was the biggest wall of that sort ever built." The sheer scale of the shoot even required the crew to break out cranes to navigate the airspace around production to move props and equipment around.

Atreides home planet Caladan has made an appearance in all three films of the trilogy. The visual opposite of Arrakis, Caladan was shot on the northwestern Kråkenes region of Norway. The agrestic shoreline is hugged by verdant cliffs and the frosty Norwegian Sea. Caladan's cameos in Part Three come as brief flashes, but Vermette also winks to the filming location in an Easter egg on Arrakis. “In the movie, in the interior courts of Alia's meditation room, there's a tree,” says Vermette, “I had the idea of putting a pine tree, a Norwegian pine tree there, because it's the roots of their family; it's something that anchors their family. The only pine tree in all of Arrakis is right there.”

For years, I was a girl who would say “yes” when I meant “no.” It was only after I decided to stop drinking that I realized how much my “yes-girl” tendency had fueled my drinking cycle over the years.

Over the years, both through my own recovery and my work in helping others break their drinking patterns, I have come to see that most people who eventually come to question their relationship with alcohol don’t have a “drinking problem;” instead, they have a “drinking solution.” In my case, my inability and unwillingness to say “no” had created a lot of problems that I ended up turning to alcohol to solve.

I would say yes to a football weekend marathon when I didn’t even know what a quarterback was, and then turn to a 6-pack to make time pass faster. I would say “yes” to hosting the relative whom I couldn't stand, then try to push away my resentment with a bottle of wine. I would say “yes” to helping out at a neighbor’s garage sale after a long week and end up trying to recover from exhaustion with a few cocktails.

As I started to help others break their old drinking patterns, I realized I was far from the only person who got caught in this pattern. In fact, I came to nickname this particular drinking pattern “the Yes-Effect Drinking Loop.” It’s one of the 10 most common invisible drinking loops that keep an unwanted drinking pattern alive.

In most unhealthy drinking patterns, drinking is only what we see on the surface. The Invisible Drinking Loop is a term I created to help people understand the hidden forces that keep their drinking patterns alive. An invisible drinking loop is often driven by the need to solve a problem, fueled by the belief that alcohol can help, and maintained by the habit loop in which drinking has become the default routine to cope with or deal with certain situations or emotions.

In the “Yes-Effect Drinking Loop,” drinking has become the solution to deal with the side effects that arise when a person struggles to say no.

As is true with most drinking loops, simply stopping drinking doesn’t break the loop.

With alcohol out of my life, I found myself sitting through events that I had no interest in, battling the desire to reach for a drink to make time pass faster, hanging out with people whose company I didn’t enjoy, missing the quick mental escape alcohol could offer, and dealing with overwhelming cravings by the end of the day after I once again stretched myself too thin in the effort to please others instead of honoring my own limitations.

It took me a long time to realize that simply stopping drinking doesn’t solve the problems; instead, it exposes them. I needed to learn to find another way to address the problem that I once attempted to solve through drinking, and that meant learning how to say no.

Learning to say no after years of saying yes is not easy. When faced with a choice between disappointing others and disappointing myself, my default response was to choose the latter. Somewhere along the line, I had been conditioned to believe that fulfilling others’ expectations, even those of people whom I wasn't even that close to, was a way to keep myself safe, liked, and in good standing.

Yet, I failed to see the hidden cost of all the efforts to keep others happy; I kept myself unhappy. So much so that alcohol became the only way I knew how to push down my disappointment, exhaustion, and resentment.

Learning to say “no” started with a radical decision to honor my own preferences rather than automatically prioritize others’ expectations. Don’t get me wrong, it doesn’t mean becoming self-centered or never making compromises in life. But it does mean taking an intentional pause before giving in to my compulsive need to keep others happy. And when faced with the choice between disappointing others and disappointing myself, I wanted to make that choice consciously, rather than by default.

Of course, giving myself permission to say no was one thing. Actually knowing how to say it was another. After years of automatically saying yes, saying no felt like a skill I had not yet learned.

That's why Skill Expansion is one of the core pillars of my approach to breaking an old drinking pattern. Truly growing beyond alcohol often requires us to develop new skills and tools to navigate the challenges we once relied on alcohol to handle. For the Yes-Effect Drinking Loop, learning to say no is one of those skills.

When British author Ian McEwan, one of my favorite storytellers, thinned out his home library of duplicates and extras to make room for new stock, he took boxes of books into the park next door and gave them away to passersby. To his surprise, the only ones accepting these gifts were women. He came away from this experience with the realization that if females ever give up on reading, this remarkable invention of human technology will surely die with them.

Thankfully, females have not yet turned their back on the printed page, but ominous trends in reading habits for girls and boys suggest that at least the level of enthusiasm for reading among both genders is in decline. And as motivation to read slips, so does reading skill.

Where this relationship is most pronounced, however, is with boys, who have significantly lower levels of reading engagement than their female counterparts and fall well short of girls in reading achievement on global, national, and state-level reading assessments. When it comes to enjoyment of reading, time spent reading for pleasure, and diversity of text read (i.e., newspapers, magazines, fiction, nonfiction) girls in the U.S. and in most countries of the world have significantly and consistently higher levels.

I’ve often discussed in my books on the topic of boys and reading, as well as in speeches and lectures at conferences, that for those of us with sophisticated literacy skills, we hope the same for the guys within our care. This is because we desire boys, and indeed all youth, to experience the joy and benefits of the type of highly developed literacy abilities that we value and that have in very real ways contributed to our personal enrichment and professional accomplishments. However, there’s no shortcut to getting there. We can’t expect boys who rarely read to ever possess the level of literacy we wish for them. Parents who accommodate their sons’ non-reading habits are in some ways enabling stunted literacy growth.

Research tends to do a good job of documenting the decline in reading engagement and achievement without always specifying the causes. Thus, it’s helpful to look at the literature related to motivating students to read and then by inference appreciate the reasons for the lack of motivation. For example, researchers have discovered factors that positively influence motivation to read, such as (a) creating contexts for free self-selected reading, (b) giving students more text choices, and (c) structuring collaboration for reading and learning. Looked at from the opposite perspective, then, one can infer that students are disengaged when time is not set aside for free reading, when students’ choices about what to read are restricted or non-existent, and when students only read in isolation without opportunities to discuss and share texts.

These same lessons from research can help guide parents who are eager to create supportive environments for reading for their sons.

I’m fond of saying at the conclusion of my talks on this subject that “A boy’s love of reading doesn’t begin with scholarship, it begins with discovery." The more we do to help boys discover or rediscover the joy and pleasure of reading, the greater the chances they’ll become engaged and skillful readers for life.

12 Best Acting Performances In Steven Spielberg Movies, Ranked

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Steven Spielberg is one of, if not the most iconic directors working today. The filmmaker’s first project dates all the way back to 1971 with the release of Duel on ABC (a direct to television film), and he has since gone on to helm countless other titles. In total, Spielberg has directed a whopping 36 projects, and is still active to this day, with the release of Disclosure Day coming earlier this year.

Many of the classic films from the last forty years can be attributed to Spielberg. The director helmed 1993s Jurassic Park, for example, and he also did the first four Indiana Jones films, along with Schindler’s List, Saving Private Ryan, Jaws, and many, many more. Impressively, Spielberg has switched genres frequently in his career, never staying in one area for too long (his two most recent projects, for example, are The Fablemans and Disclosure Day, and both are vastly different).

Spielberg isn’t just known for his pure directing talents, however. He’s also made a reputation for himself as one of the best “actor’s directors” working today; that is, having the ability to work with all kinds of unique performers and get the best out of them each and every single time.

There’s plenty of evidence to back up that claim, too. Just as some of the best movies of the last forty years have come from Spielberg, some of the best performances from actors have come in these projects as well. And while the actors naturally deserve great credit for these turns, Spielberg should also get his plaudits for being the mastermind behind these performances.

One of Spielberg’s earlier films was the iconic Jaws. Everyone knows the story of this one — a giant shark circles the waters of a coastal town, with a small group of townsfolk warning others of the dangers, but are up against a mayor who is unwilling to shut down the beaches.

Interestingly, Spielberg reportedly assembled a small group of A-list actors that he wanted to appear in the film. At the same time, the director was also hesitant to choose anyone too well known, as he didn’t want their fame to overshadow the movie. As such, the likes of Robert Redford, Paul Newman, and Steve McQueen, all names Spielberg had in mind, were not in Jaws.

One name that was in Jaws, however, is Robert Shaw. The English actor was well known at the time, both in the United Kingdom and the United States (although he was especially famous in the former, his homeland). Before Jaws, Shaw had appeared in the likes of From Russia With Love, The Taking of Pelham One Two Three, and A Man for All Seasons (for which he received an Academy Award nomination).

In Jaws, Shaw plays Quint, a grizzled and hardened former soldier who survived a massive shipwreck during World War II. He, very famously, has a strong hatred for sharks, but along with that, he also carries extensive knowledge of these animals as well. Shaw himself is arguably the standout performer of the film, especially because of his speech on the USS Indianapolis, in which he says that sharks have “lifeless eyes, black eyes, like a doll’s eyes.”

Another standout performer from Jaws is the film’s lead hero, Roy Schneider, who plays Chief Martin Brody. Like Shaw, Jaws ended up being the biggest role of Schneider’s career (and one he would reprise in future sequels, including Jaws II).

Ironically, Chief Brody has a deep-rooted fear of the water, which makes his quest for the shark even more intense. The audience can truly feel Brody’s anxiety and stress through Schneider’s performance, which, in turn, makes the movie feel more lived in and real. At the same time, Schneider does brilliantly to ensure his character isn’t defined by fear, making this one of the very best performances in any Steven Spielberg movie.

Just four years after Jaws, Steven Spielberg launched what ended up being another one of his most classic projects. Spielberg developed the character of Indiana Jones alongside George Lucas and Lawrence Kasdan, and then helmed Jones’ first movie, Raiders of the Lost Ark.

Harrison Ford’s first performance as Indiana Jones remains just as iconic as it was nearly fifty years ago. Ford exudes confidence and swagger, making Indiana Jones one of the very best heroes in movie history. After the first Star Wars movie in 1977, it would have been hard to predict that Ford would come up with another character just as iconic, if not more so than Han Solo, but he does just that in Raiders of the Lost Ark (thanks, in part, to Steven Spielberg’s stellar direction).

As far as intimate projects go, arguably no film in recent history has been as meaningful to its director as The Fablemans. The 2022 film serves as Spielberg’s very own origin story, showing his upbringing and how he became interested in filmmaking in the first place. His parents play big roles in the film, especially his mother, who’s played by Michelle Williams, in what is one of the finest performances of her entire career.

Williams has given many memorable turns over the years, but The Fablemans truly stands out. Williams is truly outstanding in this film, playing a character who is so conflicted (and someone that Spielberg himself clearly has complicated feelings about). It’s far from the easiest role to play, especially when factoring in that this is her director’s mother, but Williams deserves great credit.

Arguably the main attraction of Jurassic Park as a film are the dinosaurs. The special effects were exceptional in 1993 and remain so to this day, and along with Spielberg’s direction, help to make these prehistoric creatures feel truly alive. Yet, at the same time, the human characters are just as impressive in Jurassic Park, particularly Jeff Goldblum’s Dr. Ian Malcom.

Malcom is a mathematician who excels in chaos theory, and is highly skeptical about John Hammond’s Earth-shattering invention right from the get-go. Goldblum plays Malcom tremendously, making him a witty character that, at the same time, also demands to be taken seriously. Goldblum helps tie the entire movie together, in fact, making this another top-tier performance in a Steven Spielberg film.

Spielberg incredibly made not one, but two incredible films in 1993. One was the aforementioned Jurassic Park, while the other was a vastly different story — Schindler’s List. The film told the true story of a German industrialist named Oskar Schindler who put his life on the line to save thousands of Jewish people from the Holocaust.

Liam Neeson plays Schindler in what is likely the finest performance of his entire career. There’s so much emotion entrenched in Schindler’s List, and that doesn’t just come from the script or Spielberg’s direction (both of which are excellent). Neeson is extraordinary here, proving himself more than capable of delivering an iconic dramatic performance.

Another iconic 1990s film from Spielberg is Saving Private Ryan, which focuses on a mission to save Private James Francis Ryan (Matt Damon) after his three brothers were killed during the Battle of Normandy. Ryan is the core subject of the film, but Tom Hanks’ Captain John Miller is the one leading the mission.

Hanks is often labeled as one of the best actors to ever live, and Saving Private Ryan is often cited as evidence for this. It’s for good reason, too, as Hanks is this film’s standout. Despite Hanks’ immense fame, it’s easy to forget that he’s there at all, and instead, audiences are likely to simply see Captain Miller, which makes Saving Private Ryan feel all the more realistic.

Arguably the most divisive entry on this list, Tom Cruise received mixed responses to his performance as the leading protagonist in Spielberg’s War of the Worlds remake. The film itself received mixed reception, too, but Cruise does deserve a lot of credit for his performance here. Cruise plays a single dad incredibly well, and embodies what it feels like to be a parent during moments of danger.

One of the very best movies that Steven Spielberg ever made also features Tom Hanks - Catch Me if You Can. The film, which centers on Leonardo DiCaprio’s Frank Abagnale Jr., is actually a true story that you have to see to believe. DiCaprio’s Abagnale Jr. is eccentric, coming up with lies on the spot. As a result, he lives several different lives throughout the film, all with the FBI hot on his trail.

Hanks is, predictably, excellent as the FBI agent leading the charge. Yet, he’s outshone by DiCaprio, only in his early 20s at this time. And despite the fact that this was relatively early on in DiCaprio’s career, it remains one of his very best, and one of the very best performances in any Steven Spielberg film.

Whoopi Goldberg had her breakout role in Steven Spielberg’s 1985 adaption of The Color Purple. It’s no wonder that Goldberg became a star after, too, as she steals the film, vastly improving the story and making it more than worth the watch. A huge reason for this is the pain that Goldberg embodies throughout, making her character feel like a truly three-dimensional and real human being.

Together, they lost more than $800,000 in savings that month.

The report, "State of the Industry: Visa CEDP," finds that the payments industry moved quickly to adapt when Visa introduced CEDP in 2025, but the businesses paying the fees largely did not. Visa began charging a 5-basis-point participation fee on Level 2 and Level 3 volume in April 2025 and switched on automated data validation in October 2025. Level 2 rates for small business cards ended in January 2026, and Level 2 ended for corporate and purchasing cards in April 2026.

The penalty for falling short is steep. A $500 commercial or purchasing card transaction that qualifies for Visa's verified Product 3 rate costs about $8.85 in interchange (including the 5-basis-point participation fee). Downgraded to the non-compliant rate, the same transaction still incurs the participation fee and costs $13.60, an increase of 54%. Those downgrades land on top of the record $198.25 billion U.S. merchants paid in card processing fees in 2025, according to Nilson Report data cited by the Merchants Payments Coalition.

"A year in, the businesses most affected by CEDP are the ones that have done the least about it," said Jeremy Layton, Founder of Verisave. "Many finance teams saw their costs go up and assumed it was simply the new price of accepting commercial cards. Usually it isn't. The data is breaking somewhere between the merchant's systems and the processor, and that can be fixed without switching providers."

Some businesses have responded by passing the cost on to customers. One Verisave client that was losing nearly $500,000 a month to CEDP cited the program as the main reason it began surcharging card payments. The report notes a limit to that approach: card brand rules cap surcharges at 3%, and a business that misses CEDP rates is likely to end up with an effective rate above that cap. Surcharging alone can't recover the full cost.

The report finds that most failures are structural rather than the result of merchant error or fraud.

"In the accounts Verisave reviewed, 93% missed discounts they had already earned," said Canyon Payments CEO Alex Burgin. "We look at the payload, not the rate. When we open up the transactions, the failure is almost always structural: order totals that don't add up to the line items, commodity codes sent as plain text, Level 3 detail captured up front and dropped before settlement. That is not a pricing problem. It is a data problem, and once you can see exactly where it breaks, the fix is usually straightforward."

NFL predictions: Our picks for Giants-Jaguars, the full Week 11 slate

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The New York Giants face major test this week when they face the Jacksonville Jaguars. It is also a game that will provide a huge swing in the Giants' expected chances to make the playoffs. Will the Giants upset Jacksonville? See what our Big Blue View staff members think about that game and the rest of the Week 11 action in this week's staff picks.

I think there's a path to a win for the Giants, if they can find it. The Giants are one of the very best teams in the NFL at forcing drives to end in punts. At the same time, the Jaguars will be without their starting center and the Giants are one of the best teams in the League at creating quick pressure. That's important because Trevor Lawrence has been one of the quarterbacks most affected by pressure in the NFL.

At the same time, the Giants have rediscovered the explosive pass with J.J. McCarthy, which has been the weakness of the Jags' defense.

If the Giants can score touchdowns while disrupting Lawrence, they've got the chance to come away with the upset. We've seen the Giants rebound after bad games, as well as play much better than anticipated when they're underdogs. Maybe both will happen here.

The flip side of the coin is simpler and scarier to me: The Giants have one of the worst red zone defenses in the NFL, and the Jaguars have one of the best red zone offenses in the league. If the Giants can't stop them between the 20s, this game could get ugly fast.

Jacksonville is one of the hottest teams in the league with a young and explosive offense that can run the football. Their defense is aggressive but doesn't NEED to bring the blitz — they blitz at about a league-average rate, but Anthony Campanile does an excellent job utilizing simulated pressure to harass opposing quarterbacks. Jacksonville is third in offensive EPA, 4th in defensive EPA allowed, and 1st in Net EPA — they're a juggernaut based on their performance and the advanced analytics.

Still, that doesn't mean the Giants don't have a chance — they just must play efficient football and avoid the catastrophic mistakes that have robbed them of wins. It's possible, but against the most efficient team in the NFL, it will be difficult. John Harbaugh will have the Giants ready and prepared for the game, and I believe it will be more exciting and competitive. Jacksonville is concluding their third straight road game, which could give the Giants a slight advantage at home, but I ultimately believe the better team finds a way to win.

It took 11 weeks, but for the first time this season, the Giants will face a team with a winning record when the 7-2 Jaguars come to town on Sunday. Not only is Jacksonville running away with the AFC South (they lead the division by four games), but they're steamrolling the competition. They've won their last three games by a combined 75 points. This is easily Big Blue's toughest test of the season, so far.

Jacksonville is very good on both sides of the ball. They're fourth in offensive DVOA, third in defensive DVOA, and third in overall DVOA. They also have an excellent field goal kicker with crazy range. If you look at both rosters, it's hard to find clear advantages for the Giants at any position group outside of tight end, and even there, Jacksonville has good players.

Can the Giants win this game? Yes. They're at home, they're getting an extra three days of rest, and as we saw when they played Houston, they aren't scared and can surprise as a big underdog. They'll need to dial it up on defense and pressure Trevor Lawrence into the kinds of mistakes he used to make before Liam Coen got there. They also might have a little extra motivation after letting last week's game against the Commanders slip away at the end.

I won't be surprised if John Harbaugh's team hangs in there and keeps it as a one-score game most of the way. But in the end, until the Giants show me that they can beat a really good team, I can't pick them to do so.

You can talk yourself into believing that the Giants can beat Jacksonville this Sunday. They needed a miraculous last-second 57-yard field goal to beat the Eagles team (with Jalen Hurts) that the Giants defeated (without Hurts). They lost to the Texans team that the Giants beat the following week. The woeful Titans were within a TD against them last week with 5 minutes left. So indeed, it could be a competitive game. Unfortunately, the Jags have also won games by 24, 29, 35, and 23 points this season. They have scored 31 or more in each of their past four games, and over 40 in two of those. The Giants have only scored more than 30 points once, and only then because of a 99-yard pick-6 on the last play of the game.

Trevor Lawrence isn't necessarily playing any better this year than in the past. He's a QB that simultaneously makes great passes and puts the ball up for grabs, yet unlike in years past, he's cut down on the INTs while getting the TDs. Part of that may be due to 5'10" wide receiver Parker Washington (a 2023 6th-round pick), who had a breakout 2025 and is on pace for 1,000 yards. Running back Bhayshul Tuten, a 4th round 2025 pick, isn't quite in the Jahmyr Gibbs – Kenneth Walker class but he's not far below and is a missed tackle machine. A lot of the credit has to go to head coach Liam Coen, who also calls the plays and turned this team around as soon as he was hired. Lawrence, like most QBs, has trouble with pressure, but he is not too fazed by the blitz. Finding a way to disrupt his rhythm is a key to any chance the Giants may have.

On defense, the Jaguars are strong at linebacker with fourth-round 2023 pick Ventrell Miller, one of the league's best (note how many mid-round picks the Jags have hit on), along with Foyesade Oluokun. They also put on a decent pass rush with Josh Hines-Allen, Travon Walker, and Dennis Gardeck. In the secondary, Travis Hunter is playing more like a No. 2 pick, having mostly given up his evening gig as a receiver.

Are the Giants a serious playoff contender? This is the type of game they can use to make a statement against a team that is in the Super Bowl conversation. I think they'll put up a fight. I also don't think they're there yet.

I want to convince myself to pick the Giants to defeat the Jaguars. I really do. I just can't. The Jaguars are a team with justifiable Super Bowl ambitions. They are a well-rounded team with a high-powered offense, a very good defense, and one of the best field-goal kickers in the business in Cam Little.

The Giants are a good team. They are resilient. They will give the Jaguars their best shot. Maybe, just maybe, it will be good enough to pull off an upset that would go a long way toward ensuring that the Giants make the playoffs.
