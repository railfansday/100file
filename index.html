Honda shows off its next electric motorcycle, and it’s wilder than you thought

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Honda has been getting increasingly serious about electric motorcycles, but if you want to see just how weird the company thinks the future could get, take a look at the Honda EV Outlier Concept.

I got a chance to see the wild-looking machine in person at EICMA in Milan, where it looked like almost nothing else on the show floor. And that’s exactly the point.

The EV Outlier Concept originally debuted at last year’s Japan Mobility Show as Honda’s attempt to imagine an electric motorcycle that isn’t simply a conventional motorcycle with the engine swapped for a battery and motor. In fact, Honda describes the concept as looking toward the future of electric motorcycles beyond 2030.

Instead of a centrally mounted motor driving the rear wheel, the EV Outlier uses motors inside both wheels, giving it all-wheel drive and allowing Honda’s designers to completely rethink the rest of the motorcycle’s layout. The battery is housed in that large central black body, while the rider sits extremely low and behind it.

Honda describes the design philosophy with three words: “Gliding, Ecstasy, and Low.” The first refers to the smooth, nearly silent feeling of electric propulsion, while the second describes the instant acceleration and massive torque available from independently controlled front and rear motors. “Low” is more literal: Honda deliberately dropped the rider’s seating and eye position to create an entirely different sensation from a conventional motorcycle.

There’s even a bucket-style backrest designed to support the rider under hard acceleration and let the rider pivot through corners around their hips.

Up front, traditional mirrors are replaced by cameras, while a wide digital cockpit can display things like lean angle, riding modes, front motor torque adjustment, and what the two wheels are doing in real time.

Will Honda actually build this thing? Almost certainly not exactly as you see it here. That’s what concepts are for.

But the EV Outlier is still interesting as a statement of intent. Honda is obviously still playing around with the freedom that electric drivetrains offer, and the company is asking what motorcycles could become once designers no longer have to work around engines, fuel tanks, transmissions, and traditional drivetrains.

And judging by what I saw at EICMA, Honda’s answer could get pretty wild.

The Buffett Indicator Is Flashing Red. These 3 Dividend Stocks Could Be Safe Havens.

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The stock market is looking overvalued, according to this indicator.

Investors listen to Warren Buffett because he has demonstrated his sharp understanding of the stock market over time. Leading Berkshire Hathaway for six decades, he delivered market-beating returns. Buffett retired at the start of the year, but today he's chairman emeritus and remains involved in the investing world.

And Buffett's words of wisdom still ring true, meaning it's an excellent idea to refer to them as needed when investing. Right now may be a particularly good time to do this. This is because Buffett once said the market was "playing with fire" when it did something that it's doing again right now. The Buffett indicator, a measure of the stock market's total value in relation to the size of the U.S. economy, has surpassed 200%. In fact, it's now at 281%. This signals that stocks might be overvalued relative to economic output.

In such an environment, with the Buffett indicator flashing red, declines may be on the horizon. Here are three dividend stocks, companies that offer you passive income, that could be safe havens.

Target (TGT +4.73%) has proven its commitment to rewarding shareholders as it has lifted its dividend for more than 50 straight years -- and this has landed it on the list of Dividend Kings. With such a track record, it's likely the company will aim to maintain this momentum. So, when you buy Target stock, you can be pretty confident that you'll see dividend growth year after year. This year, Target paid out a little more than $1 billion in dividends in the first half.

The retail giant pays a dividend of $4.64 per share, representing a yield of 2.9%, greatly surpassing the S&P 500 dividend yield of about 1%.

Investors also will like Target's recovery story. The company has struggled to grow in recent years amid various headwinds, but new chief Michael Fiddelke launched a plan earlier this year that's starting to bear fruit. The company is revamping store displays and refreshing merchandise, leaning into AI to improve growth, and lowering prices on many items. This, along with Target's offering of essentials, may boost revenue in the quarters to come.

Johnson & Johnson (JNJ +2.13%) is another Dividend King you can count on for passive income growth and earnings strength. The pharma giant pays a dividend of $5.36 per share, at a yield of 1.9%. The company's free cash flow of more than $19 billion on a trailing 12-month basis shows it has the financial strength to keep this going.

You'll also like J&J for its leadership in pharmaceutical products, through its innovative medicine business, and in medtech. Including the impact of currencies, these businesses helped J&J's revenue to advance 6% in the recent quarter to $25 billion. This, along with 29 products generating $1 billion or more in revenue annually, put the company on track for more than $100 billion in revenue this year -- that's a first for J&J. These current products, as well as a strong pipeline, are reason to be confident that growth may continue from this point. And that makes J&J an excellent stock to add to your portfolio in any environment.

'Godzilla Minus Zero' Exposes the Biggest Problem with Christopher Nolan's World War II Epic

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Director Takashi Yamazaki made his new Godzilla movie in direct response to Christopher Nolan's film

In 2023, two Oscar-winning blockbuster movies tackled the topic of nuclear weapons and the bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in very different ways. Christopher Nolan's Oppenheimer told the story of the atomic bomb through the eyes of its creator, J. Robert Oppenheimer (Cillian Murphy), while Takashi Yamazaki's Godzilla Minus One continued the 70-year cinematic tradition of using a giant radioactive monster as a metaphor for nuclear warfare.

Notably, neither movie actually depicted those nuclear explosions or their immediate aftermath onscreen, but three years later, one of those directors has done exactly that — and it's not Nolan.

Light spoilers ahead for the plot of Godzilla Minus Zero.

While Nolan followed up Oppenheimer with The Odyssey, which in many ways feels like a spiritual sequel about another brilliant man whose actions changed the course of history (arguably for the worse), Yamazaki delivered a direct sequel in Godzilla Minus Zero. The film, which is currently stomping its way across movie theaters worldwide and dominating the U.S. box office, features a brutal depiction of the immediate aftermath of the bombing of Hiroshima.

Just before the climax of the film, we see a flashback in which one bloodied and stunned character stumbles through the streets of the obliterated city. The camera then points directly at the charred remains of several humans who once lived there. Yamazaki juxtaposes this directly with images of Godzilla's destruction in perhaps the most unsubtle metaphor in a movie released this year.

With that one scene, Yamazaki pulls off the one thing Nolan was never brave enough to do: he shows the actual, awful carnage that fuels both director's Oscar-winning movies. And as it turns out, this isn't a coincidence.

Back in March 2024, right after winning an Oscar for Best Visual Effects, Yamazaki responded to a question comparing his and Nolan's movies with an intriguing promise.

“As a person of Japanese ancestry and descent, my response to Oppenheimer [is that] I would like to dedicate a different film to that when that day comes,” Yamazaki told the press.

Speaking to Time Magazine more recently, Yamazaki offered a bit more insight into how Godzilla Minus Zero became that response.

"In my mind I was thinking I'd develop a film that looks directly at Hiroshima," he said. "But then I began thinking: Wait — Godzilla is itself a metaphor for nuclear war. Maybe I can combine the two and make a Godzilla movie that is also an answer to Oppenheimer."

Godzilla is itself a metaphor for nuclear war.

The result is a stunning cinematic sequence that's in direct conversation with Nolan's film while also pushing the legacy of the Godzilla franchise to a bold new place it's never ventured before. For Yamazaki, however, it's not just about one-upping other movies, it's about showing the horrors of nuclear warfare to a modern audience that might otherwise never see them.

"It was important to show as many people as possible what the effects of an atomic bomb would really look like," he said. "I wanted to evoke something similar because it's very important for it to feel relatable — to jog our imaginations in a way that says, 'Wow. This is real. It is a very violent act.'"

Nolan devoted much of Oppenheimer to exploring the brutal reality of nuclear war, but he never went so far as to actually show us what happened in Japan. The director did offer an explanation for why at the time, though it's not particularly satisfying — especially in the wake of Godzilla Minus Zero.

At a public event in 2024, Nolan explained why he decided not to show the actual bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in his movie.

"The feeling for me as a filmmaker was very strongly that to depart from Oppenheimer’s experience would betray the terms of the storytelling," Nolan said. "He learned about the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki on the radio — the same as the rest of the world."

It's true that Oppenheimer is told from the main character's perspective, but there is one exception. While most of the film is essentially Oppenheimer's POV, several black-and-white sequences intercut throughout show us the same series of events from the perspective of antagonist Lewis Strauss (Robert Downey Jr.), including some events that Oppenheimer never witnessed first-hand.

So it's not like showing Hiroshima or Nagasaki would have broken the rules of Oppenheimer set out by Nolan, it's just that he chose not to do it. Of course, that's a valid choice, even if it's arguably one of the film's only flaws. But with the benefit of hindsight after seeing how Yamazaki depicts that horrifying event — and how just a few seconds of screentime hits harder than Nolan's entire movie — it's hard not to look back at Oppenheimer with a sense of disappointment.

Maybe after watching Godzilla Minus Zero, Nolan himself will feel a similar sense of regret, but considering the director almost never makes sequels, I doubt he'll revisit the events of World War II and the bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki any time soon. Thankfully, we have Yamazaki's brilliant Godzilla sequel, which might not even exist if it wasn't for Oppenheimer in the first place.

Une application ne peut pas seulement agir. Elle doit confirmer, informer et prévenir. Lorsque les premières applications de la Fabrique ont eu besoin d'envoyer leurs propres messages, les limites des services externes sont rapidement apparues. Spore est né pour reprendre cette fonction essentielle.

Une application peut fonctionner parfaitement et rester muette.

Un compte est créé, mais rien ne le confirme. Un paiement passe, mais aucun reçu n'arrive. Un mot de passe doit être réinitialisé, mais le lien n'est jamais envoyé. Une erreur survient, mais personne n'est prévenu.

L'e-mail n'est pas un accessoire ajouté à la fin.

C'est la capacité d'une application à s'exprimer : confirmer une action, transmettre une information et prévenir lorsqu'une attention est nécessaire.

Sans cette capacité, la relation entre l'application et son utilisateur reste incomplète.

Avec les premières applications sont arrivés les premiers services externes à payer.

Les API d'envoi d'e-mails sont très attirantes au départ. L'intégration est rapide, les premiers messages sont souvent gratuits et l'infrastructure semble disparaître derrière une simple requête.

Pour un projet qui commence, c'est exactement ce qu'il faut.

Les messages deviennent plus nombreux. Une deuxième application arrive, puis une troisième. Il faut ajouter des domaines, suivre de nouveaux quotas et passer aux offres payantes.

Chaque application apporte alors son abonnement, sa console, sa clé d'API, ses limites et sa facture.

Nous ne payions pas pour une nouvelle capacité. Nous repayions, application après application, pour leur permettre d'accomplir la même fonction : envoyer un message.

Le problème n'était pas que ces services fonctionnaient mal.

Leur offre gratuite permettait de commencer rapidement. Mais une fois l'envoi d'e-mails intégré au cœur de l'application, changer de service devenait plus difficile.

Ce qui n'était au départ qu'une commodité était devenu une dépendance.

À chaque nouvelle application, nous pouvions recommencer : ouvrir un compte, configurer un domaine, intégrer une nouvelle API et accepter un nouveau tarif.

Ou construire cette capacité une fois, puis la partager entre toutes les applications.

Spore est né pour donner aux applications de la Fabrique une infrastructure d'envoi commune.

Une application lui confie un message. Spore s'occupe du domaine, de l'identité d'envoi, de la signature et de son acheminement.

Synthiz peut envoyer un lien de connexion. Lungor, une facture. Nakoda, une alerte. Chaque nouvelle application bénéficie de la même capacité sans devoir reconstruire toute la chaîne.

L'objectif n'est pas d'envoyer davantage d'e-mails.

November 9: Chase Comet 161P/Hartley-IRAS

Challenge yourself to pick up Saturn’s moon Iapetus, which tonight reaches greatest eastern elongation 9.4’ east of the ringed planet. The two-toned moon is tidally locked to its parent world, which means it rotates in time with its orbit. At eastern elongation, Iapetus’ darker hemisphere faces earthward, reducing the moon’s magnitude to nearly 12. This, combined with its distance from Saturn, makes it more of a challenge to find, but you can give it a try if you’ve got a larger scope.

Try around 9 P.M. local time when Saturn is highest, some 50° above the southern horizon. It’s the brightest point of light in this region of the sky, hanging just below Pisces and to the far upper left of Fomalhaut, the brightest star in Piscis Austrinus (much closer to the horizon). Even if you can’t locate Iapetus or don’t have a large scope, Saturn is magnificent through a telescope. Its disk spans 19” and its rings are roughly 43” across, showing off their southern side. You should definitely spot its largest and brightest moon, mid-8th-magnitude Titan, just over 2’ west of the planet. Depending on your scope and when you look, you may also spot at least three other moons: 10th-magnitude Tethys, Dione, and Rhea, closer to the planet. Tethys lies to Saturn’s west, while Rhea and Dione are to the east.

InfoQ Homepage Articles LLM Reliability Layers Under Test: Accuracy, Yield, and the Limits of Verification

Nov 10, 2026 15 min read

Clinical notes, incident reports, support tickets, and forum posts are prose, and a language model can read them. What it produces is not dependable. Ours was right about one answer in three on the task below and reported full confidence on two-thirds of its answers; wherever a wrong answer costs something, that is unusable.

We tested six layers, checks wrapped around the model, that each evaluate the model's output and flag what is wrong so that it can be put right: check each answer against something deterministic, send provable failures back to the model [1], sample it several times and vote [2], have a second model judge it [3], withhold what cannot be corroborated, and send the rest to a person. The theory we set out to test is that these layers are additive: each one catches errors the layers below it missed, so accuracy rises with every layer added. That assumption is what justifies stacking them at all. We ran all six on the same records, each priced, with the free check entered as a competitor. The theory did not hold. The layers sorted the answers by how far to trust them and corrected almost none.

Presented by: David Iparraguirre - Senior Product Marketing Manager at Datadog, and Jessica Yang - Product Manager at Datadog

Testing it needs a graded task, so we chose an annotated corpus: CADEC, the CSIRO Adverse Drug Event Corpus [4], 1,250 forum posts about two drugs, every adverse reaction marked as a span, the writer's exact words such as "rectal bleed", and given a SNOMED CT code. A gold answer for every mention lets us score each layer's verdicts instead of trusting them — and that assumes gold is the only right answer, which is not always true. Running one development document through the pipeline, the span "extremely sick" is gold-coded 213257006 |Generally unwell|; the model answered 39104002 |Illness| — an equally active, equally general reading that shares no wording with the span or with gold's own name. Naming either is a real answer; only one is scored correct. We did not build a CADEC system or tune for it: the aim was to evaluate open-weight models and measure how much each layer raises their accuracy, and CADEC is the instrument.

A supervised system does far better. CONORM [5], fine-tuned on 875 of CADEC's 1,250 files, reaches an end-to-end F1 of 0.72 under lenient span matching. Our zero-shot extractor, everything it returns, reaches 0.47 to 0.50 under the same matching on our development split, 0.39 to 0.43 span-exact. We are not competing with it: it needs those 875 annotated files, and still cannot say which of its answers to trust. Our held-out split was spent once: 60 documents, one run; every other number is development-side and says so. Two things would move the absolute numbers: the judge is small, and CADEC, public since 2015, is almost certainly in its training data. The comparisons between layers should hold.

The model is never asked for a code, and everything above it depends on that choice.

Figure 1: The pipeline, left to right, on one illustrative post (CADEC is non-transferable). Teal: a model call; grey: deterministic code. Each card shows what that step produces for the example; code appears for the first time in the last one. Image: the authors.

The model quotes each reaction; a retriever with no language model in it searches 227,554 keyword-to-code rows — every description in the SNOMED CT Australian edition of 31 July 2026 — embedding each quote with granite-embedding:30m, 384 dimensions, run locally, and showing the model a menu of the 20 nearest concepts to pick from. Codes are written as 271782001 |Drowsy|, SNOMED's bar notation, throughout.

We chose this shape by measuring the alternatives on 40 development documents, three cold runs each: recall the code from memory, name the concept and look it up, or pick from the menu.

Figure 2: F1 of the extractor alone under three ways of getting the code, one dot per cold run, with tokens per run and unparseable replies beneath. Image: the authors.

Recalling the code is not weak but broken: eight to twelve times worse than naming the concept. It answered null on up to 38 percent of records, and of the codes it committed to, 13 to 18 percent exist in no SNOMED release.

F1 is span-exact throughout: a wrong boundary is both a false positive and a false negative.

That is the extractor the six layers sit on. Before measuring what a layer adds, we had to know how much its output moves between runs of the same inputs.

Three identical runs of the unchanged extractor differed by four points of F1, at temperature zero.

We ran the extraction step three times, cold, on the same 40 documents. Two runs were byte-identical. The third diverged and finished with 98 correct mentions against the pair's 87. Grouped by mention, the runs give 233 mentions:

Where all three found the same span, they agreed on the code 84 percent of the time, so what moves between runs is mostly the reading, not the coding. We did not ask why those 22 were coded differently, and we should have.

The variation is a property of this model: four other families run three times each, llama3.1:8b, mistral:7b-instruct, qwen3:8b, and granite4:micro-h, returned byte-identical output every time. Our extractor, gpt-oss:20b, is the only mixture-of-experts model of the five and the only one that varied. Why, we could not settle: the diverging request, sent alone eight times, returns one reply; it moves only inside a full run. We kept it for its 6.5 extra points of F1 over llama3.1:8b.

From then on, every change was measured on three runs and reported as the range across them.

The two free layers did their jobs: the vocabulary check sorted the answers, and refusal withheld the doubtful ones, and neither changed an answer. Of the three paid layers, self-correction almost never fired, voting did not help, and the judge worked, but nothing read its verdict.

Development split, three runs of 40 documents; figures below are ranges across the runs:

Then we removed the three paid layers and replayed refusal: what it passed differed by one record on the first run and by none on the other two.

Voting and the judge's verdicts are good for diagnosis, not repair: a split vote marks an answer the model cannot reproduce, and a judge answering not on this menu marks a failed menu, not a failed pick. We did not use them that way, and should have.

Every verdict carries information. None makes the system produce more right answers, and they do not stack.

Figure 3: The first run's 230 records under each shipping rule. Dark green: right code on the exact span; light green: exact span, wrong code; light teal: right code, boundary off; grey: neither; amber: to a person. Dotted lines: the most the extractor found. Image: the authors.

Figure 3 reads each verdict as a shipping rule over the same 230 records. Three things hold.

First, any verdict raises the accuracy of what passes, because withholding is a layer's only lever. No rule ships more than the 88 right answers the extractor found, so every gain in accuracy is paid for in right answers held back. Accuracy alone flatters any withholding layer. Yield, right answers as a share of all records, cannot be flattered, so we report both.

Second, the price differs by verdict. ACCEPT is the most precise rule and the most expensive: moving to it removes 128 errors at the cost of 49 right answers and 177 records for a person, because the check can endorse only 32 percent of mentions whose wording matches the vocabulary. The menu-shown judge's verdict is informed rather than lexical, and it is the one rule whose F1 beats shipping everything, 0.420 against 0.397.

Third, the verdicts nest rather than add. Nearly everything the free check endorses, the judge endorses too, so a second verdict mostly confirms the first and can only remove records, never recover one the first held back; that is why removing the paid layers changed one record. Where every verdict agrees, the answers are almost all right; where only a vote does, almost all wrong. Each verdict is a different trade between errors, lost answers, and reviews; we recommend none.

The held-out split, run once, tells the same story. Before any check, one answer in three was right. Of those, the vocabulary check marked ACCEPT, five in six were right; of the rest, one in five. The check changed no answer; it only sorted them.

The domain knowledge was never missing. The model loses at reading, and nothing above it can see what it did not read.

Figure 4: Where the development split's 226 annotated mentions go through the shipped extractor, gpt-oss:20b, first run. Each bar is what the stage above kept; the dotted steps are what each stage lost. Teal: a model call; grey: deterministic code. Image: the authors.

Once a mention is found, the pipeline is reliable: the right concept reaches the menu for 93 percent of found spans and the model picks it 81 percent of the time. The loss is at finding. Nearly half the annotated mentions are never quoted as the annotators marked them, and none of the reasons are medical: single words go untouched where the annotator marked them, boundaries stretch — an extra adjective swept into a span the annotator left bare — and figures of speech are read literally, an idiom for exasperation coded as a nocturnal behaviour. Two domain-adapted models did not help: BioMistral-7B could not reliably return a parseable reply, and SapBERT as the retriever's encoder improved the menu and worsened the answer. A worked example and a denied-reactions rule did.

No layer can add a mention. Every layer above the extractor checks, re-asks, votes on, judges, or withholds an answer the extractor already gave; none can find a mention it missed. So the most any stack can ever get right is what the extractor found in the first place: on the held-out split, just over half the annotated mentions, a detection F1 of 0.521. The one way past that ceiling is a second reader: a different model family shown the post and the first model's spans, asked what was missed. We did not build it. Its additions would be proposals, needing the same measurement as the extractor's.

Four of the five lessons were held on other data. What changed is where the model loses, and where the free check can be trusted.

Seven further corpora [6–10], five model families; for each lesson, does the data agree?

The judge, the nesting of verdicts, and the diagnostic use of split votes were measured on CADEC only.

Every layer passed its own tests. The two defects that mattered sat between the layers and inside the metric.

The first was the unread verdicts. The second was the metric: voting overwrote codes without re-running the vocabulary check, so records shipped marked verified for a code they no longer held. Fixing that moved the held-out F1 from 0.204 to 0.204, because precision and recall cannot tell an unwarranted answer from a wrong one. We built six layers to decide which answers to trust, then scored them with a metric that cannot see the difference.

Both defects share a shape: a load-bearing fact recorded once, so nothing could disagree with it. Three checks built from that lesson, gatecheck, crosscheck and stagecheck, are reached from the repository below; stagecheck is a standalone package with no dependencies.

What transfers is not the layers but six practices, each earned above.

We set out believing that stacking reliability layers buys reliability. Measured end to end, they sorted the answers by how far to trust them and fixed almost none: worth a great deal, and not what we bought them for. The model reads. Almost everything else belongs to code, and one choice to you.

Code, ledger, decision records, and every figure's source. CADEC is non-transferable; we ship document IDs, never text.

This creates a costly gap between what the system says, what is actually in the store and what customers experience, while leaving AI and other technology investments to act on incomplete records.

And when the system is wrong, associates do what they have to do to keep the store running. Nearly half (49%) correct the record themselves and 44% raise a stock adjustment. But among associates who take action, 42% bypass the official system entirely, while 68% use at least one informal method, including paper notes, team chats, personal phone photos and apps or memory. The result is a 'shadow store' in which vital information exists outside the record that head office, dashboards and AI systems rely on.

"Retailers do not have an AI adoption problem as much as a store truth problem," said Samuel Mueller, CEO and co-founder of Scandit. "Associates are spending valuable time repairing these gaps, yet much of that work disappears into unofficial tracking systems. Before retailers ask AI to make more decisions based on inaccurate store data, they need a shared, accurate view of what is actually happening on the shelf and across their stores."

Associates' trust in stock data does not build gradually. The survey highlights it stays low until the data is very good. Only 8% of associates completely trust their store's stock information, and only one in four say it is accurate more than 70% of the time. Among associates who put accuracy anywhere up to 50%, roughly a quarter report high trust, and that figure barely moves until accuracy passes 70%, when it rises sharply.

"This is not a gradual improvement problem," said Andrew Smith, Managing Partner and co-founder of ThinkUncommon and a RETHINK Retail Top Expert since 2021. "Moving stock accuracy from 46% to 60% may look like progress in a board report and still change nothing on the floor. Trust only begins to move once accuracy clears 70%, and until then associates do what sensible people do: they check the shelf themselves and either correct the system or just do what they need to do to help the customer in front of them."

The consequences extend beyond productivity. Associates report spending an average of 4.1 hours each week handling customer questions that store technology should be able to answer, and are asked to physically check whether an item is in stock 5.85 times a week on average. More than one in five say they make those checks more than ten times a week.

The findings point to a practical lesson for technology deployment: associates who participated in a pilot reported using most store tools at a rate of 74%, compared with 61% among those who were consulted before rollout and those who were never asked. Half of pilot participants reported high trust in their stock data, compared with 25% of those who were never asked for input.

InfoQ Homepage News Google Cloud Spend Caps Reach General Availability with API and Monthly Resets

Nov 10, 2026 3 min read

Google Cloud has made spend caps generally available on Cloud Billing Budgets, letting customers pause new usage of a service in a project once a monthly budget is reached, without deleting resources. The GA release adds an API, estimated live costs that trigger caps within minutes for AI services, and automatic monthly resets, but enforcement remains limited to Gemini API, the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform, Cloud Run, and Cloud Run functions.

A spend cap is a budget with enforcement attached: according to the documentation, when estimated spend on the capped service reaches the target amount, new requests to that service in that project are blocked, in-flight requests run to completion and are charged, persistent resources keep running, and nothing is deleted. Usage covered by committed-use discounts is paused along with pay-as-you-go usage, while Provisioned Throughput for AI models is never halted. Billing administrators and project owners receive alerts at 50, 80, and 100 percent of the cap.

The company changed three things between the July public preview and the GA release:

The limits are as specific as the features: a cap covers one project and one service for a calendar month, so folders, organizations, labels and multi-service budgets are out of scope, as are reseller accounts and subscription charges such as Gemini Enterprise seats. The estimate that drives enforcement is calculated at list price and excludes credits and discounts. Enforcement is also not instant: the documentation states that "any cost overages are billed as normal" and advises setting the cap "slightly below your absolute limit." Compute Engine, Cloud Storage, and networking are not among the eligible services, which means the cap addresses a runaway model or agent loop and not a forgotten GPU instance.

The narrower scope drew criticism when the preview expanded in October. On a Hacker News thread, one commenter who had asked Google for the capability a year earlier to control AI spend called the result "very crude, not granular like the rich IAM system," noting the four-product, month-only design. Another observed that Cloud Run had been added that morning, so the service list was still growing.

Developer Simon Willison argued in October that pay-by-usage services should default to hard caps, because coding and personal agents make it far easier to spin up code that calls paid APIs, and that "most businesses and individuals would prefer errors to a surprise $10,000+ bill." He cited Google's caps alongside a project-level spend limit in AWS's new account experience, which pauses the whole project and stops its resources when reached, preserving data for 90 days, and which AWS is rolling out to a limited number of customers. Azure's spending limit remains confined to free accounts and credit-based subscriptions; Microsoft states it isn't available for subscriptions with commitment plans or with pay-as-you-go pricing.

Spend caps pair with early anomalies, a daily baseline model that flags directional cost variances per service and names the top SKUs behind a spike, which Google positions as the detection half of the same control loop. Both are configured from the Budgets and Alerts page in the Billing console or through the Budgets API.
