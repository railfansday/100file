Si complica la trattativa tra la Lega Serie A e Avenue Capital per la cessione di una quota del business dei diritti televisivi internazionali. Secondo quanto riportato da Reuters, alcuni club del massimo campionato italiano avrebbero espresso perplessità sull'operazione, rallentando il percorso verso un possibile accordo.

Il fondo statunitense, con sede a New York, ha messo sul tavolo 320 milioni di euro per acquisire circa il 10% della nuova società destinata a gestire i diritti internazionali della Serie A, comprese le attività legate alle trasmissioni televisive, alle sponsorizzazioni e al settore delle scommesse. Una proposta che attribuisce alla società una valutazione complessiva di 3,2 miliardi di euro.

Non tutti i club, però, sono convinti di concedere ad Avenue Capital l'esclusiva nelle trattative. Secondo tre fonti interpellate da Reuters, alcune società ritengono che la Lega debba mantenere la possibilità di esaminare eventuali offerte alternative mentre approfondisce la proposta del fondo statunitense.

Al momento Avenue Capital rimane l'unico soggetto attivamente impegnato nel negoziato, pur senza aver ottenuto formalmente l'esclusiva. Le fonti non hanno specificato quali siano i club contrari né il loro numero, ma le resistenze emerse sarebbero sufficienti a rallentare l'operazione.

Per arrivare all'approvazione definitiva sarà necessario ottenere il voto favorevole di almeno 14 delle 20 società di Serie A.

Non sarebbe la prima volta che il calcio italiano tenta di aprire agli investitori esterni. Nel 2021 era stata valutata la cessione del 10% del business dei diritti televisivi nazionali a un consorzio guidato da CVC, ma il progetto era naufragato per la mancanza del consenso necessario tra le società.

Anche questa volta, dunque, la posizione dei club sarà determinante. L'offerta di Avenue Capital rimane sul tavolo, ma l'intesa è tutt'altro che raggiunta.

Bernard Vellut : « On veut que ce soit une belle fête »

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

À quelques jours du choc face à l’AS Saint-Étienne au Parc des Sports d’Annecy, pour le compte du 7e tour de la Coupe de France, le GFA Rumilly Vallières est en pleine effervescence. Le club haut-savoyard cultive un modèle unique porté par quatre co-présidents et un esprit résolument familial. L'un d'eux, Bernard Vellut (à droite sur la photo), revient pour nous sur les coulisses de cette ascension fulgurante, les défis structurels du club et l'excitation qui entoure la venue des Verts.

Bonjour Bernard Vellut et merci de nous accorder ces quelques minutes d’interview. Le club de Rumilly Vallières a la particularité d'être dirigé par quatre co-présidents avec chacun un rôle dédié. En ce qui te concerne, tu as en charge les instances de football et les collectivités territoriales. Comment est née cette organisation assez unique et comment vous répartissez-vous la charge au quotidien ?

Effectivement, nous sommes quatre co-présidents. Comment cela est-il arrivé ? Moi j'étais président du club de Rumilly et Luc Chabert était président du club de Vallières, à trois kilomètres de chez nous. On a donc évoqué un rapprochement de nos deux clubs, plutôt que de se tirer dans les pattes, sans parler encore de fusion. Mais il y avait toujours quelque chose qui clochait au niveau de détails plus ou moins importants. C'est toujours une question humaine de toute façon.

À un moment donné, on s’est dit qu'il fallait arrêter de se piquer les joueurs et réfléchir à faire quelque chose de plus grand. Avec Luc Chabert, qui a une très grosse entreprise de fromagerie avec une dizaine de laiteries, on s’est dit : « On y va ! ». On s’est donc réunis une fois, deux fois, trois fois. Au début ça tiquait un petit peu, mais on est arrivés à se dire qu'on allait commencer par tout le cursus jeunes. Les jeunes, eux, c'est d’abord la couleur du maillot, les histoires de personnes ils n’en ont rien à cirer ! On a fait deux années comme ça avec les jeunes et ça a tellement bien marché qu'on a ensuite souhaité fusionner tout le club. De là est née la structure des quatre co-présidents : j'avais un vice-président avec moi et Luc Chabert avait le sien de son côté. On s’est dit qu’on n’allait pas faire d'histoires et qu'à quatre co-présidents, on ne serait pas trop pour gérer le club.

Les choses se sont déroulées de la meilleure des façons. Au-delà de la présidence, on est tous devenus amis, ainsi que nos épouses respectives. Quand tu as quatre co-présidents comme ça qui mènent la barque, tous les gens derrière voient que ça marche super bien et l'engouement est parti. Au niveau de la répartition : Bruno Piccon s'occupe de toute la partie jeunes, Luc Chabert gère les partenaires, François Baudet s'occupe de la communication et du matériel, et pour ma part, je m'occupe des instances, des terrains, de la mairie de Vallières, du district, de la Ligue et de la Fédération. Je fais également partie du comité directeur de la Ligue où je représente les clubs nationaux de la région Rhône-Alpes, ainsi qu'au siège à Paris.

En dehors de cela, on a tous notre boulot, bien sûr. Toutes les trois semaines, on se réunit et on mange ensemble pour échanger sur les perspectives, les actions en cours ou à venir, et ce qui a été bien ou moins bien fait. Derrière, on a un comité directeur pour décider des orientations dans les différentes commissions, et trois semaines après, ces commissions nous rendent compte de leurs résultats. On avance comme ça, sans compter les coups de téléphone et les messages tous les deux jours... On communique beaucoup ! On est obligés de travailler comme cela maintenant parce qu'on a quand même 800 licenciés, ce n'est pas rien.

Comme tu le disais, le club est né d'une fusion en 2018, et il se retrouve aujourd'hui à une très belle place en championnat (le club est actuellement 2e de sa poule en National 1, à 3 points de Nîmes avec un match de retard, ndp²). Le club a aussi connu une épopée fantastique en Coupe de France en 2021. Quel regard portez-vous sur cette ascension fulgurante et est-ce que le monde professionnel est une ambition ou un doux rêve ?

On a l'habitude de faire les choses les unes après les autres. Quand on est montés, on voulait voir à quel niveau on pouvait se situer. Aujourd'hui, il ne faut pas se leurrer, les joueurs comme le staff sont pratiquement tous professionnels. Comme nous avons un budget serré, nous souhaitons avancer pas à pas et ne surtout pas brûler les étapes.

Au niveau du coach, nous avons un gars super, Cédric Rullier, qui a essentiellement recruté régionalement. On ne voulait pas recruter trop loin parce que la vie est très chère en Haute-Savoie et qu'il faut se loger. Maintenant qu'on a un niveau honnête avec de l’ambition, on va chercher un petit peu plus loin, mais toujours des joueurs ciblés sur leur mentalité et leur respect. C'est après les qualités sportives bien sûr, mais un joueur qui ne rentre pas dans notre moule mentalement, on ne le prend pas. On veut garder notre esprit familial, même si on s'est beaucoup professionnalisés.

On a énormément de bénévoles et on veut que la mayonnaise prenne entre eux et les joueurs. C'est ce qui fait notre force aujourd'hui : le groupe vit très bien, l'entraîneur est exceptionnel et les bénévoles sont hyper dévoués. Dès que tu demandes quelque chose, ils sont tous derrière toi pour t'aider. Aujourd'hui, c'est fantastique !

La Coupe de France, cela n’a rien à voir avec le championnat, les émotions sont multipliées par dix ou quinze. En 2021, lors de notre exploit, on a commencé en gagnant aux tirs au but contre un club de district, un truc de dingue. Petit à petit, on a passé les tours : on a sorti Prix-lès-Mézières et le FC Annecy aux tirs au but, puis Le Puy en huitièmes, Toulouse en quarts, avant de tomber contre Monaco en demi-finale. C'était complètement fou.

Le seul bémol, c'est que ça s'est joué pendant le Covid, donc il n'y avait pas de supporters au stade. Mais ça ne nous a pas empêchés de nous retrouver le soir des victoires. La mairie et la gendarmerie ont été très sympas avec nous parce qu'ils savaient qu'ils ne pouvaient pas faire autrement. J'ai une petite anecdote pour le match contre Monaco : le préfet avait donné l'autorisation à deux ou trois équipes de jeunes de nous accompagner à la sortie du restaurant à midi. Sauf qu'on s'est retrouvés à 3 000 dans les rues ! Les gendarmes n'ont rien pu faire, c'était un truc de fou. C'est une expérience qu'on ne reverra peut-être jamais, en tout cas pas moi, mais c'est tout le mal que je souhaite au club.

Depuis, ça fait trois ans qu'on se fait éliminer aux tirs au but. On s'était dit que cette année, il fallait que ça change pour aller chercher un 32e ou un gros club. On n'est pas encore en 32e, on a eu quelques soucis et on est encore passés aux tirs au but au tour précédent. J'ai l'impression que c'est dans la tête : quand tu rencontres une équipe plus faible, tu te mets à son diapason. Mais avec les joueurs qu'on a aujourd'hui, si on tombe contre une équipe plus forte, ils savent hausser leur niveau. On ne lâche rien. Par exemple, hier à Saint-Priest, il y avait 2-2 à un quart d'heure de la fin. On fait rentrer le banc, pouf, on met 4-2, et si le match continue on en met encore deux ou trois. C'est formidable.

Parlons un peu des infrastructures du club et de vos perspectives d'évolution. De quels terrains disposez-vous actuellement ?

On a un terrain en herbe à Rumilly avec un synthétique à côté. Le centre de Vallières est consacré aux jeunes et aux matchs de Ligue pour nos réserves (l'équipe 2 joue en R1 et l'équipe 3 en R3). Là-bas, on a un terrain en herbe et un synthétique tout neuf, deux jolis terrains.

Là où le bât blesse aujourd'hui, c'est à Rumilly : notre synthétique doit être refait et on voudrait améliorer le terrain en herbe. En ce moment il est beau parce qu'il pleut, mais l'été il est sec et l'hiver il gèle. Ce n'est pas une vraie pelouse de stade, elle a 50 ans. Les jardiniers s'en occupent très bien, mais il faut refaire la surface. On hésite entre synthétique, hybride ou herbe. On se bat un peu avec la mairie et la communauté de communes pour avoir une vision sur les prochaines années. Un beau terrain amène du beau jeu et des spectateurs, c'est un tout. Les municipalités ne sont pas contre, mais il faut trouver les fonds et les subventions.

Vous pouvez bénéficier d'aides financières des collectivités ?

Oui, la mairie nous donne 100 000 euros et la communauté de communes 100 000 euros aussi. Pour le département, les subventions dépendent de la division dans laquelle tu évolues. Avec la réforme des championnats et les changements d'appellation, on est en train de regarder à quoi on a droit en tant que premier niveau amateur de France. C'est un combat de tous les jours. Il faut rien lâcher, tout en gardant un bon état d'esprit et en évitant les conflits. Ça a déjà bien avancé par rapport à nos débuts, mais il en faut encore plus.

Quel est votre budget actuel et est-il stable ?

On est à 1,9 million d'euros. Quand on a fusionné, on était à 600 000 euros. Aujourd'hui, on doit avoir le dixième budget de notre poule. On est dans une poule Sud très relevée avec des clubs comme Nîmes ou Toulon. C'est quasiment une petite division supérieure, capable de rivaliser avec le milieu de tableau au-dessus.

Il y a aussi quatre clubs de la région lyonnaise dans votre groupe. Comment vous positionnez-vous face à eux, notamment sur le recrutement ?

Ils ont un gros avantage sur nous : ils ont des pépinières de joueurs partout, avec 20 ou 25 clubs au kilomètre carré. Nous, à part Annecy avec qui on travaille en partenariat, on est assez isolés. Lyon est à 120 kilomètres. Et puis à Rumilly, on est à côté d'Annecy et de Genève : un T2 coûte 800 ou 900 euros par mois, ce n'est pas 300 euros comme en Lozère ou dans le Centre. C'est pour ça qu'on fait très attention à qui on fait venir.

Justement, comment êtes-vous organisés pour recruter sans cellule de recrutement professionnelle ?

Nos deux coachs travaillent énormément à la vidéo et vont voir beaucoup de matchs. On a aussi un directeur sportif à plein temps et une petite cellule composée d'anciens joueurs du club. On les envoie observer des matchs à Mâcon, Bourg ou Thonon avec des fiches précises sur un joueur ciblé. On travaille aussi avec quelques agents qu'on connaît bien, même si ce n'est pas notre tasse de thé parce qu'un agent ne te dira jamais que son joueur est mauvais.

Avant de recruter un joueur, il est visionné trois ou quatre fois, en vidéo et en physique. Ensuite, le directeur sportif l'appelle pour sonder sa mentalité, et celle de sa compagne ou de sa famille, car c'est très important. On leur explique le coût de la vie ici. Niveau infrastructures, on est équipés avec salle de muscu et bains froids, mais on n'a pas des salaires de 4 000 euros comme dans le Midi. On ne veut pas qu'un gars arrive et dise qu'il ne peut pas vivre ici. Après, on se voit, on discute beaucoup avec Luc Chabert, le directeur sportif et les coachs. On marche au feeling, et en discutant avec la maman ou la copine, on voit vite si on ne se trompe pas sur la mentalité. En moyenne, entre la visio, les appels et les repas tous ensemble, on se voit plusieurs fois avant de signer. On prépare déjà la saison prochaine, en regardant plutôt vers le nord que vers le sud, car un joueur qui habite à Nîmes ou Hyères au bord de la mer avec un bon salaire est très dur à faire venir en Haute-Savoie.

Vous faites aussi monter des jeunes de votre réserve, j'imagine ?

Oui, mais on part de loin. Quand on a lancé la fusion chez les jeunes en 2016, le niveau n'était pas très élevé. Aujourd'hui, toutes nos équipes de U15, U17 et U18 évoluent en Régional 1. Notre équipe réserve, qui joue aussi en R1, est composée à 60 % de jeunes du club. Ils ne sont pas encore prêts pour briguer une place de titulaire en équipe première, mais chaque année, le coach en intègre quelques-uns. Cette saison, trois jeunes du club font tous les entraînements avec le groupe, et un ou deux sont vraiment prometteurs parce qu'ils haussent leur niveau.

C'est très dur d'atteindre le niveau national quand des gamins n'ont pas eu une grosse formation dès l'âge de 7 ou 8 ans. Maintenant, on commence à récolter les fruits de notre travail : ceux qu'on a pris à 7 ou 8 ans en ont 13 ou 14 aujourd'hui, et on voit qu'il y a un vrai niveau. Pour nous, faire monter des jeunes du club, c'est aussi l'avantage de ne pas avoir à leur chercher un appartement ! Mais en attendant, on recrute surtout des joueurs qui ont un bon niveau en National 2 et qui ont faim, plutôt que d'aller chercher des gars de Ligue 2 pour qui venir jouer chez nous serait vécu comme un échec.

Tu disais que les joueurs fonctionnent comme des pros. Ils vivent tous du football aujourd'hui ?

Statutairement on est amateurs, mais dans le fonctionnement, on est comme des pros. À part deux ou trois jeunes en service civique qui montent dans le groupe, tous les joueurs vivent de ça. Ils s'entraînent une à deux fois par jour. Comme les matchs ont souvent lieu le vendredi soir et qu'il faut partir dès le jeudi midi pour aller à Châteauroux ou ailleurs, il est impossible qu'ils travaillent à côté.

Sans entrer dans les détails indiscrets, quel est l'ordre d'idée des rémunérations ?

Je ne vais pas donner de chiffres précis. On fait des contrats d'un an, et on a deux ou trois joueurs qui sortent un peu du lot. Mais globalement, nos salaires sont 30 à 40 % moins élevés que dans les clubs du Midi. Ils en vivent, mais ce ne sont pas des salaires de cadres. Après, il n'y a pas que l'argent : les joueurs connaissent les conditions et le cadre de vie avant de signer. S'ils acceptent, ils savent où ils mettent les pieds et ça se passe très bien.

Un mot sur le vestiaire et le staff : vous avez notamment Cyril Martin-Pichon, formé à l'ASSE et qui a beaucoup joué dans les équipes de jeunes et même en réserve, qui est un cadre chez vous, et un staff très fidèle au club.

Oui, Cyril Martin-Pichon a quitté le système pro parce qu'il n'y adhérait plus tellement. Il pense au collectif et au plaisir avant tout. Ça fait trois ans qu'il est chez nous, c'est un excellent joueur avec une mentalité irréprochable, un vrai cadre.

Pour le staff, notre entraîneur Cédric Rullier est passé par Annecy, Chambéry et Lyon. Ça fait trois ou quatre ans qu'il est là. Il est diététicien à l'hôpital de Chambéry et, depuis l'an dernier, il a mis son métier entre parenthèses pour se consacrer uniquement au club. Tout le reste du staff est issu du club : son adjoint Damien Guerrier, notre jeune entraîneur des gardiens qu'on a formé, et nos deux préparateurs physiques. Ce ne sont pas des gens arrivés sur le tard, on les connaît depuis dix ans, depuis tout petits. Ils sont rémunérés par le club, sont exigeants — on améliore nos équipements chaque année — mais ils sont surtout dévoués à 100 %.

J'ai été impressionné par les statistiques de Rumilly cette année : vous avez une puissance de feu phénoménale en attaque ! Le nombre de buts que vous marquez est impressionnant.

C'est bizarre parce qu'en début de saison, on se disait justement qu'on était un peu courts en attaque ! Et finalement, on marque beaucoup de buts, je crois qu'on a la meilleure attaque de toutes les poules confondues. C'est grâce à notre force mentale et au coach qui tient bien son groupe. Les joueurs savent que si ça ne va pas, ils peuvent partir, mais à côté de ça, ils ont un cadre hyper convivial avec les bénévoles qui sont aux petits soins pour eux.

Il y a des joueurs qui se révèlent, comme Fortier qui avait dû marquer trois buts l'an dernier et qui est dans tous les bons coups cette saison. Attackers in Binance Breach Targeted Known Weakness, Missing Controls

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

News, news analysis, and commentary on the latest trends in cybersecurity technology.

Blockchain complexities, such as attacker-deployed sweepers, hobble security controls designed to limit the damage of an exchange compromise.

On Oct. 29, cyber thieves transferred around $6.34 billion in various cryptocurrencies from at least 11 wallets owned by exchange Binance to attacker-controlled addresses. Binance seemingly had not put in place security controls to quickly detect malicious authorizations and disable them through a pre-tested kill switch, experts say — which should put other crypto firms on notice.

While Binance's internal security systems issued an alert in minutes, the attackers exploited a feature of Ethereum smart accounts — a relatively new type of account abstraction introduced to Ethereum with the EIP-7702 proposal in 2025 — to essentially install malicious code, known as a "sweeper." The sweeper code transferred all the funds held in a primary treasury wallet at Binance — consisting of more than five dozen cryptocurrencies totaling approximately $2.5 billion — to the attacker's address created minutes before.

Related:Getting Past the Post-Quantum Cryptography Discovery Phase

In the end, the wallet was left with a single ETH coin, according to an analysis by blockchain security firm BlockAid.

Large transactions from other wallets to the same address, initiated by the attackers, triggered internal alerts at Binance. The company took countermeasures, but the smart-account authorization continued to function for about four hours, siphoning additional funds.

"An EIP-7702 authorization [is] a standing delegation," says Ido Ben-Natan, co-founder and CEO of BlockAid. "Once it is on-chain, attacker-controlled code can move that hot wallet's funds, and anything later deposited to it, until the same wallet ... [revokes the authorization]. It carries no amount and no destination, so withdrawal controls never see it."

Despite a sweeper function enabled through EIP-7702 authorization being a known threat, Binance noted in a Nov. 3 post-mortem report that the authorization hobbled the companies efforts to stop transactions from flowing out onto the blockchain.

"Once established, these authorizations enabled the attackers to continue moving assets independently of Binance's normal withdrawal infrastructure," the company stated in a post-mortem report. "Binance needed additional transactions to revoke these on-chain authorizations ... suspending withdrawals and shutting down hot wallet services did not automatically invalidate existing on-chain authorizations."

Developers were the initial weak point, enabling the EIP-7702 authorization attack to occur. One developer downloaded a compromised version of Homebrew, an open source package management tool for Macs, while a second developer fell for a fake job interview, during which they were given a software "assignment" that compromised his system, according to Binance's post-mortem.

Related:Investors Dish on New Startups & What They'd Back Next

In the end, the attackers used their compromise of the two developers' systems — as well as a third developer's system — to gain signing privileges, creating 50 pre-signed authorizations to flood the targeted wallet, known among blockchain analysts as "Binance 14," at the right time.

The same targeting of developers happened during the ByBit Ethereum heist on Feb. 21, 2025, says Avi Sambira, vice president of North America at incident-response firm Sygnia. In that case, cyber thieves compromised the Mac of a Safe{Wallet} developer nearly three weeks before the actual theft, according to Sygnia's analysis of that incident. Safe{Wallet} is used by Ethereum and other decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs) to enforce multisignature security.

"[A]n AWS token was used and attackers operated inside Safe{Wallet}'s cloud environment for roughly two weeks before modifying the signing interface," Sambira says. "Binance's disclosure appears consistent with the same model, with initial reporting indicating endpoint and internal-tool compromise preceded credential, token, and[/or] container access to wallet signing."

Related:AI Agents as Insider Threats: Inside Microsoft's Bet on a Unified Security Stack

Like that incident, the FBI placed blame directly on North Korean cybercriminal operators, releasing a list of more than 600 Ethereum addresses, 19 Bitcoin addresses, and 1 SOL address linked to TraderTraitor, a moniker for the group behind the attacks.

Exchanges and crypto-finance firms need to treat their developers, vendors, and suppliers as critical elements of their infrastructure. Attackers are increasingly looking to compromise developer identities, repositories, CI/CD pipelines, cloud control planes, and third-party integrations, Sambira says. Those assets only sit one or two trust boundaries away from withdrawal authority.

"TraderTraitor's evolution is less about abandoning old techniques than combining them into a more sophisticated trust attack," he says. "Fake recruiters and malicious coding assignments establish the foothold, [while] stolen sessions, cloud credentials, and trusted interfaces turn that foothold into transaction control."

When the cyberattackers used their pre-generated authorizations, they sent 50 at the same time, because every transaction needs a valid nonce — a sequential counter — so the attackers used a window of 50 authorizations and needed only a single one to match.

The nonce spray to install a malicious sweeper is a new, but known, threat. In a paper presented at the USENIX Security conference in August 2026, researchers from Hong Kong University of Science and Technology and two other Chinese schools revealed that at least 924 malicious smart contracts had been enabled through the EIP-7702 mechanism, leading to at least $2.3 million in losses.

In its own research, BlockAid saw at least $1.1 million in losses across four instances, and predicted that the first 10-figure attack would arrive before the end of 2026. They were off by a factor of 100.

The attacks can be stopped — or at least blunted — by a combination of controls. Any company processing cryptocurrency transactions in real time should use simulations to detect malicious rules, enforce policies on the allowed size of transactions and their destinations, issue alerts based on detecting a burst of large transactions, and deploy a fast kill switch that has been pre-built to pause signing and stop wallet refills, says BlockAid's Ben-Natan.

Binance seemingly had none — or only some — of those controls in place. In the end, it took nearly four hours — the attack started at 20:05 UTC and the last transaction was recorded at 23:56 UTC — for the company to stop transactions from being recorded on the blockchain, subsequently freezing and recovering about $2.85 billion in attempted stolen assets. The company did not respond to requests for comment.

Binance highlights the importance of having good monitoring and the ability to detect anomalous transactions.

The difficulty in responding to the attacks underscores the problems that cryptocurrency exchanges and infrastructure firms face to combat theft and fraud. In many ways, Binance had created an overlapping set of controls to detect attacks and block transfers in near real time. However, the state-sponsored attackers found a way to bypass those controls through the EIP-7702 sweeper.

The pressure on cryptocurrency firms will only rise, because attackers can exfiltrate the funds quickly, says Ari Redbord, global head of policy at TRM Labs and a former federal prosecutor and US Treasury official.

"For years we've seen hacks against large financial institutions, tech companies and even government agencies — the difference is that in crypto you can steal funds in real time," he says. "There's no clawback window, and the money is gone in minutes."

Crypto-finance firms need to have visibility across four domains: identity and authority governance, infrastructure and execution integrity, custody and transaction authorization, and on- and off-chain detection, says Sygnia's Sambira. Companies that only monitor transactions on the blockchain are seeing changes too late, he adds.

In addition, crypto firms should rigorously track all identities and systems that can initiate, approve, modify or execute transactions, and be ready to suspend those accounts' privileges, he says.

"Controls with the greatest containment value involve the pre-authorized ability to revoke identities and tokens, pause transfers, isolate wallets and rebuild affected infrastructure within minutes," Sambira says. "Those capabilities must be tested before a crisis hits and retested regularly."

Testing and practicing these controls are critical, because companies need to know that they will work in real-time, says TRM Labs' Redbord.

"The actionable part for any company touching crypto is that these patterns are recognizable — if you watch in real time, you can act in the first moments, when it matters most," he says. "This is all about speed."

Monitoring transactions can detect suspicious activity quite quickly and, in the case of the cryptocurrency thefts targeting Binance and ByBit, can stop attacks before too much value is lost. The keys to adequate responses are speed and relationships to the various stakeholders, says Redbord.

"Issuers like Tether and Circle can freeze the addresses holding stolen tokens, and then burn those tokens and reissue the same amount to the rightful owner," he says. "The funds don't have to be chased down. They can be made whole on-chain."

Nearly all issuers, exchanges, blockchain intelligence firms and many global law enforcement agencies are part of the Beacon Network, created by TRM Labs and other industry players, for early detection of and intelligence sharing about cryptocurrency incidents. In addition, cryptocurrency-reliant businesses should ensure that any exchange with which they do business has withdrawal limits and allowlists, isolates their signing systems on computers reserved for that purpose, and constantly trains their engineers against social engineering, Redbord says.

Organizations should also reduce measures of time to detect, time to contain, and time to freeze to seconds. The approval of major transactions should require multiple signatures from systems that are only used for that purpose, and businesses need to have a response plan that keeps them operational while they recover.

Binance has taken some of these steps, including identifying the affected systems, cleaning or replacing them, and replacing wallets, it stated in its initial incident report. They've also completed "enhanced" security scans across employee devices, rotated credentials, and deployed enhanced AI-based behavioral anomaly detection, the company stated.

"There are a number of security measures we have implemented and are implementing that will remain private to prevent malicious actors from compromising our security," the company stated.

How Agent Anomaly Detection scores on benchmarks we didn't write

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AI agents now read email, book travel and move money for the people who use them. Every tool call also pulls in text that someone else wrote, and whoever controls that text can try to steer the agent. OWASP calls this agent goal hijack and ranks it first in its Top 10 for Agentic Applications (2026).

In September we announced Agent Anomaly Detection in Private Preview on Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform. It reads your agents' traces and logs asynchronously, without adding latency, and flags sessions where an agent acts outside its intended boundaries. We tested this feature against benchmarks for this top risk of agent goal hijack.

Security tools tend to look good on tests their own team wrote, so we ran five non-Google-written, public agent-security benchmarks through Agent Anomaly Detection without changing anything. In four of them, the harmful instruction hides in data that a tool returns. In the fifth, the user asks for harm directly.

The model itself didn't follow about half of the harmful instructions it read. It refused three in four direct requests for harm, and ignored or failed at about 30% of the instructions hidden in tool output. Of the ones that were executed, Agent Anomaly Detection flagged 87% for post-execution review. It raised false alarms on 2% to 16% of benign sessions, depending on the tools the agent uses. Poisoned memory is the clear gap: when instructions planted in the agent’s long-term memory made it act, Agent Anomaly Detection caught 4 of 18.

We ran each benchmark case through an agent built with the Agent Development Kit (ADK) using gemini-2.5-flash, on the benchmark's simulated apps. The agent sent its traces and logs to Agent Anomaly Detection, as a customer's agent would. Then the benchmark's own Python check read the final state of the apps, such as whether money left the account, and labeled the session. Deterministic code decides the label, not a model, so an injected instruction can't mislead the grader, and Agent Anomaly Detection cannot cheat the benchmark. Each agent first ran at least 300 benign sessions, because Agent Anomaly Detection learns how each agent usually behaves.

We also recorded whether a tool returned the harmful instruction to the agent. In many cases, the agent never opens the email or file that holds it. So each harmful session ends one of three ways:

In AgentHarm, the user sends the request, so the agent always reads it.

The five benchmarks are AgentDojo (NeurIPS 2024), InjecAgent (ACL 2024 Findings), Agent Security Bench (ASB, ICLR 2025), AgentDyn (2026) and AgentHarm (ICLR 2025). ASB plants the instruction in the agent's long-term memory. In all, Agent Anomaly Detection scored 1,774 sessions.

Agent Anomaly Detection flagged 87% of the harmful instructions that the agent carried out, and raised a few false alarms on ordinary office work. It's strongest on goal hijack and tool misuse, and weakest on poisoned memory.

Each finding carries a threat category, and four of Agent Anomaly Detection’s five categories align with the OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications. We graded each OWASP category on what this study showed, not on what Agent Anomaly Detection is designed to catch.

Public benchmarks concentrate on goal hijack and tool misuse, so our evidence is strongest there. We did not find public benchmarks with labeled agent traces for cascading failures or for identity and privilege abuse. Shared benchmarks for those categories would help the whole field.

By benchmark, Agent Anomaly Detection flagged 85% of the carried-out harmful instructions on AgentDojo, 87% on InjecAgent, 99% on AgentDyn and 81% on AgentHarm. Over all harmful sessions, including the ones that the agent never read or resisted, it flagged 45% to 62%. In the 141 sessions where the agent never read the instruction, nothing happened, and it flagged just 1.

Results held across the apps. In 8 of the 11 apps, Agent Anomaly Detection flagged at least 95% of the carried-out harmful instructions. The lowest rate was in AgentDojo's Slack app, at 63%. Many of those instructions ask for one small action, such as a page visit, which looks like normal browsing.

Agent Anomaly Detection also flags many requests that the agent refuses. In AgentHarm, the agent refused most requests and carried out 108 of 412, and Agent Anomaly Detection still flagged 55% of the refused sessions, because the request sits in the user's own message, which it reads. When the agent didn't follow an instruction hidden in tool output, it flagged only 11% to 25%, because the instruction sits deep inside a tool result.

False alarms were rare on ordinary office work: on the three injection benchmarks, whose benign sessions are office tasks, Agent Anomaly Detection flagged 2% to 4% of benign sessions. On AgentHarm it flagged 16%. Those benign tasks are twins of the harmful ones, so they post to social media, run terminal commands and download files. High-impact tools in normal use look more like misuse.

Agent Anomaly Detection did less well in five areas. It flagged only 11% to 25% of the instructions that the agent resisted, because a resisted instruction changes little in how the agent behaves. It missed more small actions, like single page visits, because they look like normal work. And it raised more false alarms for agents whose everyday work uses high-impact tools. If your agent is one of them, expect more findings at first, and triage them by severity and threat category.

The other two areas are new agents and poisoned memory. Some Agent Anomaly Detection methods need a few hundred sessions of history: on AgentDyn, it flagged 33% of carried-out harmful instructions when each agent had about 220 benign sessions, and 99% with 360. And it caught only 4 of the 18 sessions where poisoned memory made the agent act.

Protect your agents with defense in depth. Use controls that stop harmful actions before they run, and Agent Anomaly Detection to find what gets through.

We used one agent model and simulated apps, and the benchmarks' benign tasks are likely simpler than your production traffic, so your false-alarm rate can differ. Agent Anomaly Detection returned no result for about 6% of the sessions we sent; counting those as misses lowers the overall rates by 1 to 2 points.

Catch Go bugs that fuzzing misses with Gemini and a constraint solver

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I help lead Google's agent skills program, currently with 175 agent skills that supplement community collections like go-skills. To keep that many skill files consistent, I wrote skill-lint, a standalone Go CLI that checks SKILL.md files and applies LLM-generated line edits before they ship. Even with skill-lint's own unit and fuzz test suite, a small slice or line-number mistake inside its Markdown parsers or edit applier can sit dormant until an unusual file or edit range triggers a runtime panic in the linter.

In Go, every slice or index operation that can panic at runtime compiles down to an explicit bounds-check trap in machine code, after the compiler's optimizer strips out the checks it can already rule out. To hunt for crashes, I compiled key functions from my code to see which panic traps survived optimization, and Gemini 4 Argon translated the path to each trap into rules for the Z3 constraint solver. An LLM-written solver model isn't a sound proof system, but it gives the agent an independent way to reason about control flow and synthesize concrete Go reproducers, and every crash it finds is real because go test runs the offending bytes directly. That workflow caught all three bugs I planted in test parsers and turned up three latent bugs in skill-lint.

In this post, I'll walk through how the Gemini and Z3 pipeline works, why go test -fuzz missed two of those crashes (and the one-line change that fixes it), and where this approach fits in a day-to-day Go workflow.

A recent post from Anthropic showed how formal verification catches latent bugs in C and C++ by having frontier AI models write machine-checked Rocq proofs across OSS-Fuzz libraries. Instead of sampling random inputs like a fuzzer, a formal tool works backward from each failure condition to solve for the exact input that triggers it. Full theorem proving is still too heavy for most day-to-day Go projects, so I wanted to see how much of that bug-hunting power an agent can get from an LLM and a constraint solver in about a minute per function.

In C and C++, reading or writing past the end of a buffer corrupts memory. Go catches out-of-bounds access at runtime instead, compiling every slice or index operation it can't prove safe into an explicit branch to runtime.panicBounds that panics, crashing the process unless recovered.

Before generating machine code, Go's SSA prove pass already eliminates most of those checks by tracking slice lengths and loop variables. That optimization pass leaves behind only the runtime.panicBounds traps the compiler couldn't rule out on its own, giving the solver a short list of candidate crash sites to target.

I compiled each target with GOOS=linux GOARCH=riscv64 go build -gcflags="-S -d=ssa/check_bce/debug=1" (-S prints the assembly listing and ssa/check_bce/debug=1 logs every surviving bounds check) and passed both the Go source and the disassembly to the model. Looking at Go source alone, you can't see which bounds checks the compiler already removed or where a register gets truncated to 8 bits; in the disassembly, both show up. I targeted RISC-V because it's the instruction set Anthropic's post targets for its Rocq proofs, and Go cross-compiles to it out of the box.

From there, Argon read the Go source and disassembly and returned a Z3 script modeling the path to each surviving trap, along with a patched function whenever it flagged a bug. Z3 then checked whether those constraints could be satisfied, and whenever it found a crashing input, go test replayed those bytes against the real function. Using an LLM saved me from translating assembly into solver constraints by hand. Argon's translation dominated the runtime, at about a minute per function, while Z3 itself finished in milliseconds.

Anthropic's approach relies on interactive theorem proving, where experts model machine semantics and Rocq checks LLM-generated proofs against those rules. In contrast, my workflow uses a constraint solver based on satisfiability modulo theories (SMT), relying on Argon to translate assembly directly into Z3 constraints. While a constraint solver doesn't provide a complete correctness proof without a semantic model, it is highly effective for finding bugs. Z3's check-sat command returns sat when input constraints can be satisfied and unsat when they cannot.

A sat answer ("definitely buggy") comes with concrete bytes aimed at the trap, and go test runs them against the real function. Once go test panics, you have a concrete bug reproducer regardless of whether Argon's model was sound. Like a Bloom filter for bugs, an unsat answer only means "maybe correct." Trading away a correctness proof is what makes the approach so practical: you get most of the bug-finding value of formal methods for a tiny fraction of the effort.

Anthropic's setup proved no-crash properties on whole binaries under a 64 MB budget; I checked individual functions on fresh 64-byte slices where cap(buf) == len(buf), so any slice past len(buf) panics. (If you want the full-binary Rocq setup for Go, theorem-labs/oss-verify includes a Go 1.27 runtime overlay that makes the runtime single-threaded and deterministic.)

For the fuzzing baseline, I ran unseeded go test -fuzz on Go 1.27.3 against each planted bug: one 3-second run and three 60-second runs, each with a fresh fuzz cache so a short time budget wouldn't explain a miss.

I started with three safe parsers to make sure the pipeline didn't flag clean code. The compiler's prove pass removed every bounds check in a TOML key parser on its own, and Z3 returned unsat on the remaining traps in an HTTP header scanner and a varint decoder.

Next, I pointed the pipeline at skill-lint itself. A whole Go CLI is too large to hand to a solver directly. Even a 10-line parser links 1,559 symbols from the Go runtime, and the 28 MB skill-lint binary has 32,321 symbols in total. Following Functional Core, Imperative Shell, I left CLI flags, network calls, and file I/O to go test -fuzz and go test -race, and ran the solver only on pure functions and slice helpers extracted from file-reading code.

Of the five functions I tested, two scanners came back unsat (including a CRLF line splitter with six surviving bounds checks), and the other three had bugs. The sharpest was in ApplyReplacementChunks, which validates LLM-generated {StartLine, EndLine} edits against the initial line count and sorts them bottom-to-top so earlier edits don't shift later line numbers:

Z3 immediately returned sat because sorting by StartLine doesn't prevent two chunks from overlapping. In the extracted helper, which checks bounds once up front, replacing lines 3 and 4 of a 4-line file with one line shrinks lines to 3 elements, so a second chunk spanning lines 2 through 4 evaluates lines[4:] and panics with slice bounds out of range [4:3]. The other two bugs were similar accounting errors. In the local fixer, target line numbers were checked once per violation before the inner chunk loop applied deletions that shrank the slice (a latent panic if a fixer ever returns a second chunk after a deletion, though DeleteLineFix only returns one today). In the prompt packer, a truncation check reserved a flat 100 bytes for a ~70-byte wrapper plus file path, overshooting the 2 MB context budget in the shipped code when a path exceeds about 30 bytes and panicking in the extracted helper.

When you check a function modularly like this, the target has to include its own entry-point guards (like the bounds check and sort in ApplyReplacementChunks). If an internal helper assumes preconditions that are only enforced by its caller or stated in a doc comment, Z3 will happily invent inputs that violate them. Because each extracted helper included its entry guards, false positives weren't an issue: across all six buggy functions in my tests, the first sat assignment Z3 returned triggered a real panic in go test without needing to filter out non-crashing candidates or loop back into the solver.

Once I pulled these helpers away from file I/O, a 3-second fuzz run caught all three bugs too. To make sure Argon wasn't leaning on source comments in my test files, I also reran the skill-lint targets with all comments stripped; Argon and Z3 still caught all three bugs and returned unsat on the two scanners and all three patches.

Staying entirely inside the target language makes a whole class of bugs hard for an agent to see. When an LLM reviews Go source directly, a line like if int(total)+4 > len(buf) looks like a textbook bounds check, and when it runs go test -fuzz, target-language runtime details like spare slice capacity or multi-step precondition checks can hide the crash indefinitely. Asking the agent to translate Go and assembly into a constraint solver forces a change of representation that strips away surface syntax and makes every bit width and slice bound explicit, while go test turns any solver hit back into a concrete Go reproducer.

For most Go code, fuzzing pure helpers with slice capacity clipped to length will catch bounds bugs in seconds. A solver is worth the extra minute on the traps that survive both the compiler's range prover and a quick fuzz run, especially in parsers and editors that compute offsets from multi-field headers or line ranges.

Single-threaded solver models don't see data races or deadlocks, so concurrent code still belongs with the race detector, deterministic concurrency testing, and Go 1.27's goroutine leak profiles.

A Practical Workflow for Writing Better AI Image Prompts

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The way you write a prompt will impact how the generator interprets it. A detailed description of the prompt gives the generator a better understanding of the Subject, location, Composition, Lighting, and Style of the visual. The good news is that creating a prompt is a repetitive task that can be approached as a structured workflow. Rather than guess which words are likely to generate successful images, you can build a prompt step-by-step, test it, review the results, and improve the details. Here is a practical process to follow.

First, you need to determine the Image you really want to create. Many poor prompts start out as vague ideas and not concrete visual objectives. Example: "create an Image of a Coffee Shop." This identifies an object (a coffee shop) but leaves nearly every other aspect undefined. The resulting Image could be a contemporary coffee house, a coffee cart on the side of a busy street, a Minimalist coffee shop interior, or a bustling restaurant. To help you avoid this type of generic prompt before you write it, ask yourself some general questions. What is the primary Subject of my Image? What is the Subject doing in my Image? In what location does my scene take place? What would I want the viewer to see first in my Image? Do I prefer a specific visual Style? Do not feel obligated to write an entire paragraph of detail here; just jot down a few key thoughts. Example: Subject: barista preparing coffee Location: small neighborhood cafe Action: pouring steamed milk into a cup Lighting: soft morning light Visual treatment: realistic Editorial photography Atmosphere: calm and relaxing These initial notes serve as the basis for developing a more effective prompt.

In order to develop a standardization to your prompts, consider developing a template for organizing your prompts. A possible way to organize your prompts is based upon the following categories: Subject + Action + location + Composition + Lighting + visual Style + important features Each category answers a distinct question. Subject: what is the principal feature? Action: what is happening? Location: where is it occurring? Composition: how is the scene organized? Lighting: what kind of light characterizes the picture? Visual Style: what kind of aesthetic should the generator strive for? Features: which features such as texture, color, background elements etc. Are critical? Using this format let’s break down our previous example. Initial basic prompt: “barista making coffee.” Expanded prompt: “barista pours steaming milk into a ceramic coffee cup inside of a small neighborhood coffee shop. Wooden countertop. The espresso machine is visible in the ground. Soft morning window light. Close up. Editorial photography. Warming natural colors.” While both prompts contain the same Subject and location, the expanded version of the prompt provides additional detail about the Setting and the Composition and therefore provides greater clarity to the Image generator regarding the desired outcome.

Adding too many extraneous details can overwhelm both you and the generator. Too many extraneous details can cause conflicting directions toward what aspects of the Image are visually emphasized. Think about it this way: imagine developing a scene description including all of the following: Minimally styled Very highly detailed Darkly atmospheric Brightly lit Cinematic Flat illustrated Photographically realistic Some of these stylistic directions may compete depending on what you intend for your final Image. Determine which stylistic characteristics are essential to your final Image. If you plan to create an Image resembling a product photograph, then you may want to emphasize product styling (product, materials, background, Lighting and framing). If you are working on an environmental scene then you may want to focus more on location/Setting, atmosphere, spatial relationships, size, placement and proximity of various elements within your Image. There is value in separating essential data from non-essential decorative data. Data necessary to convey what your Image intends to communicate constitute the essential Details of your Image. Optional Details add character or personality to your Image, but they should not detract from your fundamental message.

Your description can influence how the Image appears. For instance, when describing a scene as “cinematic photography” you are suggesting a very different visual path than if you described it as “clean commercial product photography,” or “Editorial hand drawn illustration.” It is helpful to view vocabulary choices through the lens of intended visual outcomes versus merely accumulating adjectives that sound impressive. Consider these examples: Documentary photography: natural/ observational. Editorial photography: visually composed and suitable for publication. Commercial product photography: focuses primarily on the item/object being photographed, along with presentation and Details. Illustrative minimilism: reduced forms and reduced visual complexity. Cinematic imagery: dramatic presentation of atmosphere, framing and light. They are general creative directions — not necessarily specific technical parameters. Different generators may interpret identical Style language in their own way. The best way to learn is to experiment with a description and examine your results.

One of the most valuable practices in developing prompts is controlled testing. Suppose you have created an initial generated Image that correctly represents your Subject and Setting but has overly harsh Lighting. You could revise your entire description and have no means to verify whether any changes were made to produce better results. A better strategy would be to retain your original description and alter only your Lighting direction. Version a: “a ceramic artist shaping a clay bowl in a small pottery studio with handmade ceramics on shelves behind him/her and bright overhead light illuminating his/her work. Documentary photographed for realism.” Version b: “a ceramic artist shaping a clay bowl in a small pottery studio with handmade ceramics on shelves behind him/her and soft morning sunlight coming through windows illuminating his/her work. Documentary photographed for realism.” Version b differs from version a only in respect to Lighting. Use this methodology to change: Camera angle/framing Background element(s) Color palette Visual Style Secondary object(s) This allows you to perform experiments in a manner that yields meaningful results. You Start to realize which descriptions affect those results that are relevant to you.

Different ai-generated Image tools can interpret the exact same prompt differently. A description that successfully generates a viable Composition in one tool may emphasize different visual aspects in another. One Result may focus on the Subject while another focuses on the surrounding environment/atmosphere. Therefore, it may be beneficial to evaluate multiple tools utilizing the same prompt. Identify a simple visual concept and utilize identical wordings across all tools you wish to investigate. Then analyze all of the produced images. Review: Are there any differences in how clearly defined the Subject is? Is there similarity/difference in regards to how closely related is the Composition to what was requested? Were the Lighting and color interpretations similar/different? Did the most important elements appear in all versions? Does the output match your original intention? Don’t judge one tool solely based on one output. Generated images can exhibit variability and one Result may not be representative of what that tool can consistently produce. The goal behind evaluating multiple tools is so you can understand how each tool manages your creative needs.

Even though an Image failed to meet expectations you can still derive useful insight from it. Identify what did not go as planned. Was the Subject poorly identified? Was there too much concentration placed on background elements? Was there too much width in the composition? Were there stylistic differences between what you intended and what was generated? Be able to articulate what specifically went wrong in detail. Example: “The correct pottery studio is shown; however the bowl was too small in frame.” From this determination we can infer that we need to make adjustments to get closer to our intended vision. We can adjust this description by focusing on a more detailed level: “Close-up Composition featuring the ceramic bowl as the main subject.” Or, “the scene seems artificially rendered.” We could address this issue by providing more specifics regarding the environment; specifying natural lighting; removing unnecessary visual elements; etc. The most important thing is to convert dissatisfaction into an actionable modification.

As soon as you find a prompt that produces usable results, save it. A library of prompts can be as simple as a document or even an excel sheet that includes: Category Description field name Prompt description Tool used Main Subject description (or concept) Style intended for the tool Desired Result type (i.e., product Image, lifestyle scene etc.) What worked well Revision (or next step) Over time this becomes a resource for future projects. You may note that certain approaches tend to work better for product images while others are better suited to lifestyle scenes, environments or illustrations.

Starting from a blank page every time can make image generation feel unnecessarily difficult. Prompt guides and examples provide a way to explore different structures, visual approaches, and descriptive techniques. They can also help beginners understand how a short concept can be developed into a more complete image description. NeutronField.com focuses on AI image prompts and guides for the image generators people use, with an emphasis on what different prompts and tools contribute to the finished image. Exploring examples can help you identify useful language, compare creative directions, and develop your own approach. The important step is to adapt references to your actual image rather than copying a prompt without considering its purpose.

A productive image-generation workflow does not end when the first prompt is submitted. It follows a simple cycle: Define the intended image. Write a structured prompt. Generate an initial result. Evaluate the composition and visual details. Identify the biggest mismatch. Change one or two relevant instructions. Generate again and compare. This process helps reduce random experimentation. It also makes your decisions easier to explain and repeat. You do not need a complicated prompt for every image. Sometimes a short description is enough. Other projects require more careful direction, particularly when the composition, materials, lighting, or atmosphere are important. The skill is knowing when to add detail and when to keep the instruction simple.

Writing effective AI image prompts is a practical creative skill. It combines clear visual thinking with experimentation and careful observation. Start with the subject, describe the scene, choose the visual direction, and add only the details that support the intended result. Then test the prompt, study the output, and make focused improvements. As you work with different generators, you will develop a better understanding of how they interpret descriptions and which prompting approaches suit your projects. The goal is not to find one perfect formula. It is to build a repeatable process that helps turn an idea into a more intentional image.

That's not a repo bug. .git/shallow lists two commits, not one. Each is a grafted boundary — a commit git treats as having no parents, even though both clearly had real history before them. One is old; the other, 4e493ca, is a commit from earlier today's publishing run ("docs: log second dev.to run — generate_commit_message injection test"). A single shallow clone normally records exactly one boundary, at whatever commit was HEAD when --depth was applied. Two separate grafted roots, one of them from hours ago, means this isn't a one-time clone setting — something in this environment's provisioning has drawn a new "history starts here" line more than once across this repo's life, and the oldest commit any git log --all in this container can see is from 2026-08-20.

docs/project_notes/issues.md, meanwhile, narrates events back to 2026-06-21 — the original nodejs/undici contribution, the ANTHROPIC_API_KEY/OAuth switch, the GitHub token scope issues. Almost two months of this pipeline's own documented incident history sits entirely outside what git log can see from here. Not deleted — the markdown entries are still there, committed, readable — just unreachable from the one command this account's own articles keep citing as their source of truth.

Every "verified live via git log --all" claim this account has published is really a claim about whatever the shallow boundary happened to be at the time that specific session ran. The 2026-07-29 entry's false wasn't wrong when it was written — it was accurate for that container, on that day. It just can't be treated as a permanent property of this repository, the way "verified via git log" usually gets written in these posts (including by me). A fresh container with a deeper or shallower fetch than the one that ran a given check will get a different answer to the exact same command, pointed at the exact same repo on GitHub.

I checked whether scripts/check_key_facts.py — the one script in this repo built specifically to audit the memory system's own claims against reality — would have any way to catch this class of drift:

It doesn't, and structurally can't without a change in scope — it checks whether files mentioned in the docs exist on disk, not whether a git command a past article ran would still return the same thing today. That's a different kind of drift than anything it was built for: not "the docs mention a file that isn't there," but "the docs mention a verification result that isn't reproducible in a different container."

I didn't try to fetch the missing history — git fetch --unshallow would need the full ref history to still exist on the remote and the right credentials to pull it, and "deepen this container's clone" doesn't fix the underlying issue, since the next container gets whatever its own provisioning step gives it, independent of what I do in this one. What I did instead: stopped treating "verified via git log --all" as a self-certifying phrase in my own future write-ups. The honest version of that claim is narrower — verified against whatever history this specific container's clone could see, which may not be the repository's full history — and it's worth stating that scope limit explicitly, the same way a flaky-benchmark or a small-sample measurement would get one. A command returning cleanly isn't the same as the command having seen everything; git log --all will exit 0 and print a confident list of commits whether it's looking at 50 commits or 5000, with nothing in its output distinguishing "this is everything" from "this is everything I can currently see."

If your own team's audit trail depends on a shell command's output and that command runs inside a fresh container or CI checkout each time, it's worth running git rev-parse --is-shallow-repository once before trusting the rest. The answer isn't guaranteed to be the same twice, and nothing will tell you it changed unless you ask.

The Keychron V6 Ultra Hybrid 8K HE (catchy name, right?) attempts to forge ‘The One Ring’ of keyboards. One keyboard to rule them all, to do and be everything for everyone. The master keyboard.

As such, it’s the latest in a very small selection of keyboards that offer dual mechanical and magnetic capability. If you want to hot-swap from magnetic back to mechanical switches, you can. But the V6 takes things further with, as I alluded to above, an altogether more holistic approach. The V6 Ultra Hybrid 8K HE also packs everything else Keychron has to throw at a keyboard — which essentially means every feature that actually exists right now.

8K polling, tons of magnetic features and customization in the Launcher app, gasket mounting for comfortable typing — all with a reasonable price tag. It also offers my holy grail feature at checkout: a magnetic and tactile switch, for elite gaming and typing without even needing to hot-swap in the first place.

So, like, is this legitimately the perfect hybrid board? The best gaming keyboard and the best mechanical keyboard all rolled into one? I think so. Keychron has done it. Find out more in my full Keychron V6 Ultra Hybrid 8K HE review.

Where to even start? Proper MX/HE hot swap, bags of gaming and productivity performance, reasonable price — this is a Goldilocks keyboard.

The V6 Ultra Hybrid 8K HE’s main feature is denoted by the “Hybrid” part of its (terribly convoluted) name. This board is dual mechanical/magnetic switch compatible, meaning you can swap between the two.

The V6 Ultra Hybrid takes any standard 3-pin or 5-pin mechanical switches. It also supports a wide range of magnetic switches with the N Pole facing downwards — that means Gateron x Wooting Jade switches, Gateron KS-20 switches, and any of Gateron’s ever-growing line of dual rail magnetic switches. Now Keychron isn’t the first to do dual MX/HE. Glorious did it a while back with the hilariously overpriced GMMK 3 HE. More recently, Lofree had a pop with the Hyzen, but with typically Lofree results: great looks and typing but rank amateurism in the software department (pivotal to a magnetic board). Keychron, in usual Keychron fashion, is the first to do a proper job of it, with the widest magnetic hot-swap scope while still keeping things affordable.

The long and short is that the V6 Ultra Hybrid has more actual hot-swap potential than any other magnetic board that I know of. Most magnetic boards claim to be hot swappable, but only support a couple of switch variants from the same specific magnetic switch line — often the variants available at checkout. In reality, that usually makes hot-swap claims severely hamstrung at best; meaningless at worst. Not so here. Again, the Lofree Hyzen also supports an impressive roster of compatible magnetic switches, but lacks the professional polish of Keychron’s boards.

The “hybrid” moniker also has another related meaning. The V6 Ultra Hybrid is a hybrid board for both work and play, offering powerful gaming performance while capable enough to handle the rigors of work and heavy typing.

The bit that excites me most are two of the magnetic switch choices available from stock. The V6 Ultra Hybrid ships with one of four Gateron Ultra Fast Lime magnetic switches, two of which are tactile. Yes, tactile magnetic switches — this is the biggest news in my opinion.

All magnetic switches up to now have been linear in operation. Sure, being able to hot-swap those is fine, but swapping out 108 switches from a full-sized keyboard is a ginormous time suck. So it’s not like you can just quickly switch from tactiles for work to magnetic for gaming. If, like me, you enjoy tactile switches but want magnetic gaming performance, you’ve so far been left out in the cold. Not anymore.

The V6 Ultra Hybrid also packs plenty of Keychron’s productivity heritage. There’s a gasket mount, for bouncy and comfortable typing; Keychron’s steeply-raked and deeply-sculpted OSA profile keycaps, which I find ideal for fast typing; and screw-in stabilizers for minimal big key wobble. As you can see, my typing speed and accuracy were both above average on the V6 Ultra Hybrid, helped by paring back the actuation to 2.6mm — my personal sweet spot and where I type-test all magnetic boards. I could (and indeed did) type on this thing all day.

Thanks to Gateron’s magnetic switches, you also get every elite gaming feature under the sun right now: adjustable actuation; rapid trigger; dynamic keystroke for multiple commands per actuation; custom SOCD resolutions for last key prioritization (A.K.A. ‘Snap Tap’). There’s 8K polling too, although on a keyboard this is tantamount to snake oil, if you ask me. All of this is customizable through Keychron’s fantastic web launcher.

I used the board for everything, from competitive gaming on “Counter-Strike 2” and “Wardogs,” through to more relaxed titles like “Total War: Rome II,” “Valheim,” and “Dune: Awakening.” It never put a foot wrong.

Admittedly, if you want a board purely for competitive FPS gaming, you’re probably better off with a plate-, tray-, or top-mounted magnetic board like the NuPhy Air60 HE, NuPhy Field75 HE, or Keychron Q16 HE 8K — these mount styles aren’t as comfortable, but move around less than a gasket mount, making them less likely to cause accidental inputs at super low <0.5mm switch actuations.

If you need an all-rounder for both work and play, though, I seriously can’t think of a better board than the V6 Ultra Hybrid.

As this is a Keychron board, you get all the expected goodies. Aside from some QC issues I’ll cover later, the board is built well, using premium materials. The case is sturdy ABS plastic with very little bend and flex — impressive given this is a long board.

The keycaps are doubleshot PBT, which will stand up well to wear and tear without going shiny, unlike ABS keycaps. PBT is also grease resistant. I did my usual test, typing on the keyboard after eating Monster Munch (a fried corn snack here in the U.K.), and the V6’s keycaps picked up a lot of grease but were easy to wipe down. Not quite as impressive as the caps on the Logitech G Pro X2 Rapid, which completely resisted the same grease altogether — like not even a single smear.

The V6 features a decent 4,000mAh battery, plus triple Bluetooth, 2.4G and wired connectivity, toggleable via a switch on the case. There’s a macOS/Windows toggle, too, but Keychron still supplies separate sets of Windows and macOS command keys rather than dual-labeling its keycaps. This is a longstanding frustration of mine. I switch between the two OS’ constantly for work. It’s great to have the toggle, but it undermines the ease if I have to switch keycaps too. A small issue, but one that’s so easily solvable by dual labelling — as proved by the Lofree Hyzen and Lofree Flow 2.

When I reviewed the original Keychron V6, which I awarded a full five stars, one of my favorite features was the price. It offered major performance for a very reasonable sum. The V6 Ultra Hybrid 8K HE is pricier. It costs $164 versus the $99 basic V6 and the $119 V6 Ultra (which has faster polling, better internal damping, nicer switches, etc — just an all-round upgraded package).

However, the V6 Ultra Hybrid packs a lot more functionality than the lower-tier V6 models thanks to the dual MX/HE switch compatibility, and way more gaming features when using HE switches. An extra $45 over the standard V6 Ultra is fair game for all that, at least in my book.

Let’s look at the hybrid MX/HE competition, too. The Lofree Hyzen costs a whopping $279 — sure it’s made from aluminum and is beautiful to behold, but the software is janky as hell. The Glorious GMMK 3 HE starts at $179, but you can easily get up to near $500 for choice productivity upgrades (and to make it look less ugly than stock). By those two yardsticks, the V6 Ultra Hybrid 8K HE is the best value-for-money hybrid board on the market, and it ain’t even close.

At the time of writing, the V6 Ultra Hybrid 8K HE is in pre-order, after its Kickstarter campaign, with estimated shipping starting December 2026.

Keychron is a major keyboard brand (arguably the dominant productivity market force) that absolutely does not need to crowdfund its boards. Besides, as someone who has tested several of Keychron’s crowdfunded boards, I can tell you they’re already developed and practically finished by the start of the campaigns (because most Keychron boards are 95% the same under the hood). Oh well. That’s just unfortunately how Keychron chooses to operate, but you can rest assured that there’s usually very little chance of a crowdfunding rug pull from this brand.

There’s very little I don’t like about the V6 Ultra Hybrid 8K HE, aside from its ridiculously long-winded name. It’s a little boring to look at, I guess, and I saw some QC teething issues, but nothing that should seriously dissuade you.

Ah, look: black plastic case, gray rounded keycaps, a tiny bit of color on the ESC key. It must be a Keychron! Yawn. I’m not saying the V6 Ultra Hybrid is bad-looking, but it’s the same old Keychron design we’ve seen for years now. And it’s getting kinda boring.

Now, as someone who has owned, tested, used, or edited the reviews of literally hundreds of keyboards, this probably affects me more than you. I see a lot of Keychrons; you’ll probably only see the one you buy.

But man, I wish Keychron would make some more exciting decks in its more affordable V- and K-series sometimes. Pretty boards with plenty of color, like the NuPhy Halo75; or handsome futuristic designs like the Lofree Hyzen and Lofree Block. Come on, Keychron, spice it up a bit.

As Keychron has grown, I’ve seen firsthand its once stellar reputation for build quality start to drop. A few years ago, every single Keychron board we tested was built like a tank. Now we’re seeing frequent issues with sticky switches or other build quality concerns. Not all the time, I must say. And Keychron is always keen to hear from us when this happens, so we always tell its representatives and receive a replacement in quick order. But it’s getting to the point now that I’m seeing a pattern I can’t ignore.

My first V6 Ultra Hybrid review unit suffered both a sticky switch (solved by adjusting debounce time) and a USB dongle port that was far too large with no magnet inside, meaning the 2.4G receiver would fall out. Keychron’s representative has assured me this was a pre-production teething issue, and issued me a full production unit where the problem has been fixed. But as I said above, similar issues are becoming too common now from this once wholly reliable brand, and it’s a problem I need to call out. Keychron, if you’re reading, please step your game back up.

I utterly adore this keyboard. I won’t be using it long-term because I prefer a 75% layout (so I‘m eagerly waiting for a V2 Ultra Hybrid 8K HE). But I absolutely love what this deck signifies.

As is so often the case in the productivity keyboard arena, it takes Keychron to turn its attention to an innovation to truly bring that feature into the mainstream and make it work. Dual MX/HE compatibility is now here, with proper wide-ranging switch support for both mechanical and magnetic. And there’s a tactile magnetic switch on offer to boot.

If you want one keyboard that excels for literally every purpose, without breaking the bank, it’s the Keychron V6 Ultra Hybrid 8K HE. End of story.

The 10 Most Ambitious Documentaries of All Time, Ranked

Yes, documentaries can be a bit formulaic, and there are conventional ones that have interviews, voiceover, and fairly mundane visuals that all serve to depict something objectively, or give the impression that things are being explored objectively, but that’s not every documentary, by any means. You can put that criticism on any genre, really, because there are generic action movies, horror films, comedies, etc.

Documentaries seem to get that criticism the most often, though. It’s not really fair, and maybe one way to combat that sentiment is by highlighting some particularly cinematic and groundbreaking ones. So, the following might go some way toward that, because these feel like some of the most ambitious documentary movies of all time, with the lot of them being interesting for different reasons and certainly unexpected compared to what you might picture when you think of “ordinary” or “normal” documentaries.

Since Apocalypse Now is a tremendously ambitious – and unusual – war movie, it’s inevitable that any behind-the-scenes documentary about its production is going to be, at the very least, also quite interesting. Yet everything that happened behind the scenes with this movie was more chaotic than you'd expect, even if you were expecting chaos, and so Hearts of Darkness: A Filmmaker’s Apocalypse is ultimately a dizzying watch.

It doesn’t mess around in showcasing the hectic production of Apocalypse Now, and it’s about as psychologically intense and even as surreal as a good chunk of the actual movie is. It might sound daunting, just because of its approach and the fact that it’s almost three hours long, but it’s honestly as artistic and mesmerizing as sports footage can get, even if you're not hugely into watching footage of sports.

You notice the three-hour runtime to some extent, sure, but Tokyo Olympiad does remain interesting throughout, with everything captured in unbelievable detail. It's far more than just a clip show, as all the camera angles are a good deal more interesting than what you'd see on a TV broadcast, with the editing playing a huge role in enhancing everything and making it all feel like a largely hypnotic viewing experience.

Historical value/context is important when it comes to Man with a Movie Camera, since the existence of this film is such that the whole documentary format might look entirely different today had this movie never been made. No hyperbole, but it's likely one of the most important and influential documentaries of all time, using inventive visuals and creative editing techniques to show how life was in the Soviet Union during the 1920s.

That really is the premise, though. Man with a Movie Camera is simple, and outside the style, there isn't really a whole to this silent film. Yet it’s the style that makes it dazzling and engaging to this day, doing some interesting enough things with camera tricks and editing to make it feel exciting and very alive, all to the point where it’s worth checking out, even if you might assume that a listless documentary that’s nearly a century old would be dull. Maybe other listless 100-year-old documentaries would be. Not Man with a Movie Camera, though.

Two filmmakers were credited with making The Five Obstructions: Lars von Trier and Jørgen Leth. The former’s the one you're more likely to have heard of, and he does feel somewhat in control for much of this documentary, but that’s largely got to do with Leth being more of the subject here, since The Five Obstructions involves Lars von Trier challenging him to remake one of his old short films five different times, and with absurd rules on each occasion.

Well, technically, the rules get more unusual with each new remake, and then those shorts Leth did make (or seemed to make) are shown in quite a bit of detail, so that’s where his directing definitely comes in. There might be more involvement on his part, as it’s hard to know how much of The Five Obstructions is genuine and how much is two Danish filmmakers playing a feature-length prank on the audience, but in any event, it’s all very engaging to watch, and certainly interesting to think about, too.

Flee is an animated documentary, which is interesting enough on its own (see also another documentary from 2008 that’ll be mentioned a little further down). It’s about a man’s life experiences, basically, including his time as a refugee and how he grappled with his sexuality while surrounded by people who weren't always accepting of it… and the animation itself is a way to protect his identity, essentially.

The whole thing ends up being rather moving and quite memorable. Rightly sometimes regarded as one of the greatest films of all time, including non-documentaries, Shoah is a mammoth documentary and one that people owe to themselves to watch at least once in their lives, though you do need to devote a ton of time to finishing it. The runtime stands out and has to be addressed, since Shoah goes for more than nine hours, using that length of time to hammer home the enormity of the event it explores.

It’s made up entirely of interviews with people recalling the Holocaust, including survivors, witnesses/bystanders, and even a few perpetrators. There’s no archival footage or much else beyond those interviews, with the approach nevertheless being used to form a sprawling and daunting narrative by stringing together all those different testimonies. Shoah is a challenging watch, but it has to be, also feeling about as essential and historically significant as war documentaries can get.

O.J.: Made in America could well be the greatest crime documentary of all time. However, it's also more than a documentary about one particularly publicized event and subsequent criminal trial, using the story of O.J. Simpson and his much-discussed trial to unpack a whole range of things, including the American dream, the country's history of racial conflict, and, more broadly, investigating American culture throughout the second half of the 20th century.

Sure, it’s seven and a half hours long, which means it has room to do a lot, but it’s still impressive how much is packed into that runtime. Also, it has to be acknowledged that it exists in a strange middle-ground between movie and miniseries, as it was screened theatrically and therefore won an Oscar for Best Documentary, but most would've watched it as a miniseries in five parts, and the Academy Awards changed the rules after O.J.: Made in America won. Oh well… point is, it deserved to win, and it’s easily one of the most monumental documentaries of the century so far.

Depending on where you spend your time online in 2026, you may feel like You Can See Everything is one of the biggest movies of the year. At least in some circles, it’s one of the most discussed, but then also if you want to look at things more objectively, or outside certain circles, it has already earned a lot for a documentary (and, at the time of writing, it’s only just starting to get released outside the U.S.), and the level of critical acclaim has been similarly remarkable.

As for the content of You Can See Everything… it’s wild. It’s best not to say anything about why it’s wild. But it is. It’s a big deal. It’s hard to stop thinking about, once seen, and moves so well for such a long and static documentary, if it can even be called a documentary in the first place.

A film that covers a particular war within an extensive conflict that’s still relevant to this day, Waltz with Bashir is uniquely presented and shattering as a film. It has director Ari Folman taking a personal approach to covering the 1982 Lebanon War, wartime atrocities committed, and attached post-traumatic stress disorder, with the film being about his attempts to recover his memories from his involvement in the conflict as a young man.

Waltz with Bashir explores the conflict – in particular an infamous event known as the Sabra and Shatila massacre – not from the victims’ point of view, which has made it controversial. However, it doesn’t feel like it’s in favor of the attack’s perpetrators, showing the horror of what happened and condemning both it and other atrocities around the same time. It feels distinctly anti-war, with Folman addressing what he feels he can personally speak to, doing so in a way that feels self-reflective and critical. Not everyone agrees with that, but in any event, what this does as a wholly distinctive and unusually presented documentary is impressive, and it does feel like Waltz with Bashir inevitably achieves what it set out to do.

Of all the documentaries here that feel like so much more than “just” documentaries, Koyaanisqatsi is perhaps the most groundbreaking, unusual, and compelling. There isn't anything here by way of voiceover or interviews, and you don’t even get all that much text explaining the things you're seeing and hearing, but Koyaanisqatsi does still get its message across through all its sights and sounds.

Different people will get slightly different things from it, yet the environmental message and the exploration of a conflict between humanity and the natural world are still very much effectively conveyed, not to mention ultimately hard to miss if you pay even the slightest amount of attention. And Koyaanisqatsi is worth paying much more than a slight amount of attention. It’s easily among the most memorable and radically presented documentary movies of all time, and it’s lost none of its power when watched today, more than four decades on from its initial release.

Gulf crude exports reached 18.4 million barrels a day in October, still 26% below pre-war levels, the IEA said

Iraq pumped 4.6 million barrels of crude a day in October, an increase of 540,000 barrels a day from the previous month, the International Energy Agency said in its monthly oil market report on Friday.

That was the highest level since September 2019, according to Bloomberg.

Iraq's output ran 170,000 barrels a day above its implied target of 4.43 million, the IEA said. The agency put the country's sustainable capacity at 4.87 million barrels a day.

Gulf producers excluding Oman exported 18.4 million barrels a day in October, which is 26% below pre-war levels, the IEA said. Those exports rose by 950,000 barrels a day from the prior month, according to Bloomberg. Producers in the region are sending some cargoes on low-profile tanker runs across the Strait of Hormuz and are shipping other crude by alternative export routes that never touch the strait.

Global oil supply rose by 2.3 million barrels a day to 105.4 million in October, the IEA said. That followed a 3 million barrel-a-day increase in September. The agency said the gains came from high Gulf exports despite continuing attacks on energy infrastructure and tankers.

Saudi Arabia produced 9.99 million barrels a day in October, up from 8.93 million in September, the IEA said. Kuwait produced 2.03 million barrels a day, up from 1.58 million.

The IEA's estimates differ from figures that Gulf producers submitted to OPEC. Iraq told OPEC it produced 4.888 million barrels a day in October, above the IEA's 4.6 million. Saudi Arabia told OPEC it pumped 10.563 million barrels a day, compared with the IEA's 9.99 million.

The recovery in Gulf output follows disruption from the U.S.-Israeli war on Iran, which began in late February.

10 Classic Rock Albums To Listen to Before Kicking the Bucket

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

​​​​​​Joining the choir invisible. Shuffling off this mortal coil. Ceasing to be. Biting the dust. Wearing wooden pajamas. Vacating the earthly meat prison. No matter how it's said, it all means the same thing: death. Death is the great equalizer, the one thing that is inevitable regardless of one's race, religion, income or social status. Every day brings us closer to hopping the twig.

But hey, you're still on this side of the grass, right? Unless you're a well-read zombie, but that seems unlikely (but not impossible... discuss). So you have a choice to make: sit idly by, paralyzed by the thought you may have had your last Slurpee; or make the most of the time you have left. And if — hopefully — you've selected the latter, before kicking the bucket you've got things you want — nay, need — to do, see, and hear. Thankfully, your friends here at Collider have compiled a list that speaks to the latter: 10 rock albums you need to listen to before going belly up, ones that are not only awesome but offer guidance as you prepare for the inevitable. We're dying, literally, to share those with you now.

There comes a point in everyone's life where it's time to drop the charades and get real. That means raw, emotional honesty with others and yourself, and there is no better album that wears emotional honesty on every track like Fleetwood Mac's 1977 classic Rumours. The story behind the album is legendary at this point, recorded as the interpersonal drama among the group was akin to a soap opera, with separations, outright spite and heartbreak, making the atmosphere in the studio, shall we say, tense. A lesser band would have buried that under upbeat pop/rock songs, recording in separate sessions if needed. Not Fleetwood Mac: they owned that energy and put it all on vinyl, creating a timeless masterpiece that is relatable, real, and, in the case of "Don't Stop," even hopeful.

Getting real with ourselves potentially opens up a proverbial Pandora's Box of questions about life, death, time, money, or even one's mental health. While it may not provide any answers to these questions, Pink Floyd, with their iconic album Dark Side of the Moon, sure as all hell wax philosophical about those same themes. Like Rumours, the album has an emotional honesty that resonates only with the fluid cohesion that comes from being a concept album. As a result, Dark Side of the Moon is less a record than it is an experience, one that should be made before going up to the "Great Gig in the Sky."

Speaking of experiences, seeing KISS in concert is one that is an absolute must. Or at least was, given the band gave their last performance at Madison Square Garden on December 2nd, 2023. So if that's on your bucket list, you now only have two options: wait for the KISS virtual avatars to hit town, or listen to their 1975 album Alive! Opt for the latter. Their breakthrough album, consisting of cuts from their first three albums (KISS, Hotter Than Hell and Dressed to Kill), achieves something that other live albums have mostly failed to accomplish: the feeling of being there. Alive! captures the bombastic, take-no-prisoners energy of a KISS live show and puts it on vinyl. Twice, that is, with that raw power requiring containment on a double album. Good luck replicating that, avatars.

The "when" of kicking the bucket is assured. The "how," on the other hand, is another story. There are millions of ways one could go, from horribly tragic to an eternally looping end as a Darwin Award winner. You could, as alluded to in the aforementioned Rumours, even "Go Your Own Way." And if that's the case, as Van Halen cheerfully suggests on their 1984 album, you "might as well jump" (but in all seriousness, please don't — help is always available). The last album with long-time frontman David Lee Roth (until 2012's A Different Kind of Truth) is also, arguably, their best, with "Panama," "Hot for Teacher," "Drop Dead Legs" and "Jump" the standouts. Really, though, the album is so good it doesn't really matter where you "jump" in.

It's ironically fitting that the last album the Beatles recorded before their demise, one that includes "The End," would show up on a list like this, no? As Collider explains, Abbey Road is arguably the greatest record of the 20th century. It's high praise, and even if you may disagree — you shouldn't, it's pretty damn good — then it still stands tall amongst the crowd. Noted as much for its iconic album cover as its tracks, Abbey Road has contributions from all four of the Beatles, with George Harrison and Ringo Starr each providing their strongest entries ("Something" and "Here Comes the Sun" for Harrison, "Octopus's Garden" for Starr). Even the much-maligned "Maxwell's Silver Hammer" is far better than its reputation. The album has a blend of genres that work as a whole, serving as a snapshot of the band at the apex of their creativity.

The Beatles fell apart after recording Abbey Road, while Australian rockers AC/DC could have done the same after the death of original frontman Bon Scott, and even toyed with the idea of disbanding. Instead, they stayed together, with Scott's father encouraging them to do so. They recruited Brian Johnson and set out to record what would be the definitive AC/DC album: Back in Black. It was created as a tribute to Scott, with the all-black cover a sign of mourning, while simultaneously launching a new era for the band. That new era came with a commitment to elemental, arena-ready guitar riffs and straight-forward rock, with a frontman who brought his own energy instead of being a Scott-lite. To say it worked out pretty well is an understatement: Back in Black hit the top of the UK album chart, became the best-selling album that never reached number one in the US charts, and is currently the second best-selling album of all time in music history. There is legitimately not a single track that sucks, from opener "Hells Bells" (which, hopefully, aren't what you hear on the other side) to "Rock and Roll Ain't Noise Pollution."

These classic rock albums are masterpieces from beginning to end.

Another album that showcases the band's mastery over a wide range of genres is Led Zeppelin's Led Zeppelin IV. "Black Dog" and "Rock and Roll" are straight bangers, "Going to California" has an acoustic folk vibe, while "When the Levee Breaks" takes the original blues song by Kansas Joe McCoy and Memphis Minnie from 1929 and reinterprets it as a slow-groove, blues-rock masterpiece. Every member of the band is at their best, which comes through the album's most prominent track: "Stairway to Heaven." The delicate acoustic softness of its opening melds seamlessly into its hard-rock crescendo and a fiery solo from Jimmy Page that arguably stands as his finest, with Robert Plant owning the track with his uncanny vocal range. And hey, when you do say goodbye to this world, it's nice to know you don't need to waste your time finding the elevator to Heaven.

And if you make that trek to the top of the stairway, you'll find "Paradise City," which is also a track from one of rock's all-time greatest debut albums, Guns N' Roses' Appetite for Destruction. Neither the album or the band entered the music world quietly, but rather with a raw, explosive danger. They took the makeup, tights and hairspray that marked the hair-metal era and made it irrelevant overnight, ditching the sexualized, party-time dynamic in favor of a gritty, punk realism. The album balances radio-friendly(ish) tracks like "Sweet Child O' Mine" and "Welcome to the Jungle" with darker cuts like "Mr. Brownstone," making it an album of all killer, no filler. Why waste the time you have left listening to crap?

Knowing that this life is fleeting, you may want to get familiar with the fella that'll be hosting you for eternity after you've bought the farm: God. To that end, you'll want to throw on Stryper's game-changing 1986 album To Hell With the Devil. With it, the holy-roller band did the unimaginable by making Christian rock music actually good. Like, MTV good, where they were in heavy rotation with the likes of Mötley Crue and Poison. They had all the bombast and flash of their secular hair-metal peers, even better than most, with a message that was radically different. "Honestly" is as good a power ballad as you'll ever hear, while standout tracks "The Way" and "To Hell With the Devil" showcase the blistering fretwork of Oz Fox and vocalist Michael Sweet's uncanny pipes. IDFA Artistic Director Isabel Arrate Fernandez On Festival’s Competition Lineups, “Huge” Interest In Elizabeth Holmes Doc, And AI In Documentaries

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The 39th edition of IDFA – the world’s biggest nonfiction film festival – is underway in Amsterdam, gathering the documentary field’s top talents, Oscar seekers and emerging filmmakers with dynamic work.

“It’s hectic, all the last-minute things,” artistic director Isabel Arrate Fernandez told Deadline just before the kickoff. “From the lineup competitions to also the industry program we have, there are many exciting things going on.”

The opening night slot Friday evening goes to White Tiger Column, a world premiere directed by Robert Mentov, Dwight Jantzi, and Bryan Dickie that’s set within the resistance movement opposing Myanmar’s military-dominated regime. The festival calls it “a striking example of documentary cinema… The film offers a moving portrait of a courageous generation.”

White Tiger Column is programmed in International Competition, along with films from Morocco, Madagascar, Burkina Faso, Brazil, France, Germany, the Republic of Georgia, and other countries. An Incomplete Day in the History of Georgia 10.06.93, directed by Salomé Jashi, examines a critical moment for Georgia in 1993 when the country, recently freed from Soviet domination, debated measures to replace the Russian ruble. Considering Vladimir Putin’s attempt to reassert Russian dominance over much of the old USSR – including Ukraine – it’s a film with immediate relevance. Watch on Deadline

A baker’s dozen of features is programmed in the festival’s Envision section, which platforms films with bold stylistic choices. “The way we look at both of our competitions,” Arrate Fernandez notes, “they are of equal value… In Envision, we are really looking at filmmakers that are challenging documentary, are challenging the form.” Arrate Fernandez adds, “If we don’t have that, then how does a genre, how does cinema evolve?”

One of the Envision titles, Ragazzi al fiume, examines the experience of three young Afghan immigrants to Switzerland. The IDFA program notes the documentary directed by Pablo Briones “gradually evolves into an observational portrait of isolation, loss, and the longing for a peaceful future.”

Fellow Envision Competition documentary The Black Box Is Orange focuses on the Israeli town of Shlomi that “was built on land belonging to the destroyed Palestinian village of Al-Bassa, near the Lebanese border,” according to the IDFA program. “Beginning with the Nakba mass displacement of Palestinians in 1948, its residents were forced out and citizens of the newly established state of Israel were settled there. All traces of the previous inhabitants were to be destroyed, including all Palestinian archives.”

That film is directed by Yuula Benivolski, an Israeli Jewish filmmaker born in Canada and raised in the village of Shlomi. (Two years ago, she posted a video on YouTube discussing her decision to renounce her Israeli citizenship. “It wasn’t a decision I made lightly… Yes, I did it in response to Israel’s horrific assault on Gaza,” she explained, before adding, “Whether Israel realizes it or not, all this death harms its own people as well.”).

More than 250 films are programmed across the festival’s sections. Among them are some of the strongest contenders for Oscars, including To Hold a Mountain, the Sundance winner directed by Biljana Tutorov and Petar Glomazić; Once Upon a Time in Harlem, directed by David Greaves and based upon material shot by his father, William Greaves (this festival is separately screening a large selection of William Greaves’s work); Musk, directed by Alex Gibney; Closure, directed by Michał Marczak, and You Can See Everything, the box office sensation about Elizabeth Holmes that’s directed by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim. Do they know the imprisoned Theranos founder in the Netherlands? Maybe so, maybe not, but enthusiasm for the film has certainly crossed the Atlantic.

Interest in You Can See Everything “has been huge,” Arrate Fernandez affirms. “The festival screenings sold out very quickly… It’s definitely generated some attention, to put it mildly.”

IDFA has become an increasingly important stop for filmmakers in Oscar contention, in part because of the plethora of Academy documentary branch voters who are expected to attend. (“It’s difficult to count them, I can tell you,” Arrate Fernandez notes. “Let’s see.”). Last year’s winner in IDFA’s International Competition, A Fox Under a Pink Moon, has become a leading contender for Oscar consideration this year. (That film’s protagonist and co-director, the young artist Soraya Akhlaghi, designed this year’s IDFA’s poster and contributed artwork used in the festival trailer. Watch it below).

Arrate Fernandez mentions other films in the 2026 IDFA lineup making a strong bid for Oscar recognition.

“The winner of Cannes, Rehearsals for a Revolution; Traces, a Ukrainian film talking about sexual assault as a war crime against women; Closure, NAZA… there are many interesting films there that I hope will make it,” she says. “Even though they are not the films with the bigger budgets and everything for their campaigning, I really hope they get far.”

14th, another top Oscar contender that will screen at IDFA, is among the documentaries that make use of AI – in the case of Ava DuVernay’s film, to animate vintage photographs. For the first time ever, the artistic director tells us, IDFA asked filmmakers to complete a form indicating if they had incorporated AI into their film’s production.

“Last year, we noticed that sometimes we were wondering while watching films and now we have this, we can go back and see what they filled out just to understand,” Arrate Fernandez comments. “But also, it’s part of the conversations that are taking place with regards to how to use [AI], what is acceptable? There are so many questions, so I think that really [speaks] to conversations that are being held within the industry.”

To that end, IDFA will be hosting two panel discussions on AI, part of a robust lineup of industry talks. A panel titled “Fighting Back – Cinema as an act of resistance?” will feature Oscar winner Laura Poitras, who is bringing They’re Here to IDFA, a short film she directed with Rachel Lauren Mueller that examines the fatal ICE surge in Minneapolis. Fellow panelists include Sofía Bordenave, director of Forest Up in the Mountain, and Mohanad Yaqubi, director of Revolutionaries Never Die. Fernandez is slated to moderate a conversation with this year’s IDFA Guest of Honor, filmmaker Tatiana Huezo.

This is the second year of Arrate Fernandez’s leadership as artistic director. Last year, she faced questions after IDFA revealed it would not invite to the festival what it considered to be Israeli state-supported organizations, such as DocAviv. On its website, IDFA spells out its position under a section titled “Principles and guidelines” governing the 2026 festival.

“For films, makers, and organizations from countries where freedom of expression is under pressure or human rights are violated, it is essential to assess and investigate their independence carefully, alongside the artistic selection process,” IDFA writes. “In this way, we aim to prevent governments and organizations involved in and/or suspected of serious human rights violations from gaining a platform at IDFA.”

The IDFA policy statement continues, “Films, makers, and organizations are never excluded on the basis of their origin and/or nationality, but rather on the basis of any possible relationship with regimes or organizations held internationally accountable for serious human rights violations.”

Arrate Fernandez tells Deadline, “We also really looked at what we did last year, how we’re implementing things, what are our internal processes. And basically, for… a film work or a film project, everything just went through the regular route of being evaluated by our different teams of programmers on the creative criteria. And the moment that films make it through the different phases and end up on a short list, then in cases of countries… that are known for human rights violations, we check the independence of this film or of this project in the sense of also going about checking credits, who are financiers. At the same time, it’s not a blanket approach in the sense of, ‘Oh, this financier, it’s out.’ We also look at the film, so we still have our case-by-case approach.”

She continued, “Then in terms of accreditations… we have the similar line in the sense of having this case-by-case approach.”

American Israeli filmmaker Rachel Leah Jones (Advocate) will be presenting her new project, Podium (You Have Three Minutes), at IDFA Forum, the event’s pitching, co-financing and co-production market for nonfiction and new media projects. Logline: “What does resistance sound like when delivered from the parliamentary podium? Since Israel’s founding, Palestinian lawmakers have taken the mic to appeal, demand, denounce, and warn — articulating a powerful counter-history of the state itself.”

Avi Mograbi, described in the IDFA program as “one of Israel’s most distinguished filmmakers,” heads to the festival for the Dutch premiere of his short documentary Wild Asparagus (Halayon), which bowed at the Venice Film Festival. In Collapse, Israeli director Anat Even returns to Kibbutz Nir Oz, where she lost her home and friends in the Hamas sneak attack of October 7, 2023. She films what remains there and also attempts to enter Gaza, documenting “a gathering of settlers who plan to build in Gaza and proclaim this by chanting, ‘Occupy, expel, settle,’” the festival program reads. “An introspective and revealing film essay on loss, grief, revenge, dehumanization and indifference.” (The program description includes this note: “This film contains content on potentially sensitive topics.”).

The production company for Collapse is listed as France.

“You won’t find films that have as a production country Israel” in this year’s festival, Arrate Fernandez indicates, “but there are Israeli filmmakers. The thing is also that we don’t ask people for their nationality… We have several films that are taking place in Israel and others that are taking place in Palestine or in the Occupied Territories.”

Just about every corner of the globe is reflected in the festival lineup. IDFA runs through November 22, a chance for documentary filmmakers and lovers of the form to gather in person in one of the world’s great cities.

Says Arrate Fernandez, “In a world where storytelling is being formatted by decision makers, AI, algorithm, numbers, I think it’s important to treasure these spaces.”

Younger adults reported the most changes. Modification rates were 54% among users ages 18 to 39 and 30% among users 65 and older.
