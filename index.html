Why CSS zoom Shrinks Every Slide to 30%

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 22 • Originally published at forge.workstyle.tech

📝 Originally published (in Japanese) at forge.workstyle.tech.

TL;DR A slide fitting check measured a box whose min-height kept it at the viewport height. The scrollHeight and clientHeight comparison then kept reporting that the content did not fit, even as its zoom changed. The search reached its lower bound of 0.3 on both mobile and desktop.

All slides shrank to 30% because the box used for the fit check had a min-height set to the viewport height. Measuring the box kept reporting that the content did not fit, even as the scale changed, so the binary search shrank it to the 0.3 lower bound. The fix was to temporarily set the content’s min-height to 0, measure its offsetHeight, and then restore the original value.

I used CSS zoom to add a system that shrinks only content that overflows. It measures the height, then uses binary search—a method that narrows the search range by half each time—to find a scale that fits. But after someone reported that a little scrolling remained on the last page, I tightened the fit check, and the display broke right away.

At first, I thought, “Maybe it still doesn’t fit.” The opposite was happening: the content fit, but the function was reporting that it didn’t.

At 02:41 on October 11, someone checking the page asked me to “adjust the content so it fits on one screen in every case.”

At 02:45, I added a system that uses zoom to shrink the content only on slides that overflow. The padding used for page navigation stays at its original size. On portrait phones, the content shrank to around 30% and became unreadable, so I switched to a two-column layout in landscape mode.

I also measured the scale for different screen sizes. It was 88% at 390×844, 67% at 375×667, and 75% at 360×740. In landscape, it was 80% at 844×390 and 72% at both 667×375 and 740×360. At 1280×720, it was 83%. At 768×1024, 1440×900, and 1920×1080, the content fit without scaling.

At this point, I proceeded with the deployment and commit even though one test was still failing. It was the stylesheet size limit check. The stylesheet was 56,000 bytes against a 52,000-byte limit, so I raised the limit to 56,000 bytes to make the test pass.

At 03:08, I got a message: “There’s still a little scrolling on the last page.”

I added a 2px buffer to the measurement. The 02:45 version reported “+1px of breathing room.” At 03:10, I changed it to leave a 2px buffer. I also remeasured every time a page loaded and every time visualViewport fired a resize event.

Then, at 04:20, I got another message: “Is it broken on desktop?” I checked the screen. It wasn’t just mobile: every slide was at 30% on both desktop and mobile. I fixed it at 04:30.

That’s when I took another look at what I was measuring.

To center short content, I had set the viewport height as the box’s min-height (a CSS property that sets an element’s minimum height). Even when the content was short, the box itself stayed as tall as the viewport.

scrollHeight is the height of the content, including anything that overflows. But when min-height stretches the box to the viewport height, scrollHeight can’t be less than the viewport height either. clientHeight is the element’s inner height.

The check compares against a value 2px less than the viewport height. So even content that fits exactly fails the check. No matter how much the scale changes, the result is always “doesn’t fit.”

The code uses binary search to find the scale. If the content doesn’t fit at 1x, it searches for a scale that does. If the condition is never met, the search can’t find an answer, no matter how long it continues. Eventually, it shrinks to the lower bound of 0.3.

The 02:45 version used “+1,” so the check passed even when the box and viewport were the same height. By adding a 2px margin in the 03:10 version, I turned that same height into a “doesn’t fit” result.

Until I found this difference, I was only checking whether anything overflowed. I wasn’t checking how far the scale had shrunk. Only after everything got smaller did I finally check the result of the fit check itself.

The fix measures the content height after temporarily setting its min-height to 0. It uses the content’s offsetHeight (the element’s height), then restores the original min-height after the check.

After the fix, no slides needed to shrink at 1920×1080. At 1280×800, only the final page was scaled to 95%; at 1366×768, only the final page was scaled to 88%. At 1280×720, the final page was scaled to 82% and the cover to 99%. At 390×844, slides stayed at 87% or higher; at 375×667, they stayed at 66% or higher.

I also checked screenshots of every slide at 1280×800 and 1920×1080. I didn’t just measure the scale percentages; I checked how the slides actually looked.

I haven’t confirmed whether this version with a 30% scale appeared only in the preview or also after it was published.

Five APIs on Cloudflare Workers, and the bugs that nearly shipped

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Five products, 37 endpoints, 470 tests, one deploy command each. This is the architecture, the pricing maths against the market, and the four bugs that would have made the whole thing useless.

One pnpm workspace, five Workers, one shared package. Each Worker is its own product with its own name, key, quota and failure domain. A bug in the PDF parser cannot take down email verification.

The shared package does the dangerous work once: error envelope, CORS, request ids, timing headers, OpenAPI generation, SSRF-safe fetching, and the marketplace proxy-secret check. Every route is registered through app.openapi(createRoute(...), handler) so the spec is generated from the same source as the handler. No hand-written YAML to drift.

That gave 37 endpoints and 470 tests. Every app passes tsc --noEmit, and every route was answered with a real curl before it was listed anywhere.

Marketplace APIs sell on search intent. Someone types "email verification api" because their signup is leaking bounces. They do not want a platform. The product has to name the job.

So: email verification, on-page SEO audit, document text extraction, text analysis, PII redaction. Five jobs with clear intent, all things a developer wants to call once and forget.

The interesting part is not the price, it is what the market charges. I put the numbers on a public page, including where I am expensive:

Against that, a flat $0.05 per call is wrong in one place and right in others:

Publishing that table was the most useful marketing decision I made. It says what the product is bad at, which is the only reason anyone believes the rest.

1. The SSRF guard blocked every hostname. The validator ran an IP-range check against the hostname string, and hostnames are not IPs. Every request was refused. The fix was to resolve first (DNS-over-HTTPS), then range-check the resolved IPs, and only range-check the literal when the input is actually an IP.

2. The OpenAPI import failed with "Failed to parse JSON". Not a schema problem: PowerShell's Set-Content -Encoding UTF8 writes a BOM, and the marketplace importer rejects it. Download the raw bytes or write with UTF8Encoding(false).

3. Every marketplace call returned 401. The proxy secret had a trailing newline from echo secret | wrangler secret put. Trim both sides in the comparison. Obvious in hindsight, invisible for two hours.

4. The listings were private. For days. The publish page renders a visibility toggle that flips to "Public" on click, and nothing is sent to the server until you press a Save button in a sticky bar at the bottom of the page. I clicked the toggle, saw it turn green, and reported the listings as live. They 404'd for everyone but me. That is the worst kind of bug: the UI agrees with you.

Measure the client-visible number, not the server one. Every response from these APIs carries x-response-time-ms and Server-Timing, and the Worker does its work in about 16ms. A client round trip from India measures 172ms, because the rest is distance. Quoting "under 100ms" invites a buyer to measure something they will never see. I replaced every latency claim with a measured number and published the p50/p95 table.

Same for accuracy: I wrote endpoint lists from memory, and three of the five were wrong. Testing the docs like code (a script diffs every documented path against the live OpenAPI document) found it.

Disclosure: I built these APIs and I sell them. The comparison page names where a competitor is cheaper, and the latency numbers are measured from one machine rather than a load test. Treat this as a build log, not an independent review.

The whole design is built around one idea: the artillery must survive, because it wins the damage race, and everything else exists to protect it and feed it targets.

The controller does all of its math in fixed point (1 unit = 65536). Sine and arctangent come from precomputed lookup tables (SIN, 4096 entries; ATAN, 257 entries), angles are 12-bit values 0–4095, and distances are integer square roots. There are two reasons. First, the sandbox rewards deterministic, cheap arithmetic: no float surprises, no expensive Math.atan2 in a hot loop. Second, every geometric decision in the controller — turning, leading a shot, dodging a shell — is an angle, and the tables make those angles effectively free.

Pathfinding is the classic trap for a per-tick budget: run A* every tick and you blow the CPU limit. So hello() builds five BFS flow fields over the passable grid before the match starts — one each for the home objective, the far objective, the spawn, and two artillery firing posts — and tick() only does lookups. A unit that needs to move reads three steps along the field direction from its current tile and drives there. Diagonal moves are blocked when either orthogonal neighbour is impassable, so units never clip corners. When a unit is blocked for more than two ticks, it reverses with a hard steer for 14 ticks — a cheap stuck-recovery that fixed more losses than any clever steering ever did.

Target selection is a single score: a kind bonus (drone −6000, missile −3000, APC −1200, engineer −300) plus four times the target's effective HP. Drones and missiles die first because they are cheap and dangerous; everything else is ranked by how much health it has left.

The artillery does not shoot at single targets. It collects every contact plus recently seen (but currently invisible) contacts, predicts each one's position at shell flight time, and looks for a cluster: if two or more predicted positions fall inside one splash radius, it fires at their centroid. Single targets are only engaged if they are slow (MBT, APC, artillery, missile, engineer) or slow-moving, and the shot is skipped entirely when ammo is low and the target is fast. After every shot the artillery scoots sideways — perpendicular to the threat axis, in alternating directions — far enough to be out of the enemy's lock range by the time the next missile arrives.

The controller tracks missiles, including ones it has only seen in memory, and predicts where each missile will be. If a missile can reach the artillery, the MBT drives to a point between the artillery and the predicted missile position, exactly two body radii away, and holds there — a moving screen. Separately, every unit dodges incoming shells: if a shell will land within splash radius plus two body radii in under two seconds, the unit drives directly away from the impact point. The match is also split into two phases: hold the home objective first, and only push to the far objective once home is held, nothing threatens it, and we are not already ahead on score.

In 2019, Boeing 737 MAX 8 operators were losing approximately $150,000 per aircraft per day in revenue. Today, each 737 MAX 8 generates around $200,000 per day, although airlines don't publish specifics, and revenue varies widely depending on how a carrier uses the aircraft.

The revenue made by each 737 MAX 8 per day is not pure profit. It is offset by depreciation or leasing costs, as well as operational and maintenance expenses. There is also a set-up cost when an airline first brings the jet into its fleet, requiring crew training and maintenance infrastructure, varying in severity depending on the degree of pre-existing fleet commonality with the Boeing 737 family.

Airlines are reluctant to publish exact data on how much money they earn for each aircraft in their fleet. However, answering this question is uniquely possible for the 737 MAX 8 because estimates emerged during the 737 MAX crisis. Each analyst had their own estimates at this time. IBA estimated total lost earnings of $150,000 per day. The Conversation reached a similar conclusion, based on three round trips a day and an average of 145 tickets sold per flight. It multiplied these sales by the US Department of Transportation average airfare in the US at the time of $343.28.

Southwest Airlines had the largest MAX fleet at the time with 34 aircraft, generating approximately $5 million in lost revenue per day. Across the entire 737 MAX fleet globally, the losses would have been closer to an eye-watering $60 million per day. This is why the crisis was so disastrous for Boeing, as there was an expectation that the manufacturer would absorb much of these costs.

IBA also notes other costs that had to be absorbed. Leasing for the jet was estimated at $360,000 a month. Staffing costs for two flight crew and four cabin crew were estimated at $5,000 per day. Airlines also had to figure out ways to adjust or increase their capacity to honor passengers' pre-existing bookings. IBA states that the cost to deal with 1,000 disrupted passengers would have been $100,000.

10 Best James Bond Copycats And Knockoffs In Film History

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

At long last, James Bond is back. Created by Ian Fleming, James Bond has become one of Britain's greatest cultural experts. Sean Connery played the suave secret agent back in 1963 with Dr. No, and we've been treated to a steady stream of Bond films ever since. Now, Robert Pattinson has been cast as the new James Bond; production is sure to move quickly, and it won't be long till we know more about the next Bond.

While you wait, though, there are so many films that have been influenced by Bond. Some are homages, others are parodies; others simply operate in the same genre and pay a tremendous debt to 007. Regardless, here, we're going to present the very best movies that are in direct, open conversation with James Bond.

Released in 2003, Johnny English wasn't subtle about its Bond influence (in fact, writers Neal Purvis and Robert Wade had already written Bond, and penned more films throughout the Daniel Craig Bond era). "I loved the Bond movies as a child and I still do," star Rowan Atkinson reflected in an interview with the Daily Mail. "They are hugely inspiring, a wonderful combination of glamour and adventure. The books were written in the Fifties and were all about Britain as a fading power, but they had this brilliant secret agent who could make Britain look great again."

Johnny English is Bond with the comedy dialed up to 11, deliberately crafted as the comic equivalent of James Bond. Atkinson's hero is the man who wants to be Bond but is far too incompetent, and yet who stumbles into saving the world in one delightful mishap after another. The themes in play are surprisingly deep, though, and go on to become a major part of the actual Bond franchise.

What if James Bond had to go home to his wife and family? That was how Arnold Schwarzenegger pitched True Lies to James Cameron, after watching the 1991 French action farce La Totale! Releasing in 1994, True Lies was a learning curve for Cameron, who excelled at action but had never done comedy before. Like so many entries on this list, True Lies is quite open about its love for Bond; there's an amusing scene where the hero unfastens his diving suit to display a tuxedo, pure Bond humor.

That said, don't think True Lies is pure Bond. Cameron sees this as an action comedy informed by Bond, and he intended it to be a return to a children's view of spies; the suave, sexy secret agents of lore, who get the girls and beat up the bad guys. "Which is a lot more interesting than what spies are in real life," he observed in press events back in 1994. "A bunch of liars without character."

Starring Henry Cavill and Armie Hammer as Napoleon Solo and Ilya Kuryakin, The Man From U.N.C.L.E. is a fantastic spy movie based on a hit 1960s TV series. But that series in itself owes a lot to Bond, and Fleming himself was involved in the show's early development. In fact, Fleming was the one who came up with the name "Napoleon Solo." So if you feel The Man From U.N.C.L.E. reminds you a little of Bond, there's a good reason.

Guy Ritchie's 2015 film turns Henry Cavill into a James Bond replacement, and it's everything you'd expect from a Ritchie spy movie: action-packed, high octane, with tremendous humor and joy. Unfortunately, the film wasn't quite as well-received as it deserved; the script needed a bit more polishing, meaning The Man From U.N.C.L.E. didn't quite make the most of its star billing. Cavill himself has repeatedly said he'd love to play Bond, although his more recent answers suggest he's keen on becoming a Bond villain instead.

Back in the 1960s and 1970s, the popularity of James Bond led to a boom in the spy genre. Charlie's Angels was one of the most interesting Bond-adjacent TV shows, because it turned the "sexy Bond girl" trope on its head; the girls were the stars. That original show was hardly subtle about its roots, and the 2000 film (and its 2019 reboot) followed the same vein. "Austin Powers, plus throw in a little James Bond and The Matrix and Something About Mary," director McG observed. "That's Charlie's Angels."

Unlike other entries in this list, Charlie's Angels isn't a straightforward knockoff. But it's in direct conversation with Bond, making it particularly interesting; think of this as a counter to the male-dominated spy genre that Bond inspired. Back in 2000, the heroes were explicitly referred to as "Bondettes" by critics, who hailed the movie as "Barbie meets Bond." There's even a gold bikini, an amusing nod.

Kingsman was literally born because two writers were tired of James Bond. Director Matthew Vaughn had been in the running for Casino Royale; one day, over beers, comic book writer Mark Millar asked him what he'd have done with it. The resulting conversation sounds to have been equal parts therapy session and commiserations, but it inspired something. As Millar recalled in an interview with The Guardian:

"Matthew said, 'There’s something cool about that Bond Begins idea, the origins of a superspy.’ And I was telling him about a James Bond retrospective the Guardian had done which I’d thought was really interesting. It included the story of Terence Young, the director of Dr No, taking Sean Connery out and turning him into a gentleman."

Kingsman is the result, with comics evolving into spy movies where everyday people get their chance to turn into the next James Bond. Think of Kingsman as a Bond reaction more than anything else, an attempt to bring the fun back, especially in light of the Daniel Craig era. It's best seen as part of a conversation about the spy genre as a whole, a deliberate reaction to the seriousness of Craig's Bond, which the creators dislike.

Released in 1965, The Spy Who Came In From The Cold is—​​​​​​​in a very real sense—the "anti-Bond." Directed by Martin Ritt and starring Richard Burton, it's inspired by John le Carré's ​​​​​​​exceptional novel. And it would be no exaggeration to say le Carré was unimpressed with Bond. As he explained when describing Bond:

"You know him well. He is the hyena who stalks the capitalist deserts, he is an identifiable antagonist, sustained by capital and kept in good heart by the charms of a materialist society; he is a chauvinist, an unblinking patriot who makes espionage exciting. Bond on his magic carpet takes us away from moral doubt, banishes perplexity with action, morality with duty. Above all, he has the one piece of equipment without which not even his formula would work: an entirely evil enemy."

The Spy Who Came In From The Cold is gritty and realistic, casting a harsh light on spycraft and portraying it as inconsistent with Western values. If James Bond glamorizes spies, this movie strips away that mystique and leaves viewers challenged as to whether this is something we should even support. This is Bond, but purposefully flipped to negative, in an absolutely fascinating way.

Trust Christopher Nolan to blend James Bond with time travel. "I’ve always been a huge fan of the Bond films, as anyone who has seen any of my films would know," he noted in an interview with The Playlist. That said, for Tenet, he deliberately chose to blend Bond with John le Carré's more critical approach.

"In the same way Memento tried to use the familiarity of the tropes of the film noir, Tenet is an attempt to use the audience’s familiarity with the spy movie genre, the Bond movies to be able to take it someplace a little crazier, a little different and have us experience it in a different way.”

Think of this as a Bond reinvention, with more than a little science-fiction added into the mix. Tenet leaves viewers with a lot more questions than your typical Bond blockbuster, and it shows just how powerful these formulas really are—and how they can be adapted in new ways.

Starring former Bond Pierce Brosnan and Geoffrey Rush, The Tailor of Panama is based on another classic. le Carré novel. Rush is a former convict turned tailor who's forced to become a spy by Brosnan's cold, aloof MI6 agent. In the aftermath of Bond, Brosnan made no secret of his desire to ride his 007 fame to success, and took on a string of clearly Bond-inspired roles. This was one of the best, not least because—as Brosnan agreed—he plays "Bond as a schmuck."

"I know the rules," Brosnan told The Reader. "I know the joke, I know the gag. I know what we were playing with here. I know the hijinks of what he was up to using me as Andy Osnard, the sleazebag. This morally mangled dude." The casting is perfect, and turns this into one of the best non-Bond films ever made.

"There's nobody more English than an Englishman who no longer lives in England," Mike Myers told Total Film in an interview in 2018. "My house was almost a little colony of Liverpool. Things like James Bond weren't just great movies, they were part of my heritage, so I wanted to honor my parents with tales of World War II, or one of the greatest film series ever made. I always had jokes about the inconsistencies of the James Bond world and the magical realism, so that's what I did."

It's easy to dismiss Austin Powers as a Bond parody, but it's really more of a homage; a loving tribute act, with the script penned in just two weeks by someone who grew up adoring James Bond. "Audiences weren't sure what to make of it at first," Myers recalled. "It was people old enough to know the tongue was firmly implanted in the cheek and young people who just liked the enthusiasm and primary colors. It hung in there and then one day, it ripped."

Released in 2002, The Bourne Identity stars Matt Damon as Jason Bourne, an amnesiac assassin on the run. Loosely based on the spy novel by Robert Ludlum, the core concept is surprisingly similar to Fleming's 1964 novel You Only Live Twice; there, as in The Bourne Identity, James Bond's mission goes wrong and he's rendered amnesiac after being knocked into the sea. Ludlum was a huge fan of Fleming, writing a tribute in Entertainment Weekly on the 30th anniversary of the Bond films:

"Fleming was a contemporary nexus, a vital connection, as well as a necessary contribution, that forced my generation of suspense writers to look deeper into the intrigues—political, geopolitical, and international—than we might have before he arrived in print. Fleming was a bridge over critical waters: He romanticized terrible inequities by obliterating them. But by doing so, he led those who followed him, followed in the wake of the extraordinary promotion and acceptance worldwide of the novels and the movies and eventually the videocassettes, to make those genuine inequities and intrigues perhaps—only perhaps—a touch more literary (a pretentious term, and certainly arguable)."

Personally, I consider The Bourne Identity to be one of the best spy films of the 21st century (so far). But there's no way that James Bond similarity isn't intentional.

GTA 6 Makes It Nearly Impossible to Play Without Killing Anyone

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

In my defense, I don’t know how to drive IRL or in GTA

Grand Theft Auto 6 is out now, and for my initial playthrough of Rockstar’s latest giant open-world game, I wanted to try something a bit out of left field: How long could I play without killing anyone? I’d do my best Batman impression: brutally beating my foes, crippling them with excessive medical debt, but keeping them alive.

Yeah, that didn’t last long. Turns out trying to not commit crime in the game that’s all about committing crime is a difficult task.

The first mission was easy enough. Jason has to rescue drug runner Donnie from a gang of rednecks who keep tigers in cages. (Surely that will end well for them.) After I rescued him, I got into a fist fight with a beefy boy and knocked him out. So far so good. Jason had a pistol on him and could pick up weapons around the swamp base, but, trying to avoid a shootout at all costs, I stealthily booked it away from the rednecks and toward the water, avoiding any detection (and potential for violence).

That was the opening mostly-on-the-rails mission, but things quickly went south when I was left to my own devices. During the next in-game day, players are given a few mission options. I met up with Cal, Jason’s buddy who’s not cut out for a life of crime, and we drove to a police impound lot. I needed to steal a car with a trunk full of weed, and this is where shit hit the fan.

Look, I never learned how to drive in real life, and am not the biggest fan of it in video games. Driving for more than a few feet without hitting anything or anyone is an impossible challenge in games, and driving in GTA 6 feels especially difficult; I can never figure out the right speed to go, and sharp turns are the bane of my existence.

So maybe I was always doomed to run over a cop on my way out of the impound lot.

Police officers swarmed Jason after he unhooked the red Phoenix and hotwired it. Too much was happening at once: an on-screen message about how I could open the gate, cops flinging open the driver’s side door and trying to rip Jason from the vehicle, and police radio chatter pouring out of my controller speaker. I’m not ashamed to say I restarted this mission several times just trying to figure out how to get out of the lot by carefully not running over any cops.

And then, who pulls up in front of the gate I’m about to crash through but a do-gooder cop hopping out of his vehicle? I assumed the giant pool of blood leaking out of him after our collision meant he wasn’t long for the world.

I did get caught on that attempt (again, driving is hard), and had to restart. On my next attempt, I got away blood free. Did that mean I succeeded in not killing anyone, or did the roadkill cop mean I failed? Didn’t matter, since soon after I killed someone and there was no going back.

If you need to check Snapmatic, pull over

After purchasing a longarm from an Ammu-Nation, Jason gets jumped. This was my first fistfight, meaning I had to navigate on-screen messages telling me how to fight while trying not to die. I was just pressing buttons, y’all. Instinctively I pressed whatever button flashed up on screen, which meant, after knocking out one dude, I picked up his knife and started attacking with it. Knives — much more lethal than Jason’s fists!

I killed one goon before dropping the knife and knocking out the third and final guy. I checked my playthrough stats and, yep, that guy was definitely dead. And I was barely two hours into the game. There’s a lot to do in GTA 6, and plenty of control schemes to learn just to be able to do all that stuff. Players have already accidentally kicked bodega cats (heinous) because of a fumbled button press.

Still, not to be deterred by my early-game kills, I tried to soldier on with committing as little murder as possible. I soon learned this was impossible. During the “Hey, Arnold” mission, Jason gets into a car chase with one of landlord Brian’s old buddies. Arnold’s got goons on motorcycles protecting him and, ya know, I said I wasn’t very good at driving.

I tried my damndest not to kill them, but that wasn’t going to happen. I accidentally ran into some, and they didn’t survive their facetime with Brian’s big-ass truck. But, hey, at least I got to spare Arnold at the end.

I’ve played a few hours since, and have tried to keep up my “no killing” policy. It’s especially tough in shootouts as Rockstar’s third-person shooting has never been the most fluid, and aiming for nonvital parts of people — when getting shot at myself — is a tall task. Still I’ve kept it up. I’ve enjoyed how you can incapacitate enemies by shooting them in the legs; the hit indicator will turn yellow to show that they’ve been wounded, but not killed. Now, is that guy I shot — but didn’t kill! — who then lay unmoving and face-first in knee-high water really going to get back up again? Who knows, but I did my best to not kill him.

GTA 6 is hyperrealistic, to the point that sometimes people can’t tell the difference between GTA 6 footage and real life. That realism extends to each of its hand-crafted NPCs. They look and behave so much like real people that I’m hesitant to just pull out a gun and start blasting, a sentiment that prospective players shared ahead of the game’s release.

Violence toward very-real-looking humans is something I’ve thought a lot about this year, and the restrictions on it in 007 First Light is part of what made that game really appeal to me. Pragmata and Onimusha: Way of the Sword are two of my favorite games this year, and I have to think that’s partly due to the fact that the violence in them is directed toward robots and demons, respectively. I don’t really have to think twice about slicing a people-devouring demon in half. Firing on small-time crooks in Vice City? That’s something that will give me pause.

I don’t know how much longer I’ll try to keep up my self-imposed difficulty modifier and continue on with as little violence as possible. After all, the appeal of this series is, ya know, committing crime. Hey, sometimes you gotta shoot some dudes in GTA 6 before they shoot you. But it was certainly fun while it lasted.

Research Says Cold Muscles Produce Less Power. Here’s How to Protect Your Performance on Frigid Runs.

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Experts share how to prepare, pace, and recover so you can run strong when temperatures fall below freezing.

When temperatures rise, running performance tends to drop. Both the research and anyone who’s put in miles on a hot summer afternoon can confidently tell you that much.

But what about when the mercury moves in the opposite direction? Does colder weather in the winter—ranging from just a bit chilly to downright frigid—take a toll on your running too?

A recent review published in Temperature suggests it does. According to the study’s authors, when our muscles get too cold, neuromuscular function becomes impaired. On the run, this can contribute to stiffness, less power output, and decreased endurance, all of which may cause your winter workouts to feel harder than usual.

The good news? You can still run strong when the temps drop—as long as you make a few key training adjustments before, during, and after you log your miles in the colder months of the year. Here’s how to do just that.

There’s no exact number on the thermometer at which runners can universally expect their power, speed, or endurance to decline, Stephen Cheung, PhD, the lead author of the aforementioned review, tells Runner’s World.

Temperature is just one of the many factors that contribute to how well we perform in the cold, Cheung explains. “It’s a function of the environment, as well as how much heat you’re generating, the speed that you’re moving at, and of course, the amount of clothing that you’re wearing,” he says.

Each of these factors varies significantly from runner to runner and affects how quickly their bodies lose heat. You only need to consider those in your run group who run shirtless when it’s freezing versus those who bundle up when it’s 50 degrees Fahrenheit to understand that we all thermoregulate (maintain our internal body temperature) differently. And the equation only becomes more complex when you account for wind and moisture—both in the environment and what you create yourself from air resistance and sweat.

Heather Milton, CSCS, lead exercise physiologist at Primary MD and cofounder of Master My Run, agrees. “It would be great to be able to say, when the temperature outside reaches [a certain point], your pace slows down by this many seconds per mile—but that’s not how it works,” she says.

Keeping this in mind, Milton says the cleanest reference point for runners to begin paying more attention to how they prepare for runs in the cold is when the temperature drops below freezing. Generally speaking, the colder it gets from there, the more noticeable the negative effects on performance will be, she explains.

Cold weather leads to vasoconstriction, Milton explains, which means our blood vessels constrict more, so the muscles farther away from the core don’t receive as much blood flow. If your fingers or toes have ever gone numb in the cold, for example, you’ve experienced the joy of vasoconstriction. Our bodies prioritize heating our core over our extremities because it helps our vital organs maintain a consistent temperature, which is essential for survival in dangerous conditions.

This physiological response, however, doesn’t bode especially well for running performance.

“The cold slows nerve conduction so you have to work harder for the same power output,” Milton says. “And as temperatures drop further below freezing, that slower nerve conduction and the effects of vasoconstriction are going to be even more noticeable.” As a result, your overall exercise capacity decreases, meaning you may not be able to run for as long or at the same pace you normally would.

What else might you notice when temperatures drop? “Physically, if your muscles and tendons are cold, they’re going to be tighter, less elastic, and you won’t be able to contract them as strongly,” says Cheung. “Stiff muscles can also put you at greater risk for tears or strain injuries.”

Milton says this analogy, used by an old coach of hers, provides an easy way to understand what’s happening: When your muscles and tendons get too cold, they’re like a cold rubber band that can snap when pulled.

If you’re a runner who dislikes the cold, you might wonder if the smarter choice is to just run on the treadmill this winter. Don’t worry: As long as you prepare appropriately and use caution, you can absolutely continue logging regular miles outdoors.

Running well outside in the cold starts with warming up indoors first, says Milton. “An indoor warmup increases your internal metabolism and enhances blood flow, which helps prepare your muscles for contraction,” she explains. This also produces heat so your internal body temperature is more resilient to the cold outdoor environment.

Your winter warmup does not have to be any different or longer than your usual routine, Milton says. But you do want to make sure that you’re getting in a solid five minutes of prerun drills to adequately raise your body temperature and activate your muscles.

She recommends starting with slower, more controlled movements that help activate the core and the glutes, such as planks with leg raises, lunges with rotation, and hip CARs (controlled articular rotations). Finish with quicker drills that require your nerves to fire more rapidly, such as leg swings and butt kicks.

Equally important to warming up is layering up—with an important caveat.

When dressing for winter runs, keep in mind how you personally respond to cold temperatures, says Cheung, because everyone has different tolerances and susceptibilities. “I get very cold in my hands, so I’ll wear really thick gloves, even when I may be wearing relatively little on my torso,” he adds. “Pay attention to what’s an issue for you and adapt your clothing to both the conditions and your own peculiarities.” Cheung emphasizes this because you don’t want to end up sweating too heavily, which can then leave you chilled and clammy.

If you need a starting point for your winter running wardrobe, aim for two to three layers of breathable, moisture-wicking fabrics. If possible, choose a wind- and water-resistant shell for your outermost layer, suggests Milton. For your lower body, wear shorts over your tights to help keep your glutes warm. Finally, cover any exposed skin on your head, ears, and hands, and opt for a pair of wool socks—all of which can help ensure adequate blood distribution to the extremities, Milton adds.

When you head out for runs in the cold, slowly build up to your usual pace.

Gradually easing into your workout gives your muscles more time to adjust, and it can also help your airways get used to the colder temperatures, Milton says. You don’t necessarily need to go slower for your entire run, but you should try to match your rate of perceived exertion (RPE) with your typical effort, rather than aiming for your usual pace or heart-rate zone.

“Think about it the same way as if you were running on an extremely hot day,” Milton says. “Let’s say your RPE for an easy run is about 3 out of 10. Keep that 3 out of 10, even if your pace is a little slower.”

If you find that your run feels more difficult than usual, slow down if you need to, but don’t stop completely, Cheung advises. “Don’t take long breaks, because that’s when you aren’t generating as much heat. Just keep moving so you have that constant heat generation,” he says.

If you start to get cold on a run, try speeding up a little bit to stoke that metabolic heat production, Cheung says. But make sure you do it safely: Don’t force the effort in icy conditions or when your muscles feel especially stiff. Scouting out run routes with more tree coverage to block wind on blustery days can also help you stay warmer, he adds.

As soon as you finish your workout, change out of your sweaty running gear and into warm, dry clothes, Milton says.

Even if you’ve only sweated a small amount—for example, maybe you just have some dampness under your armpits—you still want to remove any wet fabrics against your skin. “Even that small amount of wetness can start to drop your core temperature,” Milton explains. If you wait until you’re near shivering, you’ll face an uphill battle to get yourself warm again.

You can also opt for a relaxing shower and hot chocolate—extra marshmallows.

From the Archive: Ina Garten Tapped a Few Famous Friends for Her '90s Farmhouse Renovation

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Take a look inside the sunny, five-room farmhouse.

Welcome to Archive Dive—my new excuse to obsess over the absolute treasures hiding in House Beautiful’s 130-year-old archives. Every month, we’ll dig up a favorite story from the past (epic room makeovers, wildly bold design swings, and style moments that were way ahead of their time) and talk about why they still resonate today. It’s part history lesson, part design deep-dive, and 100 percent a love letter to the iconic taste that’s defined HB for over a century.—Jo Saltz, House Beautiful Editor in Chief

After Ina Garten moved the now-shuttered Barefoot Contessa specialty store from Westhampton to East Hampton, she and her husband, Jeffrey, decided to put down roots in the neighborhood. But rather than buy a new-build home, Garten transformed an old farmhouse into a light-filled, five-room retreat designed for hosting—and feeding—guests.

In our latest Archive Dive, we revisit the 1994 renovation, which includes Garten’s search for the right paint color, her no-nonsense decorating approach, and her love of the property’s grounds. She took each project in stride with help from famous friends like Martha Stewart and the late interior designer Robert Currie—and Jeffrey, of course. The result was a relaxed home with an entertaining-ready atmosphere and a garden that would make any green-thumbed foodie proud. Read on for the original story.

With the help of Robert Currie, the owner of a fabulous food emporium in the Hamptons planned her nearby house for the casual ease of the legions of guests she feeds in her spare time.

Are you full of self-doubt in the dark of the night? Should you buy that old house? Should you change your career? Then follow in Ina Garten’s (bare) footsteps. She has done both with such breathtaking speed and success that the average worrier blinks in awe. She bought a Westhampton, New York, specialty store (oddly shaped breads, ravishing vegetables, seafood salad to go) from a business opportunities ad practically on the spot.

“I didn’t know how to slice smoked salmon or tell whether the Brie was ripe,” she confesses. But in no time, she turned her emporium, Barefoot Contessa, into a foodie’s fairyland.

After Garten moved the store to East Hampton she converted a cramped, run-down old farmhouse in the village into an airy five-room refuge and party venue for herself and her husband. “I just trusted that if the house had good ‘bones,’ I could figure it all out,” she laughs. No insomnia. No agonies. And, incredibly, no mistakes.

She’s a nurturer, too, a definite Earth Mother, which is certainly why she fled her career in nuclear energy policy planning for the far more congenial field of food. Her personal policy is summed up simply: lots of entertaining.

When her husband—a former Columbia professor, author, and investment banker—worked in New York, she encouraged his uptight fellow bankers to loosen their ties and shed their jackets by dishing up platters of fried chicken and other finger foods. In East Hampton she gives Sunday afternoon parties with plenty of easy food “like crabcake sandwiches,” and leaves all the doors open so guests will feel free to wander out and eat on the porch, or anywhere else that seems inviting.

She often serves in her sunny kitchen, which contains the requisite serious fridge and stove, but lacks the usual array of self-important utensils. This is a kitchen with nothing to prove.

The toughest decisions of her fix-up were the wall colors. She thought at first that she might like the whole house vanilla (a true foodie’s choice), but test patches were too white or yellow.

“I had samples all over the walls, and the painter was yelling ‘Stop already’,” but finally she hit on a toasty khaki for her bedroom. She was still stuck for the first floor when she spotted the bedroom primer paint: khaki halved with white. A mental lightbulb turned on, and the painter went to work. The house is now two values of that changeable shade with browny greens in the upholstery derived from the tapestry-covered living room sofa.

There is not a stick of unnecessary furniture in the farmhouse either, just several old oversize tables, a few pieces of portly upholstery, and in the bedroom, a brass bed (of which a friend said, “That’s not a bed, it’s a temple”), plus two chairs. No windows are curtained.

“Everything,” Garten feels, “should do something, have a function, not just sit there. I like things to be spare.” And bare as well, like her new floors, which are crafted of newly purchased old wood. Are the original floors still underneath? Nope. With a sanding and a coat of polyurethane, they are reincarnated as a kitchen countertop.

Garten actually bought the house for its grounds, because she yearned to garden. She had lived for a time in rented houses, but when she found herself planting expensive trees in rented lawns, she realized it was time to own some land.

Outside her kitchen doors she has cut square beds for tomatoes, which mix quite cheerfully with dahlias, and for roses knee-deep in catmint. She likes to combine “mess with structure,” but her structures are always imaginative. Instead of a single stake for each tomato plant, for instance, she does a row of tepees. Or she lines her disheveled strawberry beds with a spine of iris.

A Manhattan florist friend, Tom Pritchard of Pure Mädderlake, helped her with the garden design and did a very clever thing. Architect Labs, a Palo Alto startup, said in a research blog post that its AI systems discovered, designed, and verified Frostbite, a general-purpose CPU with a new instruction set “built from the ground up.” Across 25 workloads, a four-tile Frostbite cluster delivered 4.59 times the performance per watt of a similarly sized “silicon-proven” dual-issue, in-order CPU core. Those numbers come from a simulated 7nm design that has not yet been made in silicon, and the company says it can’t name the comparison core for legal reasons.

Conventional CPUs have hardware that “rediscovers, at runtime, which instructions actually depend on each other.” Multicore designs also need deep cache hierarchies and coherence protocols, which keep every core’s view of shared memory consistent, but scale poorly and cost area and power. Architect Labs’ alternative is what it calls “multithreading for free,” where the program itself exposes the parallel work, without threads, locks, or out-of-order hardware required for that discovery.

Frostbite’s programs are compiled to interaction nets, a model of computation from 1990 in which a program is a graph of nodes and computation happens by rewriting pairs of connected nodes, and the chip is designed around this distinct paradigm. Pairs of nodes being rewritten never overlap, so they can run in any order, or all at once, and arrive at the same result. Every value is also used exactly once, so data can stay in small local memories instead of large shared caches.

An example of the difference given by the team is a 5x5 chess-like board with five queens, placed so none can attack another. A typical program searches for solutions one board at a time. As an interaction net, the same search breaks into 3,183 small operations, and the longest chain that has to run in order is only 262 steps, so on average about 12 operations are ready to run at once at each step. Nothing about the program was rewritten to get there, as the parallel work was already intrinsic.

Each of the chip’s tiles, small identical processing units linked by an on-chip network, has its own small local memory. Four tiles, plus four L3 nodes, form the cluster that’s benchmarked against the conventional core for the performance claims, but the full layout has 16 tiles. The design reached 800 MHz in early synthesis but ended up at 500 MHz in the final layout. It may come as some surprise that the AI which built Frostbite started with what the company calls “zero architectural priors,” though the team first picked the workloads it had to do well on, and this sort of blank slatism may have its advantages in avoiding bias. Instead, the agents read papers and benchmarks, built Python models, and then selected one concept for focus. The original 141-page PDF output, which contained a programming model, runtime, and high-level blocks, was deleted after the first register-transfer level (RTL) design was generated, because “many of the numbers and estimates were well off what was physically possible.”

That PDF was only ever a starting point. Once the agents picked an architecture, they moved away from English — as it can’t get every detail right or catch every mistake, according to the team — and wrote the spec in the company’s own language, called ASL. ASL translates to Lean, a proof checker, so it can be machine-checked, which the company says stops a large class of bugs and cuts down on agents gaming their objectives. From this, the agents devised Arcmodel, a software model of the whole chip written in ASL, before any circuit design existed.

As Ritchie Roesch wrote recently at Fuji X Weekly, “It’s not so much if someone’s camera will become a permanent paperweight, it’s a matter of when and how many, and whether it will be yours.”

The safer (and less litigious) route for the enterprising vibe hacker lies in an entirely new kind of hardware. ESP32 modules, which package networking chips, microphones, speakers, and more into a simple and hacker-friendly package, are suddenly everywhere in hardware communities. (Think Raspberry Pis, but even cheaper and more versatile.) So sure, you can use Claude Code to jailbreak and de-Amazon your Kindle, but why do that when you can buy a device like the M5Stack PaperMono, an ESP32-based e-paper device you can make into whatever you want?

ESP32 modules are perfectly suited to help you build the kind of hyper-specific device Apple or Samsung would never consider. With a few dollars and a few prompts, you can build a way to see real-time sea conditions, rig up a monitor for your whole DJ system, or just play Metal Gear Solidon the go. You can even have a dedicated device for your family’s shopping list.

The arms race between device manufacturers and vibe hackers is only going to continue. As AI models get more powerful, and both the tools and their users get more sophisticated, it’ll be easier and easier for anyone to point their bot at their TV, their car, or their washing machine and see what happens. Some manufacturers will try their best to keep the hackers out; others will look for ways to safely embrace them. Some users will find incredible new functionality for their tech; others will find they’ve created extraordinarily large and expensive paperweights.

Before long, we’re going to see a lot of ESP32 devices turned into more consumer-friendly hardware. Meta is already leading the charge with its Muse Charm and the Muse Gadgets program, through which you can either buy a nicely packaged ESP32 module or use your own to quickly spin up a dedicated AI device. This kind of gadget — just a simple computing module in a pretty box — is going to be everywhere.

At the same time, we’re already seeing some vibe hackers turn into proper software vendors. Gathin, for instance, has spent a lot of time reckoning with the responsibility he feels for what happens when people use CleanYourTV to hack their televisions. He’s tweaked the prompts he shares to first take a backup of your TV’s software in order to make it easy to restore if something goes wrong, and to make note of every change it makes along the way. The CleanYourTV system also checks its progress as it goes and spins up agents to manually test if something has gone wrong, and Gathin hopes it’s increasingly foolproof.

But it’s not foolproof. It might never be. Devices change all the time; every software update introduces new possibilities and new problems. So Gathin recommends the same thing I hear from vibe coders and vibe hackers all the time: Never give the bots access to something you can’t afford to lose. “Even for fiddling with stuff,” he says, “I have an external computer which runs through Linux. So I just use it like a sandbox environment to test things out.” If you’re going to try and vibe-hack your phone (which lots of people are doing) or your computer (which lots of people are also doing), do it carefully. And make lots of backups.

The HomeView isn’t a replacement for a Sonos One, a full-size HomePod, or a similar-size speaker. Those are much better speakers. In size and sound, the HomeView is closer to the HomePod Mini. I tested it alongside the Mini 2 (which sounds marginally better than the first-gen Mini) and found the tabletop HomeView mostly comparable, with these caveats: The HomeView has better bass and is slightly louder, but it’s not as clear and is less room-filling.

The difference comes down to the hardware. The Mini 2 has a single full-range driver and two passive radiators, whereas the tabletop HomeView has two drivers in the display, plus a woofer and two passive radiators in its base. While the woofer adds some oomph, the HomeView’s display, which sits over the speaker base, partly covers it, affecting the sound compared to the Mini. Apple added an accelerometer to the display to account for changing the angle. Still, the Mini, which doesn’t sound as loud, fills a room better, also aided by its 360-degree sound. (The HomeView does support Spatial Audio with Dolby Atmos, which the Mini 2 doesn’t.)

The wall-mounted HomeView has only two drivers, with computational audio Apple says is “tuned specifically for on-wall placement.” It sounds fine but is noticeably thinner than the tabletop version, and bass is basically non-existent. I wouldn’t recommend it for its sound quality, but it’s great for talking to a visitor through your video doorbell.

I like the sound of two Minis paired in stereo and use this setup in my office and my bedroom. They also work as speakers for my Apple TVs in those rooms.

If you have $300 to spend on a good-sounding smart speaker setup, go with two Minis over a HomeView. The HomeView’s real benefit is the screen. I cover its uses extensively in my full review, but for audio in particular, I like that the screen acts like a jukebox, making it easy to change songs, select new music, and scroll through your podcasts or audiobooks — without needing to find a quarter! With a HomePod, you rely on Siri voice control or your Apple device to select content.

You can AirPlay to the HomeView as with any other AirPlay speaker, and audio handoff works the same as it does on HomePods. Tap your phone to the HomeView, and whatever is playing transfers to the speakers and the screen. Handoff also works in reverse, letting you send any content back to your phone. One addition with the HomeView: You can now hand off video.

In testing, handoff worked for Apple TV, Apple Music, Apple Podcasts, Spotify, Pocket Casts, YouTube Music, FaceTime, and phone calls — although phone calls sometimes dropped. YouTube and YouTube TV videos also handed off, even though they don’t have an app on the HomeView. But Netflix and Amazon Prime Video only played audio, though both played video in the Safari browser.

One word of caution: Handoff is aggressive. As with HomePods, if you’re holding your phone within a few inches, the HomeView will try to hand off content.

HomeView doesn’t let you set a default speaker to permanently route audio to instead of using the built-in speaker. However, you can play audio to any AirPlay speaker or group of speakers. I tested this on HomePods, Sonos speakers, and a Samsung TV, and it worked smoothly.

For native apps (Apple Music, Podcasts, and Safari), the HomeView remembers the last speaker or speaker pair you used and automatically plays content through those when you return. If it’s been a while since you opened the app, it pops up the option to choose the last-used speakers.

Things get messier with third-party audio apps such as Spotify and Audible, and with Apple apps that use the CarPlay interface, such as Apple News and Apple TV. The HomeView only automatically uses the last speakers if you choose content from the HomeView screen. If you use Siri voice control, it defaults to the HomeView speaker.

So, “Hey Siri, play ‘Femininomenon’ by Chappell Roan from Spotify” played music from HomeView’s speaker and not the stereo pair of Minis in my office I’d last been listening to Spotify on. I could direct it to play music there by adding “on Office,” but that’s more of a mouthful than just “Hey Siri, play ‘Femininomenon’ by Chappell Roan,” which is all you need to say if you use Apple Music.

Which brings me to another one of your most-asked questions — and my biggest complaint about this device as a smart speaker: How does it support third-party audio apps? Like second-class citizens.

Spotify works on the HomeView, but with limitations that apply to all non-native and third-party apps: they only show up when your phone is connected to the HomeView via your profile, so only you can use them.

Apple has taken a shortcut here: Rather than building a dedicated HomeView experience for app developers, the HomeView uses Apple’s existing CarPlay platform. This makes it easier for developers to put their apps on the HomeView — if an audio or video app supports CarPlay, it already works on the HomeView (there’s no support for other types of CarPlay apps).

In practice, this creates frustrating limitations, including no search function, requiring a user profile on the HomeView, and your phone on the same Wi-Fi network. It also results in slower performance and a laggy, inconsistent experience compared to the native apps.

I tried several apps: Pocket Casts, Amazon Music, YouTube Music, Spotify, and Audible. They all appeared in my HomeView app drawer, and I could access them, choose content via streamlined interfaces, and then play audio on the HomeView or AirPlay from it to any speakers.

But CarPlay apps don’t work as well as the built-in audio apps — Music, Podcasts, and Safari. They take longer to load and respond more slowly. You also can’t type a search query in any of them, as you can in the native apps, because they don’t have a search field or keyboard. Instead, you have to scroll through content to find what you want or use Siri voice search.

Me: “Siri, resume my Audible book.” Siri: “Shuffling latest songs from Apple Music.”

There’s no search box because you’re not supposed to type while driving; only I’m not driving — I’m in my kitchen! This shows how much of a CarPlay port the apps are; they haven’t been updated to account for the fact that this device is stationary and in your home. Case in point, when I first pulled up the Apple TV app (the only CarPlay video app currently supported), a warning popped up telling me to make sure I was stationary before watching any video.

Playing audio through CarPlay apps is also buggy. It didn’t always bring up the music control screen, so I had to use my iPhone to control it. Plus, the Audible app often refused to load at all, and didn’t work with Siri voice search. Me: “Siri, resume my Audible book.” Siri: “Shuffling latest songs from Apple Music.”

CarPlay support feels like a stopgap, and Apple needs to work with developers to refine the software and add HomeView-specific controls for a better, more integrated experience.

Much of the confusion around the HomeView stems from treating it like a smart speaker when it’s really a smart display. I get it; I’ve spent the last decade frustrated by smart displays from Google and Amazon that promise a lot but rarely do it all well.

If you don’t care about the other features of a smart display and just want a smart speaker, then the reason to buy a HomeView over a HomePod is if you have a use for it as a touchscreen music controller. This lets anyone in the household easily browse and control what’s playing. Bear in mind, though, Apple Music (with a subscription) is the only app anyone can use to play music on the HomeView, as CarPlay apps require a user profile.

If you want a smart speaker primarily to play music and are happy using your voice or phone to control it, go for a HomePod. If you use Spotify or other third-party music services, consider an Echo Dot or Google Home smart speaker; both let you set a default speaker and a default music provider — such as Spotify.

If you already own HomePods and use Apple Music, the HomeView’s touchscreen will fit in nicely as a simple way to control audio. Just don’t buy one expecting it to fully replace a dedicated smart speaker.

Volvo EX90 assembly at the Volvo Ridgeville factory in South Carolina

The Volvo and Polestar decisions came under a Commerce Department rule finalized in 2025 aimed at controlling connectivity and self-driving technology. The new restrictions on certain Chinese- or Russian-linked software take effect for the 2027 model year. Hardware restrictions will follow in 2030 and apply to both gas and electric cars, including ones assembled on U.S. soil.

Chinese giant Geely has a majority stake in Volvo Cars, so the Swedish automaker had to apply for specific authorization to keep importing and selling connected cars. Polestar is another Sweden-based automaker that is controlled by Geely. It applied and was denied authorization to keep selling cars from model year 2027 onward. Even though Polestar is a Volvo spinoff and also has a Swedish connection, the country’s trade minister said Polestar didn’t want the same assistance as Volvo. That’s one difference we know between their applications, but the U.S. government hasn’t explicitly said why things shook out this way, and Polestar did not appeal the decision.

Mercedes-Benz EQS SUV production in Alabama

The proposed Connected Vehicle Security Act would write existing restrictions into federal law and extend the U.S. sales ban to automakers with more than 15% Chinese ownership, even if they build their cars in America.

China’s state-owned BAIC Group owns 9.98% of Mercedes-Benz. Geely founder Li Shufu holds another 9.69% through an investment company. Taken together, that’s just shy of 20%, and over the new proposed threshold.

For reference, Geely owns 79% of Volvo and Geely founder Li Shufu reported beneficial ownership of 60.5% of Polestar’s shares in a September 2026 SEC filing.

When asked whether the company had a plan for its Chinese shareholders to divest, Hoff declined to comment on specific conversations Mercedes has with shareholders. He was more direct about the legislation itself.

“You know, we always have and always will support anything that protects the national security of the United States. So we've never opposed this bill, but you know, in the end, we'll also take whatever steps we need to comply with it if it does get passed," Hoff said.

Mercedes says its Chinese shareholders have no seats on its management board and no say in day-to-day decisions. They could still have voting rights as shareholders. In his first year as St. Louis Cardinals scouting director, Randy Flores was watching a pitcher who threw a four-seam fastball and a curveball. The mix can be the backbone of a north-south pitching profile. But to Flores, a former major-league reliever, it looked suboptimal.

"I'm like, 'No, no, no - you sink it and slide it,'" said Flores, reciting an approach he gleaned from former Cardinals pitching coach Dave Duncan.

As a slight left-hander who did not throw especially hard, Flores carved out a playing career that lasted parts of eight major-league seasons. So, his methods had merit. But as an executive now, he recalls the scouting anecdote as something of a cautionary tale.

"Being a former player, if you're anchored too much to, 'This is how it was done when I was playing,' it's the wrong thing," Flores said.

Exploring other perspectives and means and finding a balance has been part of Flores' evolution as an executive, one that led to his hiring this month as general manager of the Houston Astros. It is also an arc to which only a handful of the majors' current highest-ranking baseball executives can relate.

Of the current presidents of baseball operations, Jerry Dipoto (Mariners), Chris Young (Rangers), Craig Breslow (Red Sox) and Buster Posey (Giants) played in the majors. So did general managers Chris Getz (White Sox) and Flores, whom the Astros hired after 11-plus seasons in St. Louis' front office.

That many top executives did not play in the majors, or at all professionally, illustrates how it is far from required. It might even invite the question of how such experience informs former players' approach to the role, if at all, as Flores settles into running a baseball operations department for the first time.

"You probably have more empathy … for what a player goes through, from the moment they sign their first professional contract to when they get to the big leagues," Dipoto said at last week's GM meetings in Las Vegas.

Dipoto and Breslow were relievers in the majors, like Flores. Dipoto posited that background could help "to understand the life of the guy who (is) not always the most thought-of player on the roster."

"Outside of that, I think it's just business as usual," Dipoto said. "We each went through our own, I guess, apprenticeship (after playing).

"You take the lessons you learn and apply them, just like any other general manager does when they get their first job. We just maybe have that slight - I don't even want to call it an advantage - that slight difference in that we have lived life in these players' shoes and probably as a result have a little bit more empathy rather than just frustration. It's a hard game."

Mariners GM Jerry Dipoto, a former major league reliever, is among a half-dozen ex-players who head up baseball operations departments. He said that experience includes empathy for players' journeys through pro ball. (Lindsey Wasson/AP)

After his playing career, Dipoto held roles in scouting before becoming general manager of the Angels in 2011 and for the Mariners in 2015. Breslow spent five seasons in the Cubs' front office, working heavily in pitching development, before the Red Sox hired him to lead their baseball operations.

"My hope would be it adds a level of credibility to the role and a level of empathy," Breslow said, "given that there really isn't anything I ask player to do that I haven't kind of been through myself, having been traded, promoted, released, started games, come in from the pen.

"And I think understanding the mindset of a player and how they're likely to respond and react to different suggestions and requests, I'm hopeful (that) is helpful in terms of understanding how to approach certain conversations."

Flores, 51, spent several years out of professional baseball after pitching his final season in the minors in 2011. He returned to the University of Southern California, his alma mater, to pursue a master's degree with a self-described goal to "chart a career path combining business and sports." He started a company that captured and provided amateur game video for use by recruiters and scouts.

In 2015, the Cardinals sought a scouting director to replace Chris Correa, who was dismissed and later imprisoned for illegally accessing the Astros' computer database. John Mozeliak, then St. Louis' GM, recalled that the Cardinals did not want to overhaul their draft process, which was producing results. Flores, who played five seasons in St. Louis, interviewed and landed the job, his first in a front office.

"There's more to leading a department than just believing in a model and running with it," Mozeliak said this month. "But I think his interpersonal skills are outstanding. I think he has the ability to walk into any type of room and be able to speak to the group."

New Astros GM Randy Flores (left), pictured with former Cardinals teammate Matt Morris, pitched in the majors for parts of eight seasons before moving into front-office work with St. Louis. (MARK HUMPHREY/AP)

Flores oversaw St. Louis' amateur drafts from 2016-26, including for the past eight seasons as an assistant general manager. In recent years, Flores gained experience in other areas of the Cardinals' operations including pro scouting and salary arbitration, he said at his introductory press conference in Houston.

"He's definitely ambitious," said Mozeliak, who left St. Louis after the 2025 season and is now the Angels' interim general manager. "And there was a certain point where he started to ask to get more exposure to different elements of baseball operations. So as he was having early success on the scouting side, it was just natural to keep letting him evolve and giving him exposure to more of the things that you really see under the hood when you're working with or as a general manager."

Chaim Bloom joined the Cardinals as an advisor in early 2024 and succeeded Mozeliak as their president of baseball operations. In their two seasons overlapping in St. Louis, Bloom said he found Flores "gifted in how he can navigate and understand any type of person or group of people you come across in this game" and appreciated "the way he challenged me to think differently about some things."

"It's hard to go into specific detail on that," Bloom said. "But just in terms of some of the discussions we would have on players or how to play the draft - and (also) some things in the pro space, just thinking about different acquisitions - the way he's able to assimilate information, hear different voices and kind of distill it down into something that's actionable, I found that really helpful."

For executives with playing backgrounds, the exchange of viewpoints is a two-way street. It's incumbent on former players to consider how their experience might limit how they view situations, said Brandon Gomes, who spent five seasons as a reliever with the Rays and is now the Dodgers' general manager under president of baseball operations Andrew Friedman.

"Obviously, we have a lot of fantastic executives that never played that have other really helpful perspectives without a bias of having played," Gomes said. "I think you just have to be aware of it; we all have biases. So just being aware it could be there and being a good listener, understanding that you may be too far anchored in that, is a good way to go about it."

Changes within the game can require it. Flores noted during his Astros introduction how access to the kind of data and technology available today might have impacted his own playing career and said he considers analytics "the fabric (and) foundation of … evidence-based decision-making" as an executive.

While data use is prevalent in front offices, though, those who can weigh it against firsthand experience playing in major-league games are less so.

"(Flores) is someone that anchored to these modern tools to help with the decision-making," Mozeliak said. "But he also has great feel for what happens inside a clubhouse or on the field. So being able to pair those two skill sets, I think, will just allow him to make better decisions along the way."

The Astros wagered as much in hiring Flores as general manager, handing him a major-league roster that finished 81-81 in 2026 and a farm system rated among the sport's thinnest by outside evaluators. Astros owner Jim Crane, who maintains involvement in baseball operations along with advisor and Hall of Fame first baseman Jeff Bagwell, touted Flores' playing career afterward as part of his "well-rounded" résumé.

Flores said last week that early on as a scouting director, he felt the need to guard against "attaching too much to my bias of my empirical experience" in evaluating players. Still, those years as a pitcher form an inextricable part of the path that led Flores to the role he assumes now in Houston.

"I understand how hard it is to play this game and I think I have some empathy for that," Flores said. "And I do believe the modern tools and curriculum and the technology and coaching that are available help drive player improvements at a pace much faster than I experienced as player via trial and error.

"So my hope is that I understand how difficult that road is, but I also drive our organization to help those players be the best version of themselves."

InfoQ Homepage News PostgreSQL 19 Released with Online Table Repacking and Query Planning Controls

Nov 22, 2026 2 min read

The PostgreSQL Global Development Group released PostgreSQL 19, introducing changes to database maintenance, query planning, replication, and application development. The release focuses on reducing administrative overhead and improving performance, with new tools for reorganizing tables without downtime and controlling query plans.

One of the main additions is REPACK, which reclaims unused table space and can reorder data to match an index. Its CONCURRENTLY option allows the operation to run without blocking reads or writes, making it a potential replacement for common uses of VACUUM FULL and CLUSTER. PostgreSQL 19 also introduces pg_plan_advice and pg_stash_advice, extensions that let administrators influence query plan selection and apply stored advice to queries by identifier, without changing application code.

Other maintenance improvements include parallel index vacuuming, changes to autovacuum prioritization, and additional monitoring views. EXPLAIN ANALYZE and related logging now report asynchronous I/O activity and WAL generated for full-page images. PostgreSQL also reports that changes to foreign key checks made bulk inserts nearly twice as fast in testing. Query planner improvements include more efficient anti-joins for eligible NOT IN clauses and eager aggregation before joins.

The release adds several features for replication and application development. Logical replication now supports sequence values, and PostgreSQL automatically raises the effective WAL level to logical when the first logical replication slot is created. The new WAIT FOR LSN command lets applications wait until a standby has replayed a specified WAL position before querying data, providing read-your-writes consistency. Developers also get INSERT ... ON CONFLICT DO SELECT for get-or-create operations, JSON output from COPY, additional SQL/JSON string methods, and IGNORE NULLS support for window functions.

Security and compatibility changes include server-side TLS Server Name Indication (SNI), which allows a server to present different certificates based on the hostname used by the client. PostgreSQL 19 also warns after successful MD5 password authentication. MD5 was deprecated in PostgreSQL 18 and is planned for removal in a future release, with SCRAM recommended instead. JIT compilation is now disabled by default because its cost estimates proved unreliable, while LZ4 becomes the default TOAST compression method when supported by the build.

The release has also prompted discussion on Hacker News about whether production users should wait for PostgreSQL 19.1.

Another user recommended waiting for the first minor release on critical production systems:

Personally, I would say, for critical production systems, it's still good to wait for the .1 release.

PostgreSQL 19 is available now. The complete list of changes is available in the official release notes.

A Common Meat Triggers a Unique Muscle-Building Peptide, Study Finds

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A new study found that lean ground pork triggered a distinctive increase in a naturally occurring peptide linked to muscle growth.

A new study suggests that the way the body processes protein after exercise may depend on more than how much protein a meal contains. Researchers found that lean ground pork produced a distinctive increase in a naturally occurring peptide linked to muscle protein synthesis, the biological process responsible for building and repairing muscle proteins.

Published in Molecular Nutrition & Food Research, the research examined how different protein sources affect amino acids and peptides circulating in the bloodstream after resistance exercise. The findings suggest that the structure and composition of whole foods may influence how protein is digested and made available to the body.

Researchers at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign analyzed blood samples and muscle protein synthesis measurements from two randomized controlled trials involving healthy young adults.

Following resistance exercise, participants consumed one of five nutritional treatments: low-fat ground pork, high-fat ground pork, black beans and rice, an isolated nutrient beverage containing amino acids and other nutrients, or a carbohydrate-only control drink. Each protein-containing treatment supplied approximately 20 grams (0.7 ounces) of protein, allowing researchers to compare the body’s responses to different nutritional sources.

Among the five treatments, lean ground pork was the only one to produce a statistically significant increase above baseline in circulating dileucine, a peptide composed of two linked leucine molecules. Blood concentrations rose approximately 90 to 120 minutes after consumption. Dileucine has been associated with muscle protein synthesis, although scientists are still investigating its specific role in muscle metabolism.

Peak dileucine concentrations after lean ground pork consumption were significantly higher than those measured after the isolated nutrient beverage and carbohydrate control. However, the differences compared with high-fat ground pork and the beans-and-rice meal were not statistically significant.

Lean ground pork also produced the highest peak concentrations of leucine, an essential amino acid involved in regulating muscle protein synthesis. These levels were significantly higher than those following high-fat ground pork, beans and rice, or the carbohydrate control, but were not significantly different from the isolated nutrient beverage.

“These findings suggest that the body’s response to protein is influenced by more than the amount consumed,” said Nicholas A. Burd, PhD, corresponding author and researcher at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign. “Seeing how the whole-food matrix affects the release of amino acids and peptides into the bloodstream gives us a clearer picture of how dietary protein may actually drive muscle protein synthesis and remodeling.”

Across the protein-containing treatments, researchers identified a modest but statistically significant positive relationship between peak dileucine concentrations and changes in muscle protein synthesis. The same association was not observed with conventional leucine measurements.

Importantly, this does not establish that dileucine directly stimulates muscle growth or that lean ground pork produces greater muscle gains than other foods.

One possible explanation involves the food matrix, which encompasses a food’s physical structure and the interactions among its components. Researchers proposed that grinding meat could disrupt connective tissue, potentially making proteins more accessible to digestive enzymes and changing how amino acids and peptides enter the bloodstream.

The results add to research investigating how whole-food protein sources influence the body’s response to exercise. Although the protein content of a meal remains relevant, differences in food structure, fat content, and digestion may affect the timing and composition of nutrients reaching the bloodstream.

For many of us, this seems like a new problem. But for one veteran game developer, the scenario is all too familiar. Howard Scott Warshaw is the developer of numerous Atari 2600 games including Yars' Revenge and Raiders of the Lost Ark. But perhaps his most famous (or infamous) game is 1982's E.T. the Extra Terrestrial, the notorious tie-in game that was so terrible Atari buried unsold copies of it in the desert.

E.T. has been cited as a contributing factor in the US video game industry crash of 1983—one of many poor-quality 8-bit titles that hollowed out the gaming market in the States. It's here where Warshaw sees a parallel to today's emerging scenario, though he doesn't necessarily believe it will end in the same way.

In a talk he gave at the DevGAMM Lisbon conference last week (via GamesIndustry.biz) Warshaw remarked upon how AI coding tools have seemed to rapidly sped up iteration times on game development. "People who used to spend weeks, months or years programming something can now do it in days with AI support," he explained.

"So what does this mean in game development? What it means is we're going to see a lot more games produced. You ever hear of the crash of '83 or '84, the big video game crash? They said the problem that happened in the crash, and I think that was true, was there was a glut of crap on the market, because the machine opened up and anybody could just pop a game out, and they did."

Warshaw pointed out that, at the time "good games were still being made" but these games were "way overshadowed by the crap: and that created a real problem in the game industry." He sees a similar situation emerging now thanks to generative AI tools. "AI is going to have the same impact on a larger scale, because on one level, so many more people can now realise and create a video game than ever could before just using AI. And that's okay, but there's going to be a lot of crap, because most games suck. That's just the way it is."

The reason for this, Warshaw says, is that, like all artistic endeavours "there's some things in life that everybody can see themselves doing, and the truth is most people who do it can't do it very well. And what AI is going to do is make that more possible for people who can't do it to do it." Yet while he thinks that most of the games that result from these ideas "aren't going to be very interesting" he also believes that "it's also going to enable the people who really do have some innovative, interesting and solid ideas to realise those, too."

It happens every December. You bring home a full, bright red poinsettia, set it on the table, and a week or two later the colorful bracts start to curl and fall. By Christmas morning, the plant looks tired and half bare.

Some of that can be blamed on poinsettia care after you get home, like a cold windowsill or a pot left sitting in water. Much of it starts at the store, though. The plant was already on its way out before it ever went in the cart.

There's an easy way to tell a fresh one from a tired one, and hardly anybody looks. Forget the red for a second. Down in the center of each cluster of colored leaves are some tiny yellow-green buds, and those say a lot more about how long the color will hold up. It's kind of a strange thing to judge a plant by, but trust the process.

Those colorful "petals" on a poinsettia aren't petals at all. They're bracts, and they color up in fall as the nights get longer. Right in the middle sit the real flowers, small knobby clusters called cyathia. Each one is a little cup holding a single female flower and several male flowers, often with a yellow gland on its rim.

Cyathia go through the same cycle on every plant. They start out tight and green or red-tipped, open to shed yellow pollen, and then drop off. After the cyathia fall, the colored bracts usually follow before long.

Some of the newer poinsettia varieties hang on to their cyathia longer. So it isn't a sure thing, but it's still one of the better clues you get in the store. Any color works for this check, whether you're eyeing red or the pink poinsettias at Walmart (which come in a four-pack for maximum festive joy).

Bend down and look closely at the center of a few bracts. Plants on the same table can be at different stages, so check more than one. Ideally, the cyathia are mostly closed, green or tipped with red, and fresh-looking. Some yellow showing is fine on its own. That's often just the gland on the side of the cup – it doesn't mean the flower has opened.

Now compare that to a plant where the buds have split open and yellow pollen is dusting the bracts below them. If you see that, or you find bare stubs where cyathia used to be, that poinsettia has been blooming a while already. It may look fine today, but it has less time left than the one beside it with tight buds.

Classic red poinsettias come in a four-pack from Costa Farms, and you can order them on Amazon – and the same quick look at the center works on a plant that shows up at the door.

The green leaves under the bracts deserve a look too. Check for yellow leaves low on the stems, or bare spots where leaves have already dropped. Cold, poor light, and watering trouble can all cause that. Pass on a plant that droops or looks wilted, along with any that have torn, bruised, or creased bracts. Bluish or whitish edges on the bracts can be a sign of cold injury.

Flip a few leaves over as well. Whiteflies, such as the silverleaf whitefly (Bemisia tabaci), are common on poinsettias, and a puff of tiny white insects lifting off when you brush the plant is a bad sign.

Lift the pot if you can. Soggy soil in a heavy pot may mean it's been sitting in water inside its foil, and roots kept that wet can rot. Pay attention to where the plants are displayed, too. Poinsettias crowded by the automatic doors can catch a blast of cold air every time the doors open, and plants left packed in plastic sleeves for days can droop and drop their flowers early – ethylene gas builds up inside the sleeve.

When you get home, use a soil moisture meter, like the Xlux moisture meter available on Amazon, to take the guesswork out of watering.

The journey home is where poinsettias tend to get hurt. When it's below 50°F (10°C) outside, sleeve or wrap the plant before it leaves the store, even for the short walk to the car. Just a few minutes of cold air can damage the bracts and leaves, and the damage may not show right away. Save the garden center for your last stop so the plant isn't stuck in a cold car while you're in other stores, and warm the car up first. Once you're inside, get the sleeve off pretty quickly.

Pick a bright room for your poinsettia. Keep the plant near a window but out of hard, direct sun, and well clear of heat vents, the fireplace, and any door that lets in a draft.

Normal house temperatures work fine, roughly 65 to 70°F (18 to 21°C) during the day, with nights around 60°F (16°C) or a little warmer. Window glass gets cold at night, though, and so can the air right beside it. Set a digital indoor thermometer, like the TempPro TP50, for a night or two, and you'll know for sure.

Stick a finger in the pot before you water. If the top of the soil is dry, go ahead, and if it's still damp, wait a day or so. That shiny foil holds water like a bowl, so poke a few holes in the bottom, or take the pot out and water it over the sink until it drains. You don't need fertilizer while a poinsettia is in bloom.

Pick a plant with tight little buds in the center, keep it somewhere warm and bright, and the color can hold for weeks after the holidays. So the next time you're standing in front of a whole table of red, skip past the brightest one for a second and look at the center first.
