Inter Campus e l'Inter prendono strade diverse dopo 29 anni di storia comune. Una separazione che riguarda uno dei progetti sociali più importanti e riconosciuti del calcio italiano, nato nel 1997 per volontà di Massimo Moratti e diventato nel tempo un punto di riferimento internazionale.

Dal 21 luglio 2026, infatti, la gestione del progetto è passata alla Fondazione We Are The Campus, sostenuta dalle famiglie Moratti e Tronchetti Provera. Un cambiamento significativo, che segna la fine della gestione diretta da parte del club nerazzurro.

Il club ha infatti modificato la propria struttura dedicata alla solidarietà, trasformando Inter Futura nella Fondazione Inter EF-ETS, un ente filantropico che si occupa di sostenere iniziative sociali attraverso risorse economiche, beni e servizi.

La trasformazione era stata deliberata nel dicembre 2024 e la nuova fondazione è stata iscritta al Registro Unico Nazionale del Terzo Settore nel maggio 2025, come risulta dai documenti ufficiali della società nerazzurra.

Inter Campus, invece, ha storicamente basato la propria attività su una presenza diretta nei territori, attraverso programmi educativi e sportivi destinati ai bambini più vulnerabili.

�Il progetto resta intatto, ma cambia la governance e vive parallelo all’Inter».

Il nome dell'Inter e le maglie nerazzurre continueranno infatti a essere presenti nelle attività di Inter Campus, sulla base degli accordi di licenza relativi all'utilizzo del marchio.

L'obiettivo resta quello originario: utilizzare il calcio come strumento educativo, favorendo l'inclusione e garantendo ai bambini il diritto al gioco.

A cambiare è dunque la struttura che gestisce l'iniziativa. Dopo quasi trent'anni, saranno le famiglie Moratti e Tronchetti Provera a portare avanti autonomamente il progetto, mentre l'Inter proseguirà il proprio impegno sociale attraverso la sua fondazione.

How an AI agent schedules my social posts over MCP

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I build Statuz, a scheduler for X, Bluesky, Mastodon, LinkedIn and Reddit. For the last few months most of my own posts haven't been typed into a composer. I ask Claude Code something like "schedule a short thread about today's release for tomorrow 9am, X and Bluesky, show me first", and it does the rest over MCP.

Here's how that works: the tools the agent calls, plain-word times, checks before saving, and why nothing goes out twice.

Statuz started as a CLI. The MCP server isn't a separate API: it registers one tool per CLI command, and the tool name is the command id with dots turned into underscores. statuz posts create is posts_create, statuz posts list is posts_list, and so on.

Each tool carries MCP's hints, so clients can tell reads from writes: posts_list is read-only, posts_delete is destructive. Some commands aren't exposed to agents at all: starting or stopping the daemon and setting secrets stay with the person.

The server also sends one instruction up front: posts_create without an at time publishes immediately, so ask first, or pass draft: true or a time. That line saved me from many accidental posts.

The at field takes an ISO time or words: "tomorrow 9am", "fri 17:30", "in 2 hours". It's parsed in the machine's time zone (or the org's on Statuz Cloud), and it never lands in the past: "9am" asked at 10am means tomorrow. Agents are bad at time zone arithmetic, so letting them pass the words through and having the server resolve them removed a whole class of bugs.

Every post is checked against the limits of every account it's going to before anything is written to the queue. Some real numbers from the repo:

If a post is too long for one destination, the call fails with invalid_input and a message per problem, like Bluesky (you.bsky.social): 342 characters, the limit is 300. The agent reads that, shortens the text and tries again, while it's still in the conversation instead of at 9am the next day when nobody is watching.

The Mac app bundles the same CLI. So the menu bar app, the statuz CLI in my terminal and the MCP server my agent talks to all read and write the same queue. What the agent schedules shows up in the app's calendar, and what I change there is what the agent sees next time it calls posts_list.

statuz mcp serves this computer's Statuz over stdio. X and LinkedIn need your own developer app keys when you run it locally; Bluesky and Mastodon need nothing.

For Claude or ChatGPT, add a custom connector with https://api.statuz.cloud/mcp and sign in. That's Statuz Cloud, which keeps posting while your laptop sleeps. It's pay per published post from prepaid credits (from $5, good for 6 months): $0.01 on Bluesky, Mastodon, LinkedIn and Reddit, $0.03 on X, $0.30 on X with a link. Drafts, scheduling, edits, agent calls and failed posts are free, and there's no subscription.

Then ask for something like: "Schedule three posts about this week's changes, one a day at 9am to Bluesky and Mastodon. Show me the list before saving." A typical run is accounts_list, a few posts_create calls with draft: true, then posts_edit to schedule them once I've said yes.

A Banksy mural with a checkered past has been located by sleuths at the British Broadcasting Service (BBC). The semi-anonymous artist painted the piece in 2018 on Scott Street Bridge in Hull, a port city in East Yorkshire, England. The BBC has discovered that it is being kept in storage in Norfolk, more than 120 miles away, and that its future is unknown.

The mural shows a young boy, apparently dressed for battle in a helmet and raising wooden sword with a pencil attacked to its tip. He cries out “draw the raised bridge!” as he seems to sit on a graffiti writer’s tag. The joke being that the work is adorned on a bridge set to be razed: It’s been closed to traffic since 1994 due to its poor condition, frozen in a raised position. Banksy also plays with the notion of the pen being mightier than the sword, attaching an actual writing implement to a mock weapon. While Banksy does not typically explain his works, some saw a commentary on Brexit; the referendum took place in 2016, and Hull voted 68% to separate from the European Union. The piece was painted over in white by vandals but quickly saved by a fast-acting window cleaner who rushed to save it. Some saw irony in the fact that authorities in Hull, named the UK’s “city of culture” in 2017, failed to protect the piece.

According to a city filing, the Hull City Council said it would pay a company some £60,000 ($79,420) to move the 22-foot-wide mural to the city’s Trinity Market, where it would also build and install a display case for the piece. The city council has not replied to repeated BBC requests as to whether the contract was awarded.

“Nobody will tell us where this painting is and when it’s going to be put on display,” local councilor Rosie Nicola told the news network. “It’s wrong.”

The whereabouts of the sculpture were hinted at in 2025, during a meeting of a committee of the council, when Angela Blake, the city’s assistant director of major projects, was asked where the artwork was. “It’s a secret,” she replied. Chris Jackson, the council’s executive director for regeneration, added: “I’m not sure we’re allowed to say.”

Engineering firm Alan Wood and Partners secured a £900,000 ($1.2 million) contract to dismantle the bridge and preserve the artwork, notes the BBC.

The artist remained anonymous for years, with various rumors of his identity catching fire. In March, Reuters claimed to reveal his given name and backstory.

OpenAI’s GPT-6 Cyber Raises the Price of More Aggressive Security AI

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 12 • Originally published at spaisee.com • Fully Autonomous

For security teams, the buying decision shifts from choosing an AI that explains vulnerabilities to assessing whether a restricted, expensive system can reliably perform the harder validation and exploit-development work. The next test is whether authorized Daybreak Red users show enough time savings, accuracy and manageable supervision to justify the premium over more accessible alternatives.

OpenAI is betting that security teams will pay a steep premium for an AI model that can move beyond code review into exploit research. GPT-6 Cyber promises stronger offensive capabilities, but its restricted rollout and high token prices make the commercial case far from settled.

The VentureBeat report describes GPT-6 Cyber as OpenAI’s latest specialized model for authorized vulnerability research, exploit development and security investigations. The product is entering a market where Anthropic and Google also offer cybersecurity-focused systems, but OpenAI is positioning its model at the more aggressive end of the spectrum.

ExploitGym scores: GPT-6 Cyber vs GPT-5.6 Cyber — see the chart in the original article.

That positioning matters commercially. Security buyers do not necessarily need another model that can explain a vulnerability or suggest a patch. They may pay more for a system that can reproduce a crash, determine whether it is exploitable and produce a working proof of concept. The question is whether those capabilities reduce enough analyst time and operational risk to justify OpenAI’s premium.

1515 Third Street · Coolcaesar · via wikipedia · CC BY 4.0

OpenAI said in its launch announcement that GPT-6 Cyber is built for authorized vulnerability research, exploit development and web application penetration testing. The company says the model can reproduce crashes, construct exploits and operate within the limits of a defined assignment.

OpenAI reports a 48.5% score on its ExploitGym evaluation, compared with 32.1% for GPT-5.6 Cyber. The announcement also cites strong results in web application testing and a separate RemoteExploitBench evaluation. Those measurements are intended to show that the model is not simply better at describing security problems. It is better at carrying out the multi-step work required to validate them.

The company says researchers used GPT-6 Cyber to reproduce 1,724 Linux kernel vulnerabilities and develop working exploits for 948 of them. It also reports 81 high-severity Firefox findings. These figures are central to OpenAI’s commercial argument because they connect benchmark performance with work that security research teams already perform manually.

Mozilla provides some independent context. Mozilla’s Firefox security advisory records numerous high-severity vulnerabilities reported by OpenAI Codex Security. The advisory supports the existence of OpenAI-assisted Firefox findings, but it does not confirm OpenAI’s broader claim of 81 vulnerabilities. That distinction is important when evaluating company-reported performance.

Amazon’s plans to build a data center campus near the Columbia River in Eastern Washington drew pushback Monday at a meeting of the Walla Walla County commissioners.

Some attendees held handmade and printed signs reading “Amazon lies,” “Big tech is shady. No more data centers” and “Water for life, not data,” according to the Walla Walla Union-Bulletin and its photos of the event.

The majority of the roughly 56 public comments were opposed to or had concerns about data center development, including worries about groundwater supplies, effects on agriculture and wildlife, and a lack of transparency, the Union-Bulletin reported. Supporters pointed to potential benefits from job creation and economic development.

The meeting highlighted continued resistance to data centers from communities concerned about higher electricity bills, depleted freshwater supplies and other environmental and social repercussions. Over the past year, tech giants including Amazon and Microsoft have pledged to limit those impacts and invest in host communities, but those commitments have not ended the opposition.

Elected officials are increasingly considering legislation and local ordinances to set standards and require greater transparency — an approach some tech leaders say could help address public concerns.

“There is a role for good laws, and if laws emerge that require that everybody do this work in a responsible way, I think that might be part of the solution,” Brad Smith, Microsoft vice chair and president, recently told reporters at a company event.

The planned Walla Walla County campus is one of two projects that would mark Amazon’s first large-scale data center developments in its home state. The second is in West Richland, part of the Tri-Cities. Amazon already operates smaller data centers in Washington.

The Walla Walla County campus would occupy roughly 134 acres at Wallula Gap Business Park, on property formerly owned by the Port of Walla Walla. Its three proposed single-story data center buildings would create about 120 to 150 full-time jobs, Amazon said.

According to a company fact sheet, the campus would use roughly 14 million gallons of water from the public utility to cool its equipment during its first year of operation. Amazon says it is working with the port to build a system that would replace the utility-supplied cooling water with nonpotable water drawn from the Columbia River. Wastewater from the campus “will meet industrial reuse standards,” the company said.

The campus would also use about 6 million gallons of utility-supplied water annually for employee needs.

Amazon’s fact sheet says the company will cover the electricity costs associated with the data center and that the “expenses aren’t added to the bills of local residents or businesses.”

The company is also working with partners to develop a nuclear power plant using small modular nuclear reactors near the Tri-Cities. The proposed plant could begin operating in the 2030s and “will provide net-new carbon-free energy for communities in the Pacific Northwest, including Eastern Washington,” Amazon said.

Despite Amazon’s assurances, many attendees at Monday’s meeting called for a one-year, countywide moratorium on new data center development, the Union-Bulletin reported. Commissioner Bertha Clayton said she would consider a pause on new applications to give officials time to draft an ordinance to better regulate the developments.

Clayton said zoning changes adopted now would be unlikely to affect the current phase of Amazon’s project. The other two commissioners have previously said they do not support a moratorium, according to the Union-Bulletin.

The proposal is undergoing an environmental review under Washington’s State Environmental Policy Act, or SEPA. The review will assess potential environmental impacts and identify measures to address them. It will also determine whether the project requires a more detailed environmental impact statement.

The proposition is pretty simple: Solar panels generate electricity, batteries store it, and the charger sends it to an EV.

Battery-backed charging is becoming more common because it lets stations deliver more power than their grid connection alone can supply, while also reducing demand on the grid during busy periods. Tesla’s biggest Supercharger site, which it opened earlier this year, also uses solar panels and Tesla's Megapack batteries. Each of BYD's T-shaped Flash chargers is hooked up to two 185-kilowatt-hour storage batteries, for 370 kWh total. The chargers can send up to 1,500 kW through one cable, or up to 1,000 kW when both are in use at the same time.

Adding solar panels into the equation means some or even all of that electricity can be generated on-site. However, BYD hasn’t disclosed the battery capacity or solar output of this new desert installation. So it's not clear how many cars the station can charge—or how quickly—on solar power alone.

How Agents Can Chart a Course for No Buyer Remorse

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The bidding war is over. Some of the decisions made are harder to undo. As an agent or broker, is it part of your professional obligation to discuss where the buyer might be in five years financially and family-wise? Probably, even if it might occasionally put them off a purchase.

A recent survey of 1,000 U.S. property owners found that 80% had at least one regret about their home, location or buying experience. Location bothered 45%, while 58% reported regrets about the home or homeownership itself.

For homeowners who bought during the pandemic frenzy, moving on can carry an added complication: the mortgage may be more attractive than the house.

A recent study in the Journal of Banking & Finance found that for every percentage-point increase between a homeowner’s fixed mortgage rate and current market rates, the probability of selling falls 18.1%. Researchers estimate mortgage lock-in was associated with 1.72 million fewer home sales between Q2 2022 and Q2 2024.

That pressure is particularly relevant now. The average 30-year mortgage rate reached 7.28% in early October, its highest level in nearly three years.

For Nick Nastos, a real estate broker and founder of Chicago’s Property Shop, that creates an unusual form of buyer’s remorse.

“You can regret the house and still have a mortgage you’d hate to give up,” he says. “That’s what makes these decisions difficult. Someone may know the home no longer fits their life, but replacing a 3% mortgage with today’s rate can completely change the math.”

Nastos gives five reasons a pandemic purchase may feel different now.

Nastos says regret alone should not determine whether someone sells.

“Start with what you would change about the house, then price out what changing homes would actually cost,” he says. “Sometimes the answer is to move. Sometimes that low mortgage makes improving the home you already own the better option.”

Suzy Minken, a real estate advisor with Compass working out of Northern Virginia, said looking toward the future while purchasing a house today is key to avoiding buyer remorse.

“It’s not something that is talked about enough,” she says. “I’ve even had buyers speak to my architect (relationships are key) while touring the house to see if they could do an addition. The architect did a review of the property and said it could be done. The buyers submitted an offer (multiple bid situation) and bought the house. They proceeded with a two-story addition before they moved into the house.”

Minken stresses that there is a need to distinguish between buyer’s remorse and the frustration of having fewer options than originally anticipated.

“Consider the young family who purchased a starter home with the intention of moving to a larger home in five years,” she explains. “Perhaps they’ve welcomed another child and need an additional bedroom. But with higher home prices and mortgage rates, the move-up home they envisioned may no longer be financially practical.

“In many ways, today’s move-up buyer has become today’s renovation buyer. Rather than moving to a larger home, homeowners are looking for ways to make their existing homes accommodate their changing needs.”

But what happens when renovation isn’t an option either?

“When buyers fall in love with a home, I encourage them to think about whether it’s already ‘maxed out’ or has room to grow with their changing needs,” says Minken. “Is there room to expand? Could an unfinished basement become additional living space? Is there flexibility in the floor plan to accommodate a growing family?

“I also encourage buyers to look beyond the house itself. Why are the current owners selling? Did they outgrow the home after having another child? Is there something about the layout or the property that made adapting it difficult? These questions can reveal important clues about how a home might serve its next owners.”

Minken encourages buyers to think beyond what they need today and consider how their needs might change over time. That includes family circumstances, the need for additional space and even commuting considerations if their employer requires them to spend more time in the office.

“We can’t predict changes in interest rates, home prices or family circumstances,” she says. “But we can ask the right questions before they make one of the largest financial decisions of their lives. Sometimes the regret isn’t buying the wrong home. It’s discovering that the next step, whether moving to a bigger house or renovating, is no longer within reach.”

Illinois Crypto Tax Draws Unlikely Alliance of DOJ, Banks and DeFi

An unusually broad coalition, spanning the U.S. Justice Department, Wall Street’s biggest lobby, the national Chamber of Commerce and a who’s-who of crypto firms, has thrown its weight behind a legal challenge to Illinois’ first-of-its-kind tax on digital asset transactions.

The groups filed briefs this month in Illinois state court supporting a lawsuit from the Crypto Council for Innovation and the Blockchain Association, which are asking a judge to strike down the state’s Digital Asset Tax Act and permanently block its enforcement.

The tax imposes a 0.2% levy on the full value of a digital asset every time an Illinois customer trades, transfers, or stores it through a broker, even when there’s no sale, gain, or change in ownership.

Critics have called it among the most punitive crypto taxes in the country, in part because Illinois imposes no comparable tax on dollars, gold, stocks or any other asset, and no other state has anything like it.

On Nov. 6, the Justice Department filed a statement of interest arguing the tax undermines federal policy and U.S. leadership in digital assets, calling it vague and discriminatory for singling out crypto for treatment no other asset faces.

The U.S. Chamber of Commerce and the Securities Industry and Financial Markets Association, which represents hundreds of broker-dealers, banks and asset managers, filed an amicus brief warning the tax reaches well beyond crypto-native platforms to the traditional banks, custodians and advisers increasingly offering digital asset products.

A separate brief filed Nov. 11 brought together crypto’s heavy hitters, led by the DeFi Education Fund, Andreessen Horowitz, Paradigm and Uniswap, and including Aave Labs, Chainlink Labs, Solana Policy Institute, Phantom, Jito Labs, Multicoin Capital and Stand With Crypto.

Their filing argues the law’s definitions are so vague they could stretch to software developers and decentralized protocols that hold no customer funds and have no way to identify users or know where a transaction occurred. The brief noted an estimated 2.64 million Illinoisans hold digital assets, the fifth-highest total of any state.

“This tax is unclear, discriminatory, unconstitutional, and harmful to the future of finance,” CCI CEO Ji Hun Kim and Blockchain Association executive Ashok Pinto said in a joint statement.

The challenge dates to August. As Decrypt reported, Illinois agreed in October to pause the tax until July 1, 2027, buying time for the court fight now underway.

10 Greatest Cat-and-Mouse Thriller Movies Of All Time, Ranked

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The world of thrillers hinges around cat-and-mouse stories, with plenty of the best thriller movies there are working as well as they do because of the characters they put up against one other in their narrative. Though just about every genre relies on its heroes and villains, thrillers oftentimes tend to do so slightly more, relying on an evenly balanced protagonist and antagonist to make its surprises as potent as possible.

There's something mythic about seeing two people who seem equally capable of taking one another down going toe-to-toe, as the smallest shift in things can thus bring major results in a story, and the matter of who comes out on top is usually not as simple as it might initially appear.

Indeed, cat-and-mouse thrillers have a penchant for throwing all the conventional rules of film stories out of the window, with villains winning, heroes being corrupted and never redeemed, and nobody being safe from a brutal demise. These are story approaches that carry some major risks with them, but plenty of cat-and-mouse thrillers pull all this and more off well, as these ten movies prove.

One of the highest-rated movies in the generally staggeringly well-received roster of films created by the Coen brothers, 1984's Blood Simple is a neo-noir crime thriller that keeps you guessing right up until its end.

Centered around protagonist Ray - who is having an affair with Abby, his boss' wife, leading said boss to hire someone to kill them both - Blood Simple is able to make a seemingly straightforward premise full of suspense. Everyone in Blood Simple seems to be in crosshairs as a result, with the movie's many twists regarding the deaths and seeming deaths of its central cast keeping you on your toes in a major way.

Unlike most of the other major cat-and-mouse movies, The Prestige doesn't focus on the story of a law enforcement officer and a criminal but rather two stage magicians, whose competition to outdo one another becomes increasingly fraught due to the bad blood and history between them.

The Prestige keeps you guessing in more ways than one with this side of its story, as the movie delivers its narrative in a non-linear form. This allows the film to provide you with information exactly as and when it chooses, and means certain narrative events aren't clear far longer than they would be otherwise, which works to leave a lot of questions lingering in the plot.

With that said, the final plot twists of The Prestige are borderline impossible to predict beforehand, and are sold decidedly well by leading stars Hugh Jackman and Christian Bale, allowing the more decidedly larger-than-life elements of the story to take you fully by surprise.

One of the darker cat and mouse movies out there, I Saw The Devil is an action thriller that veers into outright horror movie territory at times. This is due to the bleak way in which it explores its story of a South Korean National Intelligence Service member hunting down a depraved serial killer, and how the matter marks the protagonist increasingly as the story progresses.

Agent Kim Soo-hyeon's investment in taking down Jang Kyung-chul is made personal by the killer being responsible for the brutal death of his fiancé, Jang Joo-yun. This tees up Soo-hyeon to be willing to stop at nothing to find his target, with it being suggested as time goes on that justice, to him, is making Kyung-chul suffer, rather than simply ending his life.

This makes the conflict that the events of I Saw The Devil hinge around all the more tense, as we're left to question what the endgame of this matter will look like, and whether Soo-hyeon's concept of vengeance will come at the cost of his remaning humanity by the time the film is through.

Despite releasing over 30 years ago, Silence of the Lambs is still one of the first films people tend to go to when they think of talented actors such as Anthony Hopkins or Jodie Foster, with a core strength of the movie being how all its central actors deliver their a-game throughout the story.

Of course, the twisting, turning nature of the investigation to catch serial killer Buffalo Bill certainly helps keep things engaging, and doubly so after Foster's Clarice Starling turns to imprisoned cannibal Hannibal Lecter for guidance on how to find him. Thus begins a mystery not unlike if a Sherlock Holmes movie and Saw were combined, with Lecter providing Starling clues regarding the case, forcing the FBI agent to test her training in every sense before she finds her target.

Those who enjoy Silence of the Lambs also have a subsequent range of other solid works related to the film to enjoy if this core premise works for them, from other films in the Hannibal movie roster, to the Hannibal show.

In one of Harrison Ford's most compelling performances, The Fugitive follows the story of Dr. Richard Kimble, who finds himself hunted by Deputy Samuel Gerard as Kimble seeks to clear his own name regarding the murder of his wife Helen.

Kimble's escape from a prison bus does put him on the wrong side of the law, but the circumstances around his escape leave you rooting for him. At the same time, Kimble somewhat lucking out into being able to escape leaves the viewer also painfully aware that the vascular surgeon isn't exactly a hardened criminal, and sets him up to struggle with staying out of Gerard's crosshairs in a way that many other cat-and-mouse protagonists otherwise don't.

With Dr. Kimble being kind-hearted to the point of risking his own capture to save other lives with his medical know-how, The Fugitive is engaging in that it has both Kimble and Gerard working out who may really be behind things in their own ways, as Gerard gets increasing evidence that the doctor may not be the person he initially anticipated.

The fame and esteem Dirty Harry has as a movie hinges upon the fascinating dynamic between Clint Eastwood's titular protagonist, Inspector Harry Callahan, and the unhinged sniper he spends the movie hunting down known as Scorpio.

Dirty Harry is a classic tale of a hero who doesn't play by the rules and ends up being the only person who can catch a blood-chilling killer, though this places the two characters in a complex cat-and-mouse game wherein both figures play both cat and mouse at different points in the story, leaving you genuinely unsure at points as to whether Scorpio may have bested Harry for good, since the villain is a truly wily one.

As a result, Dirty Harry is one of the most lastingly significant crime thriller movies out there still today, and a film that other releases are still constantly being inspired by, making it even more fascinating to go back to now.

Haunting, engrossing, and a love letter to the world of Westerns in an era that often makes them an afterthought when it comes to cinema, No Country For Old Men is a masterclass in how to make distinctive characters that leave you thinking about them long after the movie ends.

No Country For Old Men's cat-and-mouse story is, to some degree, a cat-and-cat-and-mouse story, as sheriff Ed Tom Bell and hitman Anton Chigurh both seek answers around a case involving a huge amount of money that Llewelyn Moss has found, albeit for very different reasons. This leaves you waiting in suspense for who will be able to solve the mystery of sorts first, and who will get to Moss first, since Bell may be able to save Moss from the danger of facing a hardened criminal instead.

No Country For Old Men forgoes many of the conventional directions that other movies might take such a story in, creating a grittier overall result, and one that feels far more realistic even in its most dramatic moments, much to its advantage.

As the first film to feature famous actors Robert De Niro and Al Pacino on-screen side by side, Heat was perhaps bound to be a hit in some ways whatever its story contained. However, the movie didn't rest on its laurels in this regard, instead crafting a cat-and-mouse story for the ages that pits the two iconic acting figures against one another in its story to great effect.

Heat centers around De Niro's LAPD detective Vincent Hanna, and his efforts to detain Pacino's professional thief Neil McCauley, as well as the gang he recruits to work with him. Unbeknownst to McCauley, said roster includes a far more sinister kind of criminal, meaning the story sees both characters confront their own morals as they strive to outsmart one another, and deal with the impact this situation has on their private lives.

The way in which Vincent Hanna and Neil McCauley's characters reflect one another despite their clear differences takes Heat from being decent to being genuinely engaging, as though they're on other sides of the law, the pair form a kind of mutual respect of sorts that muddies the water for each of them, and complicates their efforts to one up the other.

Seven (or Se7en, as it's also sometimes stylized as) is one of the most well-known crime thrillers following a detective chasing down an active serial killer for good reason, with its twists, turns, and murders being just as significant and harrowing now as they were when the film debuted in 1995.

Seven's title comes from its serial killer harming their victims in a way that reflects the religious concept of the seven deadly sins, with the gluttony victim being killed via being overfed, sloth being confined so as to not move, and so on and so forth. As the body count begins to pile up, Detective Lieutenant William Somerset and David Mills become more and more committed to finding the person responsible, especially since the killer begins to actively taunt them and involve them in their wider plan.

All in all, Seven is a consistently tense experience, being almost impossible to predict in its entirety on your first watch, and repeat watches being even more compelling after you find out how Seven ends and who is responsible for these callous misdeeds.

Catch Me If You Can is in many ways the archetypal cat-and-mouse movie, starring Leonardo DiCaprio and Tom Hanks as a talented con artist and the FBI agent painstakingly tracking him down respectively.

DiCaprio's Frank is believable as someone capable of pulling the wool over the eyes of even many sharp figures, but just inexperienced enough and naive enough that you also can believe that Hanks' Carl is capable of taking him on, given the latter's own skillset. Similarly, the stakes of Catch Me If You Can are balanced just right that the matter of who will come out on top is always a matter of some suspense, especially as it's not always clear whether Frank is conning someone or even actively intending to.

Catch Me If You Can has the edge on other cat-and-mouse thriller movies as well because of its timeline, as its story goes far further event-wise than you may expect it to on a first viewing, and provides an overall picture of the central duo and wider cast that leaves you constantly guessing whether there's something more afoot - and, usually, you're at least partially right.

As Art Week NYC Kicks Off, Galleries Have Millions of Dollars of Art to Sell—And No Shortage of Naras

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Anyone shopping for a Yoshitomo Nara in New York this month has an embarrassment of riches. David Zwirner has filled two Chelsea galleries with the Japanese artist’s paintings. On opening night in Chelsea last week, hours-long lines stretched like highway caravans down 19th and 20th streets, leading to a deluge of posts similarly clogging up the art world’s Instagram feed. The shows have drawings, sculptures, and large new canvases priced as high as $6 million. But bring your checkbook and you might still be out of luck: One source familiar with the exhibition said the largest paintings are being held exclusively for museums. A few blocks away, Loïc Gouzer’s Fair Warning is offering In the Milky Lake / Thinking One (2011), a monumental painting of a little girl standing waist-deep in pale water, her eyes lowered. Made in the year of the devastating Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami in northeastern Japan, where Nara grew up, the painting has appeared in major retrospectives around the world. It carries a $10–$15 million estimate for Fair Warning’s November 19 auction.

There are more Naras to be had: Sotheby’s is offering Candy Blue Night (2001) at The Now and Contemporary Sale next week with a $5–$7 million estimate, while Christie’s will send Miss Forest / Dark Night (2016), a bronze sculpture estimated at $1 million to $1.5 million, to the block at the 21st Century Evening Sale.

Nara’s auction record of $24.95 million was set at Sotheby’s Hong Kong in 2019. Although he hasn’t come close to $20 million since—at auction, anyways—his largest paintings have continued to sell for roughly $5 million to $15 million, even in a challenging market.

The works on offer this season are hardly interchangeable. In age, scale, medium, and importance, they are all different. But the sheer number of options gives us a snapshot of the art market during New York’s first official Art Week and as the November marquee sales draw near. Collectors can buy from galleries, bid at auction, or wait to see what happens before doing either.

That last option is becoming more and more attractive.

“The market’s healthy, but it continues to be measured with a certain amount of awareness, if not suspicion, about the aggressiveness of private pricing,” Alex Glauber, an art advisor and president of the Association of Professional Art Advisors, told ARTnews.

When collectors find a work at a gallery with a comparable example coming up at auction, Glauber said, more than a few prefer to wait for the result before committing. Unless the opportunity is particularly compelling or fleeting, buyers are taking their time.

It’s an awkward situation for dealers, who have spent months preparing exhibitions that open around the same time collectors flock into town for the evening sales. Art Week NYC, a citywide festival organized by ARTnews, Art in America, and Artforum and running today through Saturday, adds another reason to make the rounds. The galleries have certainly given collectors plenty to look at.

At Gagosian’s West 24th Street spaces, two exhibitions opening just days apart could hardly be more different. Julie Curtiss’s “UNREAL,” which opened Tuesday, gives a compellingly surreal vision of New York life. In Makeover (2026), two women in fur coats and sunglasses—Upper East Side types, if you ask me—wear post-op face masks. In other works, an elephant backs into a bodega, and a couple’s legs appear between washing machines. It’s all a bit naughty, in the best way. The paintings are priced roughly between $100,000 and $300,000, according to two sources familiar with the show.

Curtiss’s auction record of $466,000 was set at Phillips in 2021, amid a white-hot pandemic-era market for wet paint. Her market has cooled considerably since then: according to data from ARTDAI, her top result in the past two years was $115,000, achieved at Sotheby’s in May for a painting similar in size to the one sold in 2021.

Two doors down, on Thursday, Gagosian will open “Meine Schule,” an exhibition of Georg Baselitz‘s final paintings and sculptures. The paintings, priced from around $1.5 million to $2.5 million, include upside-down images of the artist and his wife, Elke, drawn in thin black lines against gold backgrounds, with thick bursts of color. Two others return to his childhood in wartime Germany, showing schoolchildren holding hands beneath a Nazi banner.

We’ve seen this stuff before, but that doesn’t mean it isn’t great. The show’s most affecting work may be Strawinsky (2026), a large, white-patinated bronze figure seated with its head hanging forward and limbs collapsing around its body. The sculpture, priced at around $3 million, grew out of Baselitz’s designs for a marionette production of Stravinsky’s The Soldier’s Tale.

During a walkthrough, Stefan Ratibor, a director with the gallery, remembered visiting Baselitz at his studio outside Munich as the artist, working from a wheelchair, directed the final adjustments to the wooden model. Baselitz signed off on the sculpture and died the following night.

The story lends weight to this show as the last that he selected and designed. It also presents Gagosian with a very different commercial proposition from Curtiss: the final works of a major postwar artist whose output is now finite.

In SoHo, Hauser & Wirth has opened Rashid Johnson‘s “Birthday Boys,” on the heels of his major Guggenheim retrospective last year. Five large mosaics made from ceramic tile, mirrors, oyster shells, and other materials depict fractured, doubled figures. They are priced from $750,000 to $1.2 million, according to a gallery price list I was able to get my hands on. (A similar work from 2021, Untitled Broken Crowd, sold for $1.27 million at Christie’s London in June.) A four-and-a-half-minute film about fathers and sons, which has the awkward, endearing quality of Wes Anderson sans the color saturation or the tweeness, is priced at $200,000.

At Skarstedt, Eric Fischl’s “In Pursuit of Pleasure,” opening Thursday, has 13 paintings of people enjoying, or at least attempting to enjoy, their leisure time. There are dog walkers, sunbathers, and yoga enthusiasts, often looking rather isolated despite the company around them. Two large paintings, Goat Yoga and Polished, Professional, are priced at $800,000 each, while Dog Walker is $425,000. Several other works are already marked reserved on the gallery’s price list.

Sean Kelly, meanwhile, is showing Kehinde Wiley’s portraits of New Yorkers, priced from about $100,000 to $400,000. That range is broadly in line with Wiley’s secondary-market prices, according to ARTDAI, though two outlier results have topped it: $649,000 in 2021 and $838,000 in 2024.

It bears mentioning that this is Wiley’s first show in New York since he was accused of sexual assault in 2024, allegations he has denied, and it may serve as something of a trial balloon for whether New York collectors are ready to embrace him as before. That may also be the thinking behind Christie’s and Sotheby’s decisions to offer Wiley portraits in their upcoming contemporary day sales. The Christie’s work, a double portrait of Nelly Moudime and Najaee Hall, carries an estimate of $150,000 to $200,000, while the Sotheby’s lot, depicting an unnamed male figure, has an estimateof $80,000 to $120,000.

Together, the exhibitions offer collectors a range of possibilities well beyond the eight-figure trophies dominating the auction headlines.

Other galleries are taking a less overtly commercial approach. Almine Rech is presenting James Turrell, Greene Naftali has Lubaina Himid, and White Cube is opening a major installation by Doris Salcedo. At 125 Newbury, Mark Rothko and Adolph Gottlieb are paired in an exhibition examining their Surrealist years.

Perhaps the clearest example is Lévy Gorvy Dayan’s exhibition of Francis Bacon and Willem de Kooning. The show is not selling anything. Instead, it brings together works by two postwar giants, demonstrating the sort of scholarship and access that can attract collectors even when there are no prices to negotiate. Rule No. 1 of sales: Get them in the door.

For all the activity, Glauber thinks the traditional May and November auction seasons have become somewhat less important to primary-market galleries than they were during the market’s hotter years. Auction houses have also backed off their onetime emphasis on emerging artists, which has smoothed the friction that came about when a young artist’s work came to auction while a gallery was still trying to establish primary-market prices.

Still, the basic tension remains. A private sale requires only one willing buyer. A strong auction result generally needs competing bidders, making the public sales an imperfect but useful test of demand.

For collectors considering the Naras scattered across Manhattan, that means plenty of opportunities to compare prices, quality, and availability. For dealers, it means making the case that their work is worth buying now, rather than waiting for the next hammer to fall.
