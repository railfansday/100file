Mario Frick savours Aberdeen ‘paradise’ as heavy metal football takes precedence over cynical rave murmurings

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Although he rates it as one of his best football memories, Mario Frick learned a painful lesson after scoring against Scotland at Hampden 16 years ago.

He injured himself in the resulting melee with his Liechtenstein teammates and had to be substituted. “They gave me an injection but it didn't help,” he recalled. "In the seventh minute of stoppage time we lost 2-1 - which is football. It was my last year in international football, maybe my last goal for the international team at around 36-years-old.”

It was a reminder of the perils of over celebrating. The cynic might say the new Aberdeen manager has come to the right place. The Pittodrie side haven’t scored for three matches and have recorded the fewest shots on target in the league this season.

There doesn’t seem much danger of him erupting with joy and bumping his head on the roof of those tight Pittodrie dugouts. Albeit, he will have to wait for the chance to take a bow at the stadium having replaced the sacked Stephen Robinson during the international window. Trips to Rugby Park and then Ibrox await before he can enjoy the comforts of home for the first time against Dundee at the end of this month.

Although there was no game on at Pittodrie on Thursday, Frick's face adorned the jumbo screen in the corner as he was officially welcomed to the club ahead of being “formally unveiled”, as the club described it. Even the empty stadium, he said, gives him “goosebumps”. He regards it as a temple. Nowhere is the reverential air more intoxicating than in the Sir Alex Ferguson Lounge, where Frick was presented to reporters for the first time. “Pure football,” he said, while gazing at the photographs adorning the walls.

The Aberdeen training facility at Cormack Park is better than anything he encountered in Switzerland, where he played and managed. “It is paradise,” he said. He’s clearly keen to linger awhile, to which the reply might be: Good luck with that. While he occupies a unique position as the first Liechtenstein player to become a professional footballer, Frick is slightly less exceptional when it comes to Aberdeen.

The 52-year-old is the 27th permanent manager at Pittodrie, stretching back to 1903. That averages out at about one every four-and-a-half years, which isn’t too bad. However, as many as 16 have come and gone since Ferguson left for Old Trafford 40 years ago this month. The recent anniversary celebrations – Manchester United being the ones celebrating rather than Aberdeen, who have rued the day almost ever since – were a reminder of the significance of that November’s morning in 1986, when it was announced he was heading south.

No club is immune to the difficulties of trying to replace a successful manager, as Manchester United have spent the last 13 years proving. Aberdeen have not been completely feckless without Fergie. A Scottish Cup here, a League Cup there. They haven’t been relegated either. Not yet at least.

Steering the side away from danger is Frick’s first task, starting this weekend against the only team currently below them: Kilmarnock. He knows what’s involved having taken over at his previous club, Luzern, five years ago when they were deep in relegation trouble. They parted company this summer.

“They were bottom of the table with 18 matches and 11 points when I took over there and the whole environment was similar to now,” he said. “We saved them and progressed from there. I am sure I can do the same now at Aberdeen. I am very ambitious and now it is important to get out of this situation and we want to go to the top six.”

The noises coming out of Pittodrie in recent days is that Frick likes to play ‘front foot, attacking football’, which seems to mirror the promises made by every new manager these days. Isn’t that what Sebastien Pocognoli trumpeted when he was unveiled as Scotland manager three months ago? The alarm bells might be growing louder for Aberdeen fans. Frick explained his “heavy metal football” philosophy. “It is high pressing, it is counter pressing, it is vertical play and creating many goalscoring chances,” he said. “That is heavy metal.”

Supporters will argue that anything’s better than what a team coached by Frick’s predecessor was producing. Aberdeen chief executive Adrian Bevington referred to the new manager's “warm personality” and suggested local journalists should look forward to getting to know the new man. Frick is an urbane character though one it might be advisable to avoid crossing. Trim and sporting a military haircut, he doesn’t look as if he will accept having his authority challenged.

As for the vexed question of the captaincy, with Kevin Nisbet giving the impression that the responsibility weighs heavily on him, Frick confirmed the striker will retain the armband against Kilmarnock. “Afterwards, I will have to decide what we do,” he said.

Frick speaks five languages with various levels of proficiency. His German and Italian are perfect. “English I need to improve,” he said. “I started learning English in school but never used it.” He recently started English tuition online “with an Australian who lives in France” with a view to working at a British club. “My only ambition now is to bring success to Aberdeen,” he said.

The fans will hope that such a zeal for self-improvement is catching. When Ferguson once ordered his players to have no fears about going to Glasgow and winning, now a sense of trepidation infects so much at the club. Aberdeen have just announced that a “Hacienda Classical” rave night event at Pittodrie on 28 May will no longer be going ahead. Someone queried online whether it was a case of the calendar needing to be left clear for relegation play-offs. Confidence is rock bottom.

Frick relishes the challenge. “I am ready!” he said. “I have had a five-month holiday! I am full of energy.”

This 16-Year-Old Founded an AI Missile Startup. He’s Targeting ‘Cost per Kill’

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

At 16 years old, Henrijs Dandzbergs is too young to vote. Or drink. Or smoke. Or drive unsupervised. But he’s not too young to win half a million dollars for his AI-guided-missile startup.

In September, Dandzbergs, who was born in Latvia, was admitted to Y Combinator’s fall 2026 cohort. The startup accelerator counts companies like Airbnb, Stripe, DoorDash, and Reddit among its alumni; Dandzbergs is likely the youngest solo founder in its vaunted history.

His startup, Polemos Industries, is attempting to develop a new class of precision-guided missile that can be easily and cheaply manufactured, can be fired from portable launchers, and can resist jamming attempts. “I think missiles are the coolest thing,” Dandzbergs tells WIRED. “A lot of people that have found success in engineering early on have had a passion for blowing stuff up.”

In conversation, Dandzbergs is poised and articulate. He talks about “attacking hard problems,” breaking things down to “first principles,” and how the Ukraine conflict has forever altered the theater of war—a personal fixation, given his country's proximity to Russia. He projects an unassailable confidence in his own abilities.

“He’s a pretty exceptional person,” says Harj Taggar, a YC partner assigned to mentor Dandzbergs. “I’m still somewhat in disbelief that we’re talking about a 16-year-old.”

Dandzbergs has resolved to apply these unusual talents to figuring out how to optimize targeted killing. His startup’s guiding ambition, according to its investor one-pager, is to bring down the “cost per kill.”

As a young(er) kid, Dandzbergs was rarely in one place for long; his family moved between Latvia, Indonesia, Australia, and Spain. His surfer father, part owner of an insurance company, likes to go wherever he might find the raddest waves.

Early in his schooling, which mostly took place online, Dandzbergs demonstrated a clear talent for science and math. His room started to look like a lab, littered with schematics, robotics, Raspberry Pis, and 3D-printed components.

When Covid struck, Dandzbergs’ family returned to Latvia. His parents enrolled him in Astra Nova, an Elon Musk–linked school where students undergo an experimental curriculum built around lateral thinking and problem-solving instead of rote memorization. Dandzbergs took a class taught by a NASA employee and became fascinated by aerospace engineering. His lab creations became ever more elaborate: a bionic arm, a thermal control system for a model rocket, sensors for a self-flying drone.

Around this time, Russia invaded Ukraine. In Latvia, Dandzbergs started to see military jets overhead, and stray missiles were intercepted at the Russian border. With the sovereignty of Eastern European nations under threat, new defense startups began to pop up across the region. Dandzbergs wanted in.

“A patriotic intention to protect my country, European countries, and Ukrainian and American soldiers, and understanding the future of autonomous warfare—that’s my drive,” he says.

To stretch his technical skills, Dandzbergs started to take part in hackathons, where software and hardware engineers compete to develop the most sophisticated prototype from scratch.

In April of this year, he flew to London to take part in a hackathon held by Unicorn Mafia, an invitation-only group of developers. The prize included $10,000 in cash, a plane ticket to San Francisco, and free legal assistance with O-1 visa applications to stay in the US.

Less than 48 hours later, Dandzbergs and his two teammates, Aleksandrs Tihenko and Kai Yang, had built an AI system that could design hardware circuit boards from natural language prompts, much like how a software engineer might use AI to generate code. Though his teammates are both a few years older, Dandzbergs pushed for them to include even more intricate features in the prototype. “It was amazing,” says Tihenko. “The imagination of Henrijs in the hardware space is just crazy.”

Seeing that they stood no chance of beating Dandzbergs’ group and other front-runners, teams started to drop out of the competition, according to Fergus McKenzie-Wilson, Unicorn Mafia’s director and one of the hackathon judges. “What they built was incredibly impressive,” he says. “No one had ever seen that before.”

When the organizers crowned Dandzbergs’ team victorious, somebody handed him the mic. “I’m 16 years old,” he said.

The week after the hackathon, Dandzbergs and his teammates pitched their circuit board concept to YC. They won a place in the summer program, but Dandzbergs wavered over whether he should drop out of high school. The trio also sparred over whether the startup should focus on applying the technology to the defense sector, Dandzbergs’ primary area of interest. Dandzbergs ended up pulling out of the partnership. “It was hard,” says Tihenko. “But ultimately we just settled that the best decision for the company would be to part ways and focus on different things.”

By the summer, Danzbergs was set on building a defense startup and reapplied to YC on his own. He had spotted a gap in the market, he claimed, for AI-guided missiles that were more precise and could carry bigger warheads than the drones deployed widely in Ukraine. His products, he added, would be cheaper and easier to manufacture than hulking great rocket systems.

YC initially tried to steer him away. Dandzbergs was promising a lot of everything, all at once. It wasn’t clear whether his idea could work or whether pulling out of school was the right thing for him to do. “There’s a paternal element to it,” says Taggar. “You don’t want to be screwing up people's lives—and working on a startup is a pretty serious thing.”

Dandzbergs is up against a fleet of defense companies—from Anduril to CoAspire to Leidos—aiming to develop cheaper, mass-producible missiles. The more sophisticated the guidance system, the more difficult that task becomes.

“You can build a cheap missile that doesn’t go very far. You can build a cheap missile that doesn’t carry much of a payload. You can build a cheap missile that has a low reliability rate,” says Tom Karako, director of the Missile Defense Project and senior fellow at the Center for Strategic and International Studies. “There are trade-offs.”

But Dandzbergs wasn’t going to be dissuaded, and YC figured that somebody else would probably invest in his idea. The incubator invited him to join its fall program. “Big picture, you never know if these things are possible or not,” says Taggar. “Is it a non-zero-percent chance that it could be possible? That’s frankly the threshold, especially if it’s a really big [idea].”

So far, according to Dandzbergs, Polemos Industries has developed the software system that will guide its missiles and has begun to hammer out elements of the hardware design. By the time the YC program finishes in December, he hopes to be able to showcase a prototype missile.

If he does ultimately succeed, though, Dandzbergs will need to confront an assortment of hairy ethical quandaries. Who should have access to these new missiles? What if a technical error were to result in friendly fire? Can he make peace with his technology being used to end somebody’s life?

Dandzbergs claims that he has come to terms with these issues. He says he takes solace in the belief that his motivations are pure: to protect the US and its European allies.

By his logic, autonomous weapons are an inevitable part of the future, so he might as well be the one who builds them. “I choose not to shy away from the emergence of modern conflict as a global issue. I simply don’t have a lot of respect for people that do,” he says. “I want to be part of it. I want to move with the future of warfare.”

But others who have found themselves in a similar position say it’s difficult to know in advance how somebody will deal with the knowledge that their invention has killed people.

McKenzie-Wilson, the Unicorn Mafia director, launched a drone startup in his early twenties. Initially, he had meant for the drones to evacuate wounded soldiers, but after a missile company invested, his startup retooled its technology to drop payloads too. He left around the same time.

“I don’t think I was emotionally mature enough,” he says. “[Dandzbergs] will realize at some point. He’ll either have an issue with it or he won’t.”

Polemos Industries announced itself publicly on November 5 with a glossy social media video. Set to the ticking of a clock, it featured a set of four missiles fired into the sky from ground launchers, then blowing a convoy of four military vehicles to smithereens.

The video climbed to the top of the trending page on X. In the responses, a couple of people tagged Palmer Luckey, cofounder of Anduril, who was 19 when he started his first company. “Coming for him,” Dandzbergs responded.

Dandzbergs recognizes that people are likely to dismiss him for his age. But he considers youth one of his biggest advantages. “Please don’t say I’m sacrificing my childhood,” he says. “This is the childhood I dreamed of.”

Mark Noble Reveals Important Change Behind West Ham’s Transfer Plans

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mark Noble has provided an interesting insight into how West Ham United’s recruitment operation is working under Scott Parker, revealing that Nils Koppen is in regular contact with the coaching staff about the players the club needs.

Speaking in an interview with West Ham’s official website, Noble explained that the Director of Player Recruitment is arriving at Rush Green as early as 6.30am, watching training and discussing the squad with Parker and his coaches.

With the January transfer window approaching and West Ham desperate to secure promotion, it’s encouraging to hear that recruitment discussions are taking place. But for me, the most important part of Noble’s revelation has very little to do with what time Koppen arrives for work.

Quite frankly, whether Koppen arrives at half six in the morning is neither here nor there.

Much of the analytical and recruitment work can be carried out perfectly well from wherever somebody can plug into the internet.

What I find far more important is the open dialogue Noble has described.

He explained that Koppen watches training and that the coaching staff speak openly with him about the players they believe West Ham need.

Koppen then works with the club’s scouts and analysts in the background, allowing Parker and Noble to concentrate on coaching.

That strikes me as a huge plus and potentially a massive step in the right direction.

It also appears to represent a departure from the way things have operated previously, when questions were regularly raised about whether recruitment decisions matched the manager’s requirements.

As we discussed following Parker’s talkSPORT interview, the manager is absolutely right to concentrate on improving the players already at his disposal rather than becoming consumed by January transfers.

But that only works if the recruitment department understands precisely what he needs.

We know from our recent dialogue with a club insider that Koppen has begun looking for another striker, and Noble’s comments suggest those discussions are taking place alongside the coaching staff’s assessment of the squad.

Ultimately, I’m far more interested in whether Koppen signs the right players than whether he’s the first person through the door at Rush Green.

But if the people coaching the team and those recruiting for it are finally speaking openly about what West Ham need, that has to be encouraging.

Service Worker Keeps Stopping? That's the Contract, Not a Bug

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The message in the console is not an error. Service worker registration successful followed by silence is the designed behavior of Manifest V3, and the sooner that stops feeling like a crash, the sooner the bugs get easier.

These are the questions I answer most often, in the order they usually arrive.

Chrome terminates an idle background service worker after roughly 30 seconds without extension events. A single event may run for up to five minutes, but a pending setTimeout does not count as activity. The worker is a request handler, not a daemon.

Usually not. Open chrome://serviceworker-internals, find your extension, and read the lifecycle entries. A normal stop says the worker became idle and was terminated; a crash includes an error and a stack. If you see no exception, you are looking at the lifecycle doing its job.

Each worker incarnation starts with a fresh console. If you only log to console.log, the previous incarnation's output is gone when the new one starts.

Now the popup, or a debug page, can show how many times the worker restarted and when. That single field explains most "it randomly resets" reports.

The popup is a separate context. While it is open, messages and port connections can keep the worker alive, but closing the popup ends that. A long-running job that only worked while the popup was open was never a background job.

There is no supported way. Keepalive loops — a message every 20 seconds, an offscreen document created for no reason, a silent audio element — are detectable, burn battery, and are exactly what the platform was designed to prevent. They postpone termination; they do not survive a browser restart or a suspension.

Events that wake the worker include runtime messages, action clicks, alarms, web navigation, storage changes from other contexts and install/startup events. A timer callback is not an event; it is a callback in a heap that no longer exists. Alarms are the bridge between "later" and "an event".

Use a boot id and a sequence number per job. If the job record shows attempts: 2 and the boot id changed between them, you have proof of a restart. Without that, you are guessing from timestamps.

Deadlines, job identity, status and attempt counts. Not remaining seconds, not interval handles, not "isRunning" booleans without a lock timeout. The full model is a small record, and it is cheaper than every workaround combined.

Alarms are approximate and have a 30-second floor, so a feature that needs exact sub-minute scheduling without an open UI has no good answer in MV3. That is a real product constraint, not a misunderstanding.

vite-mv3-sw-compile-persist softens the migration: long timers become alarm-backed wrappers and marked state is restored before handlers run. It does not make the worker persistent, does not support destructured state, and cannot recover anonymous callbacks that captured locals. Anyone promising otherwise is selling a keepalive hack with better branding.

Keep the record small; a large blob read on every wake costs more than the bug it prevents.

Your "49 KB" file is actually 50.5 KB. Blame the kilobyte.

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A user picks a file. Their computer says 49 KB. Your form says "max 50 KB." The upload gets rejected anyway, and now there's a support ticket that says your site is broken.

It isn't broken, exactly. You and the user just disagree about what a kilobyte is. And honestly? Both of you are right.

There are two definitions in everyday use:

The IEC prefixes (KiB, MiB, GiB) exist precisely to separate the two. Almost nobody uses them in a UI, though. So "KB" on a screen could mean either one, and you usually can't tell which by looking.

Windows Explorer uses 1,024 and calls it KB. Raymond Chen explained why on Microsoft's own blog years ago: everybody else said KB for 1,024 too, so Explorer followed existing practice. That's still the behaviour.

macOS went decimal. Apple's support page on storage capacity says OS X Leopard and earlier reported sizes in binary, which means everything from Snow Leopard (10.6) on counts 1 KB as 1,000 bytes. Same file, different number than on Windows.

Linux / GNU coreutils gives you both. ls -lh, du -h and df -h use powers of 1,024. Add --si and you get powers of 1,000. Here's a 51,000-byte file with coreutils 9.7:

Notice the capital K versus lowercase k. That's the only hint you get. Also notice -h printed 50K for something that's 49.8 KiB, so it rounded up. Rounding is its own little trap on top of everything else.

On a machine doing 1,024 math, that looks like a "49 KB" file. If your server rejects anything over 50,000 bytes, it fails. The user isn't lying and your code isn't buggy. You're just using different rulers.

The 51,000-byte file from above works the same way: 51.0 KB in decimal, 49.8 KiB in binary.

HTML can't enforce this for you. supports accept, capture and multiple, and that's it. There's no size attribute. Any size limit lives in JavaScript or on the server, and the server is the one that counts.

PHP: upload_max_filesize and post_max_size accept shorthand. The PHP manual says plainly that 1K = 1,024 bytes and 1M = 1,048,576 bytes, and even adds a note that the IEC would call this a kibibyte. So upload_max_filesize = 50K means 51,200 bytes.

nginx: client_max_body_size 50k also means 51,200 bytes. The docs list k/K as kilobytes without saying how many bytes that is, but the parser in nginx's source (ngx_parse_size) multiplies by 1,024. Go over and you get a 413.

So here's the fun bit. A PHP or nginx config that says "50K" allows 51,200 bytes, while a hand-written check like if (size > 50000) allows 50,000. Two different limits, both called "50 KB." I'd bet plenty of apps have exactly this mismatch between their help text and their config.

One more wrinkle. nginx's client_max_body_size applies to the whole request body, not the file. PHP's post_max_size covers all the POST data too. A multipart/form-data body wraps each file in boundary lines plus Content-Disposition and Content-Type headers (RFC 7578 describes the format).

How much does that add? Sending that 51,000-byte file with curl -F produced a 51,199-byte body. About 200 bytes of overhead, which grows with longer field names, filenames and extra fields. Against a 51,200-byte nginx limit, that upload squeaks through by one byte. A file a couple hundred bytes bigger, still "under 50K" by any reading, gets a 413.

That check is for the user's benefit only. Keep the server check, since anyone can skip your JavaScript.

The main thing is to stop writing "KB" alone anywhere a limit gets enforced. Bytes are boring. Boring doesn't generate tickets.

Ravi Agrawal: Let’s start with the U.S. point of view on the Iran war. The consensus has been that Trump has three options. He could escalate militarily, but that has very uncertain outcomes. He could spin a victory and get out, but the spin is . Or he could focus on diplomacy and try to return to the memorandum of understanding (MOU) we had last summer, but that is a lot of hard work. What do you think the White House strategy is now that the midterms are over?

Vali Nasr: I think the president is actually pursuing all three strategies at the same time. Leaning in on diplomacy does not mean that he’s setting aside war plans, and asking for war plans does not mean that he is doing less diplomacy. He doesn’t have any good choices.

Ideally, Trump would end this war through a negotiated settlement, much like what happened in June, but he has to meet the Iranian price for doing that, which is actually quite steep for him. Currently, the positions of the two are too far apart. The Iranians want him to first go back to the MOU and implement it before they talk about anything else, such as the nuclear issue. Trump, on the other hand, needs the nuclear issue to be at the forefront in order to justify why he went to war for nine months.

War is also not really an option, because in order for war to be meaningful, Trump has to do something big. But something big opens the door to Iranian retaliation, particularly in the Persian Gulf, which could devastate the global economy in larger ways. I’m sure Trump is hearing from Chinese President Xi Jinping and Arab, Chinese, and Russian leaders that nobody wants that kind of damage to the global economy. So he’s settling for the third option, which is to let Iran sit under the blockade and do nothing. In my opinion, that’s not a strategy. That’s twiddling his thumbs.

The blockade is not foolproof. Iran is getting oil out and making money. At the same time, the Strait of Hormuz is still under a lot of pressure. The Iranians have actually increased the number of attacks they’re making there. Furthermore, I don’t think it’s sustainable, financially or otherwise, for the U.S. Navy to maintain the blockade long term. It is quite financially and labor intensive for the Navy to do so, and it doesn’t have any bases in the Persian Gulf that could provide relief. If the Iranians are able to sustain themselves with the amount of holes that already exist, then, ultimately, this is not sustainable.

In fact, if the blockade starts working in the way in which the U.S. treasury secretary and U.S. secretary of state say it will, then Iran is likely to escalate. So ultimately, this strategy that everybody thinks is settled on is not tenable in the long term. Either it will collapse under its own weight, or one or the other party is bound to disturb it, which might bring us back to war.

RA: I imagine that you could have said a lot of this two weeks ago. The big change that has come in the last couple of weeks is, of course, the U.S. midterm election results. Come January, the Democrats will have power in both the House of Representatives and the Senate. They feel that part of the mandate from the American people is trying to find a way to end the war. What is your sense of how Congress might impact how Trump tries to prosecute the end of this war, or dilly-dally, as you said?

VN: One outcome of these elections is a much more visible push within the United States against the war. It’s not just that the American people have spoken. If there was any shadow of a doubt that they really, really disliked this war, it has been dashed, particularly within the Republican Party. They might now think that if the Republican Party is to have any chance in 2028, this war should end now.

So there is a sense that there is more momentum for diplomacy after the election than there was before. In fact, when U.S. Vice President J.D. Vance wrote to Iran’s leadership, everybody said, “That’s exactly what we thought the consequence would be of the drubbing the administration got.”

But Trump is unpredictable. He has his own view of what’s in his interest as well as what’s in America’s interest. He may go back to a conflict with Iran, so we’re not out of the woods. But the Iranians know that the economic cost of the war is having a tangible political impact on America, as well as France and Germany, even if it is not being reflected in the stock market on a daily basis. Ultimately, these countries are likely to decide at some point that this war is not worth the political blowback it’s causing.

RA: I was going to come to how Tehran is gaming out the post-midterms scenario. You make the point that Iran may sense that it is getting some of what it wants—it is imposing costs. With nothing to lose, he may now be impervious to electoral pressure, meaning the Iranians may not have much leverage with him.

VN: That’s very true. This is a gamble. It doesn’t mean that because the elections happened, the Iranians can declare victory and say, “OK, we humiliated President Trump, now let’s sit down and talk.” Yes, they had a big win in terms of the midterm results, but now they have to deal with Trump.

I think the way they see it is that the decision to go to war is still uncomfortable for President Trump. It’s true that he’s not looking at another election, but the Republicans are looking at 2028. And ultimately, the longer this war goes on, the more the pressure it brings about, particularly if the next phase of the war is more destructive. The Iranians are broadcasting that loud and clear as a form of deterrence. They’re saying they’re going to wreak havoc on the region and the global economy, no holds barred.

If that’s the case, then President Trump still has to think about his legacy. He has to think about the future of the Republican Party. And he has to consider that if he enters into a wider war with Iran and he still isn’t able to defeat it, the scale of his failure will be even greater than it is now.

Until now, the most important factor on the American side hasn’t been some kind of a calculated, rational, technocratic national interest. It has been the psychology of one man from the get-go of deciding to go to war, to Trump’s various permutations.

Nobody—whether it’s the United States’ allies or Iran or China—can say with any certainty how Trump might actually act. So I think the Iranians are taking this one step at a time, reading the tea leaves and applying pressure where they can with Congress—particularly the new Congress coming in—to see whether they can avoid a big war and get a settlement that would benefit them.

The reasons they point to are that the amount of Gulf crude going through Hormuz has actually dropped significantly, from 83 percent to 60 percent, because Gulf states are using pipelines more and relying less and less on Hormuz. I don’t see a sign that the degree of pressure that this administration thinks it is imposing on Iran is actually being felt there and changing attitudes. When Iran sent its diplomats to the United Nations, the proposal they presented to the United States was an expansion of the Islamabad-brokered MOU, changed in ways that benefited Iran more. The Iranians referred to it as “Islamabad-Plus.”

Let’s also consider the fact that when Mecca defense alliance members and Pakistan’s army chief went to Tehran in October, Iran’s response to Vice President Vance’s letter was that Iran was sticking to its demands that Washington must fulfill all seven preconditions to resuming talks and reopening Hormuz. The United States’ pressure tactics are not working.

This administration doesn’t want to go to war or make a deal with Iran. They need to sell this “do nothing” strategy as a viable, working strategy, so they’re repackaging the blockade and saying it’s going to work this time. Two months ago, U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent was promising that there would be a run on the Iranian banks. That hasn’t happened. So I’m suspicious of these kinds of claims.

Iran is under a tremendous amount of pressure, but I don’t think the administration has a gauge of where the tipping point for them is. So it’s throwing a Hail Mary pass with more sanctions, hoping that they will do the trick, and then we get a lot of rhetoric trying to sell the current holding pattern as an actual strategy.

RA: So in response to everything you’re saying, Tehran’s strategy might be to wait out the next two years.

VN: If they have to, yes. Although, I don’t know. Iran’s supreme leader said in his last major statement that this “no war, no peace” situation is not sustainable or acceptable to Iran.

The number of mines installed in the Strait of Hormuz has also increased. More oil might go out on some days than others. So the Iranians are making the situation more unstable.

There is a difference between the way the West and Iran look at this situation. The U.S. and many observers think a move from Iran has to be really big to be meaningful. For instance, if they can’t bring the cost of oil to $200 a barrel, then they’ve lost.

But the Iranians actually do not look at things this way. Very early in the war, they understood that the price of oil is not 100 percent in their hands. What they believe works is sustained instability in the energy market. The costs of oil, natural gas, jet fuel, diesel, fertilizer, and so on are far above where they were. None of these measures by themselves will break the global economy, but if you put them together for long enough, they’re very effective.

Iran sees the result of the U.S. elections as proof of this concept. Their strategy is to boil the frog gradually. I think sometimes in the West we look at this and say, “Well, the frog is just sitting there and nothing’s happening to it, so the Iranians have failed.” So I think this is a race between whether Iran runs out of gas first or its strategy works.

Right now, I detect a certain degree of confidence from Iran. I don’t see the trepidation that observers on the outside are assuming the regime has. So either Iran has a good poker face, or it’s seeing the picture very differently than Washington does.

RA: It’s fascinating, and it’s always so hard to know whom or what to believe.

VN: We’re in the midst of a war, and there’s a lot of disinformation. There are people in the United States or in Israel who want the United States to go back to war with Iran, to “finish the job,” so to speak. After the elections, which made it very obvious that the American public is firmly opposed to this war, you could only change that public opinion in favor of war if there was this kind of threat against America, or if you could posit that Iran is a deranged and crazy regime that must be taken out. So it could possibly be disinformation.

On the other hand, Iranians have also been taking certain actions as a form of deterrence. I don’t know whether Iran actually launched a cyberattack on refrigerators in France—French intelligence claims they did, though the Iranians deny it—but it’s a message. “If you attack us, then we have cyber capabilities that could paralyze your cities.”

It’s a kind of deterrence tactic. It’s a similar case with Iran issuing warnings to air bases in the U.K., which ultimately got the United States to move its airplanes from two different air bases. Some of this benefits Iran, because it’s a way of saying, “Do not assume that the next war would be cost-free. Our reach is much farther than you assume.”

It also suggests to me that Iran doesn’t want to go back to war. Rather, its game plan is to try to get the United States to come to the table to accept their terms.

New Homes Offer More Affordability for Buyers: Report

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

With affordability remaining an issue at large in housing, new homes continue to represent a tempting alternative in a challenging market.

The latest data from the National Association of Home Builders (NAHB) found that affordability for new homes actually improved more than that of existing homes in Q3, focused on both home prices and rates.

The Q3 NAHB/Wells Fargo Cost of Housing Index (CHI) found that families earning the national median income of $106,800 needed 33% of their income to buy a median-priced new home, compared to 36% of their income for a median-priced existing home.

Low-income families (those who earn only 50% of the national median income) need 66% of their income to buy a new home, compared to 73% for an existing home.

NAHB Chairman Bill Owens noted that this was the “widest affordability advantage (for new homes) since the CHI began,” as builders “are working hard to offset rising costs.”

The report also noted that this is due in part to the difference in price between new homes ($400,400 median price) and existing homes ($416,700 median price), called out as the widest national median price gap since the CHI began.

The other attribution for the new home “affordability advantage” is the incentives and deals offered by builders.

Owens called out NAHB’s recent data on builder confidence, which saw 43% of builders offer price cuts in October—the highest share since 2020. Additionally, 65% of builders offered sales incentives.

A recent Realtor.com® report also found that many buyers purchasing new homes are seeing lower mortgage rates due to builders offering buy downs or other deals with mortgage lenders. The average starting mortgage rate of a new-home buyer was 5.01% in Q3, compared with 6.15% for existing-home buyers.

While new homes are offering better affordability than existing homes, overall housing affordability is still very much challenged.

For both new and existing homes, families earning the national median income remain fairly cost-burdened (paying more than 30% of their income for housing) or severely cost-burdened (paying more than 50% of their income for housing).

Of the 175 metro areas tracked in the report, 87 are cost-burdened by housing, and eight are severely cost-burdened. Leading the way among the eight severely cost-burdened markets is San Jose, California, where families use 79% of their income on housing.

Owens outlined that in order to move housing affordability in the right direction, “policymakers must do more to reduce red tape, address labor shortages and fix supply chains so builders can expand the nation’s housing supply.”

“As builders contend with rising construction costs, tighter lending standards and persistent labor shortages, policymakers should remove regulatory obstacles, enact permitting reform and create a stronger business environment that enables builders to produce more attainable, affordable homes,” added NAHB Chief Economist Robert Dietz.

Newborn stars are hiding in the red threads of a neighboring galaxy

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Webb peers into N159, where dusty filaments shelter some of the youngest stars in a neighboring galaxy.

Today’s Image of the Day takes us inside a cloud where stars are being born in a neighboring galaxy.

In this view from the James Webb Space Telescope, pale blue gas fills broad cavities, orange pillars jut from their edges, and dark red threads wind across the middle.

Some of the youngest stars in the scene are still tucked inside those threads.

The cloud, known as N159, lies about 161,000 light-years away in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a satellite galaxy of the Milky Way.

Webb’s image spans about 230 light-years. Across that distance, some stars are still gathering the material they need to grow, while others already shine fiercely enough to change the shape of the cloud around them.

Start with the pale blue areas. They trace hydrogen gas exposed to intense ultraviolet light from massive stars.

That radiation can knock electrons loose from hydrogen atoms. As the electrons rejoin hydrogen nuclei, the gas gives off light of its own.

A bright patch can therefore reveal where massive stars are affecting their surroundings, even if the glow itself comes from the gas.

Those stars also send streams of particles into the cloud. Over time, these stellar winds can help hollow out cavities and sculpt the denser material along their edges.

Look closely at where the blue meets the orange. Small pillars extend into the open spaces, marking places where pockets of dense gas and dust have persisted as material around them has been cleared away.

The bright knot on the left is the Papillon Nebula, an unusually compact pocket of glowing hydrogen that the Hubble Space Telescope first resolved in 1999.

In Webb’s wider view, it takes up only a small part of the frame. Its intense glow draws the eye, but the quieter areas around it hold some of the best clues to where stars are forming now.

One of the best places to zoom in is just above the blue cavity on the right. Thin red strands cross this part of the frame.

They trace molecular hydrogen, a form of the gas in which two hydrogen atoms are bound together.

Deep inside dense strands like these, gas and dust can shield developing stars from the radiation and winds of their more powerful neighbors.

Webb’s Near-Infrared Camera records light at wavelengths beyond what our eyes can see.

Some of that light gets through dust that blocks visible starlight, revealing points of light buried in the cloud.

Image processors assign visible colors to observations made through different filters.

Here, those colors distinguish the red threads of molecular gas from the orange glow of carbon-based molecules and the pale blue of ionized hydrogen.

Astronomer Nolan Habel and colleagues compared the brightness of more than 300,000 points of light across ten infrared filters.

Some gave off more infrared light than expected from a star alone. That extra light can come from surrounding dust, which absorbs energy from the star and radiates it back into space.

Many of the sources the researchers identified as possible young stars lined up with dusty strands and clouds of molecular gas.

In a preprint describing the observations, the team reported that the eastern part of N159 contains more developed groups of stars, while the western part shows signs of more recent star formation.

Some groups may be less than half a million years old. That difference suggests that star formation unfolded in stages across the cloud.

The Large Magellanic Cloud has a smaller proportion of elements heavier than helium than the Milky Way does.

The early universe was also poorer in those elements, which built up over generations of stars.

Studying a region like N159 gives astronomers a chance to see how stars form with a mix of ingredients closer in some respects to what was available long ago.

The image also captures a change that is easy to miss at first glance. In one part of the cloud, stars have already opened clearings around themselves.

In another, dense gas still shelters stars that are just getting started. Given time, much of that gas will disperse, leaving the stars exposed.

For now, the thin red strands mark places where some of the newest stars in this corner of a neighboring galaxy are still taking shape.
