Montefiore Einstein Comprehensive Cancer Center Awarded $10 Million Grant to Investigate Breast Cancer Relapse

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Collaborative research projects will focus on studies of cancer dormancy and metastasis

BRONX, N.Y., Nov. 3, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- The National Cancer Institute (NCI) has awarded a multidisciplinary and multi-institutional team led by Julio A. Aguirre-Ghiso, Ph.D., of Montefiore Einstein Comprehensive Cancer Center (MECCC), a five-year, $10 million grant to uncover the biological processes that drive breast cancer relapse and identify new opportunities to predict and prevent recurrence.

Up to an estimated 685,000 people die from breast cancer globally each year, according to the NCI, with nearly all fatal cases resulting from disease that has returned in other organs years or even decades after surgery, chemotherapy, or radiation. Research suggests that even a small, localized tumor may have already generated micrometastasesâsmall amounts of cancer cells that have spread beyond the original tumor but cannot be detected by screening or diagnostic tests. Because these cells may have traveled beyond their initial tumor location, they can remain in the body after the primary tumor is removed or treated, lie dormant for years, and later "reawaken" and cause breast cancer to recur.

Exploring Cancer Dormancy The new research will address one of the least understood and most consequential areas of cancer biology: the characteristics of early disseminated cancer cells that survive surgery and other treatments by remaining dormant. Understanding these cells' distinguishing features could help scientists detect them and develop therapies that eliminate them before the cancer returns.

"For two decades, we have been pioneering a new era of cancer research that could enable more precise, proactive treatments designed to prevent recurrence and improve long-term remission," said Dr. Aguirre-Ghiso, professor of cell biology, of oncology, and of medicine and the Rose C. Falkenstein Chair in Cancer Research at Albert Einstein College of Medicine. "By closing gaps in our understanding of breast cancer relapse, our goal is to identify who is most at risk of recurrence and find ways to intercept cancer before it returns." Dr. Aguirre-Ghiso also serves as MECCC's associate director of basic science, co-leader of the Tumor Microenvironment & Metastasis Program, and director of the Cancer Dormancy Institute.

The researchers will analyze clinical data and tissue samples from people with ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), an early, non-invasive form of breast cancer. They will use the findings to investigate the mechanisms that allow cancer cells to spread early and determine whether specific characteristics of those cells are associated with subsequent relapse.

Combining Expertise Across DisciplinesThe multidisciplinary project brings together investigators with expertise in cancer biology, pathology, epidemiology, imaging, and bioinformatics.

"Montefiore Einstein is bringing together researchers across institutions to tackle a question that no single discipline can answer: why do some breast cancer cells spread early and reappear years later?" said Dr. Aguirre-Ghiso. "By connecting patient data and tissue samples with laboratory studies and advanced imaging, we hope to identify who is at risk of relapse and find ways to prevent it."

The study, "Mechanisms of Early Breast Cancer Local and Distant Relapse," is supported by NCI grant 1P01CA295557.

Founded in 1971 and an NCI-designated cancer center since 1972, MECCC is redefining excellence in cancer research, clinical care, education and training, and community outreach and engagement. Its mission is to reduce the burden of cancer for all, especially people from historically underrepresented groups.

About Albert Einstein College of MedicineAlbert Einstein College of Medicine is one of the nation's premier academic centers for basic science research, clinical investigation, and biomedical education. Located in the Bronx, Einstein is home to nearly 1,000 M.D., Ph.D., and M.D./Ph.D. students and more than 2,000 full-time faculty members. Einstein receives approximately $200M in funding from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) each year and houses six NIH-funded research centers, in cancer, intellectual and developmental disabilities, clinical and translational research, AIDS, and two in diabetes. In partnership with Montefiore Health System, Einstein advances clinical and translational research to accelerate the pace at which new discoveries become the treatments that benefit patients. "Respect the privacy of jack and his family, Lynn. Poking and prying, demanding confidential medical information isn't very professional."

Henning defended his position in a lengthy response, arguing that the public nature of Campbell's injury made additional information appropriate.

"Sure it's professional. Jack Campbell was hurt Sunday, visibly and traumatically, on the national sports stage."

Henning continued by arguing that two days without a detailed explanation did little to ease public anxiety, maintaining that seeking additional information demonstrated concern and compassion.

That explanation did not satisfy several other commenters.

"Lynn, this is an awkward and disappointing lack of empathy from you. I hope you'll reconsider," wrote @Metz4Real.

The criticism reflects a disagreement over where legitimate public concern ends and a player's right to medical privacy begins.

The controversy comes just one day after the Lions released an official statement explaining that Campbell continued to rest and recover at a local hospital.

"Jack Campbell continues to rest and recover at a local hospital. Preliminary indications are positive and out of respect for Jack and his family we will not share further information at this time."

Henning is correct that fans care deeply about Jack Campbell. There is nothing unreasonable about wanting to know whether a player is recovering after such a frightening incident.

But wanting information and being entitled to that information are two different things.

The Lions have chosen to respect Campbell's privacy, and the reactions to Henning's post suggest that many fans believe everyone else should do the same.

For now, Jack Campbell's health and his family's wishes matter far more than satisfying the public's curiosity.

'Control Resonant' Becomes a Much-Needed Surprise Hit for Remedy

Resonant is Remedy's fastest-selling game ever

Fans of Alan Wake and Control creator Remedy Entertainment can finally breathe a sigh of relief. After executive shake-ups and fiscal underperformance at the beloved studio, it seems that the team’s latest game is finding an audience. In a new report released on Tuesday, Remedy wrote that Control Resonant “received excellent player reception and became the fastest-selling Remedy game to date.” The company's third-quarter revenue more than tripled compared to last year, surging by 226%.

What’s more impressive is that the revenue spike includes only Resonant’s pre-orders and its first seven days of sales before the end of the company’s third quarter in late September. The game was released on Sept. 24.

Remedy’s new CEO, Jean-Charles Gaudechon, was quite happy with the game’s performance. “This is our first self-published game of this scale, built on our own Control IP. Praised by players and critics alike, it marks a defining milestone for the company.” Back on Oct. 15, Remedy announced that Resonant had already sold one million copies.

Gaudechon went on to highlight how the game was praised for “Remedy’s willingness to take creative risks and surprise its players.” He added that he was happy with the game’s technical performance. "Positive commentary over its scalability across a variety of PC hardware meant a larger audience could enjoy Control Resonant. This is a testament to our in-house Northlight game engine, which enables the distinctive look and feel that has come to define Remedy’s games.” While Resonant didn't receive unanimous critical praise, reviews were generally positive, albeit not as glowing as the studio's usual output.

Resonant was clearly a big swing for the company, as Gaudechon added that the game received the studio’s largest marketing investment in its history. The United States was its largest market, accounting for nearly half of sales. On PC, China and the U.K. ranked second and third after the U.S., while the U.K. and Canada were second and third for console sales.

Gaudechon expressed satisfaction that the game reached a wider audience. “Based on analytic data, close to 50% of players were new to Control,” he said. “Reaching new players was a key goal for this game, and these early results indicate a growing audience for the franchise and for Remedy.”

In June 2025, Remedy released the multiplayer game FBC: Firebreak, which received fairly tepid reviews and failed to find players. Remedy said the game didn’t meet sales expectations, prompting a profit warning that the company wouldn't hit its fiscal targets for the year. 12 days after the profit warning, former CEO Tero Virtala left his role. Remedy posted a €14.9 million operating loss for 2025.

On Feb. 9, 2026, the company announced that former Electronic Arts executive Gaudechon was the company’s new CEO. While there were concerns that the change would make Remedy resemble the much-hated EA, at least so far, that hasn’t happened. Hopefully, Control Resonant's success means the studio will remain a bastion of strange ideas in a game industry that's increasingly playing it safe.

Why Isn't Realty Income's Stock Taking Off After Strong Earnings Results?

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Realty Income delivered another solid quarter, but its results also help explain why the dividend stock hasn't taken off.

Realty Income (O -0.68%) continues to deliver the results investors expect from one of the world's largest real estate investment trusts (REITs). Its properties remain nearly fully occupied, its dividend is well covered, and its investment program continues to generate attractive returns.

However, despite another solid earnings report, the stock hasn't generated much excitement. The reason has less to do with the company's real estate portfolio and more to do with how quickly it can grow earnings per share.

Realty Income just keeps getting it done. The REIT grew its adjusted funds from operations (AFFO) by 3.7% in the third quarter to $1.12 per share. That put its dividend payout ratio at a conservative 72.6% of its AFFO, despite continued dividend growth. It has now delivered 5% AFFO per share growth through the first nine months of this year compared to the prior-year period. Its existing real estate portfolio continues to provide steadily rising cash flow. Occupancy was 98.8% at the end of the third quarter, while its rent recapture rate was 103.5% (meaning new leases on the same property were, on average, 3.5% above prior rates).

Meanwhile, the REIT continues to find attractive investment opportunities. It invested another $2.6 billion during the quarter at an initial cash yield of 6.8%. That includes properties acquired in its U.S. Core Fund and through joint ventures, such as its recently established strategic joint venture with KKR in Europe.

The company remains on pace to invest $10 billion this year. That supports its guidance to deliver $4.45-$4.46 per share of AFFO this year, a $0.01-per-share increase from its prior range. That's a more than 4% increase from last year despite headwinds from higher interest rates.

Despite its solid quarterly results, Realty Income's share price isn't soaring. That's likely due to three factors.

Realty Income's growth remains modest. AFFO per share grew just 3.7% despite a 10.3% increase in total revenue. Meanwhile, it only increased its full-year guidance by $0.01 per share at both ends of the range. Due to its large scale and net-lease focus, the REIT isn't growing as quickly as others in the industry.

It also continues to face some headwinds from interest rates. Higher rates increase interest expenses when refinancing existing debt and also drive up borrowing costs for new investments. That's a headwind for AFFO per share growth. Higher rates also weigh on the value of commercial real estate. Realty Income's share price has fallen almost 15% from its peak earlier this year, coinciding with the recent spike in the 10-year Treasury rate.

Given its size, Realty Income requires significant external capital to fund its investment program. Even highly attractive acquisitions can have a modest effect on AFFO per share when accounting for financing costs.

Realty Income isn't going to grow fast, which is why we shouldn't expect its stock to surge, even with a solid earnings report.

The thing with Realty Income is that investors don't need rapid growth to earn an attractive return. At its recent share price of around $57.50, the REIT offers a 5.6% yield at its current monthly dividend rate. It would only need to grow its AFFO per share by around a 4% annual rate to produce an average annualized total return approaching double digits. That assumes no change in its valuation multiple, which is currently a very reasonable 12.9 times.

Meanwhile, there's the potential for additional upside. Falling interest rates and incremental earnings from its expanding private capital platform could drive faster AFFO per share growth in the future. That could also drive some multiple expansion.

Realty Income doesn't need explosive earnings growth to deliver solid returns for investors. With a dividend yield around 5.6% and the potential to grow AFFO per share by more than 4% annually, the REIT could generate double-digit annualized total returns. Those returns likely won't come in one big post-earnings move but should arrive steadily as it continues to grow its earnings and pay its monthly dividend.

Don't throw away your old AirPods — do this instead

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Instead of getting rid of your old AirPods, replace the battery and give them a new purpose.

AirPods are built to last, and they usually work for years before they stop functioning completely. You'll likely buy new AirPods before your old ones stop working, though they may no longer sound brand-new.

So what should you do with your old AirPods? Throwing them in the trash is something you certainly shouldn't do; Apple discourages this because of the lithium-ion batteries inside them. The good news is that, depending on the problem, Apple can fix your buds for a reasonable fee. Your old AirPods can become good backup earbuds, or even a gift for a friend or family member.

If battery performance is the only real problem, it's likely worth getting a replacement before buying new AirPods. Even if you've already bought a new pair, replacing the batteries in your old earbuds will make them better for someone else.

But unlike an iPhone or iPad, Apple won't open AirPods to replace their batteries. Instead, the company recycles your old AirPods and gives you new ones, charging you a lower price per earbud. For most AirPods, a battery replacement costs $49 per earbud, so $98 if you need to replace the battery in both. That's much cheaper than buying new AirPods Pro, for example, which cost $249. For the higher-end AirPods Max, a battery replacement costs $79, which is also much cheaper than paying $549 for new headphones.

If you have AppleCare, you may be eligible for a free battery replacement if any battery falls below 80 percent of its original capacity. And if any battery in your AirPods fails while they're still under warranty, Apple will replace them for free. Some independent repair shops can replace the battery inside AirPods, but this requires cutting them open and gluing them back together. You might end up saving some money, but they won't look as good as new — not to mention that they'll also lose their water and sweat resistance.

If you've lost one of your earbuds or the charging case, Apple offers replacements for them. Prices vary depending on the model; for the Pro line, a new earbud costs $89 and a new case costs $99. For standard AirPods, it's $69 to replace one bud and between $59 and $79 to replace the case.

It's thus usually worth it for the more expensive AirPods Pro, but not so much for the base AirPods. The latest AirPods 5 cost $129 from Apple, and it's not hard to find them for a little less at other stores.

Instead of tossing your old AirPods into a drawer, there are plenty of other ways to put them to good use. Earbuds that still hold a decent charge could be worth something. Apple doesn't offer trade-in credit for AirPods, but buyback services like Back Market will give you some money for them. Selling them directly on places like eBay or Facebook Marketplace usually pays more.

Or you can simply give them to a friend or family member, so they'll only have to pay for a battery replacement if needed. If you pass them on, be sure to clean the AirPods and remove them from your Apple account — new ear tips will be much appreciated.

Keeping them around is also suitable if they still work fine. Have your old AirPods ready as backup earbuds in case you lose your new ones, leave them somewhere or simply need another set when your usual pair runs out of battery. Some people prefer to use old AirPods as gym earbuds so their new ones don't get covered in sweat. If you like to sleep with your AirPods in, using your old ones can avoid draining the battery on the new ones. Or you might devote them for listening to your Roku at night via Bluetooth.

In case your old AirPods are truly dead and you don't want to spend money on them, Apple will recycle them for free. You don't even need to take them to an Apple Store, since Apple provides a prepaid shipping label in certain regions. You won't get paid for them, but at least your earbuds will be disposed of properly.

An increasing number of English football executives are pushing for the Premier League to investigate Manchester City for the seasons since 2018 or bring further charges, although it is a subject causing significant debate and viewed as a potential “dilemma” for the game.

The Abu Dhabi-owned club were last month found guilty of 114 charges of breaches of financial rules in a case already seen as one of the most controversial in football history, although they are currently in the process of appeal.

Amid a temporary lull in the story as the game waits for both the sanctions and the outcome of that appeal, many directors have been pointing to key details of the independent commission’s final verdict.

Since the document laid out how City’s sponsorship deals were inflated by disguised owner investment, with accompanying graphs, an obvious question being asked is whether that just stopped in 2018, despite commercial income accounted as staying at the same level.

As one source asked, “what changed overnight?”

In its decision, the Independent Commission found that Manchester City and its Abu Dhabi-based sponsors were ‘Related Parties’. If the relationships did not change, then that would form the basis for more charges in subsequent seasons given the club did not report this or have the Fair Market Values of the sponsorships assessed.

The ultimate reason for the initial investigation was a tranche of emails from football leaks that were first published by Der Spiegel in November 2018 and covered the period from 2009 to 2018.

While even those who want scrutiny on that period have concerns about another lengthy investigation, the wait for sanctions – expected by many sources to come this month – has sharpened focus on the period after 2018.

There is at least some possibility that the independent commission recommends titles of 2012, 2014 and 2018 are stripped. While this is currently viewed as unlikely, such an outcome would immediately raise more questions about the era of success that followed 2018.

City built on Pep Guardiola’s initial 2017-18 title winning season with the most intense period of glory the Premier League has seen, winning English football’s first four in a row to make it a total of six leagues in seven years alongside a first ever domestic treble and the 2022-23 treble that brought the Champions League.

Executives feel that questions about this have not been adequately addressed by the Premier League, although the competition’s policy and legal guidance has been to refuse to discuss the case until it is completely concluded.

The Premier League have been contacted for comment by The Independent.

There is nevertheless a sense among executives that commercial deals after 2018 will be viewed as having simply been made subject to the associated party rules, and were already assessed at fair market value.

That line of thinking has been met with some scepticism, given the nature of the guilty verdict.

The counter-argument is that associated party rules would be irrelevant here, since any investigation would be about disguised funding.

City are still strenuously insisting on their innocence, and remain bullish about an appeal.

The prospect of further investigation is also seen as representing a potential “dilemma” for the competition and its clubs. Some feel agitating executives are getting ahead of themselves, and that there should just be patience for the current case to conclude.

A concurrent view is that there aren’t the same clear grounds for another investigation without evidence like the leaked emails, and that it might even be unnecessary if the guilty verdict is upheld and the sanctions are appropriate, given what the final verdict already says.

The latter is taken to mean meaningful sporting sanctions and focus on the hierarchy responsible.

Others feel the idea of another investigation is better saved as a “negotiation tactic”, especially with how abrasive City have already been in their position.

As one source said of the guilty verdict prospectively being upheld, “when you’re seeking to agree on a potentially severe punishment, the Premier League can be shown to have given them a concession and spare them further”.

“It is pressure useful for the end game,” another added.

That could also be acutely relevant to the future of the club if this case does conclude with a forced sale under the Independent Football Regulator, or even a voluntary sale, which certain rival executives are mindful of. Any agreement not to investigate further would ensure that potential buyers are not put off by the possibility of further uncertainty and more sanctions.

Some fear that another investigation would take another half-decade or more, and that the Premier League would suffer further when many are keen to eventually draw a line under the case.

The 10 highest-paid CEOs in America, according to the 2026 Equilar 100

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Elon Musk's $132.3 billion award heads this look at 10 of the 100 CEOs in the 2026 Equilar 100, where median pay hit $39.4 million.

Running a large American company means steering it through market upheaval while keeping its finances growing. The strain of that job commands lavish rewards, and those sums raise hard questions about fairness and about who captures the gains of corporate success. In 2025, the median chief executive among the 100 best-paid at U.S. public companies collected $39.4 million. No earlier year in the long-running analysis produced a higher figure, and the median climbed 35.8% from a year earlier. Anyone who owns shares, draws a paycheck from one of these firms, or wonders where corporate money flows has a stake in how boards arrive at those numbers.

The distance between the corner office and the rest of the workforce stretched at a similar clip. The median ratio of chief executive earnings to those of a typical employee reached 475 to 1 in 2025, up from 348 to 1 the year before, a 36.5% jump. The median worker at these employers took home $101,322, a solid income that looks modest beside packages worth tens of millions of dollars. Stock awards drive the surge in executive totals. Base salaries held flat at $1.3 million, while long-term equity accounts for more than 70% of total compensation in the usual package. The research credits movements in stock value with an outsized effect on what an executive receives. A share-price rally can reshape a leader's haul by a margin no salary adjustment could match.

Equilar has partnered with The New York Times for 19 years to track what chief executives earn, and its 2026 study lists the 100 richest pay deals that boards granted to CEOs at U.S.-based or listed companies. Each company needed more than $1 billion in revenue and had to file a proxy statement for its latest completed fiscal year by April 30, 2026. Total compensation sets the order, and the figures cover awards from 2025. The report includes a table of 10 executives from that roster, showing each one's total package, change in pay, and fiscal 2025 revenue. The slides ahead follow those 10 of the 100 in the order the table presents them.

Elon Musk drew a $132.3 billion pay package from Tesla $TSLA in 2025, and all of it came as a single long-term stock award. The grant hinges on aggressive goals for Tesla's market capitalization and its operations. Nothing in the reported total came from salary, bonus, or perks. The structure turns Tesla's chief into an investor whose payday rides on the same share price outside shareholders watch.

Stock awards across the full list look small beside Musk's grant. The median award rose 44.6% in 2025 to $28.4 million, up from $19.6 million. No other package in the study comes within reach of his total. Even the second-largest deal in the table, at Figma, measures in the hundreds of millions, while Musk's runs to the billions.

Musk's package held none of the cash that pads other executives' pay. Median cash compensation, which covers salaries, bonuses, and perks, rose 3.6% to $6.1 million in 2025. Every dollar of his total depends on how Tesla's shares and operations perform. A median executive on the list draws a fixed salary regardless of where the share price lands. Musk's reward rests on his targets alone.

Musk's award surpasses Tesla's $94.8 billion in fiscal 2025 revenue, the largest sales total among the 10 companies in the table. The world that the movie describes was once much larger than Russia itself. You could arrive in Kyiv, Tbilisi, or Riga and speak Russian; quote the same Soviet films; laugh at the same jokes; and assume that underneath all the flags, languages, and newly drawn borders there was still some common social and cultural space holding the place together.

When Russia set out to restore that world by force, some of its nostalgic talking points, once confined to state television, found a wide company in the West. Tucker Carlson, who has turned it into a routine, tells his audience that Ukraine is weak, corrupt, and finished. Candace Owens flew to Russia and came back saying, “they are lying to us about Russia.” Others, such as U.S. Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene, refuse to support aid for a country defending itself from invasion.

John Mearsheimer and Noam Chomsky have built long careers explaining that whatever Russia does is somebody else’s fault. Francesca Albanese, a U.N. special rapporteur, has called for an end to arms deliveries to a country fighting against an invader.

The left and the right rarely agree on anything, yet here, some of their thinkers converge: Sooner or later, Kyiv must learn to live with Moscow’s demands. Whether it’s Owen Matthews writing in the Spectator that Ukraine is losing the war or Jeffrey Sachs insisting that Kyiv will never join NATO, the underlying belief is the same. For some people shaped by that world, its disappearance is sad. There is a little of Anton Chekhov’s The Cherry Orchard in them: They are so attached to the estate that they can scarcely imagine a world beyond it.

Leaving Russia for the West did not always mean leaving its imperial assumptions behind. Joseph Brodsky, persecuted and exiled by the Soviet state, greeted Ukrainian independence with a poem full of insults, imagining Ukrainians returning to Russian poet Alexander Pushkin on their deathbeds rather than their own Taras Shevchenko. Pushkin himself, in “To the Slanderers of Russia,” told Europe to stay out of what he presented as a family quarrel among Slavs during Russia’s suppression of the Polish uprising in 1831.

What is striking is how many people who never lived in that world have arrived at the same conclusion. These pundits identify Ukraine’s problems well. Kyiv is short of men, money, and Patriot interceptors; corruption persists; and its allies’ declarations have far outrun their policies. They are right about the diagnosis and wrong about the cure.

They also exaggerate the threat. Russia has advanced at enormous cost to Ukraine, yet a snail set off from its starting positions might have reached parts of today’s front line first. Ukraine won the decisive battle for Kyiv, when Moscow’s aim was a disarmed Ukraine under a government of its choosing. It need not recover all its territory to win. If it remains sovereign, secure, and in the West, then Russia’s larger project will have failed. The only real Russian victory is the one that these arguments point toward—a Ukraine that is left vulnerable and subject to Moscow’s will.

The worst part of this so-called realist take is the claim that it is for Ukraine’s own good. By the forecasts of these observers, Ukraine was supposed to lose in 2022, then every year since. Instead of revising the premise, they move the date forward and wait for history to rescue the thinking.

Even the more serious skeptics concede that Russian President Vladimir Putin has not changed his war aims and that U.S. special envoy Jared Kushner is naive to think that business deals will seduce the Kremlin into peace. If Putin cannot be persuaded, what is Ukraine supposed to negotiate? A concession to a man who demands the maximum will only be a down payment. Georgia learned this the hard way. Its 2008 cease-fire with Russia was followed by the latter’s invasion of Crimea; the Minsk agreements were followed by the full-scale invasion.

Another argument that keeps resurfacing is that the West provoked the war. Mearsheimer and Chomsky describe Russian aggression as a response to a menacing NATO, the supposed existential threat that Putin cites and his Western interpreters obligingly repeat. Yet Moscow was already using force in Moldova and Georgia in the early 1990s, when NATO enlargement was hardly an available excuse. You can call those ethnic conflicts, as some do, but after so many Russian acts of aggression, ignoring the reality takes real determination. The only power that has invaded anyone in the neighborhood is Russia. NATO only prevents it from doing so.

What runs through all this is an old fatalism, very familiar in Russia, in which Russian power is a constant, Western resolve is fickle, and Ukrainian resistance is doomed. It calls itself realism, but it is really pessimism, in which history can change everything except Russia’s behavior. It treats Moscow’s desires as objective reality and Ukraine’s goals as childish aspirations, in the spirit of the Brezhnev Doctrine, where small countries choose freely, provided that Moscow approves.

Soviet power once looked equally permanent; in Alexei Yurchak’s words, “everything was forever, until it was no more.” The Baltic states were told that Russia would never tolerate their joining NATO. They did not move on the map. The policy and debate did, because Western governments decided it should. Yes, they joined while Russia was weak. That is exactly the point.

If Western policy has failed, then the policy should change. If Ukraine lacks air defense, the answer is more air defense. Pressure on Russia should grow until Moscow understands that the West will hold its ground and that the war will end as a strategic loss even if Russia takes the rest of the Donbas, which it does not really need. That means guaranteeing Ukraine’s future security and denying Moscow that lever. Is there a Western consensus on this yet? No. Let’s hope one arrives, because otherwise the problems will end up far closer to home.

Why, then, does the argument keep returning? For some, it is a hatred of the West so complete that every war turns into a story about American wrongdoing. For others, it is audience. Outrage and fringe arguments sell, and Moscow makes a good backdrop for a story about Western decline. And for more than a few, it is simply clear sympathy for Russia, for its strongman, its bigotry framed as tradition, its fantasy of itself as the third Rome against a decadent West.

Then for others, it is fear. In my village in the mountains of Georgia, my neighbor, a man called Valeri, says much the same thing as these commentators, though one should be more forgiving of Valeri. Russian troops already occupy part of his country, and one morning, he may discover that his barn is on the Russian side of the line. When he says that Russia is too big and too dangerous, one can understand. His is a foreign policy of the village bench. But the same worldview, minus the cows, turns up in American studios and newspapers, among people who know that they have more choices.

Whether they echo that view from Moscow or in the West, they will find the Ukraine that comes out of this war even harder to recognize. Young Ukrainians speak more Ukrainian, consume less Russian culture, and understand fewer of the Soviet references that once traveled from Moscow to Mykolaiv. After generations of Russification, the recovery of a national consciousness is not an easy task. It may come with mistakes. But a country bombed in the name of a supposedly common history, that took tyranny and bloodshed to hold together, cannot be expected to preserve that history for the comfort of people who remember it fondly.

Putin can drop more bombs and probably will, but after nearly five years of war, it is hard to imagine Ukrainians deciding that capitulation is the answer. Like other empires, the Russian one will sooner or later collapse, including in people’s heads. Along with it will go the longing to walk into Kyiv or Tbilisi and expect the keys to fit.

Recessions, scandals and backlash: 10 midterms that hit the party in power

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These midterm election swings, from 1894 to 2018, show how recessions, scandals and policy fights cost the president's party control of Congress or big blocs of House seats

The party that wins the White House usually pays for it two years later. Since the Civil War, the president's party has lost House seats in most midterm elections. In some years those losses are large enough to hand a chamber of Congress to the opposition and stall a president's legislative agenda.

This list covers 10 of those larger moments, from 1894 to 2018. It is a selection, not a ranking. The elections appear in chronological order rather than by size. The yardstick throughout is the same: the net change in House seats, along with any change in which party controls the House or the Senate. Where historical tallies disagree, the slides describe a swing in general terms rather than settle on one disputed number.

Midterm election swings tend to follow a small set of triggers. Economic downturns are one. A financial panic, a depression or a recession gives voters a concrete reason to hold the party in power responsible. Several elections on this list followed an economic slump. Scandal is another. A president's troubles can weigh on House and Senate candidates who had no part in them. A third trigger is backlash against major legislation or against a president's bid to expand his authority. Voters who believe a party has overreached can use the midterm to register that view.

The midterm electorate also differs from a presidential-year electorate. With no presidential race at the top of the ballot, turnout typically falls. A smaller electorate can shift the balance toward whichever side is more motivated to vote. That helps explain how an opposition party can gain ground quickly while the president remains in office.

The consequences reach beyond the seat count. A House flip gives the new majority the speakership, control of committees and the power to subpoena and investigate. A Senate flip changes who controls confirmation votes on judges and cabinet nominees. Even without a change in control, a large loss can shrink a governing majority enough to give smaller factions more leverage.

Each slide below covers one election: who held the White House, what drove voters and what changed in Congress afterward.

The 1894 midterm gave Republicans control of the House of Representatives. They made a net gain of more than 100 seats as voters punished Grover Cleveland's Democrats for the depression that followed the Panic of 1893.

Cleveland began his second, nonconsecutive term in March 1893. His term had barely started when the panic took hold. The Philadelphia and Reading Railroad had already gone into receivership that February. A wave of bank and business failures followed. Railroads that had overbuilt during the previous boom went bankrupt, and unemployment climbed across industrial cities.

Cleveland's main response was monetary. He called Congress into a special session in 1893 to repeal the Sherman Silver Purchase Act. He blamed the law for draining the Treasury's gold reserve. The repeal passed, but it split his own party. Democrats from the South and West who favored silver saw it as a betrayal of indebted farmers.

Unrest spread in 1894. That spring, Jacob Coxey led a march of unemployed workers, known as Coxey's Army, to Washington, D.C. That summer, the Pullman Strike spread along the nation's railroad lines. Cleveland sent federal troops to Chicago over the objections of Illinois Gov. John Peter Altgeld, a fellow Democrat.

Trade policy offered no relief. The Wilson-Gorman Tariff Act was meant to lower tariffs. It emerged from Congress so weakened by amendments that Cleveland let it become law without his signature.

Democrats went into the November vote carrying the depression, the silver split and the tariff fight. Republicans made heavy gains across the Northeast and Midwest. Thomas Brackett Reed of Maine returned as House speaker when the new Congress met in December 1895.

The result reached beyond a single Congress. The Democratic split over silver carried into the 1896 presidential race. That year the party nominated William Jennings Bryan, and Republican William McKinley won the White House. Republicans then held the House until Democrats won it back in the 1910 midterm.

The 1938 midterm cost Franklin D. Roosevelt's Democrats dozens of House seats, though the party kept its majorities in both chambers. The result strengthened a bloc of Republicans and conservative Southern Democrats that resisted new New Deal programs for years afterward.

The losses came two years after a landslide. In 1936, Roosevelt carried 46 of the 48 states, and Democrats entered 1937 with commanding margins in Congress. That position eroded within months.

The first setback came from the White House. In February 1937, Roosevelt proposed legislation to expand the Supreme Court. The bill would have let him appoint an additional justice for each sitting justice who passed age 70 without retiring, up to six new seats. Critics in both parties called the plan an attack on judicial independence. The Senate sent the bill back to committee in July 1937, and it never became law.

The second setback was economic. A sharp downturn, now known as the Recession of 1937-38, began later that year. Unemployment rose again after several years of recovery. Republicans used the slump to argue that the New Deal had not produced lasting growth.

Roosevelt then tried to reshape his own party. During the 1938 primaries, he campaigned against conservative Democrats who had opposed his agenda. His targets included Senators Walter George of Georgia, Millard Tydings of Maryland and Ellison "Cotton Ed" Smith of South Carolina. All three won renomination. The effort, which came to be called the "purge," succeeded mainly against Representative John J. O'Connor of New York. O'Connor, chairman of the House Rules Committee, lost his primary.

In November, Republicans gained seats in the Senate as well as the House. The incoming class included Robert A. Taft, elected to the Senate from Ohio. Harold Stassen won the governorship of Minnesota the same year. Both men became leading Republican figures in the following decade.

The legislative consequences outlasted the campaign. The Fair Labor Standards Act, signed in June 1938, set a federal minimum wage of 25 cents an hour. It also required overtime pay for work beyond a standard workweek. Few major domestic reforms followed it. After the election, the conservative coalition had enough votes to block or trim many of Roosevelt's domestic proposals. His attention turned increasingly toward the fighting in Asia and the threat of war in Europe.

Republicans won control of both the House and the Senate in the 1946 midterm elections. It was the first time since 1930 that the party had won both chambers. The result left Harry Truman facing a Congress run by the opposition after his first midterm.

Truman had not been elected to the presidency. He moved up from the vice presidency in April 1945, after Franklin D. Roosevelt died, a few months before World War II ended. The 1946 vote was the first national verdict on his administration.

That verdict came as the country shifted from a wartime economy to a peacetime one. Millions of returning service members needed jobs and housing. Wartime price controls were being loosened and fought over, and prices climbed as controls came off. Shortages of meat and other consumer goods frustrated households.

Labor unrest added to the strain. Major strikes hit the auto, steel, coal and railroad industries during 1945 and 1946. Truman clashed with union leaders as he tried to keep those sectors running. Those fights cost him support among workers who had backed Roosevelt.

Republicans built their campaign around that discontent. Their slogan, "Had enough?", asked voters whether they were tired of shortages, strikes and years of Democratic government in Washington.

The new majority formed the 80th Congress. Joseph Martin of Massachusetts became speaker of the House. The incoming class of 1946 also included Richard Nixon, elected to the House from California, and Joseph McCarthy, elected to the Senate from Wisconsin. John F. Kennedy, a Democrat, won his first House race in Massachusetts the same year.

The Republican Congress pushed back against the labor policies of the Roosevelt era. In 1947 it passed the Taft-Hartley Act, which restricted union activity, and overrode Truman's veto to make it law. It also sent the states the proposal that became the 22nd Amendment, limiting presidents to two elected terms.

Truman turned that Congress into his main campaign target. Running for a full term in 1948, he attacked it as the "Do-Nothing Congress." He won that election, and Democrats regained both the House and the Senate the same year.

The 1958 midterm gave Democrats large gains in both the House and the Senate during Dwight D. Eisenhower's second term. Voters cast their ballots after the 1957-58 recession, and the result widened majorities that Democrats already held in both chambers.

That detail sets 1958 apart from most large midterm swings. Democrats had controlled Congress since winning it back in the 1954 elections. The 1958 vote did not flip either chamber. It turned working Democratic majorities into much larger ones for the final two years of Eisenhower's presidency.

The economy shaped much of the result. The downturn that began in 1957 pushed up unemployment, and industrial states in the Midwest were hit hard. Republicans held the White House while factory jobs disappeared. Many of the party's congressional candidates carried that record into November.

Two other events shaped the political backdrop. The Soviet Union launched Sputnik in October 1957. The launch set off a debate in the U.S. over science education and national defense. In September 1958, weeks before the vote, Sherman Adams resigned as Eisenhower's top White House aide. His exit followed disclosures that he had accepted gifts from Boston textile manufacturer Bernard Goldfine.

Labor issues featured in several key contests. Right-to-work measures appeared on the ballot in states including California and Ohio, and unions organized to defeat them. Both measures failed. In California, Senate Republican leader William Knowland gave up his seat to run for governor and lost to Democrat Edmund "Pat" Brown. In Ohio, Republican Sen. John Bricker lost his seat to Democrat Stephen Young.

The Senate class elected in 1958 included several figures who went on to long national careers. Democrats Edmund Muskie of Maine, Eugene McCarthy of Minnesota, Philip Hart of Michigan and Thomas Dodd of Connecticut all won seats that year. Alaska, then on the verge of statehood, elected two Democrats, Ernest Gruening and Bob Bartlett, as its first senators.

The expanded majorities went to a Congress led by two Texas Democrats, Senate Majority Leader Lyndon Johnson and House Speaker Sam Rayburn.

Republicans won back a large bloc of House seats in the 1966 midterm, two years after Lyndon Johnson's landslide win over Barry Goldwater. Democrats kept their majorities in both the House and the Senate. Even so, the result cut sharply into the margins Johnson had used to pass much of his domestic agenda.

The size of the rebound reflected how far Republicans had fallen in 1964. Johnson's landslide that year carried many Democrats into House seats that usually leaned Republican. In 1966, those first-term members ran without a presidential contest at the top of the ticket. Many of them lost.

The Congress elected in 1964, the 89th, had passed a long list of major laws. Medicare and Medicaid became law in 1965. So did the Voting Rights Act and the Elementary and Secondary Education Act. That pace of legislation gave Republicans a record to campaign against. Their message focused on federal spending and the reach of Washington.

Foreign policy also shaped the campaign. Johnson had sent U.S. ground combat troops to Vietnam in large numbers in 1965. By 1966, the war's cost was growing, and it divided the Democratic coalition. Higher prices and unrest in American cities added to voter discontent. The 1965 riots in the Watts neighborhood of Los Angeles were one example of that unrest.

The Republican class of 1966 included several figures who went on to national prominence. George H.W. Bush won a House seat from Houston, his first elected office. In Massachusetts, Edward Brooke became the first Black senator elected by popular vote. Howard Baker won a Senate seat in Tennessee. Mark Hatfield, then Oregon's governor, won a Senate seat in his state. Charles Percy defeated Democratic incumbent Paul Douglas in Illinois.

Republicans also gained ground in races for governor. Ronald Reagan, in his first run for office, defeated incumbent Democrat Edmund "Pat" Brown to become governor of California.

Richard Nixon campaigned across the country for Republican candidates that fall. The results helped rebuild his standing in the party ahead of his successful 1968 presidential run.

In the 90th Congress, Johnson's remaining domestic proposals faced a House with far fewer reliable Democratic votes. Republicans and conservative Southern Democrats held more leverage over which bills advanced.

The 1974 midterm gave Democrats a much larger House majority three months after Richard Nixon resigned over the Watergate scandal. Voters went to the polls on Nov. 5, 1974, with Gerald Ford $F in the White House and Nixon's pardon still fresh. Republicans lost seats in both the House and the Senate.

The scandal had been building for more than two years. It began with the June 1972 break-in at the Democratic National Committee's offices in the Watergate complex in Washington, D.C. Congressional investigators and reporters traced the cover-up back to the White House.

In July 1974, the Supreme Court ruled unanimously in United States v. Nixon that the president had to hand over recordings of Oval Office conversations. That same month, the House Judiciary Committee approved articles of impeachment against him.

Nixon resigned on Aug. 9, 1974, before the full House could vote on impeachment. He was the first U.S. president to leave office by resignation.

Ford took over without having been elected president or vice president. Nixon had nominated him for vice president under the 25th Amendment after Spiro Agnew resigned in October 1973. Congress confirmed the nomination that December.

On Sept. 8, 1974, Ford granted Nixon a full, free and absolute pardon for any federal crimes he may have committed as president. The pardon ruled out a federal prosecution of the former president. Ford's public standing dropped after the announcement. The decision also pulled Watergate back to the center of the fall campaign.

The economy added to the pressure on Republican candidates. The 1973 Arab oil embargo had driven up energy prices. Inflation was high, and the country had slipped into recession. In October 1974, weeks before the vote, Ford launched his "Whip Inflation Now" campaign to rally public support against rising prices.

The election reshaped the House Democratic caucus. The large class of newly elected Democrats became known as the "Watergate babies." Many of them ran as reformers.

Early in 1975, the Democratic Caucus voted to remove three longtime committee chairmen. They were Wright Patman of the Banking Committee, F. Edward Hébert of the Armed Services Committee and W.R. Poage of the Agriculture Committee. Those votes weakened the seniority system that had long decided who led House committees.

The 1994 midterm gave Republicans a majority in the House of Representatives for the first time in 40 years. The party also won control of the Senate. That left President Bill Clinton facing a Republican Congress halfway through his first term.

Democrats had held the House without a break since the 1954 elections. The 1994 result ended that run in a single night.

Republican leaders built their national campaign around the Contract with America. The platform was unveiled on the steps of the U.S. Capitol in September 1994. Its central promise was procedural. If voters gave Republicans the House, the party would bring 10 bills to a floor vote within the first 100 days of the new Congress. The proposals included a balanced budget amendment, congressional term limits, welfare changes and tax cuts.

Newt Gingrich of Georgia, then the House Republican whip, was the plan's chief architect. He became Speaker of the House when the 104th Congress convened in January 1995.

Democrats entered the campaign after several contested legislative fights. Clinton's health care overhaul, led by Hillary Clinton, collapsed in Congress in 1994 without a floor vote in either chamber. His 1993 budget raised taxes and passed without a single Republican vote. The 1994 crime bill included a ban on certain semiautomatic weapons.

The defeats reached the top of the Democratic leadership. House Speaker Tom Foley lost his seat in Washington state. He became the first sitting Speaker defeated for reelection since 1862. In the Senate, Alabama's Richard Shelby switched from the Democratic Party to the Republican Party the day after the election. Bob Dole became Senate majority leader.

The new House majority voted on every item in the contract within the 100-day window. All but the term limits amendment passed the House. That measure fell short of the two-thirds vote a constitutional amendment requires. Several bills later stalled in the Senate.

Clashes over spending between Clinton and the Republican Congress led to two federal government shutdowns between late 1995 and early 1996. The two sides later agreed on a welfare overhaul, which Clinton signed in 1996. Clinton won reelection that November, and Republicans kept their House majority.

Democrats won control of both the House and the Senate in the 2006 midterm, held on November 7 during George W. Bush's second term. The result ended the Republican House majority that began with the 1994 election.

The Iraq War sat at the center of the campaign. The conflict was in its fourth year, and sectarian violence inside Iraq had escalated. Democratic candidates framed the vote as a referendum on the war and on Bush's handling of it.

Scandal added pressure on Republicans. The lobbying scandal surrounding Jack Abramoff produced guilty pleas and put members of Congress under scrutiny. Tom DeLay, the former House majority leader, resigned his seat in 2006 after an indictment in Texas.

Weeks before the vote, Rep. Mark Foley of Florida resigned over messages he had sent to teenagers who had served as House pages. Democrats campaigned against what they called a "culture of corruption" in Washington.

Control of the Senate came down to a handful of close races. Democrats defeated Republican incumbents in Pennsylvania, Ohio, Missouri, Montana, Rhode Island and Virginia. In Virginia, Jim Webb unseated George Allen by a narrow margin. In Montana, Jon Tester ousted Conrad Burns.

The new Senate split 49 to 49, with two independents holding the balance. Bernie Sanders of Vermont and Joe Lieberman of Connecticut both caucused with Democrats. That gave the party a working majority. Lieberman had lost the Democratic primary to Ned Lamont, then won reelection as an independent.

The political consequences arrived within a day. On November 8, Bush announced that Defense Secretary Donald Rumsfeld would step down. He named Robert Gates, a former CIA director, as the replacement. At a news conference, Bush described the results as a "thumping."

In January 2007, Nancy Pelosi of California became Speaker of the House. She was the first woman to hold the post. Harry Reid of Nevada became Senate majority leader.

The election did not end the war policy it was fought over. Bush announced a troop increase in Iraq in January 2007. In May 2007, he vetoed a war funding bill that included a timeline for withdrawing U.S. troops. The new majorities instead used hearings and spending bills to press the administration through the rest of his term.

Republicans won control of the U.S. House in the 2010 midterm, Barack Obama's first. The Tea Party movement and opposition to the Affordable Care Act shaped the campaign. Democrats held the Senate, but the House majority they had won in 2006 was gone after four years.

The election came less than two years after Obama took office with Democrats controlling both chambers of Congress. That majority passed the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act, the stimulus package of early 2009. It also passed the Affordable Care Act, which Obama signed in March 2010. Both laws became central targets for Republican campaigns that fall.

The Tea Party movement took shape in 2009. Its supporters objected to federal spending, bank and auto bailouts, and the health care overhaul. They organized rallies, confronted lawmakers at town hall meetings and backed primary challenges against establishment Republicans. A televised rant by CNBC commentator Rick Santelli in February 2009, aimed at a mortgage relief plan, helped popularize the name.

An early warning for Democrats came in January 2010. Republican Scott Brown won a special election in Massachusetts for the Senate seat long held by Ted Kennedy. The result cost Democrats their 60th Senate vote, the number needed to break a filibuster.

The economy also weighed on the majority. The country was still recovering from the Great Recession. Unemployment stayed high through the campaign.

Tea Party energy reshaped the Republican field. Candidates backed by the movement won Senate seats in several states, including Rand Paul in Kentucky, Marco Rubio in Florida and Mike Lee in Utah. Other insurgent nominees lost races Republicans had hoped to win. Christine O'Donnell lost in Delaware. Sharron Angle lost in Nevada to Senate Majority Leader Harry Reid. Both seats stayed in Democratic hands, and Democrats kept their Senate majority.

The day after the election, Obama described the result as a "shellacking." John Boehner of Ohio became speaker in January 2011, replacing Nancy Pelosi.

The timing extended the effect beyond Congress. Republicans also made large gains in state legislatures and governorships just as states were redrawing congressional maps after the 2010 census. That put the party in charge of drawing district lines in many states for the following decade.

The new House majority quickly clashed with the White House over spending. A standoff over raising the federal debt limit in the summer of 2011 ended with the Budget Control Act. Standard & Poor's downgraded the U.S. credit rating that August.

Democrats won control of the U.S. House of Representatives in the November 6, 2018, midterm election, with a net gain of about 40 seats during Donald Trump's first term. The result ended eight years of Republican control of the chamber and produced divided government for the rest of Trump's term.

Republicans had held the House since winning it in the 2010 election. Going into 2018, the party also controlled the Senate and the White House. That gave Republicans full control of the elected branches in Washington for Trump's first two years in office.

Health care became a central issue in the campaign. In 2017, a Republican bill to repeal and replace the Affordable Care Act passed the House but failed in the Senate. Democratic candidates built much of their message around keeping protections for people with preexisting conditions. Republicans campaigned in part on the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, which Trump signed in December 2017.

Much of the Democratic gain came in suburban districts. Republican-held seats around cities in California, New Jersey, Virginia and Texas moved to Democrats. Several of those districts had sent Republicans to Congress for years before 2018.

The House class that took office in January 2019 included a record number of women. It also included the first two Muslim women elected to Congress, Ilhan Omar of Minnesota and Rashida Tlaib of Michigan. Sharice Davids of Kansas and Deb Haaland of New Mexico became the first two Native American women elected to Congress.

The Senate moved in the opposite direction. Republicans kept their majority and added to it, defeating Democratic incumbents in Florida, Indiana, Missouri and North Dakota. That split outcome set 2018 apart from midterms in which one party gained in both chambers at once.

Nancy Pelosi of California returned as speaker of the House on January 3, 2019. She had first held the post from 2007 to 2011, becoming the first woman to serve as speaker.

Democratic chairs took over House committees in January 2019. They opened inquiries into the Trump administration. In December 2019, the House impeached Trump on two articles, abuse of power and obstruction of Congress. The Republican-led Senate acquitted him in February 2020.

What happens to leftover campaign money after an election depends on federal rules that ban personal use but allow refunds, charity gifts, party transfers and future campaigns

Fundraising rarely stops as a race nears the finish. Candidates keep asking for donations through the final weeks, and many committees end election night with money still in the bank. Winners and losers face the same question when the results are in. What happens to leftover campaign money?

The candidate cannot simply keep it. Federal law treats contributions as money raised for a campaign, not as a personal fund. The Federal Election Campaign Act bars candidates from converting campaign funds to personal use. The Federal Election Commission, or FEC, enforces that rule and oversees how federal committees handle what remains.

That ban still leaves a candidate with several legal paths. A committee can hold its surplus for a future federal campaign. No federal deadline forces it to spend the money. It can move funds to political party committees or donate them to tax-exempt charities. It can give to other candidates and political action committees, within contribution limits. It can also send money back to the donors who gave it.

Other uses involve settling the business of the race. Campaign money can pay off debts, including loans a candidate made to their own campaign. It can cover legal bills, recounts and the accounting work needed to comply with federal reporting rules. Candidates who win can use it for certain costs tied to their duties in office. A committee that wants to close has to dispose of its funds and clear its debts and obligations first.

This guide covers federal candidates only — people running for president, the U.S. Senate or the U.S. House of Representatives. Their committees answer to the FEC. State and local campaigns follow their own laws, which differ from place to place. Those rules are not covered here. Races for governor, mayor or state legislature fall outside the scope of this guide.

Each of the 10 slides that follow explains one option or restriction for surplus campaign funds. Each slide stands on its own. A reader looking for a single answer can go straight to it. Together they show where unused campaign donations can end up and where they cannot.

A federal candidate cannot keep leftover campaign money as personal income. The prohibition comes from the Federal Election Campaign Act, which forbids turning campaign contributions into personal funds. The ban still applies after Election Day, whether the candidate won or lost.

The rule reaches beyond the candidate. Federal law bars any person from converting a committee's money to personal use, and that includes family members and staff. A surplus stays committee money until it goes to a permitted purpose.

The Federal Election Commission enforces the ban. Its test looks at the expense itself rather than the reason a candidate gives for it. If a cost would exist even without the campaign or the candidate's duties as a federal officeholder, paying it with campaign funds is personal use.

That test explains the FEC's standard examples. A mortgage or rent payment comes due whether or not someone runs for office. So do grocery bills and a child's tuition. The campaign does not create any of those costs, and the committee cannot pay them.

FEC regulations name other expenses treated as personal use in every case. They include health club and country club dues, vacations and tickets to concerts or sporting events with no campaign purpose. Clothing is also on the list. The exception is low-value campaign items such as T-shirts and caps.

Some spending gets a case-by-case review instead. Meals, travel and vehicle costs can serve both campaign and private ends. The committee can pay the share tied to campaign activity. It cannot pay the private share.

Paying relatives draws its own scrutiny. A committee can put a family member on the payroll only for real work at a fair market rate. Any amount above the value of the services counts as personal use.

Penalties range from civil fines to criminal charges. The FEC can pursue civil enforcement. The Justice Department can prosecute knowing and willful violations. Former California Rep. Duncan Hunter pleaded guilty in 2019 to a conspiracy charge tied to spending campaign money on personal expenses.

A federal candidate can keep leftover campaign money in the committee and spend it on a later run. Federal law sets no deadline for using the funds and no cap on how much can sit in the account between elections.

That later run does not have to be for the same seat. The money can support a bid for the same office or for a different federal office. A House member who later runs for the Senate, for example, can draw on money raised for earlier House races.

FEC regulations allow a candidate to transfer funds from an earlier campaign committee to the committee for a new federal race. The main condition is that the candidate is not running for both offices at the same time.

House members face voters every two years. Senators serve six-year terms. A senator's committee can hold a balance through an entire term and put it toward the next reelection campaign. A House member's committee can carry money through several consecutive terms.

A committee that keeps a balance stays registered with the Federal Election Commission. It continues to file regular disclosure reports showing its cash on hand, receipts and spending. Those reports are public, so anyone can see how much a candidate has banked.

Funds carried forward remain campaign funds. The ban on personal use applies the whole time, whether the next race is two years away or 10.

Carrying money forward does not lock in its final use. A committee holding a balance can later choose a different legal path for it. Nothing in federal law forces that decision soon after election day.

"War chest" is the common shorthand for this money. A large balance lets an incumbent start a reelection campaign without first rebuilding funds from zero.

The same rules apply to candidates who lost. A defeated candidate can keep a committee open and use the money if they run for federal office again.

This option applies to federal committees. Money raised for state and local campaigns falls under state law, which differs from place to place.

A federal candidate's campaign committee can move any amount of leftover money to a political party committee. Federal law sets no dollar cap on these transfers. A committee can send its entire remaining balance to a party in one transaction.

The permission covers all three levels of party organization. A committee can send funds to a national party committee, a state party committee or a local party committee. The Federal Election Campaign Act lists these transfers among the lawful uses of campaign money.

At the national level, each major party runs several committees. The Democratic National Committee and the Republican National Committee handle party-wide work. The Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee and the National Republican Senatorial Committee focus on Senate races. The Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee and the National Republican Congressional Committee do the same for the House.

State and local parties are separate organizations. Both major parties maintain state party organizations across the U.S. Many counties and cities also have local party committees. A candidate's committee can transfer money to these groups on the same unlimited terms as to national committees.

Sitting members of Congress regularly send campaign money to their party's congressional committees. Both parties' House campaign committees set fundraising expectations for their members, a practice commonly called party dues. A member's campaign account is one source of that money.

Once the money reaches a party committee, it belongs to the party. The party decides how to spend it, within the rules that govern party committees. That spending can include voter registration, get-out-the-vote drives and support for other candidates on the ticket.

Both sides of the transaction appear in public records. The candidate's committee reports the transfer as a disbursement on its regular filings with the Federal Election Commission. A party committee registered with the FEC reports the same money as a receipt. Anyone can look up these filings in the FEC's online database.

The absence of a ceiling sets party transfers apart from gifts to individual candidates. A contribution from one candidate's committee directly to another candidate faces a federal limit. A transfer to a party committee does not.

A federal campaign committee can give its leftover money to organizations that qualify as tax-exempt charities under section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code. No cap applies to the amount. The candidate cannot receive compensation from the donation or use it for personal benefit.

A committee can direct its entire surplus to one organization. It can also split the money among several groups. The federal contribution limits that cap gifts to other candidates and political action committees do not apply to charitable donations.

The 501(c)(3) category covers groups organized for charitable, religious, educational, scientific and similar purposes. Food banks, nonprofit hospitals, private universities and many other nonprofits fall into it. The Internal Revenue Service recognizes that status. Its online Tax Exempt Organization Search tool lets anyone check whether a group qualifies. Gifts to these organizations from individual donors are generally tax-deductible.

A candidate cannot route campaign money to a charity that then pays the candidate a salary or fee out of that gift. The donation also cannot serve as a way to deliver goods or services back to the candidate. These limits come from the Federal Election Campaign Act's ban on converting campaign funds to personal use.

Charity also takes the money out of electoral politics. The Internal Revenue Code bars 501(c)(3) organizations from participating in any campaign for or against a candidate for public office. A charity that receives leftover campaign funds cannot spend them supporting or opposing a candidate. The IRS enforces that restriction.

The gift stays on the public record. Campaign committees report their disbursements to the FEC. Itemized entries in those reports name the recipient and the amount. Anyone can search the filings to see which organization received the money.

For a candidate with no plans to seek federal office again, charity offers a permanent exit for the money. Money saved for a future race stays in the committee. A charity gift moves it out of the committee for good. One donation can clear a large balance in a single transaction.

A federal campaign committee can give its leftover money to other candidates and to political action committees. Unlike transfers to party committees, these gifts are capped by federal contribution limits.

Federal campaign finance law lets a candidate's committee contribute to other federal candidates. That gives a candidate with money left after a race a way to support others. A winning lawmaker may back colleagues in competitive seats. A losing candidate may support allies who share the same priorities.

The ceiling sets this route apart from several other ways of disposing of surplus funds. A gift from one candidate's committee to another counts as a contribution under federal law. The receiving campaign must count it toward the limit for that donor, as it would any other contribution.

Limits on gifts to other candidates apply per election. A primary and a general election count as separate elections. A runoff or special election also counts on its own. A committee can give up to the limit for each election in which the recipient is running.

Gifts to PACs work differently. The limit on contributions to a PAC is generally counted per calendar year rather than per election. The FEC publishes the current figures in a contribution limits chart on its website. Some of those limits are adjusted every two years, so the dollar amounts can shift from one cycle to the next. A committee checks the chart for the period in which it gives.

The caps restrict how much of a large surplus this route can absorb. A committee with a big balance would need many separate recipients to spend it down this way. Party transfers and charitable gifts carry no such ceiling. A committee can combine several permitted options to close out its balance.

Both sides of the transaction are public. The giving committee reports the disbursement to the FEC, and the recipient reports the receipt. Those reports appear in the FEC's online database, where anyone can trace money moving from one campaign to another.

A federal campaign committee can return leftover money to the people and organizations that gave it. Under Federal Election Commission rules, refunding contributions is a permitted way to dispose of surplus campaign funds.

A refund sends the money back to its original source. The donor recovers what they gave, and the committee's balance drops by the same amount. No new recipient gets the funds. That sets refunds apart from gifts to a charity, a party or another candidate, where the money moves on to someone else.

Refunds can go to any kind of contributor. Individuals, political action committees and party committees all give to federal candidates. A committee can send each of them its money back.

Refunds are not only an end-of-race decision. Federal rules also require committees to deal with certain contributions they cannot keep. A gift above the federal contribution limit must be refunded, redesignated or reattributed. A gift from a source barred from giving, such as a foreign national, must be refunded. Those corrective refunds follow their own deadlines. They are separate from a choice to return surplus money after an election.

Primary races create another common refund situation. Federal law treats a primary and a general election as separate elections, each with its own contribution limit. Some donors give money designated for the general election before the primary is decided. If the candidate loses the primary, the committee cannot spend that money on a race the candidate will not run. FEC rules require the committee to refund those funds or have the donor redesignate or reattribute them.

Every refund is a disbursement the committee must report to the FEC. Refunds show up in the committee's regular disclosure reports, which are public. Anyone can search those filings on the FEC website to see which committees returned money to donors.

The IRS does not let taxpayers deduct contributions to political campaigns or candidates. A donor who receives a refund recovers money that never produced a tax deduction in the first place.

A federal campaign committee can use leftover money to pay off its debts. Since the Supreme Court's 2022 ruling in FEC v. Ted Cruz for Senate, money raised after Election Day can also repay a candidate's personal loans in full.

Campaigns often end owing money. Unpaid bills to vendors, consultants, pollsters or staff remain obligations of the committee after the votes are counted. Paying them is a permitted use of surplus funds. A committee that still carries debt can also keep raising money to settle it.

Candidate loans are a separate category. Federal law lets candidates lend their own money to their campaigns without a limit on the amount. The harder question has been how that money gets paid back.

The Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002, often called McCain-Feingold, set a ceiling. A campaign could use no more than $250,000 in contributions collected after the election to repay a candidate's loans. FEC rules added a timing requirement. Loan amounts above the threshold had to be repaid with pre-election funds within 20 days of the election.

Ted Cruz challenged the restriction. The Texas Republican lent his 2018 Senate campaign $260,000 just before the election. That was $10,000 more than the cap allowed to be repaid from post-election money. His campaign let the 20-day window pass, leaving that amount unrecoverable under the rules. It then sued the FEC.

The Supreme Court struck down the limit in a 6-3 decision issued in May 2022. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote for the majority. He concluded that the cap burdened political speech in violation of the First Amendment. The majority found the government had not shown that the limit targeted actual corruption or its appearance.

Justice Elena Kagan wrote the dissent, joined by Justices Stephen Breyer and Sonia Sotomayor. She argued that post-election donations used to repay a candidate's loan flow to the candidate personally. Donors giving after the vote also know who won, which she viewed as a corruption risk.

The ruling has the largest effect on self-funding candidates. Those who lend their campaigns large sums can now seek full reimbursement through fundraising after the election. Contributions raised to retire that debt remain subject to the standard federal contribution limits.

Campaign funds can pay legal fees connected to a federal race. They can also cover a recount, an election contest and the accounting needed to comply with Federal Election Commission reporting rules. Each of these expenses can draw on money that remains after Election Day.

Recounts and election contests take place after ballots are cast. A recount retabulates the votes in a close race. An election contest is a formal legal challenge to a certified result. Both can require lawyers and staff. A committee with surplus money can use it to cover those bills.

Legal fees must be tied to the campaign. The personal-use test in the Federal Election Campaign Act applies to legal bills as well. It asks whether an expense would exist regardless of the campaign or the candidate's duties as an officeholder. Campaign money cannot pay for a legal matter the candidate would face anyway.

Compliance costs are a steady expense for any federal committee. Every committee must have a treasurer. The committee's reports to the FEC itemize what it received and what it spent. Preparing those filings takes bookkeeping and accounting work. Campaign funds can pay for that work.

Presidential general election campaigns have used separate legal and accounting compliance funds, commonly called GELACs. These accounts hold money raised for legal and compliance expenses. That money is kept apart from the main campaign account.

Public financing adds another rule. A presidential nominee who accepts a public grant for the general election receives money from the Presidential Election Campaign Fund. Taxpayers direct money to that fund by checking a $3 box on their federal income tax returns. Checking the box does not change the filer's tax owed or refund.

Accepting the grant comes with conditions. The nominee agrees not to raise private contributions for the general election campaign. The legal and accounting compliance fund is the exception. A publicly financed presidential campaign must also return surplus public money to the U.S. Treasury.

Privately raised money left in a committee carries no such repayment requirement.

A candidate who wins federal office can keep spending campaign money on ordinary and necessary expenses connected to the duties of that office. Those costs still cannot be personal expenses.

The permission comes from the Federal Election Campaign Act. The provision, codified at 52 U.S.C. 30114, lists officeholder expenses as a permitted use of contributions. The same section also covers campaign spending, charitable donations and transfers to party committees.

A newly elected member of the House or Senate still controls a campaign committee after Election Day. Under this provision, the committee's funds can pay for some costs of the job itself, not only the costs of running for it.

Campaign money is separate from the official funds Congress provides each member. Those office allowances come from the U.S. Treasury and pay for staff and offices. Campaign funds come largely from donors and stay subject to election law.

The limit on officeholder spending is the personal use rule. A cost is personal if it would exist even if the person did not hold office. A home mortgage stays off-limits for a sitting lawmaker, just as it does for a candidate.

That leaves a judgment for each cost. The question is whether the expense arises from the work of holding federal office or from private life. The FEC interprets and enforces the personal use rule. A committee unsure about a specific expense can request an FEC advisory opinion before spending the money.

Members of Congress also answer to their own chamber. The House Committee on Ethics and the Senate Select Committee on Ethics issue their own guidance on how lawmakers may use campaign funds. A lawmaker spending committee money has to satisfy both federal election law and the rules of the chamber where they serve.

Officeholder spending does not happen out of public view. The committee reports these payments to the FEC as disbursements, the same way it reports any other spending.

A federal campaign committee can close only after it has no outstanding debts or obligations and has disposed of all its remaining money. It then files a termination report with the Federal Election Commission. Until that report is filed, the committee must keep submitting its regular disclosure reports.

The two conditions work together. A committee with cash in the bank cannot simply abandon the balance and close its books. A committee that still owes vendors, staff or lenders cannot close while those bills stand. The debts and the bank account both have to reach zero before termination is possible.

Disposing of the remaining money means sending it somewhere federal rules permit. Refunds to donors, gifts to charities and transfers to party committees are among the allowed channels. A committee that wants to wind down has to move its final dollars through one of them. Only then can it ask to terminate.

Debts can take longer to clear than cash. A campaign can finish a race still owing money to consultants, pollsters, advertising firms or the candidate. Those obligations must be paid off or otherwise resolved before the committee can end. A committee that cannot pay its creditors in full can submit a debt settlement plan to the FEC on Form 8.

The termination report is the committee's final disclosure. It accounts for the last money in and the last money out. It also shows that nothing is left in the account and nothing is still owed. Once the FEC accepts it, the committee's reporting obligations end.

Until that point, the paperwork continues. A committee that has stopped campaigning but still holds money or owes debts remains registered with the FEC. It must keep filing its regular reports on schedule, even when little happens between them.

Federal law sets no deadline for a committee to close. A committee that stays open keeps its reporting duties for as long as it remains registered. Some committees stay on the FEC's books for years after a candidate's last campaign.

The filings themselves are public records. The FEC posts committee reports on its website. Anyone can use them to follow a committee's money from its final campaign through its termination report.

How American & United Airlines Are Turning 19-Year-Olds With Zero Flight Hours Into Branded Airline Pilots

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Philip Pilosian & Ceri Breeze | Shutterstock, Simple Flying

For decades, the traditional route to an airline cockpit followed a familiar pattern - aspiring pilots learned to fly independently, paid for ratings however they could, accumulated hours through flight instruction or charter work, and then competed for regional airline jobs before eventually reaching a major carrier.

The journey was expensive, uncertain, and largely disconnected from the airlines that would one day employ successful candidates. That model is now changing, with two of the largest carriers in the US becoming involved years earlier than ever before. United Airlines' Aviate program and American Airlines' Cadet Academy represent something much bigger than a response to pilot shortages.

Rather than simply recruiting experienced aviators, the two legacy carriers are increasingly manufacturing their own future workforce from the very beginning, welcoming candidates as young as 19 with no previous flight experience, financing their training, shaping their professional development, and creating airline-branded pilots long before they ever operate a scheduled passenger flight.

The emergence of structured ab-initio pilot programs marks a significant shift in how major US airlines think about recruitment. Instead of waiting for pilots to arrive after years of independent flying, both United Airlines and American Airlines have built systems that identify candidates before they have logged their first hour in an aircraft, then guide them through every major stage of their development.

This approach reflects the enormous long-term demand facing commercial aviation. Industry forecasts project that airlines around the world will require more than 600,000 new pilots over the next two decades, while United Airlines alone expects to hire more than 10,000 pilots during the next ten years.

Those figures make relying entirely on traditional recruitment increasingly risky, especially as retirements continue to reduce the existing pilot pool. Rather than treating pilot hiring as the final step in a long career journey, both airlines increasingly see training as the first stage of employment.

Candidates apply to airline-branded academies, complete standardized flight instruction, become instructors to build experience, move into affiliated regional airlines, and eventually transition into mainline operations under an established pathway. The result is a system where airlines have visibility over future employees from the very beginning, creating a far more predictable talent pipeline than previous generations ever experienced.

The academy trains hundreds of pilots each year.

Andrey_Popov | Shutterstock, Simple Flying

Perhaps the biggest attraction of these programs is the clarity they provide. Traditional flight training often required aspiring pilots to make major financial commitments without any certainty that an airline job would ultimately materialize, forcing graduates to search independently for instructing positions before competing for regional airline openings.

United Airlines' Aviate pathway and American Airlines' Cadet Academy replace much of that uncertainty with a clearly defined sequence of milestones. Students begin with private pilot training before progressing through instrument, commercial, multi-engine, and instructor certifications.

After qualifying as certified flight instructors, they accumulate the flight experience required for airline employment while teaching new students, remaining within the broader airline-sponsored ecosystem. The next step usually involves joining wholly owned or closely affiliated regional carriers.

American Airlines' process feeds pilots toward the oneworld carrier's regional subsidiary operators, including Envoy Air, PSA Airlines, and Piedmont Airlines, while United Airlines' pathway directs graduates toward participating United Express operators. Instead of repeatedly searching for new employers as experience increases, participants generally follow a planned progression.

This eventually leads them toward the mainline airline. United Airlines' pathway includes another notable benchmark before pilots can transition into the mainline operation. Aviate participants must complete 1,200 hours as pilot in command while flying for a participating regional or approved operator.

Thereafter, they become eligible to join the Star Alliance carrier as a first officer. That requirement ensures pilots arrive with meaningful operational experience while still remaining within a carefully managed development system that has followed them since their earliest training flights.

Learning to fly has always demanded a substantial financial commitment, making cost one of the largest obstacles preventing many talented candidates from pursuing airline careers. Flight training from zero experience through instructor certifications can easily reach well into six figures, particularly when housing and living expenses are included.

American Airlines has responded by integrating specialized financing directly into the Cadet Academy experience. Through American Airlines Federal Credit Union, eligible students can obtain loans of up to $148,000 covering flight training costs, housing expenses, and even additional living support.

There are no required loan payments for as long as 45 months while completing training and beginning their careers. That structure fundamentally changes the financial calculation for many applicants. Instead of assembling funding from multiple private lenders or interrupting training because of cash flow problems, students can focus on progressing through the academy with financing.

Such support is specifically designed around the unusually long timeline required for professional pilot development. United Airlines also places considerable emphasis on reducing financial barriers. Alongside financing opportunities available through training partners, the carrier, Boeing, and Chase have collectively funded millions of dollars in scholarship support for Aviate Academy students.

This includes a $3.45 million initiative designed partly to broaden access and diversify the future pilot workforce. These efforts acknowledge that expanding recruitment requires attracting candidates who previously may have considered professional aviation financially unattainable.

A closer look at the hidden factors behind the pilot shortage crisis.

While financing and guaranteed career pathways receive much of the public attention, the airlines arguably gain an equally valuable advantage through early cultural development. Instead of introducing company procedures after pilots have accumulated thousands of hours elsewhere, the airlines begin shaping professional behavior from the very first stages of training.

Students are introduced early to airline expectations surrounding Crew Resource Management, automation philosophy, communication standards, decision-making, professionalism, and operational discipline. These concepts exist throughout commercial aviation, but teaching them within an airline-specific framework allows companies to reinforce the behaviors they believe best support their own operational culture.

This creates a very different educational environment from traditional independent flight schools. This is because, rather than simply producing licensed commercial pilots who later adapt to whichever airline hires them, academy students develop while already identifying with a specific carrier's expectations and values.

Every stage, from classroom instruction through regional airline operations, reinforces those standards before candidates ever reach a cockpit. The approach also functions as an extended evaluation process, enabling airlines to observe performance, professionalism, leadership, communication, and decision-making across several years.

This acts as an alternative to relying primarily on interviews conducted shortly before employment. Technical flying skills remain essential, but the longer relationship gives recruiters a much richer understanding of how future pilots respond to training, feedback, teamwork, and operational pressures throughout their development.

One of the more interesting aspects of these programs is how effectively they transform recruitment into long-term brand building. Students are not simply learning to become airline pilots: rather, they are encouraged to think of themselves as future United Airlines or American Airlines pilots almost immediately after joining their respective academies.

That identity develops gradually throughout the structured pathway: cadets interact with airline mentors, attend company events, receive guidance from employees already flying within the network, and become familiar with company culture years before operating scheduled passenger services.

Rather than viewing regional airlines as temporary employers to leave at the earliest opportunity, participants increasingly see each career stage as part of a single journey toward the same parent airline. From the airlines' perspective, this creates a stronger organizational commitment than traditional hiring models.

Pilots who have spent years progressing through a branded system have already invested significant time, relationships, and professional identity within that company. The airline has effectively cultivated loyalty throughout the training process instead of attempting to establish it only after employment begins.

This branding strategy also differentiates the modern academies from earlier airline-sponsored recruitment initiatives. The emphasis extends beyond simply producing qualified pilots to creating individuals who already understand company expectations, recognize familiar faces within the organization, and identify personally with the airline's broader culture before reaching the mainline fleet.

The significance of these programs extends well beyond solving immediate staffing challenges. They represent a broader shift in how legacy airlines view talent development, replacing reactive recruitment with long-term workforce planning that begins almost at the moment someone decides to become a pilot.

Rather than depending on an external training ecosystem to produce candidates with varying backgrounds, United Airlines and American Airlines increasingly control much of the developmental process themselves. Standardized instruction, integrated financing, predictable career progression, and continuous evaluation produce pilots whose professional experience has unfolded almost entirely within one airline family's operational philosophy.

That level of involvement would have been unusual only a generation ago, when major airlines largely hired pilots after careers built across numerous employers, military service, charter operators, or independent flight schools. Today's model instead resembles an integrated apprenticeship stretching across many years, where technical qualifications, cultural alignment, and career progression all develop simultaneously.

For pilots, these pathways dramatically reduce many of the traditional uncertainties surrounding an aviation career. For the airlines, they accomplish something equally important by ensuring that long before a new first officer reports to operate a Boeing or Airbus aircraft. Years of training have already shaped not only how that pilot flies, but also how they think, communicate, and identify with the airline whose uniform they will eventually wear.

D&D’s World of Warcraft Crossover Is the Bold Shake-Up the Game Needed

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The crossover creates opportunities for more complex characters and fights

Wizards of the Coast has been playing things safe since updating the rules of Dungeons & Dragons 5th Edition in 2024. After lead designers Jeremy Crawford and Chris Perkins left Wizards in 2025 to join Critical Role’s Darrington Press, Wizards slowed the pace of new D&D releases. Recent books have focused mostly on updating the lore and rules for the game’s most popular campaign settings and rebalancing previously published character options. D&D’s newest release bucks the trend, delivering an ambitious collection of innovative character options, monsters, and ways to use some of the game’s less developed subsystems. It just requires venturing to a new world.

Dungeons & Dragons: World of Warcraft is the first book in the Universes Beyond line, an extension of the controversial Magic: The Gathering strategy that filled the collectible card game with licensed tie-ins. Even though WoW and D&D share many of the same classes and monsters, marrying the two games is a daunting challenge that involves incorporating lore from 22 years of video game releases and 12,000 years of conflict on the planet of Azeroth. But it also provided a unique opportunity to increase D&D’s complexity.

A big part of 5E’s appeal is how simple it is to learn and play, especially at low levels. But WoW is a game about big boss fights and characters with a huge array of abilities, and D&D: World of Warcraft reflects that with an emphasis on high-level play and more tactical combat at every level.

Sometimes the translation is needlessly confusing. WoW has some heroic classes that don’t start at level 1. D&D: WoW both introduces the term and then tells players they can start at level 1 anyway. Once you can get past the confusing language, the result is a set of rich new character options that DMs might want to consider for any game. For instance, the Death Knight modifies many of the core abilities of a Paladin, going beyond the neat building blocks of a subclass to transform a holy warrior into a necromancy-powered master of the battlefield who can pull foes into melee and charge through walls on a spectral steed. Demon Hunters, a similarly dramatic reimagining of Rangers, utilize Warlock powers and gather souls to heal themselves and harm their enemies.

The extra power is necessary when facing the denizens of WoW’s dungeons. Lead designer James Wyatt and his team capture the feel of WoW’s multiphased boss fights with a mix of legendary actions, the bloodied condition that 5.5E brought back from 4E, and the mythic actions introduced in the Magic crossover campaign book Mythic Odysseys of Theros. When bloodied or dropped to zero hit points, many enemies heal and unlock devastating new abilities that can make the fight feel more desperate as it goes on.

The book does a solid job of compressing WoW’s extremely convoluted lore and storylines to then transform them into compelling adventure hooks. There are plenty of ideas for campaigns of all levels focused on fighting back the corrupting influence of the Old Gods, the rampaging Scourge horde, or the relentless wrath of the Burning Legion, but this is a book that really shines when it comes to high-level dungeons and enemies. Once D&D characters hit level 10, they gain access to powerful magic that makes travel nearly instantaneous or quickly cures most problems. This disrupts the Tolkien-inspired fantasy most D&D games are built on. But these powers perfectly fit the tone of WoW, where players are zipping across the globe to fight apocalyptic threats before teleporting back to the safety of a cozy inn. Likewise, the newly introduced Potions of Mana, which let characters recover spell slots, would break most games, but they work when parties are expected to undergo a gauntlet of tough battles to clear out a dungeon.

Beyond enhancing combat, the book boosts the utility of some of D&D’s underutilized subsystems to get across the richness of WoW’s gameplay. The Dungeon Master Guide’s Renown system is used to track a character’s reputation with WoW’s many factions so they can earn access to powerful rewards. Crafting gets a huge overhaul as well, allowing characters to use their tool proficiencies to make magical items even if they’re not spellcasters. Likewise, the foraging action taken during exploration expands to represent WoW’s gathering skills by allowing characters to search for rare materials.

It’s a shame that this isn’t a bigger book. Wyatt and his team clearly wanted to go further into depth in the crafting and gathering section and create a recipe list where each item has its own components found in certain areas of the world. Instead they mostly abstract it, but then give some specific examples, making the section feel convoluted and far too reliant on external research. Similarly, the enemies are well-crafted and flavorful, but just a bit more text covering the environmental challenges players might face exploring dungeons and the terrain in fights would go a long way to making those adventures more immersive.

WoW and D&D have so much in common that this crossover feels natural in a way that’s less likely to be true for the Star Wars Universes Beyond book scheduled for release in 2027. But D&D: WoW gives me hope that the Universes Beyond endeavor can be more than just a business tactic to tap into existing fandom, creating a place where D&D’s designers have room to experiment with mechanics viewed as too complex for the core game. Even if you’ve never played WoW, it’s worth checking out this book for fresh ideas on how to challenge your players, especially at higher levels.

From the 1845 law to midnight votes, 15 facts about how Election Day works

The reason Americans vote on a Tuesday goes back to an 1845 law and a farm calendar, and 15 slides trace the history through ballots, amendments and traditions

The U.S. picks its presidents on a schedule designed for a very different country. When Congress settled on a single national voting day in 1845, most Americans lived in rural areas and worked in agriculture. Many had to reach a county seat to cast a ballot, and that trip could take a day or more. The calendar lawmakers chose reflected long trips, harvests and Sunday worship — not office jobs, commutes or mail-in ballots.

That 1845 law fixed the date for choosing presidential electors as the Tuesday after the first Monday in November. It said nothing about why. The usual reasons come from historical accounts, not from the statute's text. They include a harvest finished by November, roads still passable before winter and a Sunday kept free of travel. Other stories, such as Wednesday being market day, get repeated so often that they sound settled. They are commonly cited explanations, not documented ones.

Knowing why Americans vote on a Tuesday also explains how the rest of the system grew around that date. Before 1845, states set their own dates within a window Congress allowed, and voting stretched across weeks. In 1872, Congress extended the same November date to House elections. Senate races joined after the 17th Amendment gave voters, not state legislatures, the power to elect senators. The date stayed put while the rules about who could vote, and how, changed around it.

The secret ballot replaced the often public practice of voting by voice or with party-printed tickets. Constitutional amendments protected women's right to vote, banned poll taxes in federal elections and set the voting age at 18. Congress passed the Voting Rights Act in 1965. Each shift happened on top of a calendar first drawn for a farming economy.

The next group traces how elections for Congress joined the same date and how access to the ballot widened. The last group looks at the traditions and rules that surround the day now. Those include a New Hampshire town's midnight vote and the fact that Election Day is not a federal holiday. They also compare how other democracies schedule elections and trace the weeks between the vote and the inauguration.

Americans vote on a Tuesday because of a federal law Congress passed in 1845. It set one national day for choosing presidential electors: the Tuesday after the first Monday in November. That rule still fixes the date of presidential elections.

The law covered presidential elections only. It did not set the timing of congressional races, which stayed under state control for decades afterward. Voters were also not choosing a president directly. They were choosing the electors who would cast the formal votes for president on behalf of each state.

Congress had clear authority to act. Article II of the Constitution lets Congress determine the time of choosing electors and the day on which they give their votes. It also requires that the electors' voting day be the same throughout the nation. The 1845 statute used that power to fix a single Election Day for the whole country.

President John Tyler signed the measure in January 1845. The 1844 race had already been decided under the older arrangement. The new rule first applied four years later. The 1848 contest was the first presidential election held under the single-day system. Zachary Taylor, the Whig candidate, won it.

The law also included a fallback provision. It allowed a state that failed to make a choice on Election Day to appoint its electors on a later day. The state legislature could direct how that later appointment happened. That clause stayed on the books for more than 170 years. Congress narrowed it in the Electoral Count Reform Act of 2022. The newer law permits a state to extend its voting period only for extraordinary and catastrophic events.

The 1845 law set the timing of the choice, not its method. Each state legislature still decided how its electors would be appointed. South Carolina's legislature kept picking the state's electors itself, rather than holding a popular vote, until after the Civil War. The Election Day rule now sits in Title 3 of the U.S. Code, the part of federal law that governs presidential elections.

Election Day was built around the working life of the American farmer. According to the standard historical account, when Congress set a national voting date in 1845, the calendar had to suit a country where most voters lived and worked on farms.

Distance was the central problem. For many rural voters, casting a ballot meant traveling to the county seat. That trip could take a day or more.

Travel moved at the pace of a horse. In the 1840s, a voter generally made the journey on horseback, by wagon or on foot. Most roads were unpaved. Rain could turn those dirt roads to mud and lengthen the trip. Railroads were still new and covered only a small part of the country. Few farm families could count on a train to reach the polls.

A trip of that length turned voting into an errand measured in days, not hours. A voter had to leave home, reach the polling place, cast a ballot and then make the return journey.

For a farm family, each of those days carried a cost. Time on the road was time away from crops and livestock. In the standard account, a workable date had to leave room for the journey in both directions, not only for the moment at the ballot box.

That logic belonged to the electorate of the 1840s. The U.S. later became a mostly urban country. The 1920 census was the first to count more Americans living in urban areas than in rural ones. For that count, the Census Bureau treated places with 2,500 or more residents as urban. That crossover arrived 75 years after the 1845 law.

The federal voting date did not move with that shift. Election Day still falls on the schedule Congress fixed in 1845, when reaching the polls could mean a day or more on the road.

Election Day landed on Tuesday largely by elimination. Sunday was reserved for worship, and a Monday vote would have forced many voters to travel on Sunday.

The Sunday problem was the simplest to see. In the mid-1800s, Sunday was a day of worship for much of the country. Holding an election on it would have put civic duty in direct competition with church. The national date Congress set in 1845 kept voting off the Sabbath.

Monday created a second-order problem. Rural voters who lived far from the polls needed travel time before they could cast a ballot. A Monday election would have meant leaving home on Sunday. That ran into the same objection that ruled out Sunday voting itself. Tuesday gave those voters a full day to make the trip after worship.

Wednesday is where the history gets less certain. A frequently repeated account holds that Wednesday was market day in many places, when farmers brought goods to town to sell. Under that account, a Wednesday election would have cut into a working day that rural families depended on.

That market-day explanation is a commonly cited reason, not documented intent. The 1845 statute fixes the date without recording a rationale for any weekday. The reasons attached to it today are reconstructions built from the conditions of the period. They are not language written into the law.

Taken together, the three constraints point toward a narrow window. A voter could attend worship on Sunday, travel on Monday and cast a ballot on Tuesday. Under the market-day account, that voter could also be back for Wednesday's trading. The commonly repeated explanation does not address Thursday, Friday or Saturday, and the law itself is silent on them too.

The result has outlasted the farm economy that shaped it. The same Tuesday rule still governs federal elections, even though few Americans now farm or plan their week around a town market.

Election Day falls in November because the month offered a workable window in a farming country. The standard historical explanation holds that the harvest was largely finished by then, while winter had not yet closed the roads.

That explanation comes from historical accounts, not from the law. The 1845 statute that created a single national date for choosing presidential electors names the month without explaining it.

The farm side of the reasoning follows the agricultural year. Spring meant planting. Summer and early autumn meant tending and gathering crops. A vote during those months would have pulled people away from the fields at the busiest time. Much of that work was done by November.

The weather side concerns travel. Many voters went to the polls by horse, by wagon or on foot over unpaved roads. Snow, ice and mud could make those routes hard or impossible to use later in the year. An early November vote came before the worst of winter across much of the country.

Those conditions were not the same everywhere. Winter arrived far earlier in New England than along the Gulf Coast. The 1845 law still set one national date for every state.

The November timing also fit the federal schedule already in place. A 1792 law had let states choose electors during a window that closed before the electors met in December. The 1845 date fell inside that window, so the electors' December meeting did not have to move.

The first presidential election held on the uniform date took place on Nov. 7, 1848. Federal law still sets the presidential vote in November, in Title 3 of the U.S. Code. That vote comes every fourth year, in years divisible by four, such as 2024 and 2028. The date has outlasted the farm economy that is said to have shaped it.

The phrase "the Tuesday after the first Monday in November" fixes federal Election Day to a date between Nov. 2 and Nov. 8. That wording means the vote can never land on Nov. 1, even in years when the month begins on a Tuesday.

The rule works in two steps. Find the first Monday of November, then move forward one day. A simpler instruction, such as "the first Tuesday in November," would put the election on Nov. 1 whenever the month opens on a Tuesday. The extra step removes that possibility.

The 2016 presidential election shows the formula at work. Nov. 1, 2016, fell on a Tuesday, so the first Monday of that month was Nov. 7. Voters went to the polls the next day, Nov. 8. That is the latest date the formula allows.

The other end of the range appears when November starts on a Monday. In 2004, Nov. 1 was a Monday, which made Nov. 2 Election Day. The 2020 election, held on Nov. 3, landed near the start of the window.

The 1845 law that created the formula does not say why lawmakers wrote it this way. Two reasons for keeping the vote off Nov. 1 are commonly cited. Both are later explanations, not reasons recorded in the statute.

The first involves All Saints' Day. The Christian feast falls on Nov. 1. For Roman Catholics, it is a holy day of obligation, when attendance at Mass is expected. The explanation holds that lawmakers wanted to avoid scheduling a national vote on a major religious observance.

The second involves commerce. Merchants are often said to have used the first of the month to close out the previous month's accounts. Under this account, a Nov. 1 vote would have collided with a day many business owners set aside for bookkeeping.

The result is a window of seven possible dates. The formula depends only on the calendar, so any future Election Day can be worked out years ahead. The 2028 presidential election, for example, falls on Nov. 7.

Before 1845, there was no single Election Day for president. Each state picked its own date for choosing presidential electors, as long as it fell inside a window Congress set in 1792.

That 1792 federal law gave states a 34-day window. Electors could be appointed at any point during the 34 days before the first Wednesday in December, the day the electors then met to vote. A state could hold its contest early in that span or late in it.

The Constitution made this arrangement possible. Article II gives Congress the power to set the time for choosing electors and the day on which electors cast their votes. It requires that the electors' voting day be the same throughout the U.S. It sets no such uniformity rule for choosing the electors themselves. Until 1845, Congress left that timing to the states within the federal window.

Article II also leaves the method of appointing electors to each state legislature. In the early decades, many legislatures chose electors themselves instead of holding a popular vote. By the 1830s, South Carolina was the only state still doing so. Its legislature kept picking the state's electors through the 1860 election. Where a popular vote did happen, the state set its own date for it.

The practical result was a presidential election spread across weeks. Voters in one state could go to the polls well before voters in another. The national contest unfolded in pieces, with each state on its own schedule.

That staggered calendar created a timing problem. Results from states that voted early could become known before other states had voted at all. Voters in later states could cast ballots after some of those outcomes were already known.

That problem is a commonly cited reason for a single national day. Congress settled the question in 1845. It replaced the open window with one fixed date for choosing presidential electors in every state.

Congress put elections for the U.S. House of Representatives on the Tuesday after the first Monday in November through a law passed in 1872. That measure gave House races the same national date already used for choosing presidential electors.

The 1872 law arrived 27 years after the 1845 statute that first created a single national Election Day. The earlier law covered presidential elections only. It set no date for congressional contests.

Before 1872, each state picked its own date for House elections. Some states held those races on the same day as presidential voting. Others held them on different days. The country had no single date for choosing its representatives.

The Constitution's Elections Clause gave Congress the authority to make the change. The clause leaves the times, places and manner of congressional elections to state legislatures. It also lets Congress make or alter those rules. The 1872 law used that federal power to set one date for House races across the country.

After 1872, House elections shared the November date with presidential voting. The Constitution puts every House seat up for election every two years. As a result, the law placed a federal contest on the November ballot in years without a presidential race, too.

U.S. Senate seats were not part of this change. State legislatures still chose senators at the time, so there was no popular Senate vote to schedule.

Voters have elected U.S. senators directly only since 1913, when the 17th Amendment was ratified. Before that, state legislatures chose senators. The change moved Senate races onto the same national November date that voters already used for other federal offices.

The original Constitution set up the Senate differently from the House. Article I, Section 3 gave each state two senators. It also said they would be chosen by the state's legislature for six-year terms. House members, by contrast, were elected by the people from the start. The framers designed the two chambers to answer to different constituencies.

Under that system, a voter's influence over the Senate was indirect. Citizens elected state lawmakers, and those lawmakers then picked the senators. A Senate seat could hinge on which party controlled a state legislature rather than on a statewide vote.

The 17th Amendment rewrote that arrangement. Its text provides that the Senate is made up of two senators from each state, elected by the people of that state, for six years. It also requires voters in Senate elections to meet the same qualifications as voters for the most numerous branch of their state legislature. That wording tied eligibility for Senate voting to rules each state already used.

The amendment also addressed vacancies. It directs a state's governor to call an election to fill an open seat. A state legislature may empower the governor to make a temporary appointment until voters fill the seat.

Once senators were chosen by popular vote, their races joined the federal ballot on the Tuesday after the first Monday in November. Presidential, House and Senate contests now share that date.

The six-year term did not change. Senate terms are staggered. About one-third of the seats come up in each two-year election cycle. As a result, each regular November general election includes Senate races in only some states. Every House seat, by contrast, is on the ballot each time.

The secret ballot let Americans mark a government-printed ballot in private, replacing a system in which many votes were cast in public. Massachusetts adopted the approach statewide in 1888, and other states followed over the next several years.

Before that shift, casting a vote was often a visible act. In some places, voters announced their choices aloud at the polling place. Elsewhere, they handed in tickets printed and distributed by political parties rather than by election officials.

A party ticket usually listed only that party's candidates. A voter could pick one up from a party worker, carry it to the polls and drop it in the ballot box. The paper itself could show anyone watching which side a voter supported.

That visibility had consequences. An employer, a landlord or a party operative standing near the polls could see how a person voted. Someone paying for votes could confirm that the purchase had been delivered. Voters who depended on others for work or housing had reason to think twice before breaking with them in public.

The reform became known as the Australian ballot. It took that name because the colony of Victoria, in Australia, introduced it in 1856, decades before it reached the U.S. Under the system, election officials, not parties, printed the ballots. Every qualifying candidate appeared on the same sheet. Voters marked their choices out of view of others.

Private marking meant a vote buyer could no longer check what a voter had done. A single official ballot listing all candidates made it easier to choose people from different parties in the same election. Government printing also meant the state now had to decide whose names appeared on the ballot. That pushed lawmakers to write rules for how candidates qualified.

The ballot most Americans fill out on the Tuesday after the first Monday in November descends from this late-19th-century design. The private voting booth, the uniform official ballot and the expectation that no one else sees a voter's choices all trace back to it.

The 19th Amendment, ratified in 1920, bars the federal government and the states from denying the vote on account of sex. The presidential election of Nov. 2, 1920 was the first in which women across the country could vote with that right protected by the Constitution.

The amendment's core text is a single sentence. It says the right of citizens of the U.S. to vote "shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of sex." A second section gives Congress the power to enforce that guarantee through legislation.

The proposal took more than four decades to clear Congress. Sen. Aaron Sargent of California introduced the language in 1878. It became known as the Susan B. Anthony Amendment. The House approved it on May 21, 1919. The Senate followed on June 4, 1919, sending it to the states.

Ratification required approval from three-fourths of the 48 states then in the union. Tennessee became the 36th state to ratify on Aug. 18, 1920. That vote supplied the final approval needed. Secretary of State Bainbridge Colby certified the amendment on Aug. 26, 1920.

The November vote that followed kept the schedule Congress had set in 1845. Nov. 2, 1920 was the Tuesday after the first Monday of the month. Republican Warren G. Harding defeated Democrat James M. Cox in that race.

The push for a national guarantee began long before 1920. The Seneca Falls Convention in New York issued a call for women's voting rights in 1848. Wyoming's territorial legislature granted women the vote in 1869. Several Western states extended full voting rights to women before the amendment took effect. The 19th Amendment replaced that state-by-state patchwork with a constitutional standard.

The amendment did not put a ballot in every woman's hand in practice. Many Black women in Southern states stayed blocked by the same discriminatory registration rules used against Black men. Many Native American women could not vote because the U.S. did not yet recognize them as citizens. Congress passed the Indian Citizenship Act in 1924. Some states still restricted Native voting for years afterward.

Three changes between 1964 and 1971 widened who could cast a ballot on Election Day. The 24th Amendment, ratified in 1964, banned poll taxes in federal elections. The Voting Rights Act became law in 1965. The 26th Amendment, ratified in 1971, set the voting age at 18.

A poll tax was a fee charged as a condition of voting. By the 1960s it survived mainly in Southern states. There it had long served to keep Black voters and poor white voters away from the polls. The 24th Amendment covered only federal races — presidential, Senate and House contests. Its text left state and local elections untouched.

That gap closed two years later. In Harper v. Virginia Board of Elections, decided in 1966, the Supreme Court ruled that poll taxes in state elections violated the Equal Protection Clause of the 14th Amendment. The amendment and the ruling, taken together, ended the practice.

The Voting Rights Act reached further than the poll tax ban. President Lyndon B. Johnson signed it on Aug. 6, 1965. That came months after state troopers attacked civil rights marchers on the Edmund Pettus Bridge in Selma, Alabama. The law targeted tools such as literacy tests, which local officials had used to turn away Black applicants. It also allowed federal examiners to register voters in some places.

One of its central provisions, known as preclearance, required jurisdictions covered by the law to get federal approval before changing their voting rules. In Shelby County v. Holder, decided in 2013, the Supreme Court struck down the formula that determined which jurisdictions were covered. Without a valid formula, the preclearance requirement no longer applies to the jurisdictions it once covered. Other parts of the act remain in force.

The 26th Amendment lowered the minimum voting age to 18 in federal, state and local elections. Before it, most states set the minimum at 21. The push gained force during the Vietnam War. Men could be drafted at 18, but in most states they could not vote. Supporters rallied around the slogan "old enough to fight, old enough to vote."

Congress had first tried to lower the age by statute. In Oregon v. Mitchell, decided in 1970, the Supreme Court ruled that Congress could set an 18-year-old voting age for federal elections but not for state ones. That split would have forced many states to run separate voting systems for different races. A constitutional amendment resolved the problem, and the states ratified it in 1971.

Dixville Notch, a small town in New Hampshire, casts its Election Day ballots at midnight, in the first minutes of the voting day. The town has held this midnight vote since 1960.

Federal Election Day falls on a Tuesday. In Dixville Notch, voting begins as soon as Monday night turns into Tuesday morning. Most polling places in the U.S. open hours later, in the morning. That timing puts the town's ballots in before most of the country has started voting.

The tradition started in a presidential election year. The town has repeated the midnight vote in the decades since. Its ballots are now among the first cast on Election Day in the U.S.

The early start changes only when residents vote, not the rules they vote under. Dixville Notch uses the same national date as every other community. Its ballots count toward New Hampshire's results the same way any other town's do.

Election Day is a regular working day in the U.S. Federal law fixes the date for federal elections, but no federal law makes that Tuesday a federal holiday.

That leaves time off to the states. Some states have their own provisions that give workers time off to vote. Others treat Election Day as a state holiday. The rules vary, so what a voter can expect depends on where they live and work.

The date and the holiday question sit in different parts of federal law. The 1845 law and the statutes that followed it set when federal elections take place. Federal holidays are listed separately, in Title 5, Section 6103, of the U.S. Code, and Election Day is not among them.

The list sets paid days off for federal employees. Federal law does not require private employers to close on any of those days. Federal offices stay open on Election Day, and it runs like any other Tuesday on the federal calendar.

One federal holiday sits close by. Veterans Day falls on Nov. 11. Federal Election Day can land no later than Nov. 8, so the two never coincide. Veterans Day arrives three to nine days after the vote.

For voters, working-day status shapes the time they have. People with fixed shifts who vote in person that day must go before work, after work or during a break, unless they get time off. Many states also offer early in-person voting or mail ballots, which let people vote without a Tuesday trip to the polls. Polling hours, which states set, decide how much room the day itself leaves.

The result is a split system. The date is national and fixed by federal law. Any time away from work to use it comes from state law, an employer's own policy or a voter's own schedule.

Germany holds its federal elections on Sundays, while U.K. general elections have traditionally fallen on Thursdays. Many other countries also schedule national votes on weekends. The American Tuesday is one answer among several to the question of when a country should vote.

Germany's Sunday schedule moves the federal vote outside the Monday-to-Friday workweek. A voter on a standard weekday schedule does not have to arrange time off, leave early or line up before a shift to take part.

The U.K. custom sits closer to the American model. Thursday, like Tuesday, falls in the middle of the working week. Voters with weekday jobs in either country have to fit a trip to the polling place around their hours on the job.

Weekend voting in other countries follows the same reasoning as Germany's Sunday elections. For people who work Monday through Friday, a Saturday or Sunday vote falls on a day off. The trade-off runs the other way for people whose jobs include weekend shifts. They face the same scheduling problem a weekday voter faces in the U.S. or U.K.

Set side by side, these approaches show that the day of the week is a choice each country makes, not a fixed feature of national elections. Germany and many other countries put the vote on a weekend. The U.K. and the U.S. keep it on a weekday.

Election Day does not install a president. Voters choose electors in November, those electors cast the formal votes in December, and the new term begins at noon on Jan. 20 under the 20th Amendment, ratified in 1933.

The electors meet in their own states rather than in one national gathering. The Constitution requires that arrangement. Under the 12th Amendment, each state's electors cast separate ballots for president and vice president. The results are sealed and sent to Washington, D.C.

Electors are chosen through the popular vote in each state. Their December ballots are the formal step between November's results and the official count in Congress.

Congress then counts the electoral votes in a joint session in January. The sitting vice president, acting as president of the Senate, opens the certificates. A candidate needs a majority of the 538 electoral votes, which is 270, to win outright. If no one reaches that mark, the House of Representatives chooses the president. In that case, each state delegation casts one vote.

For most of the country's history, the wait was far longer. Presidents took office on March 4, a date tied to the start of the federal government under the Constitution in 1789. That left about four months between the November vote and the transfer of power.

The long gap had real consequences. After Abraham Lincoln won in November 1860, seven Southern states seceded before he took office in March 1861. James Buchanan remained president through that winter.

The 20th Amendment cut the presidential wait by roughly six weeks. It also moved the start of new congressional terms to Jan. 3. As a result, a newly elected Congress is seated before it counts the electoral votes. The measure is often called the lame duck amendment. It shortened the period that outgoing officials, known as lame ducks, serve after an election.

Franklin D. Roosevelt was the last president first inaugurated on March 4, in 1933. His second inauguration, in 1937, was the first held on Jan. 20.

The latest for gun hunters and competitive shooters.

Hunting rifles come and go and sometimes even good ones don’t stay around. The decades following World War II saw a boom in hunters and a voracious appetite for new rifles and cartridges. This was the heyday of the Pre-64 Model 70 and the Winchester Magnums, but we no longer hear much about the many Mauser sporting rifles made by FN and imported from Belgium.

I recently got my mitts on one of these rifles, which belonged to my great grandfather, chambered in .270 Win. While this gun undoubtedly rode around in the rifle rack of Grandpa Curtis’s truck when I was a youngster, I’m looking at it anew. According to my dad and uncle, Grandpa’s brother was a gunsmith and built the rifle for him. This was after he’d raised a family in a log cabin in Colorado during the Depression and developed a taste for July mule deer. He used this rifle to shoot a lot of deer and elk.

A family heirloom like this gives me a perspective on what a quality hunting rifle looked like in the 1950s, and an idea of how well it’s held up over time. It’s also fun to compare it to the rifles we have today.

It’s common to hear or read about the many sporterized military rifles that post-war hunters adopted. These would typically be either 1903 Springfields or remnant military Mauser rifles. But these FN Mausers weren’t sporterized — they were manufactured for a couple decades as sporting-specific guns. Many, including this rifle, were imported by Firearms International Corp. and marked as such on the receivers.

They were brought in and sold by various companies and in numerous formats. An ad in the November, 1951 Issue of Outdoor Life displays the JC Higgins Model 50 — which was simply an FN Mauser and sold at Sears, Roebuck. They had the chrome vanadium steel barrel and even came with a 2.5-power scope, for $135. Another ad, from the September, 1952 issue, for the Parker-Whelen Gun Catalog, lists .30/06 and .270 FN Mausers for $137.50 without a receiver-mounted rear sight. That would cost you another ten bucks. This could be a clue to the specific origin of this .270, as the front of the barrel has markings that appear to be where a front sight was once mounted, but there is no evidence of a barrel-mounted rear sight like you’ll see on Pre-64 Model 70 rifles and others like the Remington Model 721.

Ultimately, lots of these guns were imported in various forms and were sold by companies like Weatherby, Harrington & Richardson, and Colt. Complete FN rifles, according to what little I could find, had a specific pearl edge to the checkering on the grips, but it’s likely that not all of them did. Barrelled actions were also imported and built into custom hunting rifles.

This particular rifle appears to be either one that showed up as a barrelled action — possibly with a front sight. The barrel and receiver both bear the appropriate stampings for a matched pair that left the factory in Belgium. But the 22-inch barrel appears to have been cut down and re-crowned — as these were generally 24-inch barrels. It’s stamped with the year “1951” and bears matching 2837 serial numbers.

The stock has a handsome grain and good ergonomics. It lacks any checkering, but the fore-end has a nice belly and is capped with an ivory-colored plastic cap. The grip is capped with a similar plastic cap and its vertical orientation provides a wonderful balance of trigger reach and allows a crisp perpendicular squeeze. The raised comb is, in part, made from a separate block of wood. It’s pinned atop the buttstock and shaped to match, but the seams are still visible.

The floorplate isn’t hinged on this rifle. Rather, it’s detachable and retained by a spring-loaded button at the rear and retaining lip at the front. The fitting of the stock is very precise and, despite being bedded from the tang to the cap on the fore-end, very little bedding material is visible alongside the metal. Pillars around the action screws also help create an incompressible bridge between the bottom metal and action. The precise fit of metal and wood are things we’ll only see today on custom rifles and the highest-end production guns. Factory stocks — both wood and synthetic — are either CNC-milled or molded with baked-in tolerances rather than hand fit. The workmanship on my 1954 Model 70 is good, but not to the same level.

The barrel is a pretty standard sporter taper, finishing at .635 inches. I assume it has been cut down from 24 inches to 22. It’s a heavier barrel than you’ll see on classics like the M70 featherweight and contributes to the rifle’s roughly 9.5-pound overall weight.

The action and barrel share all matching and appropriate proof marks, and remain in good shape, with the bolt sliding like glass down the slick raceways. The bottom of the bolt handle is flattened and knurled for a better grip, and is generally smoother to operate than my previously mentioned M70. The safety switch is textured on both sides. It appears to be pitting because of its irregularity, but no other parts show similar signs of corrosion.

The receiver is topped with a one-piece Redfield mount and Bear Cub 2 ¾-power scope that uses a post-style reticle. In the early days of magnified optics, this gave a sight picture similar to that of a front post iron sight — to which most shooters would be accustomed. It was fitted with a Canjar trigger — a premium aftermarket upgrade, that breaks at 3 pounds, 7 ounces as set.

To test the rifle for accuracy, I fired four five-shot groups with several different types of contemporary .270 ammo. Where we would normally aggregate these five-shot groups into a single 20-shot group for each type of ammo, It was apparent that the system was limited, in part, by the optic, so I’m just sticking with the group size averages.

I believe the rifle is certainly capable of better accuracy than this but, considering the vintage scope, it proved itself perfectly capable of hitting a deer or elk cleanly out to 200 to 300 yards. Frankly, it’s not uncommon for us to test modern rifles with high-magnification scopes that average worse. A low-power optic itself doesn’t prevent a rifle from shooting accurately either, but this style of reticle requires the correct-sized target and a lot of concentration to maintain a consistent hold. The post covers about four inches of a 100-yard target. When the barrel warmed, mirage definitely affected sight picture too. I tried both a six-o’clock hold and a navy hold (dead center), and saw some vertical stringing with both methods.

So many rifles, including the Winchester, were derived from the Mauser action itself. The rifles even commanded a higher price than Model 70s at the time. While precision manufacturing, materials, and coatings have gotten better, a rifle like this one could still hold its own today.

In terms of construction, there are two different types of frames: pyramid (sometimes referred to as “cathedral”) and cross-trussed. Pyramid frames are generally less expensive and offer more headroom by design. The hollow pyramid structure keeps the top space up to the point of the covering. The biggest drawback with pyramid canopy tents is they are less sturdy and more subject to breaking or bending under heavy weight. This is because the poles that hold the point up top are single poles, angled, and not cross-trussed, which means they may bow or bend under pressure.

Your alternative is a canopy tent with cross trusses across the center and one pole that rises to create the same pointed top. This is, as you might have guessed, the cross-trussed model. These have a lower ceiling than pyramid options, but they make up for that with a much sturdier construction. The added cross trusses across the canopy make them more resistant to bending or breaking, though it also makes them more expensive and heavier to carry.

For the most part, pop-up canopies are designed to handle wind gusts up to about 15 mph with ease. By properly securing the canopy and the frame, you will minimize the danger of the frame shifting and moving, which can tear the canopy.

To date, I haven’t encountered a pop-up canopy that didn’t include some form of anchoring system. Whatever is provided, it’s essential that you use it. That said, if you choose to leave your canopy up for an extended period of time or all year long and a major storm is going to blow through, use your judgment as to whether you should take it down completely or reinforce the anchor system.
