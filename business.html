The trustee can usually provide the beneficiary with whatever in kind benefits they want, within reason, as long as the trust instrument or state law doesn't prohibit it. So if the trustee wants to purchase a car and let the beneficiary use it rent free, they can do that. (You might want that explicitly written into the trust instrument as something the trustee is allowed to do. Ask an estate planning lawyer for more information.)

As far as tax consequences, if the trustee distributes money to the beneficiary to buy a car, or gives the car outright to the beneficiary, then that's a distribution and it would carry out the trust's taxable income to the beneficiary.

If the trustee just let's the beneficiary use the car rent free, and the car remains in the trust, I don't think that is considered a distribution.

The trustee could pay for casualty (aka collision) insurance, major repairs, etc., and I think those would also not be distributions. But if the trustee pays usage related expenses like gas, liability insurance, etc., those might be considered distributions to the beneficiary.

Even if something is considered a distribution, note that distributions shift taxable income from the trust to the beneficiary, but don't usually create taxable income. Since trusts (usually) face higher tax rates than beneficiarys, distributions are (usually) a good thing when it comes to income tax. The downside is you expose the assets to beneficiary's creditors or estate tax liability.

So, yes, this can be done. For a moderately priced car that represents only a small % of the trust's total assets, it might be administratively simpler to just give the beneficiary the car, rather than trying to keep track of which expense falls into which category.

I suggest not trying to micromanage the future trustee's decision making. Tell the trustee what you want them to accomplish for the beneficiary, and and then give them discretion how best to do that. Maybe that means paying a stipend for the beneficiary's Uber rides rather than buying them a car, etc., etc.

Film & TV Creatives Want More Climate Storytelling, But Face Barriers, Survey Finds

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

EXCLUSIVE: With the United Nations Climate Change Conference COP 31 underway, the results are in from a first-of-its-kind international survey of film and TV industry professionals about climate storytelling.

Deadline has seen the survey results, which reveal 71% of people surveyed want to include climate-related storytelling in their work. However, 55% of respondents reported barriers to including climate themes in their work, and there was also a lower level of overall enthusiasm for climate storytelling among commissioners.

Deadline was first to report the survey’s launch amid talk that film commissions and the UN itself were showing an interest in it, with the hope its results would inform the conversation and lead to more support for creatives working on climate-relevant projects. According to Law.com, the next phase will center on paying creditors—chiefly landlords—and collecting outstanding receivables. (Photo from Shutterstock)

Partners at Weil, Gotshal & Manges have formally voted to dissolve the nearly century-old law firm.

Law.com reported Tuesday that 97% of the firm’s partners approved the move, which came about two weeks after the management committee recommended it Oct. 28. A three-person dissolution committee will oversee the firm’s dissolution, while Weil Gotshal executive chair Barry Wolf will act as a special consultant through the end of the year and longer if the wind-down runs into 2027. The firm will also utilize restructuring officers from management consulting agency AlixPartners and Davis Polk & Wardwell.

According to Law.com, the next phase will center on paying creditors—chiefly landlords—and collecting outstanding receivables.

Meanwhile, Law.com added that Weil Gotshal restructuring chair Matt Barr and several bankruptcy lawyers are in advanced talks to join Skadden, Arps, Slate, Meagher & Flom, which would remove one of Weil Gotshal’s last high-performing groups. Neither Barr nor Skadden responded to Law.com’s requests for comment.

Barr’s potential move would be the latest in a partner exodus that has helped bring down the once venerated Wall Street firm. According to Law.com, the firm saw nearly 50 partner exits in 2026, with sources estimating at least $500 million in lost business.

Tesla confirms rare new delivery of 50 Semi electric trucks in Chicago

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Tesla has confirmed a new batch of Tesla Semi deliveries, with its official Semi account saying today that “Chicago’s first 50 Semis” are on their way to the city.

It’s a rare data point. Tesla doesn’t disclose Semi deliveries, and it claims its new Nevada factory can build 50,000 of them a year.

The post, published on the @tesla_semi account on X at noon ET today, shows four Semis lined up at sunset under the caption “Hello Windy City.” Tesla didn’t name the customer.

Hello Windy CityChicago’s first 50 Semis en route for delivery pic.twitter.com/75kig8yWsb

But the only 50-truck Semi order tied to Chicago belongs to HMD Trucking. The family-owned carrier was founded in Chicago in 1998, runs more than 650 trucks (almost all diesel), and ordered 50 Semis right after Tesla’s production event in September.

HMD took its first truck on November 2 and says it’s the first Tesla Semi registered in Illinois. COO Michael Mahler said Tesla “has been transparent with us regarding the capabilities of Semi every step of the way.”

The company ran a Semi for more than 4,000 miles over the summer and averaged 1.74 kWh per mile, right in line with Tesla’s 1.7 kWh/mile claim. The first trucks will mostly handle local runs around Chicago, and HMD is installing its own chargers there since Tesla’s Megacharger network is still concentrated along the I-5 and I-10 corridors in the West.

Owner Henry Malukas told MotorTrend the deal is worth about $15 million. That’s roughly $300,000 per truck, in line with the ~$290,000 Long Range pricing we reported when the first truck came off the high-volume line and a long way from the $180,000 Tesla advertised in 2017.

Here’s the problem: it’s nearly impossible to tell how many Semis Tesla is actually building.

Tesla launched volume production at its Sparks, Nevada factory on September 24, after pushing the ramp out of 2025. It says the 1.8 million sq ft plant is built for 50,000 trucks a year. But it never gave a ramp schedule, and its quarterly production and delivery reports only count “passenger vehicles sold to customers.” The Semi isn’t in there.

So we’re left with proxies. Tesla says its Semi fleet has over 98% uptime, without saying how big that fleet is. Before this year, the largest Semi fleet was PepsiCo’s, at 86 trucks in 2024. California’s truck voucher program received 965 Semi applications between January 2025 and February 2026, which points to real demand but says nothing about output.

The order book is the other clue. Einride ordered 500 Semis in August, WattEV has 370 on order, IMC Logistics wants 50 for California, and the ZET SCALE alliance picked Tesla as main supplier for 2,500 trucks. And now HMD’s 50 are rolling into Chicago, one of the first Semi fleets that will have to deal with a Midwest winter. Wisconsin’s Paper Transport has also been piloting a Semi in the Chicago market since the summer.

None of that adds up to a production rate, but it does look like Tesla is slowly ramping up deliveries.

Tesla keeps the Semi numbers vague on purpose. It’s happy to talk about a 50,000-truck-a-year factory, and it posts hero shots when a batch ships, but it won’t give the one number that shows whether the factory is anywhere close to that.

The 50,000 figure is what makes the Semi look like a real business to investors instead of a niche program that took nine years to get going. Tesla has a long history of quoting capacity long before it reaches it, so we shouldn’t treat “built for 50,000” as anything more than a ceiling.

Our read: Tesla is still far from that 50,000 rate. Fifty trucks for one customer is 0.1% of a year at full capacity, and if Tesla were shipping in large volumes, a single 50-unit batch wouldn’t get its own post. But the ramp has clearly started, the order book is real, and it’s plausible Tesla gets into the thousands by the end of the year.

Good for HMD, too. A 650-truck diesel fleet in a cold market is exactly the kind of customer the Semi has to win. Malukas admitted “we don’t have real numbers yet” on savings, and HMD’s own blog is fairly sober about cost and winter range. Local routes, depot charging and a Chicago winter is the right way to find out. It all sounds plausible for the Tesla Semi.

Democrats and Republicans both understand Americans are broadly worried about the cost of living, and a former Trump administration official says it's time for both parties to take action.

In a bitterly divided Washington, DC, it remains to be seen whether lawmakers will heed that call when the 120th Congress convenes in January, with Democrats back in control of both the House and Senate.

After campaigning hard on affordability concerns, Democratic leaders say they'll use their new majorities to focus on the cost of living. Republican leaders admit they didn't heed voter concerns about the economy. Meanwhile, President Donald Trump has threatened to use his veto power freely.

The summer's 21st Century Road to Housing Act, with its dozens of provisions aimed at cutting red tape, was a rare example of bipartisan consensus. What follows that landmark legislation remains an open question.

Republicans want more deregulation while Democrats aim for more support for housing. And each side must now contend with an upswell of hard-liners pushing policies that could radically change the market.

Many of the prominent names in both parties who built the housing bill are returning, including Rep. French Hill (R-AR), Rep. Maxine Waters (D-CA), Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D-MA), and Sen. Tim Scott (R-SC). They're likely to return to the House and Senate committees on housing and banking.

"Now is a wake-up call," Trump's former Federal Housing Commissioner Frank Cassidy tells Realtor.com®. "Senate, House, White House. Everyone needs to get serious about tackling and addressing housing affordability. It can't be ignored anymore."

Congress had some bipartisan momentum to provide tax incentives that encourage the buying and selling of homes.

Colin Allen, executive director of the American Property Owners Alliance and a former housing issues adviser to Sen. Lindsey Graham (R-SC), said that could mean some ideas pitched in this Congress could come back as part of larger bills.

"They have good working relationships and have already done some bipartisan work," Allen said.

Take, for instance, the Neighborhood Homes Investment Act, which would create a new tax credit to buy and renovate homes in economically distressed areas. The bill drew 70 sponsors from both parties.

Real estate groups put in significant groundwork this year to update the capital gains tax scheme for the first time in more than 30 years. The More Homes on the Market Act came close to 200 sponsors in Congress. It would double the exclusion for many filers. It's been floating around Congress for several years and has been a target of the National Association of Realtors® and other groups.

Trump expressed some support for changing the capital gains tax scheme, though a specific plan hasn't yet materialized from the White House.

"We've done a lot of legwork to get folks behind it," Allen said. "If we're looking at a larger bipartisan bill, you can see a scenario where a few of those ideas get together. The debt limit is coming up, and there are a few other opportunities to package together."

Hill and Republicans have lobbied for more changes to the financing system. They were able to get several banking reform provisions into the Road to Housing bill. They've also floated new financial reforms to change and rescind banking rules codified in the Dodd-Frank Act and the Basel III accords after the subprime mortgage crisis. Republicans say they've chased smaller banks out of the housing market.

This summer, Hill called Dodd-Frank a "massive overreaction."

"For our smaller banks, the new regulations were a poor fit, burying them in compliance work and bureaucratic red tape," Hill said. "Instead of helping customers, their attention was diverted to filling out forms and satisfying examiners."

Mortgage reform is also a lever Congress can pull. A bipartisan group of six members of Congress pitched the First-Time Homebuyer Affordability Act this summer. It would expand access to low-interest mortgages for first-time homebuyers.

"By expanding access to financing, we can help more hardworking families achieve homeownership, strengthen our communities, and build long-term financial security," said Rep. Darin LaHood (R-IL) said.

Congress and the White Hose also found common ground on manufactured housing reform. All sides recognize they need to keep eyeing supply-side reforms, says Cassidy.

"My un-professional assessment is we'll have a certain amount of gridlock over a number of years where Democrats may come up with some suggestions," Marquez says. "But the answer to affordable housing is not just one silver bullet. It can have many elements."

School leaders are borrowing pages from marketing and advertising executives to develop tactics that will rightsize their enrollment.

The decision to close 129-year-old Perry Elementary School in Belvidere, Ill., earlier this year devastated many community members who didn't want to see their historic building shuttered.

While scores of schools across the country are closing as enrollment declines empty their classrooms, that was not the case with Perry. The 174-student school, in the end, just got old. Its aging structure needed a prohibitive $14 million to $16 million in repairs, according to Cassandra Schug, superintendent of Belvidere Community Unit School District 100.

The district's enrollment has in fact remained relatively stable, with only a modest decline in recent years, Schug says. Enrollment stands at 7,348 students in 2026, down from 7,470 in 2021.

Still, the decision to close Perry and the reaction it engendered from the community offer constructive guidance for administrators everywhere who are staring down school closures brought on by shrinking student populations.

When Perry's closure became inevitable after a 6-1 school board vote in April, the community rallied to usher its beloved school out in style, Schug says. Longtime teacher Susan Thomas spearheaded a committee to honor the school’s legacy with a citywide celebration ultimately attended by hundreds of people in May.

Thomas, who taught 5th grade at Perry for 19 years, says the neighborhood school was “so much more” than just a building. The closure decision, she says, was nothing short of traumatic.

Perry “has been a cornerstone for Belvidere. It needed to be celebrated. It needed us to be able to show our appreciation for what it had meant for so many years, and I just wanted it to be done right,” Thomas says.

The celebration allowed people across the community to visit and view old yearbooks, photos, letters, newspaper articles and student projects — all organized by decade and displayed on tables and walls throughout the gym.

“So you could walk through and just see all of that Perry history and recognize how education had changed over the years, how the building had changed over the years, and again, just really spend time sort of saying goodbye,” Schug says.

The celebration “helped us all to process what had been a really difficult time," honor the school's legacy, "create that closure, and give us a chance to celebrate those positive memories that Perry had brought to the community.”

Beginning this school year, Perry's staff members and students dispersed to other schools in the district, with students transferring through a lottery system.

Though Belvidere enrollment is expected to stay steady, district officials are carefully watching enrollment numbers and projections, especially as more public schools nationwide experience declines, Schug says.

And, she adds, she hopes she never has to close another school in her career again.

As some school communities grieve shuttered schools, districts are proactively working to boost enrollments and stave off closures. Here are three examples of how school leaders are striving to stay competitive.

The days of traditional public school systems having a monopoly on education and just worrying about teaching students are gone, says Brian Stephens, CEO of Caissa K12, a Memphis-based strategic communications firm.

In 2009, Stephens co-founded a public strategy firm that specialized in complex communication crisis management. But in 2017, as he saw more states adopt private school choice programs, the firm converted to focusing exclusively on student recruitment and retention for traditional public schools.

Currently, Caissa K12 — named for the mythical goddess of chess and strategy — works with nearly 100 districts in 26 states. The company says it has helped recruit about 55,000 students for its clients through research, communications, events and knocking on doors.

The firm is one of a growing number of players in this space.

Taking a page from the retail industry, one of Caissa K12's services for its district clients is performing "secret shopper" outreach. Caissa K12 employees or contractors act as families interested in enrolling their child and visit or connect with public and private schools to look for enrollment or communication barriers or other customer service problems.

The company works with school districts including Florida's Broward County Public Schools through performance contracts, meaning Caissa K12 only gets paid if the company meets an agreed-upon student recruitment goal.

When it comes to recruiting and retaining students, Stephens says, the greatest mistakes public schools make are not advocating for themselves and not being willing to improve flaws like hard-to-navigate websites.

Those steps may sound easy, but they are difficult for school systems with a lot on their plates, he says.

If the government and the people want public schools to "act like a business instead of a public good, then we have to act more like a business," Stephens says. "That means we need to advocate for ourselves."

Districts that are innovative and want to build trust with families "absolutely have to reach out and let people know that they exist and let people know how awesome they are," he says.

Stephens points to creative approaches public schools can take to help attract and keep students.

One district client, he notes, installed a washer and dryer at a school to support school attendance by making sure children could have clean clothes. Other schools have provided feminine hygiene products, which also boosted school attendance, Stephens says.

Eliminating outdated and abrasive policies can make a difference too. For instance, schools need to remove attendance policies that punish students for having overdue library books or unpaid fees for band uniforms, he says.

“Stepping up those things has really helped. You have to identify what the issue is that's stopping a child from attending.”

A new federal school choice program launching next year has many public school communities wondering how their students might benefit from billions of dollars in expected donations for K-12 educational expenses.

Nevada's Clark County School District, for example, is considering a collaboration with its Public Education Foundation, which would serve as a scholarship-granting organization to direct donations to benefit the Las Vegas-area district's students. Those extra scholarship funds could be targeted toward career-connected learning opportunities, technology, tutoring and more, according to an Aug. 13 presentation to the district's board of trustees.

Elsewhere, public school advocates from Iowa to Denver are also making plans to direct some of these taxpayer contributions to public schools.

And at least one new national scholarship-granting organization has formed to funnel donations to services supporting public school students. The Future School Fund, founded by Deborah Gist, former Rhode Island commissioner of education and state education superintendent in the District of Columbia, intends to help schools identify programs where scholarship funds can be used.

The federal Education Freedom Tax Credit program, which launches on Jan. 1, will guide taxpayer donations to students attending either public or private schools, who can then use the scholarships to pay for allowable educational expenses.

This first-ever nationwide school choice program is projected to raise nearly $26 billion annually for about 2.2 million scholarships by 2030, according to the administering agencies, the U.S. Department of the Treasury and the IRS.

This could overshadow the nation's current largest education program, Title I, which has an $18.4 billion budget for fiscal year 2026 budget to aid children at low-income schools. Title I serves about 26 million students in 90% of the country's school districts, according to the Bipartisan Policy Center.

Only students living in states that opt into the Education Freedom Tax Credit program would be eligible to receive scholarships. As of Oct. 22, 30 states have indicated that they will opt into the program, according to the IRS.

Participating states will approve scholarship-granting organizations to operate in their states, which will then direct taxpayer donations to families to use for education expenses.

ACE Scholarships is one such SGO. The Denver-based nonprofit is working with public and private school systems in several states interested in soliciting donations for the federal tax credit scholarships that can then be used at their schools, according to CEO Norton Rainey.

ACE Scholarships already has several charter school clients and is in talks with public school districts to manage and distribute their donation accounts, he says. The organization, he adds, will focus on directing scholarships to students with the greatest financial needs.

But while some are urging public school systems and supporters to embrace the federal scholarship program, critics have many concerns. They worry about a lack of accountability, a financial drain on public schools and potential inequities.

"Simply permitting some public school students to benefit does not ensure that resources are equitably distributed or that schools and students most in need are served by the program," according to a coalition of 16 civil rights advocacy organizations in a Sept. 28 letter to state governors.

The rapid increase in school choice options is leading more public school leaders to consider creative ways to invest in marketing to keep their enrollment numbers up, says Mellissa Braham, associate director of the National School Public Relations Association.

But that doesn’t mean they necessarily have the budgets to do so, Braham says.

“Because districts nationwide are facing really tough times right now, a lot of them are having to reduce staff, and in that light, it's painful to put additional focus on marketing, even though ultimately it does help your budget,” she says.

One way districts are approaching enrollment marketing in a small yet impactful way is by having dedicated in-house staff solicit student and teacher testimonials and create videos to post on their communication channels.

Other low-cost marketing strategies include paying to boost social media posts or have their school or district website appear on search engines more frequently instead of relying on organic search, Braham says. Some districts are paying for targeted marketing emails, too.

She points to successful elements she's seen in school marketing campaigns, including firsthand testimonials from students and families, a focus on what makes a school and district unique, and targeted emails that feel specific to the person receiving it.

But districts in highly competitive areas may need to invest in more costly marketing efforts, she says. That could include buying billboard, television or streaming ads.

“If you're in a really competitive environment with a lot of different schools to choose from, it might be an investment you have to make with the ultimate return of enrollment, which brings in funds, which allows you to support staff programs and services,” Braham says.

In districts where school choice options are fairly new, Braham says, sometimes the biggest challenge for school leaders is to understand that families are now customers and that neighborhood public schools are not the only option.

Shifting to a marketing mindset is different from sending out regular school communications, which aim to share information and build relationships, she says. And that change is “new for a lot of public schools.”

The 10 Affordable Cities Where Homeowners Are the Least Locked In by Housing Costs

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Many homeowners who began the year hoping to move may now be locked in by soaring mortgage rates that threaten to nearly double their monthly payments—but a handful of budget-friendly markets still offer relief.

These rare pockets of affordability are clustered primarily across the Midwest and South, including Pittsburgh; Baltimore; Buffalo, NY; New Orleans; Detroit; Birmingham, AL; San Antonio, TX; Akron, OH; McAllen, TX; and Philadelphia.

Nationally, the typical U.S. mortgage holder pays roughly $1,300 in principal and interest per month. If they were to purchase a home today, that payment would surge by more than 85%, or $1,100, according to a new report on America's "locked-in" homeowners from Realtor.com®.

The market's current predicament can be traced to the COVID-19 pandemic years, when historically low mortgage rates whipped up a buying and refinancing frenzy. As of July 2026, more than a quarter (27%) of all fixed-rate mortgages on record for single-family homes and condominiums were originated in 2020 and 2021 alone.

Once mortgage rates began climbing again in 2022, homebuying and refinancing both hit a wall. Over the subsequent four years, only about 1 in 4 mortgages on record was originated, highlighting the sharp pullback in activity as a result of higher borrowing costs.

"For those who purchased before mortgage rates shot sharply higher, the sentiment of 'I couldn’t afford to buy my home today' continues to ring true in most cases, contributing to historically low mobility and some of the leanest for-sale inventory on record," notes Realtor.com senior economist Hannah Jones.

In other words, homeowners who were fortunate enough to secure a sub-4% mortgage rate at the height of the pandemic are reluctant to list. Trading up or downsizing today means taking out a mortgage with a steeply higher rate, gobbling up a much bigger share of their income.

Just how much bigger? Purchasing a median-priced home at the Oct. 22 rate of 7.32%, assuming a 14.3% down payment, would translate into a monthly principal and interest payment of nearly $2,500, almost double the $1,300 typical owners currently pay.

However, in parts of the U.S. where home prices remain accessible, the "lock-in" effect is felt less acutely, keeping inventory—and people—moving and creating a self-reinforcing cycle.

"The affordability of these markets enables mobility, which means the typical outstanding mortgage rate is higher, which helps reduce the payment jump associated with moving, which reinforces affordability," explains Jones.

In the South and Midwest, approximately three-quarters of mortgages carry a rate below 6%, compared to 78% nationally.

Additionally, nearly a quarter of all outstanding mortgages in these two regions were originated within the past four years at 6% or higher, compared with 21.6% in the Northeast and 17.7% in the West.

"That mix means fewer homeowners in these regions are clinging to 3%–4% rates, which lessens the lock-in effect," says Jones.

Across the 100 largest metros, Pittsburgh stands out as the nation's least "locked in" housing market, where a seller purchasing a new home in town would see a 43% increase in their monthly mortgage payment—a modest penalty compared to the 165% in San Jose, CA.

Baltimore is a close second, with a 45% payment gap, followed by Buffalo, at 46.1%.

"A 3% mortgage is a great reason to stay in a house, but it's not always a great reason to stay in a house that no longer fits your life," Colleen Collier, associate broker at Re/Max Plus in Buffalo, tells Realtor.com.

The latest housing data reflects the market's mobility and affordability. In October, Buffalo posted the largest year-over-year increase in active listings (+34.4%) among the 50 largest metros, paired with the largest growth in new listings (+13.1%).

At the same time, the median listing price in the metro registered at $259,999, down 2.8% from a year ago and sitting more than $155,000 below the national median.

Collier notes that while more local homeowners are willing to make a move, it does not necessarily mean they are comfortable giving up their low rates. They are being compelled by life changes.

"They're retiring, downsizing, or relocating to be closer to family," says the broker. "Many are looking to reduce maintenance and their overall living expenses. Others are relocating to a warmer climate. ... They're taking the equity they've built and putting it toward their next purchase."

Collier recently represented clients who sold their home of 30 years to downsize into an apartment. They were able to get a final sale price well above asking, which was enough to cover at least 10 years of apartment living, and fund future travel plans and a new car.

"What made this experience so rewarding was that the homeowner had more equity than they realized," says Collier. "After more than three decades in their home, selling wasn't simply about moving into a smaller space. It was about creating financial freedom and enjoying the next chapter of life."

In Akron, a typical homeowner currently paying just $868 per month should expect to see that figure rise nearly 62% under present-day market conditions—well above Buffalo’s level but still a far cry from the most locked-in coastal markets.

"The financial difference between staying in a home with a low mortgage rate and purchasing another home may be less dramatic here than in higher-priced markets," Mike Valerino, CEO of Akron Cleveland Association of Realtors, tells Realtor.com. "However, that doesn't mean the mortgage rate lock-in effect has disappeared."

According to Valerino, in Summit County, where Akron is located, new listings and closed sales were down in September while the average sales price increased, suggesting that despite the market's relative affordability, homeowners are still weighing the financial implications of moving.

"Life circumstances remain important drivers of housing decisions," he says, echoing Collier's argument. "Growing families need more space, empty nesters may want to downsize, and job changes, retirement, and family considerations can create reasons to move that outweigh the financial benefits of staying put."

At the same time, Valerino says that Akron's relative affordability does not insulate buyers and sellers from the challenges affecting housing markets nationally.

"The mortgage lock-in effect may be less severe here, but the cost of the next home still plays a major role in whether someone decides to move," he says.

In roughly a third of the 100 largest U.S. metros, a move to a median-priced home would more than double the monthly payment. Ballooning borrowing costs disproportionately penalize expensive coastal and fast-growing markets, leaving local homeowners feeling virtually trapped by their lower mortgages.

According to the latest data, the most "locked-in" markets in the U.S. include Portland, ME, San Jose, and Los Angeles, where anticipated monthly mortgage payments surged by more than 140%, grinding mobility to a halt. That friction has severely squeezed inventory and put upward pressure on home prices, sidelining first-time and budget buyers.

"Because homes in these markets are already expensive, buyers typically rely on larger mortgage balances, which amplifies the impact of rising rates," says Jones.

In Portland, for instance, a current owner holding on to a 4% mortgage rate makes a median payment of $1,270 per month. If they were to purchase a home comparable to theirs with a 26.2% down payment and took out a 7.32% mortgage, their payment would jump a staggering 167% to $3,392.

Mortgage rates would have to drop a long way from their current 7.18% level to close the gap between what current owners pay and what a buyer would pay today.

Rates would need to reach 6% before half of U.S. households could afford the median-priced home, and even then a move would still mean a payment about 62% higher than what the typical mortgage holder spends now.

Crucially, however, Jones stresses that falling mortgage rates alone are not enough. Because home values have appreciated significantly over the past few years, buying a median-priced home with a 4% mortgage would still cost about 29% more per month than what existing homeowners pay on their original COVID-19-era loans.

Valerino agrees, maintaining that there is no one "magic number" that will unlock the market.

"Rates in the 5% range could make moving more attractive to some homeowners, but affordability involves more than the interest rate," he says. "Home prices, property taxes, insurance, and accumulated equity all factor into that decision."

The market is poised to loosen gradually rather than all at once as more households buy in to higher rates, softening price growth narrows the gap, and rising incomes boost buying power.

"The lower-priced Midwest and South markets, where the gap is smallest and more loans carry higher rates, are likely to see the most activity first," predicts Jones. "Large coastal markets will need both lower rates and lower prices or significantly higher incomes before sellers feel free to move."

A new tactic in the war over out-of-network bills: Health care providers are suing employers

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Bob covers the money in health care, focusing on health insurance and hospitals. His stories delve into Medicare Advantage, opaque prescription drug benefits, and how much executives actually make. He is also the author of the Health Care Inc. newsletter. You can reach Bob on Signal at bobjherman.09.

This past August, Joe Hancock, a Kentucky plumbing and heating contractor, opened a just-delivered box packed with hundreds of sheets of paper. Leafing through them, he read seven words no small business owner wants to see: “A lawsuit has been filed against you.”

The complaint, from air ambulance firm PHI Health, claimed Hancock was on the hook for almost $27,000 in medical helicopter bills. They were tied to one of his now-retired employees, who needed an emergency flight after a heart attack.

Hancock, who owns Whitehead-Hancock Plumbing, Heating & Cooling, a family-run business in Frankfort, Ky., that has been around for more than a century, didn’t understand why he was being roped into a lawsuit that featured 31 other companies — for medical charges he was never aware of.

“I was terrified,” Hancock said. “I was like, ‘What in the hell is going on here?’”

He’s not alone. The PHI lawsuit highlights an evolving tactic by health care providers in a raging battle over out-of-network bills. Previously, providers targeted health insurance companies to pay outstanding amounts that were awarded through federal mediation. Now, they increasingly are dragging the insurers’ clients — employers — into court, claiming they are responsible for bills that insurers haven’t fully paid or have delayed paying. The practice is introducing a new shock that could further torch the affordability of workers’ health coverage.

“These big suits naming dozens of employers at a time is really an escalation,” said Garrett Hohimer, a vice president at the Business Group on Health. “And it will cause employers to incur expenses that [come directly] out of their health care plans.”

A 2022 law called the No Surprises Act shielded patients from the financial trauma of many types of unexpected medical bills, but it has also given rise to a vast industry that’s driving up costs across the health care system. The law uses an arbitration process to determine how much health care providers should get paid for out-of-network emergency care, like helicopter rides, for people who have some type of commercial health coverage. STAT has reported that providers are winning most of these arbitration awards, which often are far higher than the amounts in-network providers receive for the same services.

Already, high payments tied to this process have added approximately $20 billion in extra costs to the health care system, Georgetown University researchers have estimated. A large portion of those costs inevitably come out of the bank accounts of America’s employers and consequently lead to higher premiums, emphasizing how even well-intentioned health reforms can add to the financial pressure that’s crushing workers and businesses.

PHI Health, a national chain, is arguably the most aggressive company suing employers, which it claims are not “passive” bystanders in insurers’ refusal to fully pay awards. Other regional physician practices have sued some employers as well. But PHI and its army of attorneys have filed lawsuits against hundreds of companies, demanding they collectively pay tens of millions of dollars in unpaid out-of-network claims that it won in the federal arbitration process.

PHI isn’t just suing small, local firms like Hancock’s. It’s also taking legal action against some of the largest corporations — Amazon, Walmart, Eli Lilly, United Airlines, General Motors, PepsiCo, State Farm, and Toyota, to name a few — as well as unions, counties, cities, states, public school districts, and universities.

PHI has sued other health care organizations, including hospitals, nursing homes, and medical billing firms. It also has gone after nonprofits, such as the Catholic Diocese of Cleveland. In each instance, a worker or family member at these companies took an emergency medical helicopter, often at a cost of tens of thousands of dollars per trip.

Unpacking the business — and secretive inner workings — of the U.S. health care industry

In some cases, PHI wasn’t sure what company it should be suing, but sued anyway. Several lawsuits named specific health insurers and employers as defendants, and then added “ABC CORP” or “XYZ CORP” as a placeholder. If the insurer or employer “is not in fact the plan sponsor … that covers the patient transported by PHI, then the identity of the plan sponsor is unknown to PHI, and this action is instead brought against ABC CORP,” one lawsuit reads.

Katie Keith and Ellie DeGarmo are attorneys at Georgetown’s Center for Health Policy and the Law who have tracked No Surprises Act lawsuits, including those filed by PHI. They said the scores of cases reinforce the bloat in the health care system: above-market prices that providers are winning for not participating in an insurance company’s network, attempts from insurers to not pay at all or profit off lowball offers, and a booming revenue stream for arbitrators.

But trying to wring money out of employers — and, ultimately, from their workers — is a stark divergence from the public statements of physician groups and air ambulance companies, which have painted health insurers as the bad guys for not following the rules.

“It kind of undercuts the narrative that this is provider versus insurance company,” Keith said. “In this case, this is about provider versus everyone who pays for health care and is now stuck with the bill for [arbitration] awards that have gone totally off the rails.”

STAT reached out to more than two dozen employers that were listed in PHI’s lawsuits. Most did not respond to inquiries. Of those that did, most declined to comment.

“Because this matter involves pending litigation … we will not be providing any interviews,” said Clint Mitchell, the division superintendent of Spotsylvania County Public Schools in Fredericksburg, Va. PHI Health is suing Spotsylvania County’s school system for almost $28,000.

Jay Litten, the town manager of Bridgewater, Va., told STAT the town’s health insurer, Anthem Blue Cross Blue Shield, retained counsel and was handling the case. PHI sued Bridgewater in October for more than $37,000 in unpaid awards.

PHI has boasted about its success in the law’s arbitration process. In 2024, it told prospective investors that it was “able to maintain our profitability” by winning more than three out of every four arbitration claims, and it expected “this trend of favorable” outcomes from the process would continue.

The company has some private equity backing, according to financial filings from when the company considered going public in 2023. Its largest shareholder as of 2023 was Q Investments, a family office that has a private equity firm.

PHI declined to make anyone available for an interview. In a statement, CEO Scott McCarty defended the company’s lawsuits, saying the employers PHI has sued “have a duty to their employees and a responsibility to health care providers in their communities to abide by the law and pay for services delivered.”

McCarty said health insurers are asking their employer clients to choose between paying the unpaid arbitration amounts or authorizing the insurer to continue refusing payment.

“Once an employer is on notice of nonpayment and affirmatively endorses that practice, it is no longer a passive bystander,” McCarty said in the statement. “It has made a conscious decision to allow its insurance company not to pay binding federal awards in violation of federal law.”

In PHI’s lawsuits, the company alleges insurers, and now employers, are using an “unlawful scheme to ‘low pay, late pay, or no pay’ providers, including PHI.” All the cases are still pending.

The company also claims insurers float the money from unpaid awards, which are binding decisions, on the stock market as a way to earn extra interest or investment income.

James Gelfand, CEO of the ERISA Industry Committee, a lobbying group that represents large employers, said companies don’t condone insurers delaying payments as a way to make extra money. But he said under the federal law known as ERISA, employers are legally obligated to make sure they spend their health plan assets wisely on behalf of workers. If employers believe the awards are tied to ineligible claims — or are egregiously high compared with what they normally would pay — those payments could violate that obligation.

As a result, the employers’ health insurers “are operating with our blessing when we say, ‘Hey, if the [arbitration] award is inappropriate and wrong, do not pay it,’” he said. “How can we claim to be responsible stewards of people’s money if we’re just like, ‘Oh, here you go!’ That would be crazy.”

Jenny Goins, who is CEO of the National Alliance of Healthcare Purchaser Coalitions and used to oversee health benefits for Kentucky state employees, said “slow-walking the payments” to providers is one of the only ways employers can push back if they believe the awards are based on ineligible or incomplete claims. Medical claims are not eligible for arbitration if, for example, the employer or its health plan doesn’t cover a certain service or if there is certain information or data missing.

PHI has frequently named Anthem Blue Cross Blue Shield, owned by Elevance Health, as a defendant in the lawsuits against employers. Elevance did not make anyone available for an interview. The company said in a statement that PHI is charging prices for air ambulance services that are 700% of what Medicare pays and mileage rates that are 1,500% more than Medicare rates.

“We have already paid PHI substantial amounts for services provided to our members,” Elevance spokesperson Janey Kiryluik said in a statement. “We dispute whether additional amounts are legally owed for claims that were ineligible” for the arbitration process.

PHI said only arbitrators can determine the eligibility of claims. It added that the rates Medicare pays don’t cover the full cost of emergency air care, nor can those prices be considered during the arbitration process.

The bigger issue revealed by the lawsuits, Goins said, is how “broken” the law has become, even though she believed it was well-intended. The issue has now attracted interest in Congress, where lawmakers are proposing various plans to overhaul the arbitration process.

“Greedy people see an opportunity to make more money,” Goins said. “I also see there’s a true opportunity to shine a light on this.”

In September, PHI dropped Hancock’s company as a defendant, one month after its attorneys served him with court papers. Hancock said his heating, cooling, and plumbing business of 30 employees is not self-insured — meaning he buys his health insurance from Anthem, which takes on all the risk of his employees’ medical care.

PHI’s McCarty blamed Anthem in a statement, saying it “does not always receive clear or consistent information from insurers identifying whether a plan is self-funded or fully insured. In this case, after the lawsuit was filed, Anthem’s counsel confirmed that Whitehead-Hancock was fully insured, and PHI promptly dismissed it, without settlement.”

Other businesses are getting released from PHI’s lawsuits, too. It’s unclear if they were wrongfully lumped in, like Hancock’s company, or if they are paying PHI’s demands to clear a costly legal headache.

PHI’s legal team “threw a handful of darts, and they’re going to try to rattle as many cages as they can,” Hancock said. “It’s not right. It shouldn’t be legal.”

Unplugged Performance, the biggest name in Tesla aftermarket tuning, wants to keep the Cybertruck alive. The company publicly offered on Tuesday to take the Cybertruck line off Tesla’s hands, funded by a consortium of investors.

It comes just four days after Elon Musk said Tesla would wind down Cybertruck production at Giga Texas around mid-2027, with the truck selling at roughly 12% of the 250,000 units a year Tesla planned for.

UP posted the pitch on X as “an open offer to Tesla.” The company says it fully supports putting Cybercab first and would run Cybertruck production “at no cost to Tesla’s floor space, headcount, or focus.”

In exchange, Tesla would collect a license fee plus FSD and Robotaxi network revenue on every vehicle built. UP even offered to let Cybercab get first call on battery cells. “We’ll wait our turn,” the company wrote.

UP founder Ben Schaffer made it clear that he supports Tesla’s all-in move to autonomy, but he wants to keep the Cybertruck program alive.

And it isn’t stopping at the pickup. UP says it would build the Cybertruck, a three-row “CyberSUV,” and an unmanned “CyberCargo” hauler. Two vehicles Tesla has sometimes referenced, but they were never unveiled.

The post passed 240,000 views within hours, and the replies are mostly Cybertruck owners and Tesla shareholders begging Musk to say yes. Neither Musk nor Tesla had publicly responded at the time of writing.

UP has a lot riding on this. It sells an entire Cybertruck product line, the UP Invincible lift and armor kits, and it has scored several contracts to turn Cybertrucks into police vehicles for law enforcement agencies. CEO Ben Schaffer once described the company’s purpose as doing “the things that Tesla maybe wants to do, but doesn’t have the bandwidth to do.” This is that idea taken to its extreme.

Musk framed the Cybertruck’s end as a space problem. He said Tesla needs to “make room for increased Cybercab production.”

If the Cybertruck program was successful, Tesla wouldn’t be killing it. Tesla also produces Model Ys at Giga Texas, and it is not shutting that down to make room for Cybercab.

As we reported when Tesla announced the wind-down, that explanation doesn’t hold up. Giga Texas has more than 10 million square feet of floor space, the Cybertruck line runs at about a quarter of its 125,000-unit installed capacity, and Texas had only a few hundred Cybercabs registered as of late October.

The real problem is buyers. Tesla delivered 8,295 “Other Models” in Q3, and we estimated 7,000 to 8,000 of those were Cybertrucks, a pace of about 30,000 trucks a year. That was a good quarter by Cybertruck standards.

Even that number flatters it. S&P Global Mobility data showed SpaceX bought 1,279 Cybertrucks in Q4 2025, about 18% of US registrations that quarter. Tesla also tried a cheaper RWD trim, killed it, launched a $60,000 AWD version, then raised prices by $5,000 in August. None of it fixed demand.

I’ll give UP credit for this: the offer calls Musk’s bluff. If the Cybertruck is really dying because Cybercab needs the room, here’s someone volunteering to take the line off Tesla’s plate. Problem solved.

Of course, it’s more complicated than that.

There are many ways this could fail or never happen in the first place, but there’s also a slight possibility that makes this possible.

First off, the backlash is real. If you look on social media and forums, yes, most of the discussion is about how Cybertruck is a failure, and Elon is lying about why Tesla is killing it, but there’s also a serious group of people, especially Cybertruck owners, who are genuinely upset that Tesla is killing the program because they love their trucks.

Most people have never driven a Cybertruck, but the steer-by-wire makes it a very addictive vehicle to drive, and even though the price is high, the value proposition for the specs is not bad compared to the very limited options for electric pickup trucks.

In other words, I wouldn’t necessarily bet against this effort, especially if they add an SUV variant and show the program some love with some tweaks here and there.

I could see a group of wealthy Cybertruck fans pooling money under Unplugged to make this happen, but getting Tesla to agree could be hard. It’s not unheard of for an automaker to sell a vehicle program to a dealer, like the Lotus Seven and Studebaker Avanti, but it would be a first at this scale.

You could make sense of it by looking at UP to Tesla as AMG to Mercedes. It’s not too big a stretch, considering UP isn’t just your average Tesla tuner. It even worked with the automaker on its own version of the Model S Plaid.

If UP were to become for Tesla what AMG has become for Mercedes, it could make some sense for the company to run a separate vehicle program that started under Tesla, but even as I write this, I realize that there’s very little chance this will happen.

But I wish them the best of luck, and I would be happy to see the Cybertruck live on.

This '60s Japanese Thriller Is One Of The Best Movies Streaming On HBO Max

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Ben Affleck's new kidnapping thriller "Animals" made a splash on Netflix this year — at least in terms of viewership. Critics dismissed the picture, and justifiably so, given how shaky the "Animals" script is. Yours truly clocked that "Animals" is basically an unauthorized remake of Akira Kurosawa's "High and Low," and I was not the only one.

In both movies, a rich man who has just invested all his money in a venture (a business takeover in "High and Low," a mayoral campaign in "Animals") receives a phone call that his son has been kidnapped. Except, the ransomers mistakenly took one of his much poorer employee's sons — so, does he still bankrupt himself to save a kid that isn't his?

I would highly suggest turning off Netflix and flipping over to HBO Max (where "High and Low" is currently streaming) to watch Akira Kurosawa show you how it's done. Several of the filmmaker's classics are currently streaming on there, including "Seven Samurai," "Rashomon," "Throne of Blood," and "Yojimbo." Like with "High and Low," all of those movies also star Toshiro Mifune, Kurosawa's go-to leading man at his peak. But "High and Low" is my personal favorite of the bunch and Kurosawa's magnum opus.

Kurosawa is distinguished for his samurai movies or Jidaigeki (Japanese historical dramas). He often adapted William Shakespeare's plays, too, moving their stories from Europe to to the east: "Throne of Blood" is literally just "Macbeth" in feudal Japan, and it's one of the best "Macbeth" movies to boot.

"High and Low" stands out in that collection for its contemporary setting and source material. (It's adapted from a procedural novel, "King's Ransom," by American author Ed McBain.) While he may not have had the Bard to draw on this time, Kurosawa still made some pure cinematic brilliance.

If I were ever tasked with assembling a list of "old" and/or international movies to convince people that black and white or non-English-language movies can be great, "High and Low" would be on there, 100%. That's not just because it's great (it is), but it's great popular entertainment: a taut suspense thriller and investigative procedural. It's not at all "pretentious," as untrained viewers might call it.

"Someone wants a ransom for the wrong kid, but only you can pay it" is also a great moral dilemma for a thriller and one that slots neatly into anywhere in the world with class disparity. That's why "High and Low" has been remade in many different settings. We know what a hero would do in this situation, but we're not sure if Kingo Gondo (Toshiro Mifune) is a hero from the beginning.

Now, the movie wisely stacks the deck in Gondo's favor so he doesn't come off as an aloof rich guy. He wasn't born rich, he worked his way up, so he knows what losing everything is like, and that makes the sacrifice extra painful. And while he works in shoe manufacturing, he's got a creative spirit. He's planning a leveraged buyout because he wants to make high-quality shoes his way instead of saving money on a cheaper product.

Would you sacrifice all your savings and life's work to save an innocent child? You probably just answered "yes," but it's still an abstract question for you. "High and Low" portrays how someone would act when the choice and its consequences become real. The most impressive balancing act in the movie is that it both does and doesn't feel like a foregone conclusion when he agrees to pay the ransom.

"High and Low" doesn't end when the ransom gets paid. The kidnapper escapes, setting off the second half, where Gondo is largely absent and the police characters take over to try and find the kidnapper. "High and Low" is as bifurcated as its title, with the two halves divided by setting: Gondo's hilltop mansion in the first half, the town below in the second, with the train sequence where Gondo delivers the ransom as a fitting bridge. (It's worth noting that a more literal translation of the original Japanese title, "Tengoku to Jigoku," is "Heaven and Hell.")

During that first half, Kurosawa uses Gondo's living room like a theater stage. Even confined to a single house, the picture looks huge. The screen is wide enough that you can keep track of each character's movements, and the film blocks no frame carelessly. The contained setting, and immense pressure placed on Gondo, still give the first half a claustrophobic feeling. The second half entering the town below is a pressure release, but your attention doesn't go slack because there's still a mystery to solve. The ending of "High and Low," where Gondo and the kidnapper finally come face-to-face, is a triumphant denouement.

Many great American filmmakers have looked to Akira Kurosawa, like "The Godfather" director Francis Ford Coppola. Watching "High and Low," it's easy to understand why.

However, with The Boys ending earlier in 2026, it left a big gap in the world of R-rated superhero shows, and while spinoffs such as Vought Rising and The Boys: Mexico will continue this universe, things simply won't be the same without the main series. Fortunately, there is already an abundance of great competitors out there.

Originally, it looked like Gen V would be The Boys' replacement before being canceled, but if you are looking for R-rated hits that are even better than anything this violent franchise produced, these six projects are definitely worth checking out.

Despite lasting just two seasons, The Punisher feels like a complete series that extends Frank Castle's story after first appearing in Daredevil. Getting to learn about the tragic events that turned him from a loving husband and father to a merciless killing machine is fascinating, and The Punisher certainly doesn't pull any punches with its violence.

As we've seen in the 2004 movie, characterizing Frank isn't always easy, as without the right nuance, he can feel empty and hard to connect with. However, Jon Bernthal delivers an outstanding performance that shows the humanity behind this vigilante, making it easier to root for him, despite not always agreeing with his actions.

The first season comes across as more personal, with Frank getting vengeance on those who were responsible for the death of his family, and the supporting cast is a key part of his journey. Ebon Moss-Bachrach, Amber Rose Revah, and Ben Barnes all do a great job with their respective characters, and while it can be an uncomfortable watch at times, The Punisher's story is ultimately very satisfying.

Season 2 presents a different vibe, with Giorgia Whigham's Amy Bendix bringing out the more fatherly side of Frank. Not only does he risk his own life to protect Amy, but he stands up for injustice outside his own personal grievances, with the final scene showing him fully embracing his Punisher persona.

Considering the character has been brought back as part of the MCU and even had his own special, it's clear Bernthal made a big impact as the Punisher, and the show successfully delivers exactly the type of brutal action needed to make it an all-time great that eclipses The Boys.

With James Gunn and Peter Safran stepping down from their leadership positions at DC Studios, the future of Lanterns is uncertain, but after just one season, it has already made a huge impression. Culturally, the show was massive, as it rejuvenated interest in the Green Lantern mythos and had people constantly bringing new theories and memes every week.

Sure, it definitely proved divisive as comic readers had major issues with the character and story direction, but as a whole, Lanterns was incredibly popular. The season 1 finale pulled in over 10 million viewers in just three days, and even those who hate the show can't deny how exceptional Kyle Chandler is as Hal Jordan, with Aaron Pierre shining opposite him.

Even though the series leans more into the mystery and detective genre than classic superhero elements, there are still several fun action sequences that definitely qualify it as a superhero project over something like The Penguin. Additionally, Lanterns sets up John Stewart to be more powerful in Man of Tomorrow, which should make up for the lack of major fight scenes.

It's rare that a modern show makes such a seismic impact in just nine episodes, but in terms of quality and consistency, Lanterns trumps The Boys, despite the latter having higher highs. The desire for a second season and Hal's return highlights how good the HBO series really is, and hopefully there's still a future for this surprise hit.

Much like Lanterns, Peacemaker's future is uncertain, even more so since James Gunn directed it, but it seems like season 2 was always planned to be the last anyway. Still, in just 16 episodes, Peacemaker manages to turn a bunch of obscure and original characters into fan favorites who audiences became 100% invested in.

John Cena's Peacemaker first appeared in 2021's The Suicide Squad, and while he was originally portrayed as arrogant and overconfident, viewers came around to him until he betrayed Rick Flag. That made centering a show around him extremely risky, yet Cena delivers a career-high performance full of emotion and vulnerability, adding so much depth to this anti-hero.

With an absolutely phenomenal cast for support, the series really thrives in its character work, making it almost essential that they return in future DCU projects. Both seasons feel incredibly different as they take place under completely different hierarchies, yet Gunn's vision allowed them to connect and achieve a similar quality.

Once again, it's a show that had people talking every week, predicting what would happen next, and somehow, people managed to guess season 2's most outlandish twist. At its core, Peacemaker is fun, hilarious, brutal, and surprisingly deep, cementing it as one of the greatest live-action superhero shows of the 21st century.

Its killer soundtrack only gives it more personality, and it has two of the best superhero show opening credits ever made, showing how much care was put into crafting this legendary series.

After the highly underrated 2009 movie, it wasn't clear how much legs the Watchmen franchise had in the highly competitive superhero market, but the TV show that premiered a decade later didn't disappoint. The series follows mostly different characters than the film, creating a unique spectacle, but one that is every bit entertaining.

Even though it's an original story, the tone and visuals capture what made the comics so great, and it isn't afraid to dive into culturally relevant topics. It tackles issues like racism in a mature manner, while having a compelling direction that isn't easy to predict, keeping viewers on their toes throughout all nine episodes.

Like the other entries on this list, the acting is top drawer, with Jeremy Irons standing out as the unhinged Ozymandias. The score is also worth mentioning, since it helps create the moody tone that makes Watchmen so gritty. That said, the show isn't afraid to get a little goofy at times either, with the humor separating it from DC's usual brand of comedy.

The story ties together nicely, and while the ending is packed, Watchmen still sticks the landing, making it no surprise it's so widely celebrated. It won 11 Emmys and was nominated for 26 in total, emphasizing the quality of this limited series, which didn't overstay its welcome.

Over 10 years ago, Daredevil debuted on Netflix, and to this day, many still see it as the greatest superhero show ever made. As a blind lawyer by day and a ruthless vigilante at night, Matt Murdock is the perfect protagonist for a dark, bloody show, and the three-season masterpiece couldn't have portrayed him any better.

Matt possesses all the charm and charisma needed to make him instantly likable, yet his disability and tragic origins cement him as a true underdog. Luckily, Foggy Nelson and Karen Page act as the optimal allies for him, which makes it all the more heartbreaking when he pushes them away, even if it's supposed to be for selfless reasons.

The drama is ever-present, and the choreography is perhaps still the gold standard for superhero projects, with that iconic one-shot hallway brawl remaining relevant all these years later. That's not to overlook Frank Castle's prison fight or the office brawl between Daredevil and Bullseye in season 3.

Speaking of Bullseye, he and Kingpin were so good that they both returned for Daredevil: Born Again with the same actors, confirming these villains were irreplaceable. Daredevil also has fantastic cinematography, with vibrant colors and shots helping forge an identity that the Disney+ show couldn't replicate, despite trying its best.

Between the fantastic cast, gripping storylines, flawless editing, and all the other qualities Daredevil possesses, it's no surprise it has such a stellar reputation. It is a superhero show that changed the genre forever, and while The Boys did the same, it simply doesn't measure up to Netflix's Daredevil.

While The Boys deserves a lot of credit for breaking the traditional Marvel and DC dominance by becoming one of the biggest superhero shows of the past decade, its Prime Video competitor did the same and is even better. Invincible arrived two years after The Boys, but the animated hit is far more consistent, and still has a healthy future ahead of it.

As a faithful comic adaptation, Invincible retells the story of Robert Kirkman's original series, albeit with some tweaks and additions. Its vast roster of characters is among its biggest strengths, as alongside having a fleshed-out, complex protagonist, there are so many wonderful heroes, villains, and other supporting allies that make the universe feel alive.

There is no holding back when it comes to fights either, as the gory injuries make even The Boys look tame at times. Invincible also does a great job of adding genuinely hilarious gags and jokes into its otherwise serious narrative, which is far less shocking or in your face than its Prime Video counterpart.

Some of its very best episodes have near-perfect ratings on IMDb, putting them among the greatest in TV history, and mixed with a fantastic soundtrack, A-list cast, and constant drama, there is no denying that Invincible is a better superhero series than The Boys.

The fact that the show managed to overcome its biggest weakness by shortening the gap between seasons to release regularly means it is bound to keep getting better, and with several seasons still to come for Invincible, it deserves to be recognized as one of the best in the genre.
