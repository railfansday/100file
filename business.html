MLB rumors: Corey Seager willing to waive his NTC, plus latest on Mets, Cubs, Blue Jays, Guardians, more

The uncertain 2026-27 Major League Baseball offseason is fully underway. The ongoing CBA negotiations and the near certainty of an owner-implemented lockout on Dec. 2 loom over all, and it may indeed mean a flurry of trades and free agent signings leading up to that date. That was certainly the case leading up to the last lockout of 2021-22, and this time around we've already seen some leading examples. Just recently, for instance, the New York Mets landed the biggest fish in the pond by signing ace Tarik Skubal to a record contract. Not long after, the Chicago Cubs fortified their rotation with Trevor Rogers and their outfield with Teruaki Sato, and Freddy Peralta is headed to the Baltimore Orioles. More's surely to come before lockout day arrives.

Thanks to that prospect of a hastened pace, offseason rumors are flowing. Speaking of which, we'll be here each day to round up what's out there. You can find Tuesday's supply just below.

The Philadelphia Phillies are open to moving Trea Turner off of shortstop given that he's now a pronounced defensive liability. Speaking of openness, veteran shortstop Corey Seager of the Texas Rangers is willing to waive his no-trade clause to join the Phillies and upgrade the position for them, NBC Sports Philadelphia reports.

Now 32, Seager will always have health and durability concerns, but he remains a plus defensive shortstop who produces well at the plate by positional standards. Signed to a 10-year, $325 million deal prior to the 2022 season (just before the last lockout, in fact), Seager is owed another $155 million through the 2031 season. The Rangers are reportedly looking to shake things up. The Phillies, meantime, are trying to keep their contention window open. It's a fit both ways, at least in theory. Such a move would likely mean a move to the outfield for Turner, who himself has very little trade value thanks to his ongoing decline and big contract.

The Toronto Blue Jays have thus far failed to make a big addition this offseason, but they still have targets on the board. The Jays, after missing out on Tarik Skubal, Freddy Peralta, and Trevor Rogers, have moved on to targets like right-hander Kaima Taira out of Japan and lefty Adrian Morejon, the Athletic reports. Taira, 27, ranked No. 2 on our list of notable international free agents this offseason. Here's part of that write-up:

"Listed at 5-foot-8, Taira gets outlier extension down the mound (close to seven feet despite his height), which creates a low release window. Hitters rarely see the ball come from where Taira releases it. He's not a gimmick pitcher either. Taira sits mid-90s with his four-seam fastball and backs it up with a cutter, sinker, sweeper, slider, changeup, and splitter. The sweeper and slider are distinct pitches, as are the changeup and splitter. The Seibu Lions used Taira as a reliever in 2024 and 2025, then started him in 2026, and he handled the assignment well. The breaking balls are just OK, and the fact of the matter is that pitchers this height don't often hold up under a starter's workload. I would assume the team that signs Taira will give him a chance to start. In the long run, though, he might be a reliever, perhaps even a multi-inning setup man. Taira was officially posted on Nov. 1."

Morejon, meanwhile, is particularly intriguing. He's young for a free agent at age 27, and he's coming off a dominant season in the San Diego Padres' bullpen. Thanks to his elite stuff and full repertoire, he's also a strong candidate to transition to the rotation moving forward, though there's reporting that he'd rather stay in the bullpen. That makes him a coveted presence on the market, and the Jays have the resources and willingness to land him.

As noted above, the Chicago Cubs have been energized on the market lately, but they still have work to do after losing so many contributors to free agency. On that front, the club may be eyeing the aforementioned Taira, who's making the leap from Japan, the Athletic reported before the latest flood of moves.

While Taira's role in MLB is TBD, the Cubs still have vacancies in the rotation for 2027 and beyond.

The Cleveland Guardians have agreed to a free-agent contract with left-hander Kris Bubic, Sportsnet reports. The contract is for $17.5 million over one year, according to ESPN. Bubic, 29, made nine starts for the Kansas City Royals last season, and after the trade to the Los Angeles Dodgers, he was used out of the bullpen. Bubic has an outlier fastball thanks to its rise and his unusual release point, and he's an interesting "ceiling" candidate in the rotation. However, his substantial history of injuries may mean he's best used out of the bullpen.

The Detroit Tigers have added infield depth by agreeing to terms with Ha-Seong Kim on a one-year, $5 million free agent contract, ESPN reports. Kim, 31, is coming off a rough partial season with the Atlanta Braves, but he has a more palatable OPS+ of 91 for his career. He's been a primary shortstop in recent years and also has significant time spent at second base and third base.

Even after achieving a reunion with veteran starting pitcher Sonny Gray, the Minnesota Twins are still looking to fortify their roster for a return to contention in 2027. The club still hopes to make one more significant addition this offseason and add another player who may not grab as many headlines, the Athletic reports. Atop the wishlist are a first baseman or a second baseman and another lefty reliever.

Even after the splash addition of Skubal, the Mets may not be done adding starting pitchers in the service of a rebound season in 2027. On that front, the Mets may be interested in adding a starting pitcher on a short-term deal for next season, the Athletic reports. That would likely limit them to the back half of the market, especially with so many bigger names reaching agreements.

Hang most of your laundry on a folding rack or a line, since an electric dryer costs about 40 cents a load at average U.S. electricity prices. A $30 rack pays for itself after about 75 loads you hang instead, and you can still keep the dryer for towels, sheets, and rainy weeks.

The average electric dryer uses just over 690 kilowatt-hours of electricity a year, a field study the Northwest Energy Efficiency Alliance published in 2026 found. At the average U.S. residential rate of 18.16 cents per kilowatt-hour in August 2026, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration, that comes to about $125 a year.

The same study found the average household runs 307 full drying cycles a year, so each load costs about 40 cents to dry, and a load you hang costs nothing. A gas dryer costs less to run than an electric one, so hanging its loads saves less.

At 40 cents a load, a couple who wash seven loads a week and hang five of them save $2 a week, or $104 a year, and cut their drying bill by more than 70%. Their $30 folding rack pays for itself in 15 weeks.

You don’t need a yard. A folding rack stands in a corner, a wall-mounted rack folds flat when it’s empty, and shirts dry on hangers hooked over the shower rod. A retractable line can stretch across a tub or a balcony, but check your lease or homeowners association’s rules for a ban on outdoor clotheslines before you hang one outside.

If you hang towels too, take them down while they’re still a little damp, then soften them with 10 minutes in the dryer. Air-drying also spares your clothes the dryer’s heat, which helps them last longer.

For the loads you still dry, clean the lint screen every time and use the moisture-sensor or auto-dry setting instead of a timer, so the dryer stops once the clothes are dry. Run those loads back to back, too, while the drum still holds the last load’s heat.

U.S. consumer confidence surged to a nearly two-year high after midterms

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Conference Board index climbed 11.4 points from October to 103.3 as views of the job market improved

David Paul Morris / Bloomberg via Getty Images

The Conference Board said its Consumer Confidence Index rose 11.4 points to 103.3 in November from 91.9 in October, a level last seen in January 2025.

The Present Situation Index, which reflects consumers' views of current business and labor market conditions, rose 13.7 points to 132.8. The Expectations Index, which reflects their short-term outlook for income, business and the labor market, rose 9.9 points to 83.6. The survey ran from Nov. 1 to Nov. 17.

The reading exceeded the Dow Jones consensus forecast of 92.2, according to CNBC.

"Consumer confidence rallied again in November, potentially due to lower uncertainty following the midterm elections," Fergus McCormick, senior economist for global indicators at the Conference Board, said in a statement. "The gain in confidence was led by stronger perceptions of present and future labor market conditions."

The share of consumers calling jobs "plentiful" rose to 30.8% from 26.2% in October. The share saying jobs were "hard to get" fell to 18.4% from 21.4%. The labor market differential improved 7.6 percentage points to +12.4%. Consumers also planned a small increase in spending on goods and vacations, the Conference Board said.

Confidence rose among all age groups, with Gen Z respondents posting the largest gain. Sentiment improved for Democrats and Independents, but Republicans grew less confident. Republicans still held the highest confidence of any political affiliation.

Consumers' average 12-month inflation expectation declined for a second consecutive month to 5.7%. McCormick said households remained unhappy about steep prices and mounting expenses, though some comments struck a more hopeful note about the broader economy.

Consumer confidence is closely tied to what drivers pay at the pump, according to The Associated Press. Gas prices averaged $3.61 a gallon nationwide on Tuesday, down from $4.30 a month earlier.

The University of Michigan's November consumer survey, which came out last week, found sentiment up 7.3% from October, according to CNBC.

The Conference Board's index ended a streak of three straight monthly declines in October, after hitting its weakest reading since 2014 the month before.

2028 Honda CR-V Is a Fuel-Sipping Hybrid Family SUV Worth Waiting For

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Honda’s bestseller goes hybrid only as it enters its seventh generation and will arrive in 2027 with a fresh design inside and out.

Honda’s bestselling model—and the third-bestselling vehicle in the United States through the third quarter of 2026—is getting a complete makeover. The CR-V will follow the path set forth by the sixth-generation Toyota RAV4, going hybrid only as the CR-V enters its seventh generation for the 2028 model year.

We expect the 2028 CR-V Hybrid to wear a sharp new design, with spy photos showing slim daytime running lights above a series of thin horizontal grilles, while the rear end features a light bar that stretches the full width of the SUV. It will likely also be the first vehicle in the U.S. to wear Honda’s new “H” logo, which was originally introduced for its future EVs but will now be applied to other vehicles in its lineup after Honda hurriedly canceled said EVs earlier this year.

The cabin is also expected to get a significant makeover, with the Avancier—a three-row SUV sold in Asia—providing hints about what to expect. The Avancier skips out on most real buttons and knobs, instead putting nearly every function into a large screen mounted to the dashboard, a dramatic shift from Honda’s current setup that prioritizes physical controls. The Avancier’s interior is wrapped in white upholstery, accented by glowing ambient lighting, and features a squared-off steering wheel. Honda also appears to be switching to a column-mounted gear selector.

While Honda was once led by the Accord and Civic sedans, in today’s SUV-obsessed market, the CR-V has become the Japanese automaker’s most important model. Its biggest rival, the RAV4, was overhauled for 2026 and went hybrid only. Now, the CR-V will follow suit to stay competitive in the incredibly crowded compact SUV segment. Hybrid sales of the current-generation CR-V have been strong, accounting for over half of the SUV’s total sales so far this year, making it a perfect choice to launch Honda’s new platform and hybrid powertrain.

The CR-V will arrive on a new platform that Honda promises will be stiffer and lighter than before, taking a modular approach that will lead to greater parts sharing and reducing costs. This platform will presumably be adapted to underpin future versions of the Accord and Civic, along with Acura models such as the upcoming next-generation RDX Hybrid.

Honda will ditch the standard 190-hp 1.5-liter four-cylinder engine for the 2028 CR-V, which will arrive exclusively with the company’s new hybrid powertrain. The fifth generation of Honda’s two-motor hybrid setup will continue to use an Atkinson-cycle four-cylinder, along with a pair of electric motors, but the 2.0-liter engine will be a fresh design, featuring a new cooling system. As before, the electric motors will power the wheels most of the time, but the gas engine can also be engaged to directly drive the wheels.

The electric motors are more compact than before, and Honda has developed a new battery too. The company says the new hybrid setup will bring improved power and efficiency, targeting 10 percent better fuel economy than the outgoing hybrid powertrain. The current CR-V Hybrid makes 205 horsepower and is rated at up to 38 mpg combined. We wouldn’t be surprised to see power increase to over 220 hp—the RAV4 makes 226 hp in FWD form and 236 hp with AWD—and fuel economy nudge up to just over 40 mpg.

The CR-V is expected to come with front-wheel drive standard and all-wheel drive optional, although it’s unclear if the latter will see the addition of a third electric motor. Honda will also likely include the S+ Shift feature seen on the Civic Hybrid and Prelude, which simulates a traditional transmission’s gearchanges and allows the driver to manually shift with the steering-wheel paddles.

The CR-V’s chief rival will continue to be the Toyota RAV4. However, the Hyundai Tucson Hybrid and Nissan Rogue Hybrid, which both enter a new generation for 2027, will also be strong competitors. The Jeep Cherokee, Kia Sportage Hybrid, and Subaru Forester Hybrid will be in the mix too, along with nonhybrid compact SUVs like the Volkswagen Tiguan.

The move to only offer a hybrid powertrain could see the CR-V’s entry price climb, which might alienate some buyers. Honda will also need to make sure it can match or exceed the efficiency of rivals like the RAV4 while still providing adequate performance and a spacious, usable cabin, given how closely matched the compact SUV space is these days. Moving away from physical interior controls could also upset the Honda faithful who are used to the company’s simple-to-use buttons.

Production is set to kick off at Honda’s factories in East Liberty, Ohio, and Greensburg, Indiana, in April 2027. Sales should begin shortly after, and with the current CR-V Hybrid starting at $37,580, we expect the next-generation model to come in at around $38,000.

To give stock, transfer the shares to the recipient’s brokerage account or a child’s custodial account. Here's How Much Of A Flight Airline Pilots Actually Hand-Fly

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

guys_who_shoot | Shutterstock, Simple Flying

On a typical commercial flight, airline pilots only spend a small proportion of their time physically controlling the aircraft. Pilots normally hand-fly takeoff and part of the initial climb, while the autopilot handles most of the flight until approach. According to a 2026 report by the International Air Transport Association (IATA), commercial aircraft operate with automation engaged for approximately 90% of the time.

On a long-haul flight, a pilot may spend just a few minutes controlling a Boeing 787 or Airbus A350, although operating procedures and weather conditions can influence this substantially. Moreover, a pilot not physically moving the controls does not mean the aircraft is operating without human supervision. The crew remains responsible for managing the flight path, monitoring the automated systems, and taking over whenever necessary.

Airborne manual flying normally starts with takeoff, which remains a pilot-controlled maneuver on conventional commercial airplanes. Taxiing is generally manual as well, but is not included in the airborne flight time. The pilot maintains directional control, rotates, and establishes the initial climb. Although various systems can manage engine power, this should not be confused with the aircraft performing an automatic takeoff. The autopilot can only be engaged once the aircraft has reached its approved minimum engagement height.

After departure, the crew can generally engage the autopilot relatively early, sometimes only a few hundred feet above the ground. According to IATA's 2020 Aircraft Handling and Manual Flying Skills Report, many surveyed pilots reported engaging it around 400 or 500 feet above ground level, while others preferred to continue flying manually through 5,000 or even 10,000 feet. The exact altitude depends on the aircraft and airline.

Once engaged, the autopilot can handle most of the climb, cruise, and descent, following the route and altitude instructions managed by the crew. On arrival, the pilot may disconnect it early during a visual approach or keep it engaged until shortly before landing. The final flare and touchdown are normally performed manually, although suitably equipped aircraft can also carry out automatic landings under the appropriate conditions.

Estimates of how much time airline pilots spend hand-flying differ substantially, partly because there is no universal operating procedure. Back in 2011, airline captain Rory Kay, co-chair of a Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) advisory committee on pilot training, said that pilots were using automation "for all but approximately three minutes of a flight".

Another committee member, captain Bob Coffman, provided an even lower estimate for some regional airline operations. According to Coffman, pilots at commuter airlines might fly with the autopilot disengaged for approximately 80 seconds during a typical two-hour flight. Both estimates are historical rather than current industry averages.

Other pilots take a somewhat different approach. In an interview published by Johnny Jet, airline pilot Spencer Marker explained that most airline pilots spend ten minutes or less manually controlling their aircraft. However, he personally preferred to hand-fly departures until reaching 10,000 feet and disconnect the autopilot approximately five miles from the runway when weather and operating conditions allowed.

The length of the flight also makes a significant difference. Ten minutes represents almost 17% of a one-hour flight, but only 1.7% of a ten-hour service. On an ultra-long-haul operation, such as Qantas' nonstop Perth (PER) - London (LHR) route, the proportion can become even smaller.

From filling out paperwork to maintaining communication and constantly checking the instruments, the pilots are kept busy on the flight deck.

Although the autopilot physically controls the aircraft for much of a flight, it is not an independent system that simply receives a destination and makes every decision. Modern airliners use several connected systems, including the flight management system, flight director, autopilot, and automatic thrust controls. These each perform different functions, and the crew must ensure that the appropriate modes are selected and that the aircraft is following its intended flight path.

There is also an important difference between flying manually and flying without any assistance. A pilot can disconnect the autopilot while keeping the flight director engaged, which provides guidance on the primary flight display. Automatic thrust can also remain active while the pilot controls the aircraft's pitch and bank. Flying using basic instrument indications without flight director guidance, known as raw-data flying, demands additional proficiency.

During cruise, the pilots continue monitoring navigation, weather, fuel consumption, aircraft systems, and instructions from air traffic control. Changes to the assigned route or altitude must be correctly entered and verified. Automation also helps distribute workload between the pilot flying and pilot monitoring, especially during departures and approaches where both pilots may be managing several tasks simultaneously.

The time spent holding the controls therefore provides little indication of the crew's overall workload. Even with autopilot engaged, the pilots must decide whether the selected automation remains appropriate.

The pilot landing an ultra-long-haul A350 needs two consecutive hours of rest in the second half of the flight.

Airlines can take remarkably different approaches to manual flying. Some encourage pilots to use the highest available level of automation throughout most of the flight, while others explicitly allow manual flying whenever weather, air traffic, and cockpit workload permit. Manual flying opportunities therefore depend heavily on each airline's standard operating procedures (SOP).

IATA's 2020 survey, which received 5,650 responses overall, found that only 36% of the 5,241 pilots answering the relevant question said their airline allowed and supported manual flying without limitations. A separate question attracted 3,452 responses, of which approximately 37% admitted hesitating to disengage the autopilot and take manual control. Some respondents also feared criticism for hand-flying even where procedures permitted it.

Complex departures, congested airspace, and reduced visibility can increase workload, making automation the safer option. Some approaches and airspace procedures also require automation for precise navigation. An airline therefore needs to balance manual flying opportunities against the operational conditions of each individual flight.

IATA's survey also found differences during landing, with many pilots disconnecting the autopilot around 1,000 feet above the runway when conditions permitted. Others did so earlier or at the minimum altitude allowed by the relevant procedure. An approach flown with the autopilot engaged is not automatically an autoland; in most cases, pilots still take control before touchdown.

Modern airliners are incredibly automated, but pilots remain fully responsible for every decision during a flight. What do they actually do?

One particularly interesting difference concerns the type of flying performed by the crew. Short-haul pilots might operate several sectors on the same day, giving them multiple opportunities to practice takeoffs and landings. Long-haul pilots, on the other hand, can spend most of a working day on one flight, and augmented crews may share the workload during particularly long services. Consequently, each pilot may perform far fewer takeoffs and landings.

A 2017 study surveyed more than 1,500 commercial pilots about manual flying and airline procedures. Only 35.5% of long-haul pilots agreed that they had sufficient opportunities to hand-fly, compared with 79.9% of pilots operating short- and medium-haul flights. Pilots flying aircraft such as the Boeing 777, Airbus A380, and Airbus A330/A340 were among those reporting the fewest opportunities.

The same study also identified a clear difference between airline cultures. Among pilots working in supportive procedural environments, 68% felt they had sufficient opportunities to maintain their manual flying skills during normal operations. That figure fell to just 26% among pilots whose airlines imposed more restrictive procedures.

The difference is also not simply explainable by aircraft age or technology. United Airlines continues operating its Boeing 767 fleet even as newer widebodies enter service, and both generations rely extensively on automation. Accumulating flying hours on long sectors does not necessarily mean practicing basic aircraft handling.

While most landings follow a stable approach, almost all unstable approaches are followed by landing.

Concerns about reduced manual flying opportunities have been raised by aviation regulators for well over a decade. The Associated Press reported in 2011 that a draft FAA study had examined 46 accidents and major incidents, alongside hundreds of voluntary reports and observations from more than 9,000 flights. It found problems involving manual aircraft handling or automated flight controls in more than 60% of the accidents and 30% of the major incidents reviewed. These were findings from a selected sample, rather than percentages applying to all airline accidents.

The FAA subsequently issued Safety Alert for Operators 13002 in 2013, encouraging airlines to provide suitable opportunities for manual flying. A further safety alert in 2017 called for manual flying proficiency to be reinforced through both training and normal line operations. It recommended manual instrument departures and arrivals, upset recovery, and training across different automation modes.

The issue has not disappeared. In its 2026 flight path management report, IATA identified manual handling as an important safety concern and called for training and airline policies that develop both manual and automated flight path management skills. Full-flight simulators can reproduce unreliable airspeed, unusual attitudes, and other situations unsuitable for practice on commercial flights.

The French factory Mercedes built for the tiny Smart city car, and that Jim Ratcliffe turned into the home of his Land Rover Defender tribute, could soon be building BYDs.

BYD confirmed today that it has entered exclusive negotiations to acquire Ineos Automotive’s plant in Hambach, France. If it closes, it would be the Chinese giant’s second passenger car factory in the EU after Hungary, and the first time a Chinese automaker buys an existing car plant in Europe.

The other half of the deal is Ineos packing up. Alongside the announcement, Ineos Automotive said it plans to move Grenadier and Quartermaster production to the US, which it says accounts for about 70% of its sales. It has shortlisted US sites, hasn’t named any, and is aiming to start production there in 2028.

The math is simple. Every Grenadier shipped from France to the US gets hit with a 15% tariff, and the Quartermaster pickup falls under the 25% tariff on imported pickups. When 7 out of 10 of your customers are American, building your trucks in Lorraine stops making sense.

Ineos says it will keep building both models at Hambach “for the medium term” if the sale goes through, so the two companies would share the site for a while.

Neither side disclosed a price or a closing date, and the sale still has to go through consultation with employee representatives. BYD will soon find out that France is nothing like China when it comes to employee representation. There’s also no word yet on which BYD models would come out of Hambach or when. Same for the plant’s roughly 1,000 employees: BYD made no commitment on how many it would keep.

BYD executive vice president Stella Li said the company wants to give European consumers “vehicles built in France” that use BYD’s technology.

Hambach opened in 1997 as “Smartville,” Mercedes’ factory for the Smart Fortwo, and it later built the all-electric Smart EQ Fortwo. Ineos bought it from Mercedes at the end of 2020 after dropping its plan to build a new factory in Wales, and Grenadier production started in 2022. Ineos says it has put nearly €540 million into the site since 2019.

There’s also a bit of irony in who’s buying. Ineos unveiled its first EV, the Fusilier, in early 2024 while romanticizing gas cars at the same event. The motivation is regulatory. China-built BYD EVs face a 17% countervailing duty in the EU on top of the standard 10% car tariff, imported Chinese hybrids are now subject to quotas, and the EU’s proposed “Made in Europe” rules would tie EV subsidies and public contracts to how much of a vehicle is built locally. A French plant solves a lot of those problems at once.

Europe matters more than ever for BYD because home isn’t going well. BYD brand sales in China fell 40% to 1,544,699 vehicles over the first 10 months, and its global passenger vehicle sales are down 2.7%.

This is a smart, cheap move for BYD. Building Szeged took years, including land disputes with private owners. Hambach comes with a trained workforce, a paint shop, a supplier base on the German border, and a seller that’s motivated to leave. You don’t get a much easier entry into France, but the easiest in France is still probably more difficult than anything BYD faced in China when it comes to deploying new production capacity.

The 30,000-unit capacity is small, but BYD isn’t buying it for what it builds today. It’s buying a “Made in France” label ahead of local content rules.

And yes, it’s funny that BYD is moving into the home of the Grenadier, a vehicle that exists because Ratcliffe loved the old Defender, days after a court in The Hague barred BYD’s Ti7 from the EU for infringing Land Rover’s Defender design.

It's a remarkable outcome for the buyers opted out of the which picket fence dream, and instead invested in properties that often offer a more affordable entry point into homeownership. But that outcome may be harder to replicate in today's rapidly changing housing market.

Condo values fell 0.8% year over year in August 2026, even as single-family home values rose 1.8%, according to data from Intercontinental Exchange. And as home price growth cools, expenses that were easier to stomach during years of rapid appreciation stand to take a bigger bite out of homeowners' returns.

Even so, those national numbers tell only part of the story. In some regions, condos substantially outperformed townhouses over the decade examined by Realtor.com, challenging the notion that owning a piece of land necessarily leads to stronger appreciation.

Understanding what drove those differences—and how the economics of owning each property type are changing—offers some clues as to where buyers might find the greatest opportunity to build equity over the next decade.

Single-family homes appreciated 87.3% from 2014 to 2024, according to Realtor.com senior economist Hannah Jones. Townhomes nearly matched that pace at 86.5%, while condos trailed only slightly at 82.7%.

That modest gap are striking given the disparity in purchase prices. In October 2026, the median existing condo or co-op sold for $365,100—nearly $57,000 less than the median single-family home, according to the National Association of Realtors (NAR).

“Single-family homes are still the most popular type of homes purchased in the U.S. However, condos do appeal to buyers who want a smaller square footage and less upkeep,” explains Jessica Lautz, NAR deputy chief economist and vice president of research. “Condos are also significantly more affordable than single-family homes, which allows buyers who are downsizing or those just starting into homeownership to embrace condos.”

Still, the numbers show townhomes have been edging closer to single-family performance.

“Over the last decade, condos and townhomes appreciated almost in lockstep, but townhomes have pulled slightly ahead recently as buyers chase ‘house-like’ living without the single-family price tag,” says Jones.

While both property types have delivered strikingly similar returns from a national lens, as in all things real estate, the real gains come down to location, location, location.

In the Midwest, condo prices rose 78.3% over the last decade, outpacing the 70.7% growth of townhomes. The South followed a similar pattern, with condos climbing 66.7% compared with just 53.1% for townhomes.

The dynamic flips in higher-cost regions. In the Northeast, townhomes surged 80.5%, far ahead of the 58.6% gain for condos. And in the West, townhomes appreciated 83.8%, edging past condos at 78.9%.

Affordability explains much of the split, says Jones.

“In the more affordable Midwest and South, condos have gained ground faster than townhomes. In higher-cost regions like the Northeast and West, townhomes have appreciated more quickly,” she explains.

In lower-cost markets, condos benefit from their clustering in downtown areas and their appeal to buyers chasing city living. But in expensive, supply-constrained metros, the premium shifts toward townhomes.

“Townhomes are commanding a growing premium as buyers seek a house-like property when single-family homes are out of reach. In more affordable markets, condos—often concentrated in dense city centers—have captured stronger growth as buyers compete for limited housing options,” she adds.

“Townhomes look set for steadier growth,” says Jones. Part of that is because of what you get when you buy one.

When you purchase a townhouse, you own both the structure and the parcel of land it sits on. That land ownership gives you more control, typically fewer restrictions, and it can be a stronger driver of long-term appreciation.

“Generally townhouses have strong appreciation potential because the land itself appreciates in a more reliable way,” explains Aman Sharma, a real estate agent at SERHANT.

Eric Hughes, a real estate investor and coach with Rental Income Advisors, agrees. “The price growth of townhomes will track extremely closely with the price growth of single-family homes in general,” he says.

That connection to single-family homes is key. The “single-family feel” broadens their appeal to a wider range of buyers—families, downsizers, and even first-timers looking to get their foot in the door.

They also tend to come with lower HOA fees than condos. That not only frees up more buying power for the mortgage itself, but it also makes resale easier since future buyers are less likely to be put off by steep monthly dues or looming assessments.

Builders have taken notice, too. Townhouses accounted for a record 18.7% of single-family housing starts on a one-year moving average basis in the third quarter of 2025, according to the National Association of Home Builders.

But Jones warns that this investment isn’t free of potential headwinds. In markets where single-family inventory is expanding and prices are softening, townhome demand may take a hit as more buyers opt for detached homes instead.

“In tight urban markets, condos can still shine as buyers get creative,” says Jones.

Even though the appreciation rate of condos has lagged behind townhomes and single-family homes nationally over the last decade, they can still deliver strong returns in the right conditions.

“In big urban markets, condos are often the most desirable type of urban residence, and can appreciate strongly,” says Hughes. That means that in places like Miami Beach, New York, and San Francisco—where land is scarce and demand for city living is strong—condos dominate.

“There are certain parts of our city where highly amenitized condo high-rises perform better than townhouses from an investment standpoint,” says Miltiadis Kastanis, executive director of sales at Compass, based in Miami.

Lifestyle amenities are another draw. Concierge service, pools, gyms, and other perks make condos especially attractive to luxury buyers or second-home seekers who prioritize convenience. For full-time residents, those amenities can also translate into higher perceived value when it’s time to sell.

But again, Jones stresses that there is greater potential for some bumps in the road for condos, ranging from rising ownership costs to competition from single-family homes and townhouses.

The experts we spoke to stress that your return on investment isn’t just about appreciation and loan paydown. The hidden costs of homeownership can chip away at returns. However, it’s worth noting that even single-family homes can fall prey to these same traps.

“While single-family homes may not have an HOA fee, some communities do even for single-family homes,” says Lautz. “Maintenance can be more expensive when a homebuyer must account for costly items such as roofs, which a condo owner is unlikely to be responsible for.”

While it’s true that owners of single-family homes must shoulder all their own maintenance costs, condo owners risk getting hit with special assessments that may cover repairs or upgrades to facilities they don’t use, or work that should have been done properly the first time around.

Rick Bannon, global real estate adviser at One Sotheby’s International Realty, points to a condo in Miami where residents were hit with a $1 million special assessment just to bring the building up to safety standards.

That’s why Joe Luciano, broker at Re/Max Bentley’s, takes special care when helping his clients through a condo purchase.

“When advising clients on a condo purchase, we always want to get the meeting minutes to see if there’s any chatter around potential projects coming up and then also the financials to see if they have the appropriate amount in reserve funds to cover any surprises,” he says.

Those reserves are set to become an even more important consideration for buyers. Beginning Jan. 4, 2027, Fannie Mae will raise its standard minimum replacement reserve requirement from 10% to 15% of annual budgeted assessment income for condo projects subject to its Full Review process.

While the change could help protect condo owners against the financial fallout of deferred maintenance, it could also put upward pressure on monthly dues in underfunded communities. And buildings that fail to meet mortgage eligibility requirements could face a smaller pool of buyers.

Outside of special assessments, Luciano says monthly HOA fees can take a big bite out of a homeowner's return on investment.

Nearly 3.4 million American households paid at least $500 a month in condo or HOA fees in 2025, according to data from the U.S. Census Bureau. Roughly 1 million paid at least $1,000 a month.

“When looking at the two as investments side by side over 10 years, the appreciation could be similar, but paying an extra $300 to $500 a month is a sunk cost of $36,000 to $60,000 that didn’t go toward your mortgage principal,” explains Luciano.

At the extreme end, he points to luxury Boston high-rises with $2,000-plus monthly HOA fees that have seen stagnant appreciation in recent years.

If the last 10 years prove anything, it’s that condos and townhouses will both continue to deliver strong returns—just not always for the same reasons, or in the same places.

Townhomes have shown they can reliably shadow the trajectory of single-family homes, powered by land value and a “house-like” appeal that buyers continue to prize. Condos, by contrast, thrive where affordability and density drive demand, making them especially potent in urban cores and lower-cost regions where buyers are squeezed out of detached homes.

The next decade may sharpen these divides. As affordability pressures deepen, first-time buyers could lean more heavily into condos, reviving demand in downtowns that have seen uneven recoveries since the pandemic. At the same time, zoning reforms and the ongoing shortage of single-family homes could keep pushing buyers toward townhouses as the closest substitute for a detached home. Policy choices—from property tax incentives to how cities handle condo reserve requirements after recent safety crises—will also influence which type of property captures the bigger share of appreciation.

For investors, the lesson isn’t to pick one winner and walk away. It’s to understand how local affordability, supply constraints, and regulation interact to tilt the playing field.

Mason & Fifth guesthouses are composed of fully furnished private apartments where guests have the flexibility of staying anywhere from one night to several months at a time.

The company has three sites across London, with one in Primrose Hill, another in Westbourne Park, and now Belsize Park.

The Belsize Park location occupies Ornan Court, a 19th-century mansion block that once formed part of the Ivy Bank residential estate.

Architecture practice New-works adapted the building for hospitality use, establishing a series of communal spaces at ground level that could serve as a social hub for guests.

At its heart is a large shared kitchen, centred by a chunky tiled breakfast island, followed by a lounge area with intimate groupings of timber-frame chairs and two khaki-coloured sofas, complementing the glossy green fireplace.

Just off this space is a cosy snug that’s been painted a warm ochre hue. It’s separated from the lounge by a custom-made curtain that design studio House of Quinn made from earth-toned fabric offcuts.

Holloway Li is behind the design of the interiors. Instead of doing a through-and-through overhaul, the studio instead aimed to develop a “circular economy aesthetic that felt warm, domestic and low-impact”.

“That ambition aligned with Mason & Fifth’s own sustainability agenda and allowed us to explore how historic buildings like this can be retrofitted in imaginative and progressive ways,” said the studio’s creative director Alex Holloway.

In each of the guesthouse’s 56 apartments, the bedrooms are furnished with demountable wardrobes in lieu of fitted joinery.

The wardrobes are made up of lightweight timber parts fixed together with metal brackets rather than glue so that, if necessary, they can be unassembled, flat packed, and re-built elsewhere.

Holloway Li worked with Marble Collective to create countertops and splashbacks for the apartment kitchens; all are crafted from limestone that’s been rejected from quarries because of inconsistencies.

The top edge of the splashbacks has been left slightly craggy and unfinished, further celebrating the stone’s natural flaws.

Almost all of the walls throughout the guesthouse have been simply rendered with multi-finish plaster, and any surface imperfections have been left on show.

“In many cases, the most sustainable decision is restraint,” explained Holloway Li’s senior designer Carrie Coningsby.

“Drawing on principles akin to kintsugi and spolia, the approach celebrates intervention rather than concealing it, embedding repair into the visual language of the space,” she added, referencing historic practices focused on reuse and repair instead of replacement.

Mason & Fifth Belsize Park was shortlisted in the sustainable interior category of this year’s Dezeen Awards.

Other projects in the running included the winning Cloudhaus Hotel by RooMoo, which is outfitted with materials salvaged from abandoned mountain cabins, and the London Coffee Factory by Steve Jensen Design, which is structured around a reclaimed timber framework.

The Nine Movies Written Or Produced (But Not Directed) By Quentin Tarantino, Ranked

Tarantino’s latest non-directorial filmmaking project comes in the form of The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth, a sequel to Once Upon a Time in Hollywood that focuses on Brad Pitt’s cool, calm, and collected former stuntman. While Tarantino did not direct the Netflix movie (he handed those duties over to Seven and Zodiac director David Fincher), he wrote the screenplay for the movie and, in turn, imbues it with many of his trademark qualities as a filmmaker.

As far as sequels to horror movies go, Hostel: Part II is far from the worst the genre has had to offer. Nonetheless, it still doesn't stack up very well to the rest of the Tarantino movies he didn't direct, largely due to the quality of the movies that follow it.

Marring Hostel: Part II from start to finish is a story that bears far too much of a resemblance to its predecessor, to such an extent that the film feels more like a remake than a sequel. Originally released in the U.S. as half of the Grindhouse double feature alongside Quentin Tarantino’s Death Proof, Planet Terror fully and delightfully embraces the aesthetics of 1970s exploitation cinema, complete with intentional film scratches, missing reels, and over-the-top practical gore.

Because of the extensive changes to his script, Tarantino opted for a "story by" credit rather than a full screenplay credit. He publicly criticized it, said he never watched it all the way through, and later published his original screenplay to highlight its differences from the final product.

Upon release, Stone’s film was derided for its graphic brutality and scathing indictment on sensationalist media, going so far as to claim the press turns murderers into celebrities.

Many retrospective viewers, however, celebrate its hyper-edited visuals, shifting film formats, and focus on celebrity crime coverage as particularly prescient in an era of the 24-hour news cycle, social media algorithms, and true crime obsession. A beautiful film punctuated by Christopher Doyle’s painterly cinematography and distinct color-coded chapters, Hero further compels with its Rashomon-style unreliable-narrator structure and its deft blending of philosophical storytelling with wire-fu choreography.

Before you buy a print book new, look for a used copy, which often costs a few dollars. Online, compare sellers by what you’d pay with shipping, not the listed price. Then check library book sales near you, where many books cost even less.

A hardcover that costs $30 new can cost $2 at a library sale or $6 with shipping from an online used-book seller. Books hold up well, so a used copy in good condition reads just like a new one.

Online, the catch is shipping. On a marketplace like AbeBooks, each seller sets its own shipping charge, so a $1 copy with $4 shipping costs more than a $4 copy that ships free. Locally, browse used bookstores and thrift stores, and watch for library book sales. To get a particular edition, search by its ISBN, the number assigned to each edition and format of a book. A hardcover and a paperback of the same title each have their own.

The price per pound on a shelf tag misses how long each pound lasts. A pound of a calorie-dense food feeds your pet for more days than a pound of a less dense one.

The analysis, released Monday, paints a softer picture of the rate limits Republicans imposed on a supplemental Medicaid payment stream in last year’s broad budget reconciliation law, also referred to as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act or the Working Families Tax Cut Legislation.

Hospitals and other healthcare industry groups, as well as advocates and Democrats, have said the law’s billions in projected savings—from state-directed payment changes as well as other policy areas like Medicaid work requirements—are likely to devastate providers already operating at thin or negative margins.

Specifically, the Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation (ASPE)’s analysis showed that an estimated 7.8% of Medicaid state-directed payments reached rural hospitals in FY2025, or $11.5 billion out of $148.3 billion. That rate is slightly above rural hospitals’ 7% share of total Medicaid hospital spending for that same fiscal year, as estimated by the CMS Office of the Actuary.

Scaling from that baseline in line with H.R. 1’s multi-year payment rate phasedown, with some cumulative adjustments, showed that total nominal cumulative spending on rural state-directed payments would decline by 42.9%, or $81.6 billion, from 2026 through 2035 under the bill. This rate is again slightly higher than the CMS Office of the Actuary’s 40.8% estimate for all affected hospitals, which ASPE said is the result of the higher average dollar weighted payment rates rural hospitals currently receive, “which means that rural providers have more room to reduce rates to meet the new limits of 100% to 110% of Medicare.”

The ASPE report’s bottom line, however, is that $75.2 billion of the $81.6 billion reduction rural hospitals face, or 92.1%, reflects projected increases over the FY2025 baseline of $11.5 billion. The remainder, about 7.9% of the projected change, is a reduction below FY2025’s estimated state-directed payments to rural hospitals.

HHS, in a release highlighting the report’s findings, said that the language used in government reports describing the full $81.6 billion 10-year spending reduction as “savings” misses the government’s primary goal of capping future growth.

“Counting every dollar of projected savings as a cut below existing funding gives the public the wrong picture. Most of these savings are not reductions below what the program spent in 2025,” HHS Chief Economist and Chief Regulatory Officer Casey Mulligan, who was one of the report’s coauthors, said in the release. “They reflect future spending increases that will no longer occur. Our analysis makes that distinction clear.”

H.R. 1’s multi-year scale down in state-directed payment rates is set to begin in 2028, and are expected to slash some hard-hit states’ Medicaid spending by up to a quarter of their total annual Medicaid spending, per one analysis. Hospitals have broadly criticized the policy changes while spending much of this year adjusting their operations to contend with the future limits supplemental revenue, expectations of increased uncompensated care costs and year-to-year expense pressures like wage and supply inflation.

Additionally, CMS earlier this year proposed state directed payment caps substantially more extensive than what was outlined in H.R. 1 and used as the basis for Monday’s ASPE report. That proposal would extend rate limits beyond the bill’s four outlined service areas (inpatient hospital services, outpatient hospital services, nursing facility services and qualified practitioner services at an academic medical center) to “all [state-directed payments]s for all services in all states.” Hospital groups have flatly opposed the proposal, which more than triples the 10-year estimated savings.

Lawmakers last year, when seeking votes, blunted H.R. 1's funding reductions for rural hospitals by inserting a five-year, $50 billion rural health fund into the legislation. Here's How Much A Boeing 737 MAX 8 Earns An Airline On A Single Day Of Flying

In 2019, Boeing 737 MAX 8 operators were losing approximately $150,000 per aircraft per day in revenue. Today, each 737 MAX 8 generates around $200,000 per day, although airlines don't publish specifics, and revenue varies widely depending on how a carrier uses the aircraft.

The revenue made by each 737 MAX 8 per day is not pure profit. It is offset by depreciation or leasing costs, as well as operational and maintenance expenses. There is also a set-up cost when an airline first brings the jet into its fleet, requiring crew training and maintenance infrastructure, varying in severity depending on the degree of pre-existing fleet commonality with the Boeing 737 family.

Airlines are reluctant to publish exact data on how much money they earn for each aircraft in their fleet. However, answering this question is uniquely possible for the 737 MAX 8 because estimates emerged during the 737 MAX crisis. Each analyst had their own estimates at this time. IBA estimated total lost earnings of $150,000 per day. The Conversation reached a similar conclusion, based on three round trips a day and an average of 145 tickets sold per flight. It multiplied these sales by the US Department of Transportation average airfare in the US at the time of $343.28.

Southwest Airlines had the largest MAX fleet at the time with 34 aircraft, generating approximately $5 million in lost revenue per day. Across the entire 737 MAX fleet globally, the losses would have been closer to an eye-watering $60 million per day. This is why the crisis was so disastrous for Boeing, as there was an expectation that the manufacturer would absorb much of these costs.

IBA also notes other costs that had to be absorbed. Leasing for the jet was estimated at $360,000 a month. Staffing costs for two flight crew and four cabin crew were estimated at $5,000 per day. Airlines also had to figure out ways to adjust or increase their capacity to honor passengers' pre-existing bookings. IBA states that the cost to deal with 1,000 disrupted passengers would have been $100,000.

In his first year as St. Louis Cardinals scouting director, Randy Flores was watching a pitcher who threw a four-seam fastball and a curveball. The mix can be the backbone of a north-south pitching profile. But to Flores, a former major-league reliever, it looked suboptimal.

"I'm like, 'No, no, no - you sink it and slide it,'" said Flores, reciting an approach he gleaned from former Cardinals pitching coach Dave Duncan.

As a slight left-hander who did not throw especially hard, Flores carved out a playing career that lasted parts of eight major-league seasons. So, his methods had merit. But as an executive now, he recalls the scouting anecdote as something of a cautionary tale.

"Being a former player, if you're anchored too much to, 'This is how it was done when I was playing,' it's the wrong thing," Flores said.

Exploring other perspectives and means and finding a balance has been part of Flores' evolution as an executive, one that led to his hiring this month as general manager of the Houston Astros. It is also an arc to which only a handful of the majors' current highest-ranking baseball executives can relate.

Of the current presidents of baseball operations, Jerry Dipoto (Mariners), Chris Young (Rangers), Craig Breslow (Red Sox) and Buster Posey (Giants) played in the majors. So did general managers Chris Getz (White Sox) and Flores, whom the Astros hired after 11-plus seasons in St. Louis' front office.

That many top executives did not play in the majors, or at all professionally, illustrates how it is far from required. It might even invite the question of how such experience informs former players' approach to the role, if at all, as Flores settles into running a baseball operations department for the first time.

"You probably have more empathy … for what a player goes through, from the moment they sign their first professional contract to when they get to the big leagues," Dipoto said at last week's GM meetings in Las Vegas.

Dipoto and Breslow were relievers in the majors, like Flores. Dipoto posited that background could help "to understand the life of the guy who (is) not always the most thought-of player on the roster."

"Outside of that, I think it's just business as usual," Dipoto said. "We each went through our own, I guess, apprenticeship (after playing).

"You take the lessons you learn and apply them, just like any other general manager does when they get their first job. We just maybe have that slight - I don't even want to call it an advantage - that slight difference in that we have lived life in these players' shoes and probably as a result have a little bit more empathy rather than just frustration. It's a hard game."

Mariners GM Jerry Dipoto, a former major league reliever, is among a half-dozen ex-players who head up baseball operations departments. He said that experience includes empathy for players' journeys through pro ball. (Lindsey Wasson/AP)

After his playing career, Dipoto held roles in scouting before becoming general manager of the Angels in 2011 and for the Mariners in 2015. Breslow spent five seasons in the Cubs' front office, working heavily in pitching development, before the Red Sox hired him to lead their baseball operations.

"My hope would be it adds a level of credibility to the role and a level of empathy," Breslow said, "given that there really isn't anything I ask player to do that I haven't kind of been through myself, having been traded, promoted, released, started games, come in from the pen.

"And I think understanding the mindset of a player and how they're likely to respond and react to different suggestions and requests, I'm hopeful (that) is helpful in terms of understanding how to approach certain conversations."

Flores, 51, spent several years out of professional baseball after pitching his final season in the minors in 2011. "I understand how hard it is to play this game and I think I have some empathy for that," Flores said. "And I do believe the modern tools and curriculum and the technology and coaching that are available help drive player improvements at a pace much faster than I experienced as player via trial and error.

History Shows: Wayne Gretzky and Warren Buffett Can Teach Us About Investing

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Wayne Gretzky and Warren Buffett might be an unlikely team, but they shared a winning attitude that can help you make money in the stock market.

Wayne Gretzky is one of the most iconic figures in hockey. During his 20-year career in the NHL, Gretzky played in 1,487 games, scored 894 goals, and delivered 1,963 assists for a total of 2,857 points -- about 1.92 points per game.

Gretzky was so good at putting points on the board because of his uncanny ability to be in the right place at the right time. He seemed to anticipate where the puck was going to bounce next and put himself in position to capitalize on opportunities.

What can Gretzky's hockey career teach us about investing? More than you might think. Legendary investor Warren Buffett found inspiration from Wayne Gretzky. In a 2008 op-ed for The New York Times, Buffett quoted Gretzky, who famously said, "I skate to where the puck is going to be, not to where it has been."

There's a great lesson here about investing. Let's look at what Buffett meant by investing based on Wayne Gretzky's hockey skills -- and why stock market history shows that this "Gretzky-style" investing approach is a winning strategy.

Warren Buffett. Image source: The Motley Fool.

It's understandable to get caught up in negative daily news headlines. On any given day, it might seem like the stock market is going to crash, that the world is ending. But remember: Stock markets are about the future, not today.

Just as Wayne Gretzky was great at anticipating the next move of the puck on the ice, investors try to anticipate the future. They allocate capital to stocks or sectors based on expected future earnings growth.

Stock investors are constantly evaluating what's likely to happen next -- which companies are poised to keep growing, which industries are about to benefit from positive economic changes, and which countries around the world are due for a stock market surge. They want to put their money into the places where "the puck is going to be" -- not where the action is happening right now.

What does this mean for you? Take a long-term approach to investing. If you're putting money into the stock market today, hopefully you're ready and able to leave that money alone for the next five years, 10 years, 20 years, or more. You're sending that money on a long journey into the future. Even if the stock market crashes tomorrow, you're making a long-term bet that your investment will grow in the long run.

Most of the time, that's a good bet. The S&P 500 (^GSPC -3.70%) has delivered 9.98% annualized returns over the past 98 years, even with terrible crises and crashes along the way such as the Great Depression, World War II, and the 2020 pandemic.

No matter how risky and dangerous the world seems on any given day, the future of the stock market might be brighter than you imagine. It pays to be optimistic about stocks.

Gretzky's famous quote is also a reminder to avoid trying to "time the market." In his Times op-ed in 2008, Warren Buffett wrote:

Just like a hockey puck bouncing around an NHL ice rink, markets move fast. The S&P 500 can crash, but it can also bounce back even faster. If you're waiting on the sidelines, trying to time the market and choose the "right time" to invest, share prices might shoot back up faster than you can buy in.

If you miss out on the "best days" of the S&P 500, you might miss out on a big chunk of future wealth. Fidelity research found that, based on historical S&P 500 returns from the end of 1987 to the end of 2025, an investor who missed the five best days of returns over those 38 years would have seen 38% lower overall returns.

Instead of trying to choose exactly the right day to buy stocks, it's better to put your money to work as soon as you can. "Time in the market" beats "timing the market."

The Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO -3.42%) has delivered annualized gains of about 14.8% in the past 16 years since the fund's inception in September 2010. Even if the next few decades are not so fruitful and the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF only delivers the long-term stock market average return of 9.98%, that's still enough to get richer in the long run.

When you buy a low-cost index fund like VOO, you are not picking stocks or choosing individual sectors. You're making a long-term bet that the stock market itself will put your money to work in great companies that will profit from the economy of the future. In this way, investing in an S&P 500 ETF can put your money "where the puck is going to be."
