Rents Rise Annually for First Time in 3 Years as Multifamily Building Boom Stalls

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The median asking rent posted its first annual increase in more than three years in October, fueled by a sharp nationwide pullback in multifamily construction.

October marks the first month of year-over-year rent growth since June 2023, according to the latest Realtor.com® monthly rental report. The increase was modest, with the median asking rent rising by just $1 to $1,692, but the underlying data suggests more hikes could follow.

Larger units drove the rebound. Two-bedrooms led the way, with median asking rent rising 0.3% year over year to $1,889, followed by one-bedrooms, which edged up 0.1% to $1,566.

Meanwhile, studio rents continued to lag, falling 1.2% from October 2025 to $1,411.

"I see this as the market beginning to rebalance," Lissette Calderon, CEO of Neology Group, a Miami-based real estate development firm, tells Realtor.com. "We had an extraordinary amount of new supply delivered over the last few years, and it takes time for that inventory to be absorbed. With construction slowing and demand for rental housing remaining strong, I expect rent growth to gradually return, particularly in markets where employment and household growth continue to support housing demand."

Realtor.com senior economist Jiayi Xu confirms that last month's rent turnaround hinges on a shifting supply pipeline.

The post-pandemic multifamily construction boom of 2021–22 flooded the market with fresh rental units completed in 2023–25, keeping prices in check.

Now, a broad cooling in multifamily construction is easing downward pressure on rent, just as high mortgage rates continue to keep families in the rental market.

"The path ahead largely depends on whether new multifamily deliveries remain strong enough to outpace that demand," says Xu.

Multifamily developers remain pessimistic about current building conditions, according to the latest quarterly survey from the National Association of Home Builders (NAHB) released last week.

The NAHB's Multifamily Production Index (MPI) had a reading of 41 out of 100, down 5 points year over year. Any reading below 50 indicates that more builders view market conditions negatively than positively.

"It is not surprising that the MPI is down year over year given supply-side headwinds such as high interest rates and a rising rental vacancy rate," says NAHB Chief Economist Robert Dietz.

According to the U.S. Census Bureau's Housing Vacancy Survey, the national rental vacancy rate reached 7.6% in the third quarter, the highest level since early 2014.

Kip Lewis, chairman of NAHB’s Multifamily Council, also points to construction costs rising relative to rents, along with delays getting projects approved and connected to roads and utilities.

"As a result, many projects are taking longer to complete," says Lewis.

Echoing Lewis, Calderon says the building pace comes down to the economics of development.

"Construction costs, financing terms, insurance, and operating expenses all affect whether a project can move forward," she says. "When projected rents don’t support the total cost, securing debt and equity becomes more challenging. Developers want to build, but the numbers have to work."

A closer look at the housing data reveals just how dramatically multifamily construction has shifted over the past three years.

Between 2023 and 2025, roughly 1.5 million units in buildings with five or more units were completed, an average of 499,000 per year, up 37.7% from 2020–22.

Those newly completed units boosted the total rental stock by 3.2%, driven by the multifamily starts boom of 2021–23, when ground was broken on more than 1.45 million units.

That surge in rental construction is now fading. In the third quarter of 2026, the multifamily pipeline shrank significantly across the board, with starts averaging 403,000, down more than 9% from a year ago amid soaring financing and construction costs. Units under construction similarly pulled back, averaging 674,000, marking a year-over-year decline of 2.7%.

Completions took the biggest hit, plunging 26% from a year ago to an annualized rate of 330,000 new rental units in the third quarter as the 2023–24 cooldown in housing starts worked its way through the pipeline.

"Given the year-over-year decline in units under construction and higher construction costs, we do not expect deliveries to rebound over the next 12 months," says Xu.

That outlook aligns with NAHB’s 2027 forecast, which predicts a further decline in multifamily starts.

"A thinner pipeline could create pressure in markets where demand continues to grow, but it doesn’t necessarily mean we’re headed for a severe shortage or another sharp increase in rents," says Calderon. "That will depend on how much existing inventory remains available, local employment and household growth, and what is already under construction."

At the regional level, multifamily completions fell year over year everywhere in the third quarter due to fewer starts in 2023–24. The Northeast saw the most dramatic pullback, with finished units dropping nearly 50% from 2025 levels.

"Thinner deliveries could further push up rents in Northeast metros, especially those that have already returned to year-over-year growth, such as Philadelphia and Pittsburgh," says Xu.

The South came in a distant second, with completions falling 28% year over year, followed by the Midwest (-17.9%) and the West (-14.2%).

At the same time, units under construction fell over 11% in the Northeast and 8.8% in the South from a year ago, but increased 4.5% in the West and 12.9% in the Midwest, pointing to a split in active pipelines.

Despite the Midwest's robust building activity, rents are on the rise in several major market metros, including Chicago (+3.2%), St. Louis (+3.2%), and Minneapolis (+2.0%).

When it comes to new groundbreakings, starts in the Northeast surged a staggering 48.4% year over year, making it the only region in positive territory.

“Because projects take about two years to complete in the Northeast, that rebound is unlikely to lift deliveries in 2027," warns Xu.

By contrast, starts fell 12.4% in the West, 16.5% in the South, and 29.2% in the Midwest.

"I remain optimistic about South Florida over the long term, while recognizing that each project needs to be evaluated on its own merits," says Calderon. "For us, that means looking closely at location, the renter we’re serving, competing supply, and the total cost of delivery."

For renters in Western markets such as Los Angeles and Denver, who have seen some relief in asking rents, the slowdown in new construction could reverse that trajectory going forward.

A separate study from RentCafe broadly dovetails with the Realtor.com findings, reinforcing the narrative that the supply of rental units is shrinking nationwide.

Based on data from Yardi Matrix, a commercial real estate intelligence platform that relies on a distinct methodology from Realtor.com, the report found that new apartment construction dropped 27% in 2026, with the Northeast standing out as the sole region to see building activity rise, led by New York City.

If You Only Make 1 Investing Move Before 2027, History Says It Should Be This

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

One simple investment could set your portfolio up for a strong 2027.

2026 is almost over. It's that time of year when many investors take a hard look at what they wish they had done earlier and what they should do differently going forward.

There are lots of things that you could do before 2027 arrives. But if you only make one investing move before the new year, history suggests that one in particular stands out: buying a low-cost S&P 500 (^GSPC -1.03%) index fund.

Why should you consider investing in a low-cost S&P 500 fund before 2027? There are three clear reasons.

First, the third year of a four-year presidential term historically delivers the highest returns. The table below shows the S&P 500's average gain since 1950 during each year of the presidential cycle:

Data source: Opening Bell Daily. Chart created by the author.

Why does the S&P 500 typically perform so well in the third year of a presidential term? One factor is that mid-term elections in year two often lead to divided governments in years three and four. Drastic changes (which many investors fear) are less likely when neither party controls both the executive and legislative branches.

Investing in the S&P 500 has consistently proven to be a smart long-term move. The index and its predecessors have never produced a negative total return over a rolling 20-year period. And over 10 years, the S&P 500 has delivered positive total returns 94% of the time.

This track record explains why legendary investor Warren Buffett instructed in his will that 90% of the cash his family inherits be put in a low-cost S&P 500 fund. He recommended that the other 10% be invested in short-term government bonds. Buffett wrote in his 2013 annual letter to Berkshire Hathaway (BRKA -1.02%) (BRKB -1.03%) shareholders that he believed that the results from following this approach "will be superior to those attained by most investors -- whether pension funds, institutions, or individuals -- who employ high-fee managers."

You might wonder if it makes sense to hold off on investing in an S&P 500 fund and wait for a more opportune time to buy. The problem with this is that trying to time the market can be costly.

JPMorgan Chase (JPM -2.44%) found that investors who missed the 10 best days of the stock market underperformed those who were fully invested by roughly 40%. What's more: seven of those 10 best days were within 15 days of the worst 10 days.

Technically, you can't buy the S&P 500 directly. However, several exchange-traded funds (ETFs) track the index. Two of the best alternatives are the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO -1.04%) and the State Street SPDR Portfolio S&P 500 ETF (SPYM -1.01%). Both of these ETFs offer low annual expense ratios. Both should be winners over the long run.

The order does not merely prohibit threats or harassment. A judicial order "forbidding certain communications when issued in advance of the time that such communications are to occur" is a prior restraint…. "Any system of prior restraints on communication bears a heavy presumption against its constitutional validity." Such restraints are unconstitutional "except in exceptional circumstances." .As the Supreme Court held in Organization for a Better Austin v. Keefe (1971), {the party seeking to suppress speech} "carries a heavy burden of showing justification for the imposition of such a restraint." …

The remedy for false or injurious statements lies in "actions under libel laws providing for redress and punishment, and not in proceedings to restrain the publication." [But t]he October 12 Order … does not impose consequences for particular statements adjudicated unlawful. It prohibits Mr. Hughes from speaking about entire categories of people in the future. That is a prior restraint in its most direct form which is presumed unconstitutional….

Test Masters Educ. Servs., Inc. v. Singh (5th Cir. 2005) … recognized that a court may enjoin "harassing communication" and "repeated invasions of privacy." But an injunction prohibiting all communication, rather than limiting itself to "threatening or harassing conduct," "swept too broadly" and constituted an unconstitutional restraint. Id.…

Plaintiffs cannot justify the restraint merely by characterizing Mr. Hughes's commentary as malicious, coercive, or injurious. The Supreme Court rejected that reasoning in Keefe. There, the Court held that an individual's "interest in being free from public criticism of his business practices" did not justify an injunction against speech…. Plaintiffs may have remedies for unlawful conduct. But those remedies do not include an order forbidding Mr. Hughes from speaking about them at all….

Defendant also argues (again correctly, I think) that the order was unconstitutional because defendant wasn't given an opportunity to be heard before the order was entered:

The absence of notice presents a separate constitutional defect. In Carroll v. President & Commissioners of Princess Anne (1968), the Supreme Court set aside a short ex parte order restraining expression because of "a basic infirmity in the procedure by which it was obtained." The order "was issued ex parte, without notice to petitioners and without any effort, however informal, to invite or permit their participation in the proceedings."

There is a place in our jurisprudence for ex parte issuance, without notice, of temporary restraining orders of short duration; There is no place within the area of basic freedoms guaranteed by the First Amendment for such orders where no showing is made that it is impossible to serve or to notify the opposing parties and to give them an opportunity to participate.

The Court also rejected the suggestion that a later hearing could cure the problem. The availability of a subsequent opportunity to be heard "does not overcome the infirmity in the absence of a showing of justification for the ex parte nature of the proceedings."

The reason is straightforward. "The value of judicial decision-making … is substantially diluted where the process is ex parte, because the Court does not have available the fundamental instrument for judicial judgment: "an adversary proceeding in which both parties may participate." And where First Amendment freedoms are at stake, any order must be "couched in the narrowest terms that will accomplish the pin-pointed objective."

The October 12 Order fails both principles. It was entered without notice or an adversary hearing. It contains no finding that notifying Mr. Hughes was impossible. And far from identifying a narrow category of unlawful speech, it prohibits virtually any online commentary concerning the Plaintiffs, their families, or their counsel.

Carroll is directly controlling on the constitutional defect. An ex parte order restraining expression cannot stand without a showing that notice and participation were impossible. No such showing appears here….

Sotheby's makes last-minute deal to sell $180m Van Gogh painting following dispute

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Blaquier family, from Argentina, bought Blossoming Chestnut Trees (Châtaigniers en fleurs) (May 1890) in 1975

Sotheby’s today announced that a settlement has been reached over the sale of Van Gogh’s Blossoming Chestnut Trees (Châtaigniers en fleurs) (May 1890). The work has been poised to achieve a record price for the artist but became the subject of a claim by the heirs of its Jewish former owners, who called for the work to be withdrawn from the 18 November auction in New York.

The Sotheby’s statement records that the auction house is “pleased to confirm that an agreement has been reached between the Blaquier-Arrieta family and all of the legal descendants of Hedwig and Samuel Fischer regarding the sale of Vincent van Gogh’s Châtaigniers en fleurs”. The sale will now proceed as planned.

The work is estimated at around $180m ($207m with fees) and has been guaranteed by Sotheby's to sell. This is far in excess of the previous record of $117m, paid at Christie’s in 2022 for Van Gogh’s Orchard with Cypresses (April 1888).

The Blaquier-Arrieta family, from Argentina, bought Blossoming Chestnut Trees in 1975. As the Sotheby’s statement explains: “In recognition of the painting’s complex Nazi-era history and the suffering endured by the Fischer family during the Holocaust, the two families have reached a mutually satisfactory resolution and the work will be sold pursuant to their agreement.”

The provenance in the Sotheby’s catalogue entry notes, as it has done since last month, that the painting was acquired by the Berlin publisher Samuel Fischer and his wife Hedwig in 1909. Their son-in-law, Gottfried Bermann Fischer, deposited the Van Gogh with the Amsterdam gallery Paul Cassirer in 1932 or 1933—and it was then bought by Paris collectors Henriette and Marcel Kapferer in 1936.

Last Thursday (12 November), in a surprise move, the Berlin-based restitution researcher Gunnar Schnabel sent a letter to Sotheby’s, on behalf of the Fischer heirs. He questioned the 1936 sale—which he says took place three months after the family had left Berlin for Vienna—asking Sotheby’s to prove that the picture had been sold at its market value and that its sale was not forced. In the meantime, Schnabel asked for this week’s sale to be delayed.

In a statement, the Fischer heirs commented: “We are grateful for the trusting and fair negotiation and collaboration with Sotheby’s and the Blaquier-Arrieta family, which make it possible to acknowledge the suffering of the Fischer family during the Nazi era and honour Gisela Berman Fischer's [granddaughter of Samuel and Hedwig] history of seeking justice for her family.”

The Blaquier-Arrieta family commented: “We are pleased to have reached an amicable resolution with the Fischer heirs. We appreciate the constructive dialogue that made this resolution possible and look forward to an extraordinary result and to passing along this masterpiece to a new generation of collectors.”

No details have been released on the nature of the agreement and whether the Fischer family will receive financial compensation.

What impact, if any, the short-lived dispute will have on the sale will only become apparent with the bidding.

The Hidden Financial Stress of Thru-Hiking

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

For better or worse, I was born with the fiscal sensibilities of a Depression-era grandmother. This can be both an asset and a liability for thru-hiking. Back in the day, my frugal spending habits made it easier to save enough money to comfortably thru-hike the Appalachian Trail, but they were also an unexpected handicap once I was actually on trail.

People rarely talk about how psychologically challenging it can be to live with the stress of spending without earning for the six-ish months that a long thru-hike usually takes, and I definitely wasn’t prepared for it. Money stress comes in many forms, but most people experience it one way or another — and that struggle can follow you out onto the trail.

In today’s challenging economy, it’s more important than ever for aspiring thru-hikers to consider not just whether they can afford to hike, but how they’ll cope with the psychological stress of watching their savings dwindle with each passing mile. It’s easy to take fiscal responsibility too far and get caught up in an unhelpful scarcity mindset. Don’t let that be you.

Hiking is hard. Don’t make it harder by beating yourself up for literally no reason.

Thru-hikers often talk about how easy it is to spend way too much money on zero days. For me, spending money in town was one of the hardest things about the trail. I’m not saying I didn’t spend — the lure of a soft bed and a hiker-sized restaurant meal was usually enough to overpower my frugality — but I agonized over every purchase in every single town. There were times when I even felt so guilty about the spending that I considered ending my hike early.

It wasn’t just that I was spending money without replacing it; it was that I wasn’t doing anything about it. I wasn’t applying for jobs or upskilling or preparing myself to reenter the job market. I was just existing in the woods day in and day out, being thoroughly unproductive, with no particular plan for after the trail. This was apparently too much for my little capitalist brain to handle.

This mindset would have been more reasonable if lack of funds was a serious threat to my thru-hiking goals. But I had been fortunate enough to save enough money that I didn’t need to worry about running out mid-hike. I spent half my time worrying about running out of money for literally no reason.

Me mentally berating myself for overspending on name-brand peanut butter, probably.

What is the solution? Should I have saved even more money ahead of time? Of course that wouldn’t hurt, but such is the nature of anxiety that I doubt it would have stopped me from stressing. Should I have taken out a Coursera subscription and spent my evenings in camp learning how to code, or treated every impromptu hiker gathering like a networking event?

Obviously not. In reality, there’s usually no easy solution to the assorted neuroses that play out in our heads. In my case, it was just a matter of learning to live with the uncertainty of not having an income or a guaranteed path back into paid work after the hike. I was afraid of not having enough or of being unable to find another job, and I just had to face that fear.

The AT forced me to confront my own limiting beliefs around money. In the years since that first thru-hike, I’ve gotten a little more relaxed about spending money on things I know I can afford without feeling guilty about it. I haven’t abandoned my frugal tendencies, but they don’t need to dictate every decision I make. I credit the trail for that growth.

“I wonder if I can get another 200 miles out of these shoes before I replace them. I wonder if I should get on a cheaper insurance plan next year. Do I have enough money to buy name-brand peanut butter in the next town? Should I get a different credit card? Should I go stoveless to save on fuel? “

I’ve written a few articles over the years about my peculiar hiking-related neuroses. As always, I want to clarify in the midst of all this doom and gloom that thru-hiking was and is one of the great joys of my life, despite my assorted pathologies and the mistakes I’ve made along the way.

Every thru-hiker will face struggles both external and internal; learning to deal with and overcome them is part of the experience, and this is just one more example. As always, do everything you can to prepare for your thru-hike, accept that you can’t plan for everything, and then enjoy the journey, hardships and all.

Many leaders decide alone which days staff come into the office. Fewer people quit when managers also ask when their workday should end

Some of the country's largest companies have spent the past few years ordering people back to their desks. At the end of 2024, Amazon $AMZN's chief executive, Andy Jassy, told corporate staff to come in five days a week. In the throes of the COVID-19 pandemic, Goldman Sachs $GS CEO David Solomon said working from home was "an aberration that we're going to correct as soon as possible." Many workers protested. But both men were determined to have employees working the same way they had before the pandemic.

Millions of office workers spent more than a year working from home. Many of them stopped working at the same hour every day and stopped answering messages late at night. Many more found meaning in their jobs. Once they were ordered back to the office, though, they had to decide whether they could maintain the same schedule without looking like they were slacking off.

Employees put up with return-to-office mandates if their manager accepts the schedule they built at home, according to Harvard Business School's Grace Mele-Cormier and colleagues. The problem is less about the time they have to come in and more about settling what time people can leave.

Mele-Cormier and his team found that employees tend to quit less often and earn slightly higher performance reviews when they're allowed to maintain the same working schedules as before. But many employees still fear that their boss will think they care less about the job if they go home at the same hour every day. They become less tense once they know their manager is on board, according to HBR's researchers.

The problem is that many managers never tell employees which hours they're expected to work. Their silence on the topic forces staff to assume they have to continue answering emails and messages at all hours of the day and night, just as they had done before the pandemic. Some employees end up leaving their roles for ones that have a clearer schedule.

Executives see the resignations, but they rarely learn that employees quit over their working hours. To executives, some teams seem to lose people for no reason while other teams don't.

Many managers never ask employees what time they need to leave each day. They assume people coming back to the office want one fixed schedule from their boss, with the same start and end time for everyone. If a manager asks each person what hours they need, the team ends up with different schedules for different people, and many managers worry they'll lose control of the team's day.

They also fear their staff will think the boss has no plan for how the team should work. Asking, though, actually leaves a manager with a better plan, according to Dan Cable, a London Business School professor. By hearing employees out, managers can spot the people most likely to quit over their working hours while there is still time to change their schedules. But Cable warns against giving employees decision-making powers. "You get a voice, not a vote," Cable told an audience at the school. "Listening shapes better decisions. It does not remove the leader's responsibility to decide."

Acting on what employees say doesn't always mean giving them what they asked for. That applies in almost every situation. After Jennifer Dulski and her leadership team laid off employees at Change.org, Dulski, then its president and chief operating officer, met with the remaining employees in several countries, according to the Society for Human Resource Management. She couldn't bring back the jobs the company cut. So her team started showing employees the company's finances instead. Once employees saw the numbers, Dulski said, they understood why their leaders had cut staff.

Other leaders change a plan or cover a cost after employees speak up. Geno Cutolo spent his first 90 days as president of staffing firm Adecco North America meeting employees across the business. He then redesigned a reorganization Adecco had already planned, to try to remove many of the frustrations and obstacles employees described to him. "It's important to act on what you hear," Cutolo said.

At Safeguard Global, the HR team heard that European employees were worried about winter heating bills, so the company paid them a monthly stipend through the winter, chief people officer Katherine Loranger said. In each case, employees could point to something that changed because they spoke up.

Employees expect the same proof after a manager asks about their hours. A manager can approve working hours, move team meetings to times everyone can make, or explain why a schedule can't change. If a manager asks and then changes and explains nothing, employees conclude the manager never had any intention to listen in the first place. Employees then stop telling managers what they need to know, and managers can't possibly tell which employees are thinking about leaving until they hand in their notice.

The Homeownership Gap Between Young and Old Americans Has Doubled—These Cities Reveal Why

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The homeownership gap between younger and older Americans has more than doubled over the past four decades, with some of the nation's most expensive cities seeing the sharpest increases, according to a new report.

The analysis, from the Urban Institute, finds that nationally just 43% of households headed by adults aged 25 to 35 owned their homes in 2024, compared with 79% of those headed by adults 65 and older. The 35.8-percentage-point difference is more than twice the 17.5-point gap recorded in 1980.

But that divide pales in comparison to the gaping chasm in some of America's most expensive metropolitan areas. Between 1980 and 2024, the homeownership gap grew by 29 percentage points in Denver, 23 points in New York City, and 22 points in San Francisco, compared with 18.3 points nationally.

But those widening gaps also expose a deeper economic trade-off: The cities offering young Americans the greatest opportunities to earn more may be the very places preventing them from building wealth through homeownership

"The cities with the strongest labor markets tend to be the least affordable, so some will take the better job and rent indefinitely, giving up equity accumulation," explains Hannah Jones, senior economist at Realtor.com®. "Others will pass up higher-paying roles or move to cheaper, lower-opportunity markets in order to buy."

In 1980, younger households in Denver were nearly as likely as their older counterparts to own a home.

At the time, 55% of households headed by adults aged 25 to 35 owned their homes, compared with 67% of households headed by adults 65 and older.

By 2024, the younger group's homeownership rate had fallen to just 37%, while the older group's rate climbed to 78%, expanding the divide from 12 to 41 percentage points.

But the decline in young homeownership may reflect something besides worsening affordability: Denver's success in attracting young workers.

"Two things are likely at work. One is the affordability decline itself. The other is Denver’s growth as an economic hub that appeals to young workers, many of whom arrive as renters," says Jones. "That adds renters to the mix and dilutes the young homeownership rate, even for people who never intended to buy."

There, younger households were already far less likely to own homes in 1980, when their homeownership rate stood at 28%. By 2024, it had slipped only slightly, to 26%.

Older New Yorkers, meanwhile, saw their homeownership rate climb from approximately 43% to 63% over the same period.

"In New York, homeownership has always been expensive and out of reach for most young households, so there was little room to fall," Jones explains. "Young homeownership slipped only slightly, which suggests the current rate is close to a floor."

She suggests low COVID-19 pandemic-era mortgage rates may have helped some older households that had been waiting to purchase finally enter the market.

And regardless of how the divide developed, entering homeownership later can carry lasting financial consequences.

Separate research from Realtor.com, co-authored by Jones, found that purchasing a home by age 30 was associated with 22.5% higher net worth—approximately $119,000 more—by age 50, compared with buying in one's 40s, even after accounting for income, education, and marital status.

Those advantages can extend across generations, too. Children raised in homeowner households were 18.4 percentage points more likely to become homeowners themselves by age 35, the same research found.

One of the Urban Institute's most striking findings concerns a milestone that comes before homeownership: establishing an independent household.

In 1980, young adults in high-cost metropolitan areas were slightly more likely than their counterparts nationally to head their own households. By 2024, that relationship had reversed.

Just 41% of young adults in expensive markets were household heads, the lowest share among the study's housing cost categories.

Instead, more are living with parents, other relatives, or roommates. Research from Realtor.com backs this up, finding that a third of young adults lived with their parents across the country in 2025.

Yet expensive cities haven't lost their appeal. The share of young households living in high-cost markets actually edged up from 28% in 1980 to 31% in 2024, partly reflecting a broader shift toward urban areas.

What's changed is their housing tenure. In those markets, the homeownership rate among younger households fell from 40% to 32% over the same period.

So young adults are continuing to pursue opportunities in expensive cities, but fewer are establishing households of their own once they get there. And another finding suggests that rapidly rising prices may be changing the age composition of metropolitan areas, regardless of how expensive those places were to begin with.

Between 2014 and 2024, metropolitan areas experiencing faster home price appreciation generally saw declines in young adults' share of the population, the Urban Institute found.

The relationship held across high-, moderate-, and low-cost markets. But Jones cautions that the findings don't necessarily mean young people are being priced out.

"That can come from young people leaving, older people moving in, or both," she says. "Florida metros on the included chart may be an example. Their older populations are large and grew significantly during the pandemic, which could erode the young share on its own."

Indeed, the relationship between home prices and demographic changes could run in either direction.

"Young people may be fleeing places where costs are climbing, or older, more financially equipped buyers may be moving in and competing for homes," Jones says. "That pushes prices up, and the newcomers then make up a larger share of the population."

Determining whether younger residents are actually leaving would require examining changes in the number of young adults, not just their share of the population.

Still, other economic research suggests that housing costs can constrain where workers pursue employment.

A May 2025 analysis from the Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond found that geographic mobility has declined across all age groups since the 1990s. Among the contributing factors, researchers identified restrictions on housing supply in economically productive cities, where high costs can discourage workers from relocating to pursue better opportunities.

The researchers also identified another potential obstacle: Older homeowners have become less responsive to rising local housing costs, making them more likely to stay put even as prices climb. In supply-constrained markets, that can make it harder for newcomers to move in.

Meanwhile, a February 2026 analysis from the St. Louis Fed found that home prices had risen approximately 207% from 2000 to 2024, outpacing the roughly 155% increase in per-capita incomes.

Together, the findings suggest that the costs of entering high-opportunity housing markets could affect younger Americans' earnings prospects as well as their ability to build wealth.

There may be a way around that trade-off for some.

"Remote work may be loosening the link between where people live and where the jobs are, which could enable more households to achieve homeownership without giving up job opportunities," Jones says.

But that flexibility is not available to everyone. Workers whose jobs require them to be physically present may face a more difficult choice between buying a home where they can afford one and pursuing careers in the cities where their skills command the highest wages.

And unlike the wealth accumulated by longtime homeowners, the career opportunities they pass up may be difficult to recover later.

The latest product from the tobacco industry is a flavorful nicotine tablet that melts in your mouth, allowing users to get a buzz as discreetly as popping a mint — and amping up concerns that products to replace cigarettes are too appealing to kids and teens.

In the United Kingdom and Switzerland, Philip Morris International recently introduced Zyn Melts, mint-flavored tablets that provide a nicotine hit as quickly as smoking a cigarette. Japan Tobacco International launched a similar product called Perlz in the U.K. in May.

And in the U.S., Nic Nac Naturals, a nascent nicotine company based in Spokane, Wash., has been selling flavored lozenges called Nic Nac since 2022; they’re now available in about 3,000 stores nationwide.

Users tuck these tablets next to their gums, similar to how they use nicotine pouches. Like pouches, the new products come in colorful packaging and flavors such as mint and berry. But with tablets, there’s no wet sachet to discard.

That makes them more appealing than pouches, said Erwin Henriquez, a global analyst for the marketing research firm Euromonitor. “It’s much more friendly to chew gum or a dissolving tablet than to put something in your gums,” he said. “You have vitamins and supplements that consumers are already used to.”

Public health advocates and researchers are concerned about nicotine tablets for a similar reason. Their resemblance to mints or lozenges “may make them appear harmless and more appealing to children and adolescents,” said Magnus Lundbäck, a cardiologist, nicotine researcher and professor at the Karolinska Institutet in Stockholm.

Tobacco companies have introduced e-cigarettes, heated tobacco, and nicotine pouches as less harmful alternatives to cigarettes. Tobacco-related diseases are the leading preventable cause of death worldwide, responsible for 7 million deaths a year.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration, which influences tobacco policy worldwide, has authorized these products because they’re less harmful than cigarettes and could help smokers quit.

But nicotine is tied to health problems even when it’s not inhaled through smoke, and the long-term health risks of these new products aren’t clear. Public health advocates say there’s little evidence that smokers are switching to pouches and heated tobacco entirely. More often, they say, people use those products in addition to cigarettes.

“The tobacco and nicotine industry is continuously developing new products that may attract new generations of users, rather than reducing nicotine dependence,” Lundbäck said. “The ultimate public health goal must be fewer people addicted to nicotine, not simply more ways to consume it.”

Henriquez, the Euromonitor analyst, said dissolvable tablets are a “natural evolution” of nicotine pouches and serve consumers’ growing appetite for stimulants as they “feel stress all the time.”

Nicotine pouch sales reached $9.2 billion globally in 2025, according to Euromonitor. The volume of pouches sold has grown 50% annually for the past five years and is expected to continue at 20.5% a year, according to a July report by Henriquez.

On its U.K. site, Philip Morris International tells consumers that Zyn Melts “offer a completely new way for adult users to enjoy tobacco-free and smoke-free nicotine moments on the go.” It compares tablets to pouches, saying tablets are for a “fast nicotine experience” while pouches have a “longer lasting nicotine and flavor experience.”

Japan Tobacco sells Perlz through Flavour Warehouse, a U.K. vaping company it controls. Its website states, “First came vapes. Then pouches. Now there’s Perlz — the ingenious new nicotine experience for existing adult nicotine users.”

Tablets contain less nicotine than most pouches, with Zyn Melts and Perlz available in 1 and 2 milligram doses.

Nic Nac lozenges are available in 3 and 6 mg doses. Nic Nac Naturals co-founder and Chief Executive Officer Nicco Magnotto said the lozenges are different from Zyn Melts and Perlz because they release nicotine over about 30 minutes, more like a pouch.

The most popular nicotine pouches, in comparison, range from 3 to 17 mg. Philip Morris International recently introduced a 1.5 mg pouch for users “new to nicotine pouches.”

Public health officials have been engaged in a yearslong balancing act with new nicotine products, weighing the potential benefits to adults looking for less harmful alternatives to cigarettes against the potential for youth to abuse or accidentally ingest nicotine.

Vapes exploded in popularity in 2018, causing the FDA to declare teen use an epidemic. Then nicotine pouches caught on, with surveys showing increased use among teens over the past few years. The industry, however, says the percentage of youth using pouches remains low.

Meanwhile, poison control centers in the U.S. reported that calls involving nicotine poisoning, many involving pouches, nearly doubled from 2024 to 2025. Three-quarters of cases last year involved children under the age of 6.

Nicotine tablets represent a new area of concern.

The industry says it’s not looking to sell to kids. In an email, a Japan Tobacco spokesperson said the company does not “design, market or promote Perlz to appeal” to minors and that the containers are child-resistant, requiring three buttons to be pressed simultaneously to be opened.

“We take steps to ensure that our advertising is directed towards adult tobacco and nicotine users only and does not have particular appeal to minors,” the spokesperson said.

Magnotto said his company does not advertise or market Nic Nac to youth and that the company’s research indicates they don’t find lozenges appealing. “It comes across as medicinal,” he said. “Pouches are the rage. Kids aren’t even aware of us.”

Asked about the potential for abuse by kids, Magnotto said his company’s container has a scientifically tested, child-resistent seal and will soon have a more secure mechanism.

Phillip Morris International did not respond to a request for comment. The company says on its U.K. website that Melts are meant for adults.

The U.K. doesn’t require regulatory approval for nicotine products, making it an ideal test market. The tablets were introduced shortly before regulations imposed by the Tobacco and Vapes Act 2026 became effective in October. That law imposes stricter rules on tobacco and nicotine products, including prohibitions on giveaways and big discounts.

Philip Morris International is looking beyond the U.K. and Switzerland. It has filed for trademark protections for Zyn Melts in the U.S. and Europe, both as a consumer nicotine product and a pharmaceutical aid to stop smoking.

The trademarks are among many the company has registered this year for Zyn, including “House of Zyn” and “Zyn Club” — signs that the company is positioning Zyn as a lifestyle brand.

Nicotine tablets have been available in the U.S. for years as smoking cessation aids, which requires a special FDA approval as a pharmaceutical product. The agency would need to do a separate review in order to authorize the sale of tablets to consumers.

Nic Nac Naturals submitted an application in 2023 to sell its nine mint and fruit-flavored tablets to the general public in the U.S.

Mitch Zeller, former director of the FDA’s Center for Tobacco Products, said nicotine tablets “could possibly be helpful” to smokers looking for alternatives in a “properly regulated market.”

First, he said, the product needs to undergo a complete scientific review, with studies showing how different users respond to it. He noted that a new FDA rule allows nicotine products to be sold while their application is pending, which means Zyn Melts and Perlz could be on shelves without any research on their appeal to adolescents.

That’s not how it should work, he said: “All of the intended and unintended consequences of something like this need to be sorted out on a pre-market basis.”

“We took a wonderful tour today and it was really impressive and really touching,” Kennedy said in a video about the visit.

The Trump administration has repeatedly emphasized the promise of faith-based addiction recovery programs, including in its 2026 National Drug Control Strategy. The Great American Recovery Initiative, co-chaired by Kennedy and Kathryn Burgum, extols the power of faith. And in late August, Kennedy tapped Monty Burks, who leads the Center for Faith at the Department of Health and Human Services, as interim executive director of the initiative. Like Kennedy and Burgum, Burks is in recovery from addiction.

Treatment and policy experts largely agree that faith communities — and a belief in something bigger than oneself — can play an important role in a person’s recovery. But some are concerned that federal grants will be directed to faith-based initiatives that don’t provide evidence-based treatment.

Sheepgate, for example, explicitly prohibits those who join its yearlong programs from using methadone, a drug approved by the Food and Drug Administration for opioid use disorder. They can only use another addiction medication, Suboxone, if they’ll be fully tapered off of it within the first three months, according to the center’s student guidelines — a quick timeline that doctors generally recommend against. It’s not alone: Research has found that faith-based treatment centers are less likely than secular ones to offer medications for opioid use disorder, and more likely to refuse clients who may be taking them.

Refusing to accept people on FDA-approved medications “is so irresponsible,” said Ayana Jordan, an addiction psychiatrist at NYU Langone who works with faith organizations on recovery efforts. “I would not want to provide federal funds to that type of recovery center because they’re actively working against things that we know can save lives.”

But Michael Hunsberger, Sheepgate’s regional president, said the issue isn’t so black and white. Sheepgate would accept people taking addiction medications, he told STAT, if the center received more funding and was able to build out the medical staff. He argued that faith-based organizations are often excluded from public funds regardless of the level of care they provide.

“We could literally allow multiple of those prescriptions and bring on different medical staff and still be excluded from certain dollars due to our faith-based beliefs,” Hunsberger said.

Medications for opioid use disorder are just a few on a long list of drugs that Sheepgate doesn’t have the capacity to distribute or monitor the use of, Hunsberger said. When somebody isn’t a good fit for Sheepgate due to any medication use, the staff tries to find another organization where they could seek help.

While the Trump administration has continued to broadly support medications for substance use disorder — which can be highly effective at preventing overdose deaths — Kennedy has repeatedly cast doubt on existing systems for treating addiction. He’s also expressed support for unconventional (and scientifically dubious) approaches to treatment, including “wellness farms” where people reside in nature and engage in manual labor. Kennedy’s own path to long-term recovery from drugs and alcohol leaned strongly on groups like Alcoholics Anonymous and his Catholic faith.

But what worked for Kennedy won’t work for everyone, experts say. As Trump’s tax-cut bill goes into effect and more people lose health coverage, “what really concerns me is that there seems to be an expectation from the Trump administration that these faith-based groups are going to fill those gaps,” said Libby Jones, associate vice president of the Global Health Advocacy Incubator’s Overdose Prevention Initiative.

The administration has not made official regulatory changes to the federal grant process, but it has gestured that the bar may be lowered for faith-based groups, Jones said. A “Dear Colleague” letter from the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration stressed that faith-based groups should receive “equal treatment” in the grants process. The Justice Department has deemed unconstitutional Biden administration regulations that aimed to prohibit the use of federal funds for “explicitly religious activities” and protect people from discrimination on the basis of faith.

Experts believe there should be clear stipulations that faith-based organizations receiving federal funding follow evidence-based care, and that federal agencies should ensure that funding goes to a diverse array of religious groups, not only to Christian organizations.

“I am always heartened to know that there is money that is being put into faith-based institutions,” Jordan said. “But we have an obligation to taxpayers — this is taxpayers’ money — to say what actually works.”

Sheepgate would welcome any oversight and regulations that may come with federal funding, Hunsberger said, as long as it does not interfere with the organization’s core identity.

“If those federal dollars change our identity as a faith-based organization, then that would change us accepting those dollars,” Hunsberger said. But, “the faith community — the majority of it — is not against the medical side. It’s just, a lot of times, we’re pitted against each other.”

The Department of Health and Human Services did not respond directly to STAT’s questions about how it may assess the efficacy of faith-based recovery organizations for future funding opportunities. “For generations, these organizations have provided hope, accountability, mentorship, housing, workforce opportunities, and the relationships that help people sustain long-term recovery, often with limited resources and little government support,” HHS deputy press secretary Lizzie Harvey said in a statement.

This year, HHS has given supplemental funding awards of $500,000 each to Alabama, Kansas, and West Virginia to pursue faith-based prevention work. Kennedy has also said that SAMHSA’s STREETS grants — which send $12 million over four years to communities in support of work on the intersection of homelessness and addiction — will also have a “new emphasis on faith-based recovery,” according to the Iowa Capitol Dispatch.

Both of those grant notices included language directing recipients to employ evidence-based practices. But another “Dear Colleague” letter written by SAMHSA leadership earlier this year warned against relying on medications alone for addiction treatment, so as to avoid what it calls “a default sentence to life-long medication use.” That guidance, which has now been baked into SAMHSA’s terms and conditions for all grant recipients, created some confusion about the agency’s support of FDA-approved medications.

“There’s an ambiguity here that just needs to be clearer,” Jordan said, adding that the administration appears to be focusing largely on white, Christian organizations.

“The methods by which we define ‘faith-based’ and ‘evidence-based treatment’ need to be clear,” she said.

In the past four months, Kennedy and other HHS leaders have visited or hosted roundtables with at least half a dozen other Christian recovery centers. It’s unclear how many encourage or allow the use of FDA-approved medications for substance use disorders.

Without strict guidance from federal agencies, funding decisions may be left to states or more local jurisdictions. The Montgomery Alcohol, Drug Addiction and Mental Health Services (ADAMHS) Board, a county-level authority in Ohio, was tapped last month for one of eight federal STREETS grants. For years, the board has funded and coordinated with both faith-based and secular recovery homes, a housing agency, behavioral health facility, street outreach and medicine teams, and peer-support services.

The Montgomery ADAMHS board only funds recovery homes that are certified with the state, the board’s housing coordinator, Kaylyn Drodge, told STAT. Astronaut Loads 35mm Film Into Analog Camera While in Space

German astronaut Rabea Rogge flew on the Fram2 spaceflight mission in April 2025, bringing her trusty second-hand analog camera.

Rogge is an Arctic roboticist, currently studying for a PhD at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology in the Department of Marine Technology. She is also Germany’s first-ever female astronaut.

Last week, Rogge shared a video on Instagram as she loaded up a roll of 35mm film while onboard the SpaceX Crew Dragon Resilience spacecraft.

While many celluloid shooters have encountered difficulties loading film on terra firma, Rogge deftly loaded a roll of Kodak Portra 160 into the camera that she calls a “sturdy companion.”

A post shared by Rabea Rogge (@rabearogge)

“Analog pictures from space,” Rogge captions the post. “My old second-hand analog camera has been a sturdy companion on all my expeditions from under -20°C [-4°F] in the high Arctic to the humid climate at sea around the equator. On Fram2 it got to launch on a rocket as well – and it still lives on so far!”

PetaPixel identified Rogge’s camera as a Minolta X-700 with a 45mm lens attached. The X-700 was the flagship SLR for the now-defunct Japanese company for the best part of 20 years. In 1982, it won the European “Camera of the Year” award, and it is still popular in the second-hand market today.

With the film loaded in the camera, Rogge was afforded stunning views of Earth thanks to the Crew Dragon’s 360-degree cupola. At 2,000 square inches, it was the largest single window ever flown to space. Rogge, along with her crewmates Chun Wang, Jannicke Mikkelsen, and Eric Philips, were also the first people to ever orbit over Earth’s north and south poles.

“Can’t imagine the face of the people from the film lab when they scanned your roll,” comments Thibaut Everaerts on Instagram.

Rogge’s camera is far from the first Minolta to ever fly from space; in fact, the first American to ever orbit the Earth, John Glenn, took a Minolta Hi-Matic (repackaged as an Ansco Autoset) with him into space.

Despite buying the automatic camera for just $40 from a drugstore in Cocoa Beach, Florida, NASA engineers modified the camera with a pistol grip so that Glenn could use the camera while wearing his bulky astronaut gloves.

The photos that Glenn took in 1962 and their resulting popularity are credited with helping NASA realize how important it was to document Earth from space.
