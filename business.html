The market price at the end of each year is directly significant because it affects the dollar balance of the IRA, which affects the RMD amount to be taken during the following year.

As I mentioned in my first post to this topic, the approach will not last indefinitely. If I live long enough, for some future years, the RMD as a percentage of the balance at the end of the previous year will be greater than the distribution yield relative to the market price at the end of the previous year.

Indonesian Cinema’s Next Chapter Is Reaching the World ‘Without Losing Our Cultural Identity,’ Says JAFF Market Director Linda Gozali

📅 Nov 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Indonesia’s film industry has momentum at home. JAFF Market director Linda Gozali wants to help it travel.

Gozali runs the industry market attached to the Jogja-Netpac Asian Film Festival (JAFF), now in its third edition. The 2024 launch tested whether Indonesia needed a film market at all, and the industry’s answer was an emphatic yes. The 2025 edition opened the door to regional and international partners. This year, the market is taking stock of how well the business holds up against economic turmoil and political instability around the world, while widening the scope for credible partnerships at home and abroad. Related Stories Qatar’s Film Committee and Watermelon Pictures Forge Partnership to Co-Produce a Slate of Films From the 'Global South' Oscar-Nominated Director Rachid Bouchareb on the Challenges of Shooting ‘Reggane’: ‘We’re Making a Period Film in the Depths of the Sahara’

“Our next chapter is learning how to reach international audiences without losing our cultural identity,” Gozali tells Variety. “We aspire to become nationally indispensable, regionally relevant and internationally connected.”

Local hits have yet to translate into comparable business abroad, which Gozali puts down to producers and filmmakers treating the home market as their first priority. “Indonesia has learned how to win its own audiences,” she says, adding that plenty of untapped local viewers still need to be brought in.

Even so, Gozali says, a good share of Indonesian horror has sold across multiple territories, and independent films have been well received on the international festival circuit. She also notes that Indonesia is not alone in looking inward first, pointing to other populous countries such as China, India and Japan.

“Any growth should not be mistaken for maturity,” she says. “JAFF Market’s responsibility is to transform that growth into sustainability.”

That thinking draws on an industry report JAFF Market produced with Cinepoint. According to Gozali, it points to a basic mismatch between Indonesia’s volume of content production and appetite for investment on one side, and its limited exhibition infrastructure on the other.

“Sustainable growth requires wider audience access, diversified distribution access, responsible production investment and stronger business intelligence,” Gozali says. “Our collaboration with Cinepoint is intended to encourage evidence-based industry decisions.”

The market can already point to results. Projects that have met collaborators for their next steps include “Pangku” and “My Mother” from JAFF Future Project (JFP), and “Tikam Samurai,” “Bandits of Batavia,” “World Without Sleep,” “Timun Mas” and “Gloomy Sunday” from the JAFF IP Connection (JiPCon) strand. Joint ventures, memorandums of understanding and letters of intent have also emerged around “Khadam” and “Badut Gendong,” along with an agreement between JAFF Market and the Jakarta government.

Gozali singles out “Slaughterground” as the clearest example of the market linking things up over time. Presented as an IP at JiPCon during the 2024 inaugural edition, it returns this year as a film in JAFF Future Project. “The IP met its destiny in JAFF Market,” she says.

The deals, she argues, have created value beyond the event itself. But she sees the market’s main achievement as bringing the whole of Indonesia’s film industry under one roof for the first time.

JAFF Market shares the regional calendar with Busan’s Asian Contents & Film Market (ACFM), Hong Kong FilMart and the Taiwan Creative Content Fest. “We are the new kids on the block so we want to work alongside the other established markets and learn and partner with them in areas that will strengthen our programs and also theirs,” Gozali says.

Every market hopes to become a long-term international hub, she adds, but JAFF Market will take it one step at a time. Some ties are already in place. The market continues to collaborate with the Adelaide Film Festival, an institutional partnership Gozali says brings continuity, credibility and a route into established international networks. It also runs a reciprocal IP exchange with ACFM.

To bring in international sales agents, distributors, financiers and streamers, the market relies on curated programs, business dialogues and matchmaking designed to move attendees from general discovery toward focused meetings with decision-makers. “We recognize that international participation must be driven by business opportunities, not simply invitations,” Gozali says. “The quality and relevance of international participation matter as much as the number of delegates or countries represented.”

“As a producer myself, I recognize that one of our industry’s gaps (besides a shortage of stories or talent), is a shortage of sustained cross-border producer relationships,” she says of the market’s Producers Exchange strand, which aims to build trust, encourage knowledge sharing and help producers find compatible partners beyond one-off projects. “Connecting the right producers today can shape the regional production and distribution landscape of tomorrow.”

For newcomers, JAFF Future Project, Film Labs and Talent Day offer a way into the Indonesian industry. Gozali says the insights gathered across the market’s programs help first-time filmmakers find their way and have shaped how they handle business with international partners.

International co-productions open up additional financing, creative expertise, shared resources, distribution networks and new audiences. Indonesia, however, would benefit from clearer regulatory frameworks, production incentives and tax rebate mechanisms. “It is important to ensure that Indonesian filmmakers are ready to become equal creative and business partners,” Gozali says.

JAFF Market is backed by Amar Bank, Indonesia’s Ministry of Culture and Ministry of Creative Economy, Jakarta City and a roster of sponsors and partners. Gozali is grateful for that backing but says the platform needs long-term, sustained support in both funding and infrastructure to keep serving the industry.

“JAFF Market must remain commercially responsible without becoming commercially exclusive,” she says. Diversified revenue streams, strategic partnerships and curated programs are meant to keep the market open to independent filmmakers and emerging production companies. “Supporting emerging talent is not simply a philanthropic endeavor; it is an investment in the industry’s future creative and commercial potential,” she adds.

As for the next five years, “Growth is not necessarily about becoming bigger,” Gozali says. “It is about becoming more relevant, useful and consequential.”

India uses $1.62 billion in Nepal aid to lock in regional influence—and perhaps ward off China, analysts say

📅 Nov 28, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

India announced a $1.62 billion economic package for flood-hit Nepal on Nov. 23, pairing reconstruction grants with a credit line for energy projects, the latest move in a decades-old contest with China over the Himalayan country’s future.

The floods that hit Nepal in late August were among the worst disasters in the country’s recorded history, killing at least 1,300 people. An estimated 84,270 people, including over 32,000 children, were affected by the floods, according to UNICEF.

“It has been three months and families are still working hard to rebuild, but efforts are hampered by the loss of assets and livelihoods they depend on,” Andrea Berardo, the World Food Programme’s deputy country director in Nepal, said in a Nov. 24 statement. “With winter approaching in Nepal’s highlands, many families are still living in temporary shelters and are facing increasingly harsh conditions.”

The money is about more than rebuilding damaged roads and homes. India—already Nepal’s largest trading partner—is using disaster aid to reinforce its ties with a neighbor that China has courted for years, and to keep Nepal’s economic future closely linked to its own, analysts say.

India’s package to Nepal includes a $377 million grant for post-disaster reconstruction and new infrastructure projects, along with a $1.24 billion line of credit for energy projects, according to a joint statement by the two countries.

Nepal is likely to use the grant for reconstruction and the credit line—which carries preferential terms and sourcing requirements from India—for infrastructure, notes Constantino Xavier, a nonresident fellow at the Brookings Institution’s foreign policy program. The credit line could also foster cross-border supply chains and support Nepal’s industrialization and manufacturing ambitions, which are essential to creating jobs in one of Asia’s youngest countries.

The announcement followed talks in New Delhi between Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi and Nepalese Prime Minister Balendra Shah. It was the first official bilateral trip abroad for the 36-year-old rapper-turned-politician since he took office in March, after 2025’s youth-led protests against corruption.

“Our commitment will be to help affected families get their lives back on track, create new opportunities and further strengthen Nepal’s capacity to deal with future disasters,” Modi said to reporters.

Shah thanked India for consistently acting as a first responder to Nepal during “times of natural disasters and difficult hours”, and invited Modi to visit Nepal. India was the first foreign government to respond to the August floods, sending relief supplies and helping restore communications.

A Post-Disaster Needs Assessment released this month by Nepal’s National Planning Commission estimates recovery and reconstruction will cost 706.65 billion Nepali rupees ($5.2 billion)—more than 10% of Nepal’s 2025 GDP of $45.49 billion, according to the World Bank.

India and Nepal have maintained close economic, diplomatic and security ties since they signed the 1950 Treaty of Peace and Friendship; bilateral trade reached $9.38 billion in the 2025–26 fiscal year.

After a 2015 earthquake in Nepal that killed more than 8,000 people, India donated $1 billion for post-earthquake reconstruction. Yet five months later, many Nepalis blamed India for a fuel shortage, accusing New Delhi of imposing an undeclared blockade after Nepal adopted a new constitution. India denied responsibility, saying protests on the Nepali side of the border were blocking its trucks.

India has long used money to play a central role in South Asia. It provided nearly $4 billion in aid to Sri Lanka in 2022, as the tourism-dependent island nation faced its worst economic crisis in more than seven decades.

“It reflects India’s growing geoeconomic engagement with its neighbors, especially in times of crises, as also during Sri Lanka’s 2022 financial default,” Xavier says of the Nepal pledge.

There may also be a geopolitical motivation behind India’s generosity. Kunda Dixit, former editor of the Nepali Times, told Reuters that India “may be trying to ensure that [Shah] does not lean too much towards China.”

China and Nepal share an 878-mile border across the Himalayas. China accounts for nearly half of Nepal’s foreign direct investment and is the country’s second-largest trading partner, though trade is heavily skewed in Beijing’s favor.

Beijing has also identified 10 projects for potential joint implementation under its Belt and Road Initiative, including a proposed railway linking Kathmandu with Tibet, road corridors and a cross-border electricity transmission line. China also responded to the floods by providing cash and relief supplies, and sending more than 20 rescue and forensic experts.

In Nepal, India disbursed more than four times as much aid as China in the 2024–25 fiscal year, according to Nepali government data cited by Reuters.

“I don’t think China will launch a checkbook competition with India in Nepal,” Yun Sun, a senior fellow and co-director of the China Program at the Stimson Center, says. “Influence and presence are measured by long-term, consistent involvement, not a one-time deal. The region would benefit from contributions from all great powers, instead of a single package.”

“Many of the smaller South Asian states, including Nepal, rely substantively on India for development and humanitarian assistance,” Michael Kugelman, a resident senior fellow for South Asia at the Atlantic Council, says. “India’s new aid package is the latest reminder that even with China deepening its footprint in South Asia, New Delhi continues to leverage reliance on its largesse to ensure ties with its neighbors—or at least most of them—remain in a good place.”

To complicate matters even further, three members of the PTT Youngin$ show up to settle some unfinished business with Raph.

You'll ultimately have to decide whether to kill or spare Raph himself as well as the gang members, and your decision will have a significant impact on how much money you walk away with.

After talking Shayna down and convincing her not to shoot Raph, three members of the PTT Youngin$ will enter the house and shoot him themselves, meaning your efforts to spare him are ultimately in vain. At this point, Lucia can either attempt to de-escalate the situation or pull out her weapon and kill all three gang members.

Killing the PTT Youngin$ will net you the largest possible reward for completing the mission. Shayna will split the gang's bag of drugs with Lucia, whose share is worth approximately $5,000 when sold to a fence. If you also looted Raph's house while he and Shayna were arguing, you can walk away with roughly $6,500 worth of goods. You'll want to get out of the area quickly afterward to avoid dealing with the police.

If you convince Shayna to spare Raph and subsequently talk down the PTT Youngin$, you'll be able to complete the mission without Lucia having to fire a single shot. Unfortunately, there's no way to actually save Raph, as the gang members will shoot him regardless of your attempts to defuse the situation with Shayna.

If you manage to convince the gang members to leave peacefully, they'll leave behind a smaller quantity of drugs that Lucia can sell to a fence for approximately $1,200. You should talk Shayna out of killing Raph but kill the PTT Youngin$ when they show up. If you tell Shayna to shoot Raph herself, she'll kill him immediately, cutting the confrontation short and eliminating your opportunity to talk things through. Considering Raph is going to die regardless of your decision, there's little incentive to encourage Shayna to pull the trigger.

Further, killing the PTT Youngin$ instead of negotiating with them will earn you an additional $3,800 worth of drugs, which is a pretty substantial difference for such a short side mission. Assuming you've also looted all the valuables throughout Raph's house, you'll walk away with approximately $6,500 worth of goods to sell to a fence, making this a more lucrative choice in this optional encounter in GTA 6.

He founded Forka Films, formerly Fourcolours Films, in 2001, and his producing credits include Kamila Andini’s “Yuni” and “Before, Now & Then,” as well as her latest, “Four Seasons in Java,” which played Toronto’s Centerpiece program and Busan this year.

“I believe countries need to recognize cinema as an important part of their cultural strategy, not merely as an industry or a source of national prestige,” he says. “This requires long-term investment, institutional support, and a commitment to building sustainable connections with the international film community.”

JAFF’s 21st edition drew more than 1,000 submissions, from which the festival picked 254 films from 34 countries. The program runs under the theme “Influx,” opening with Lawrence Fajardo’s Filipino drama “Silenced Nights” and closing with Ueda Makoto’s Japanese comedy-drama “You Are the Film.”

Isfansyah says the current social, political and economic climate pushed the team to rethink what staging a festival should mean this year. “We did not want JAFF to become a celebration disconnected from the realities around us. This is not a moment for an excessively festive atmosphere or for pretending that everything is fine.”

Fajardo’s film, he says, speaks directly to present-day anxieties. Opening with it was meant to signal that “cinema must remain connected to the world we live in, and a festival should not be afraid to confront difficult realities.”

The closer is pitched partly at younger viewers, as a reminder of where JAFF came from. “JAFF began with a love for films like this, films that may not necessarily be grand in scale or production, but possess sincerity, imagination, and a strong cinematic voice,” Isfansyah says. “I think it is important to remember that a film does not have to be big to be powerful.”

Among the submissions, he noticed filmmakers growing bolder, especially in shorts, with more of them playing with form and blurring fiction and nonfiction. “Short films are increasingly becoming spaces for creative risk-taking,” he says, rather than mere stepping stones to features.

Many films were also more political, though not always in the sense of tackling institutions or specific events. “There is a growing urgency to tell stories that respond to the realities surrounding us.”

The “Influx” theme also nods to change across Asian cinema, from a new generation pushing against established storytelling to shifts in how films get financed and reach audiences. “However, I think it is important to look beyond the idea of change as simply a matter of technological or industrial transformation. We should also ask where these currents are taking us, whose voices are shaping the direction of Asian cinema, and what kind of future we are building together,” Isfansyah says.

On Indonesia’s local box office boom, he sees no hard line between the country’s commercial, arthouse, independent and genre directors. “Many filmmakers know one another, compete in a healthy way, exchange ideas, and often support each other’s work,” he says. Commercial hits, he adds, pull more people into cinemas, lift interest in local titles and draw investment, while challenging independent work keeps the industry diverse and credible.

Premieres matter, but they have never been JAFF’s main yardstick. What counts is whether a film merits a slot and whether it has already had a fair shot at the festival’s audience. “If we believe a film has not yet had the opportunity to reach JAFF’s viewers, we will be interested in bringing it here, regardless of whether it has already premiered elsewhere.”

As it enters its third decade, the festival wants to put back into the ecosystem that built it. This year it launched JAFF Kids, aimed at children and young audiences, and JAFF Awards, which honors individuals, institutions and initiatives in Indonesia and the wider region that have helped develop film culture. Earlier programs JAFF Community Forum and JAFF Market grew strong enough to become parallel events in their own right.

However large JAFF gets, Isfansyah says it has to keep resting on emerging talent, industry professionals and academics, and keep all of them talking to one another.

“For me, being community-driven does not mean remaining small. It means remembering that the festival exists because of the people who build it, participate in it, and give it meaning.”

The safest way to chase an adrenaline rush is to read a captivating thriller book. The Oscar-winning Conclave was a surprising front-runner in a year dominated by flashier films like Anora and The Brutalist, and its source material is just as disarmingly good. The premise of a papal conclave held right after the pope’s death might feel a bit too somber, but Conclave unfolds like a tense political thriller that balances breakneck pacing with intricate detail. Mystic River is a book that delves beneath the surface, compelling us to understand how grief carries on into adulthood. There’s immense thematic weight to this book about the tussle between choice and fatalism in the face of injustice.

Many people recommend a15% savings rate with no mathematical justification. Suppose you have stable real income, 30 years until retirement, and need income for 20 years after retirement. Under a 6% real certainty equivalent discount rate, the present value of your lifetime earnings is 13.8 years of current income. The present value of your retirement needs is 2 years of income, or 15% of the present value of your earnings. So a 15% savings rate superficially makes sense.

The 5 Greatest Bands of the British Invasion, Ranked

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Throughout the 1960s, there was a huge influx of British rock and pop music into American culture. English bands were dominating the charts and the music scene was becoming more globalized than ever before. The overseas relationship was reciprocal – these British bands were reworking American blues and R&B within a rock format, infusing psychedelic and progressive rock. Concurrently, American rock music began to emulate the British sound. This cultural phenomenon changed the face of music forever.

The British Invasion is an interesting and necessary study in the way music reinvents itself – how every musician is influenced by those who came before. This time period introduced this wonderful merging of sound to an international audience, causing a dizzyingly steep learning curve that produced some of the greatest music of all time. Here are the top five most important bands of the British Invasion.

The Animals formed in 1962 in Newcastle upon Tyne. They drew inspiration from American rhythm and blues artists, getting their start with covers by iconic musicians such as Jimmy Reed and Nina Simone. They had a big hand in the genesis of folk rock with their signature song: a cover of the American folk classic “House of the Rising Sun.” Their version is raw and emotional, capturing the struggles of working-class Americans during this time. The band represented a darker and grittier side of the British Invasion canon, the opposite end of the spectrum from the British pop that was also making its way overseas.

In the latter half of the sixties, they changed their lineup and their name to Eric Burden and The Animals. During this time, they delved into the psychedelic rock space, departing from their earlier blues influences. They laid the groundwork for prog rock, influenced punk/ garage music, and inspired artists across many genres (including Bruce Springsteen, The Grateful Dead, and Rod Stewart, to name a few.)

The Kinks also formed in 1962, and their rise in popularity aligned with that of the British Invasion. They released their breakout hit “You Really Got Me” in 1964, quickly achieving international success. They were heavy hitters on the British Invasion circuit – that is, until 1966, when they were banned from touring in America. While the reason has never been explicitly made public, it is believed to be a combination of promoter disputes, chaotic behavior from the band, and visa and permit issues. Nevertheless, they continued to grow in popularity in the UK and America alike.

The Kinks’ satirical single “Sunday Afternoon” was inspired by British music hall – a form of working-class variety theater rooted in Victorian-era taverns and pubs. The track was the biggest hit in the UK during the summer of 1966. The Kinks are often regarded as the original punk band, with much of their music featuring fuzzy guitar distortion and a driving, bluesy energy. They influenced countless bands across rock subgenres, including psychedelic bands like The Doors and Jefferson Airplane, punk rock groups such as the Ramones, and Oasis and other Britpop bands.

The Who popularized many techniques and terms that would become touchstones in hard rock. Their incredible mastery of their instruments allowed them to get super creative with their recording and production. They experimented heavily with synthesizers and feedback, adding layers of texture that were unprecedented at the time. Guitarist and songwriter (and legend!) Pete Townshend had a massive impact on rock music unto himself. He popularized the use of power chords and also coined the term “power pop” in reference to the group’s sound. Townshend wrote the band’s most popular album, Tommy, which was the first time the term “rock opera” was used publicly.

Drummer Keith Moon also had a big hand in the band’s popularity and original sound. His playing was chaotic and unruly – he pushed the rhythm section out of the pocket and onto center stage. Moon was the backbone of The Who’s explosive, energetic live performances. They were the loudest band of the era, and maybe of all time – a quality that was understandably polarizing.

And now we’ve reached the greatest rock band of all time. The Rolling Stones (who are remarkably still together and actively performing) are pioneers of hard rock music. Flying takes longer than it used to – and, retirement of the Concorde notwithstanding, it’s not because planes aren’t as fast. There are more than twice as many commercial flights as there were at the time of airline deregulation, and air traffic control hasn’t been upgraded very much. There’s congested airspace and also congested airports. We also don’t build major new airports – the last one was Denver, 30 years ago. (Austin’s new 1999 airport was a converted military base, and there were others like that in the 90s.) By one estimate flights have gotten 8% longer. That means a two hour flight is 10 minutes longer than it used to be. And the single biggest factor is often tagged as strategic padding of schedules. Airlines are often chided for ‘schedule padding’ – claiming that flights take longer than they should. Often, this just reflects the reality of telling customers when they can expect to arrive – although there’s greater variance than there used to be, which isn’t great for having a gate available when you land early. Airlines do not like this! Though real airfares (even including fees) are down significantly since deregulation, longer flight times are expensive for staffing, fuel and efficient use of $50 – $100 million planes. And longer travel times historically disadvantaged an airline trying to sell tickets, because it meant their flights were displayed lower on the screen that travel agents and later passengers directly saw – which translates into fewer sales. A new study finds that airlines with more “padding” in their schedules tend to charge higher fares. That sounds like passengers paying more for a slower trip. But those extra minutes can also let United hold your plane with ConnectionSaver and get you home that night, and still arrive on time. It also means fewer delays and missed connections. So that ‘premium’ is actually a more valuable product to many consumers. I’ve argued for years that schedule padding often isn’t really padding. Sometimes an airline is finally admitting how long the trip takes. Customers can plan around an honest arrival time. There’s a new chapter by Embry-Riddle’s Jules Yimga, “The Price of Punctuality,” in The Economics of Airline Pricing. It combines 2019 flight time data with the government’s ticket price survey. It compares schedules against different percentiles of actual flight times, then examines how the resulting measures relate to fares. More padding is associated with higher fares, especially when the comparison uses the faster end of actual flight times. The relationship appears in both competitive and concentrated markets. The abstract itself allows that reliability could be a service customers value. (Interestingly, Yimga’s earlier research on schedule padding attributed higher prices primarily to higher costs rather than higher margins.) Even scheduling to the median arrival time means roughly half of trips take longer than published. It’s often better for a customer if an airline underpromises and overdelivers – or arrives ‘on time’ rather than late. Predictability matters when you’re scheduling connections or trying to arrive in time for a meeting. An airline that leaves little buffer is going to appear less reliable, so customers book earlier flights and longer layovers or even fly in the night before an important meeting. Then the buffer hasn’t disappeared, it just changes who’s scheduling it. That’s why I argued American’s investment in more realistic schedules could pay for itself. Avoiding rebooking, mishandled bags and aircraft and crews getting out of position can justify spending running an on-time schedule even before a customer chooses the airline again because the trip met expectations. American’s Dallas – Fort Worth overhaul that began in April combined longer scheduled flight times with a move from nine daily banks of flights to thirteen. Spreading flights into smaller waves reduced congestion that made some of the extra time unnecessary. One plane waiting for a gate can delay its passengers, its next departure, and other flights whose crews are stuck on board. Giving the operation room to recover can prevent that problem from cascading. In January, I covered American network planning chief Brian Znotins explaining that bookings for 40–54 minute connections at DFW were 4% below what its model predicted. Bookings for 55–84 minute connections were 8% above prediction. Customers have actually started choosing longer connections because they feel more reliable and less likely to leave them stranded. When I covered United’s media day in 2019, Greg Hart explained that 25% of its flights each day were expected to arrive ten minutes early. That gave United room to use ConnectionSaver to hold flights for connecting passengers and still arrive on time. Of course there’s such a thing as too much slack. An airline can improve its reported punctuality by moving the promised arrival later without getting anyone anywhere sooner. Excessively long connections waste customer time. And routinely beating an absurdly generous schedule doesn’t make an operation excellent. Not all padding is something customers will pay a premium for. If you had to guess which used pickup found a buyer fastest in October, the Toyota Tacoma would be a sensible bet. It’s America’s best-selling midsize truck and has a reputation for holding its value. Or you might pick the super frugal Ford Maverick Hybrid. But the truck that beat them both was the Tesla Cybertruck.

The data from the new iSeeCars study covers 1.1 million used vehicles that changed hands in October, so this was before Tesla announced it would discontinue the Cybertruck in 2027. The study looked at one- to five-year-old vehicles and ranked models by how long they’d been listed before selling.

The Cybertruck surpassed all other pickups, averaging 39.1 days, compared to 65.5 days for used pickups overall. The Maverick Hybrid came closest at 43.3 days, followed by the Rivian R1T at 45.2 and the F-150 Lightning at 47.4. The Tacoma came in fifth position.

The gap between the two electric pickups at either end of the list was even more striking. A used Chevrolet Silverado EV took 107.5 days to sell, putting it last among the 24 trucks in the study. That’s a 68-day difference between two American electric pickups. Across the wider used market, EVs averaged about 46 days to find a buyer, around nine fewer than hybrids and 15 fewer than gas vehicles.

How quickly a used Cybertruck sold seems to have changed a lot since May 2024, when some sellers were cutting as much as $30,000 from their asking prices after listing their trucks for close to $200,000. By December of that year, we reported that used Cybertrucks were piling up on dealer lots, with data at the time showing they stayed for 75 days, up from 27 in May.

Then owners got a look at what Tesla itself would pay for a used Cybertruck trade-in. One year later, in May 2025, one owner of a $99,990 dual-motor Foundation Series truck got a trade-in estimate of $65,400 after driving it just 6,211 miles.

So even though the Cybertruck was the fastest-selling used pickup in iSeeCars’ new study, it didn’t report what buyers paid, so we don’t know whether owners got their asking price or had to lower it to sell.

Tesla will build the last Cybertrucks at the end of summer 2027, and this will reshape their used market. Elon Musk originally announced production would end around the middle of the year, but then extended the run to fill more orders. And if you order a Cybertruck now, the only one you can get is the $110,000 tri-motor Cyberbeast, so anyone who wants a less expensive dual-motor truck has to look for a used example.

Once the last new Cybertruck is sold, I’d expect collectors and everyday truck buyers to look at used ones differently. Someone after a clean Foundation Series truck with low miles might pay a little extra for one close to home, especially since it could be months before another comes up for sale nearby.

At the same time, someone who just needs an electric pickup to tow a trailer has more options. If a used dual-motor Cybertruck costs about the same as a Ford F-150 Lightning or a Rivian R1T, I suspect that buyer will care more about mileage, condition, and how much battery warranty is left than when Tesla built its last truck.

Nepal: Predatory Lending Abuses Exacerbated by Conflict in the Gulf

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Loan shark victims demand justice after 38 days of protest in Kathmandu, Nepal, March 7, 2024. © 2024 Skanda Gautam/SOPA Images/Sipa via AP Photo Thousands of borrowers face exorbitant interest on fraudulent loan agreements taken out to fund labor migration and other needs.The Nepali government has criminalized abusive practices, but police and courts often side with loan sharks against distressed borrowers.Lost job opportunities due to war in the Gulf region have deepened the crisis facing many borrowers.

(Tokyo) – Authorities in Nepal should hold illegal moneylenders accountable for abuses against borrowers, including unlawful land dispossession, arbitrary eviction, threats and acts of violence, and sexual exploitation, Human Rights Watch said today. Conflicts in the Gulf region, where many Nepalis are migrant workers facing exorbitant recruitment fees, have made more people vulnerable to extortion by illegal lenders.

“Despite government commitments to protect borrowers, illegal practices are rife in Nepal’s informal credit sector, leaving those with the fewest resources dependent on moneylenders who can take everything they own,” said Meenakshi Ganguly, Asia deputy director at Human Rights Watch. “The government of Prime Minister Balendra Shah should carry out the government’s commitments to ensure that the law against fraudulent and abusive lending is consistently enforced.”

Human Rights Watch interviewed 18 borrowers, as well as local government officials, lawyers, and activists from six districts in the Terai region where informal money lending is rampant, reviewed government reports, and examined over 50 cases in Nawalparasi district.

Human Rights Watch found that many families are struggling with unmanageable debt particularly as migrant workers in the Gulf States during 2026 have faced reduced incomes or involuntary travel delays ahead of starting jobs due to the conflict between the US, Israel, and Iran. War-induced disruption amplified pre-existing abuses, including reliance on unauthorized individual agents and unreasonable recruitment fees. Human Rights Watch previously found that companies in Gulf states play a critical role in driving these illegal costs by refusing to cover costs and fees to recruiters that they know will be borne by workers.

Government investigations have shown that, under Nepal’s informal credit practices, a borrower signs or thumbprints an unsecured personal loan contract, known locally as kapali tamasuk, which can falsely list a principal amount three to ten times more than the cash received. These loans, known as meter byaj, in some cases compound at annual rates ranging from 36 percent to 60 percent or more, compared with less than 10 percent charged by banks.

As low-income households that had relied on remittances disrupted by the war have shifted their limited resources toward expenses for food, fuel, and healthcare, they have been increasingly unable to repay their loans, leading to a surge in defaults. In response, some moneylenders are forcing evictions or land transfers, including through violence, in the worst cases tying people into generational debt. Nepal authorities are not pushing for effective long term solutions beyond encouraging and facilitating debtors and creditors to enter pre-court settlements.

Following the outbreak of conflict in the Persian Gulf, in March 2026, the Nepal government suspended both institutional and individual labor permits to protect its citizens, including to Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, and Kuwait, with an estimated 2,000 Nepalis denied labor permits daily. Thousands were stopped from travelling at the international airport despite possessing valid medical clearances, verified visas, and binding employment contracts, while administrative delays also led foreign firms to cancel labor contracts. However, the authorities failed to step in to protect aspiring migrant laborers who still had to service their high interest meter byaj loans, leaving them exposed to violent enforcement by loan sharks.

“They did not just hit my father to get the money,” said one victim. “They beat him in the middle of the road during the day so that everyone else in the village would see what happens when you cannot pay the meter byaj. The local goons told us, ‘If you don’t sign over the land title today, your family will receive a corpse tomorrow.’”

While abuses, including violent attacks and forced evictions, appear to be increasing due to conflict related disruptions, many moneylenders have long wielded political and economic power over borrowers. Claiming that the justice system routinely favors the moneylenders, borrowers have staged three extended protests in Kathmandu over the last four years—the longest for 442 days—and three long marches. Each time, the government agreed to resolve debt issues but failed to honor its commitments to protect victims. Consequently, the borrowers have regularly protested at district and provincial levels as well.

Although government has criminalized such loans, borrowers and activists in Madhesh Province said that police have repeatedly refused to file reports when victims seek protection. Instead, they said, the police typically dismiss abuses and intimidation by moneylenders as “private civil disputes.”

“When the moneylender came to break our door, we ran to the police, but the officers told us to go home and settle personal transactional disputes ourselves,” said a Rautahat district migrant worker.

The police also back moneylenders when they use signed blank checks and banking laws to threaten defaulters with arrest, or use informal deeds as the basis to seize land and evict families. “The lender made me sign a blank check when he gave me a loan, and later on he filled it for 10 lakhs (US$6,500) and sent the police to threaten me with jail when the check bounced,” said a Siraha district farmer.

Borrowers also cite cases in which the authorities do not intervene even when lenders use patently unlawful loan agreements to obtain asset foreclosure orders. “The lender told me openly that he owns the courts and the administration,” said Shiv Shanker from Gaur, Rautahat district, who after facing abuses from a moneylender, is leading protests. “When the state shuts its doors on a poor farmer, death feels like the only place left to hide from this meter byaj.”

On May 5, 2023, the government criminalized informal loans as an “improper transaction,” and declared that forging loan contracts, threatening borrowers, or fabricating loan amounts, are punishable by up to seven years in prison. However, enforcement remains weak. Successive governments have established inquiry commissions to address the crisis, but over 65,000 complaints registered with inquiry commissions remain unresolved due to a lack of legal provisions and administrative bottlenecks.

These failures have contributed to deadly outcomes including suicide. Santidevi Mahato from Biratnagar died by suicide on June 21, following threats from a moneylender because she could not service her debt, people interviewed said. After borrowing 150,000 Nepali rupees (US$975) in October 2015, she had repaid 400,000 Nepali rupees (US$2,600) in installments. Yet, violating their agreement, the moneylender seized her land and threatened to evict her from her house.

Under international human rights law and standards, governments have the obligation to take steps to protect people from serious rights abuses caused by private actors, such as moneylenders, including through investigating and prosecuting serious crimes.

On July 17, the government led by Prime Minister Balendra Shah concluded a nine-point pact with protest leaders to eliminate predatory lending, after debt victims began a march to Kathmandu. The government should take immediate legal and policy steps to institutionalize its commitment, Human Rights Watch said. Nepal’s donors and international partners should assist the government to establish systematic debt relief, and grievance mechanisms that can support debt restructuring.

“The Balendra Shah government was elected with the promise of change, to overturn existing oppressive structures,” Ganguly said. “More closely regulating moneylenders and ending their chokehold on farmers and laborers is a crucial step in ensuring lasting social justice.”

Loan Sharks and Migrant Workers from Nepal

Nepal’s informal credit system is rooted in its feudal history, where local elites used predatory debt to trap peasants in tenancy and systems of generational servitude such as Kamaiya, Haliya, and Haruwa-Charuwa.

The present-day meter byaj system evolved around a spurt in labor migration during the decade-long civil war in Nepal, from 1996 to 2006, and rapidly spread after the 2007 political unrest in the Terai region. An estimated 7 million Nepalis work abroad, about half in India and a majority of the rest in Gulf countries, contributing a third of the national GDP. For many households, migration became one of the few viable ways to earn a living, while the absence of accessible and affordable institutional credit left them dependent on informal lenders to finance the costs of securing employment abroad.

Some local moneylenders and corrupt agents demanded exorbitant fees to help rural youth find jobs abroad, particularly in the Gulf States and Malaysia. Land or property was borrowers’ main collateral, but they often later discovered that their contracts were fraudulent, including falsified sums and forged documents. The schemes exploited bureaucratic complicity at government land offices to transfer property titles despite contracts being illegal, and borrowers lost their assets due to a lack of protection from the government.

In interviews, debt victims described devastating cycles in which migrant workers are forever bound to serving a predatory moneylender, while the authorities fail to intervene. Ramnath Musahar from Susta, Nawalparasi district, said the family borrowed to send two of his brothers to work in the Gulf States in 2015, but despite servicing the loan, their debt had grown enormously:

My brothers sent back Rs. 1,200,000 (US$8,000) from their wages, and we sold our plots [of land] to pay an extra Rs. 1,800,000 (US$11,000). But the lender went to the district court with a fake contract and claimed we still owe him Rs. 5,000,000 (US$33,000). The court looked only at the paper and our thumbprints; it did not look at our tears. The police came and threw our belongings into the dirt. We went from being independent farmers to beggars overnight.

Conflict Between the US, Israel, and Iran Exacerbates Debt Risks

Since the US and Israel began their assault on Iran on February 28, 2026, and Iran subsequently blocked and attacked ships traveling through the Strait of Hormuz and attacked sites across the Gulf States, the fallout is being felt far beyond the region.

In Nepal, UN agencies warned that “rising fuel prices, closure of shipping routes, and growing financial uncertainty are placing mounting pressure on economies, labor markets and vulnerable households.” According to the World Bank, Nepal’s real GDP growth could fall from 3.67 percent in 2024 to 2.3 percent in 2026 due to reduced tourism, higher transport costs, and supply chain snags. Rising inflation, a weakening currency leading to a rise in import costs, a spike in fuel costs, and stalled fertilizer supply have placed additional pressure on low-income households, and increased demands for overseas employment.

Hoping to seek jobs to support their families, many rural youths have taken loans from loan sharks, who have responded by raising rates. Many families were stuck servicing those loans, even as people were unable to travel because of conflict-related disruptions.

Workers already employed in the Gulf States are facing job losses, unpaid leave, wage reductions, and other disruptions that have reduced their ability to keep sending remittances. Compared with the same period the previous year, Gulf-bound labor approvals plummeted by 52 percent—from 228,165 down to 109,249—between the outbreak of the war in late February and mid-September, according to Department of Foreign Employment data. At least two Nepali workers have been killed due to the conflict, and 15 injured.

Many are returning to Nepal stripped of their anticipated income, and unable to service their loans. The authorities have failed to act as moneylenders crack down on defaulters, beating them, evicting them, and forcing land or asset transfers. The lenders are also seeking court-ordered asset foreclosures and even arrest warrants based on the loan contracts, including contracts that borrowers alleged contained fabricated loan amounts. As a Mahotari district borrower observed, “The law says meter byaj is a crime, but police demanded receipts I never got, so the thief walks free, and I am made the criminal.”

Women-led households, Dalits (considered an “untouchable” caste), and Indigenous ethnic communities have even fewer protections from violence such as beatings and sexual violence due to their caste, gender, and discrimination. One woman from Dhanusha district said she was raped by a moneylender under threat of eviction.

My husband was trapped in a labor camp abroad and couldn’t send money. The lender came to my house with the contract and said he would evict my children the next morning unless I “compromised” with him. He held our roof over my head like a weapon. I had to endure his abuse in silence because a poor woman in this village has no voice against a powerful man who holds her land paper.

Abuses by Moneylenders

While the conflict has exacerbated the crisis, debt-ridden families had already proliferated in Nepal, particularly in Terai districts. The authorities have failed to protect these families.

Chirai Chamar, a widowed Dalit worker in Paklihawa, Nawalparasi district, while struggling to gain control of her inherited family land, had borrowed 20,000 Nepali rupees (US$130). A moneylender offered to help with the legalities in exchange for a portion of that land. “A moneylender, from whom I had borrowed Rs. 20,000, came to me saying he will help me to acquire all the papers required to own my family land, and in return I need to give him two katthas (650 square meters) of land,” she said.

He then grabbed an additional four katthas (1336 square meters), paying Chamar a grossly undervalued sum of Rs. 150,000 (US$970). When rights activists intervened to reclaim Chamar’s land and restore her livelihood, local thugs threatened them and destroyed Chamar’s crops. Despite complaints, the police and the local officials failed to assist her.

Ashamaya (name changed), a resident of Thecho, Lalitpur, supported her family by running a small tea shop. However, when her father-in-law fell ill, she had to close the business to care for him. Left without income as the medical expenses mounted, she borrowed money from a moneylender, who charged an extortionate annual interest rate of 120 percent, which she could not pay. Under pressure, she took on another usurious loan simply to pay off the old one.

Although she settled that debt by selling a piece of land, she had to borrow again, this time to cover her husband’s medical costs, but she no longer had enough land for collateral. The moneylender coerced her into sex with multiple men after he forced her to sign a new contact for Rs. 2500,000 (UDS$16,167), amounting to five times her initial debt.

Seeking a Remedy

Debt victims have started to organize under various banners and groups, demanding that the government protect them from abusive loan sharks. In 2022, after hundreds walked barefoot for weeks from districts including Siraha, Dhanusha, Mahottari, Sarlahi, Kapilvastu, and Nawalparasi to stage protests in Kathmandu, the government at that time establisheda three-member Commission for the Investigation of Loan Sharking (CILS) in April 2023, to collect complaints, study structural issues, and resolve loan-sharking disputes.

By May 21, 2023, when the District Administration Offices stopped accepting these complaints, over 28,000 had been filed across 67 districts, with an alarming 21,552 cases concentrated in Madhesh Province. However, the commission only resolved and closed 5,115 loan disputes. Several victims reported abuses by district offices. “We went to the Chief District Officer expecting justice, but instead, the police and the lender ganged up on us, warned us that going to court would mean total land auction, and scared us into signing an agreement to pay half the inflated sum,” said a woman from Siraha.

As complaints persisted, including of violent encounters in which the police failed to intervene, the authorities largely resorted to symbolic agreements with the victims, followed by the repeated formation of new commissions. Healthcare affordability won the midterms. Now employers need to speak up.

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Employers should care about the debate over Affordable Care Act (ACA) subsidies for a simple reason: They pay for the uninsured whether or not those people ever join their health plans.

When people lose coverage, they don’t stop getting sick. They may delay treatment, skip medications or eventually show up in emergency rooms with conditions that could have been managed earlier. The costs don’t disappear, and employers are already struggling with what they pay for healthcare.

The midterm elections made clear that Americans are fed up with rising costs. According to NBC News’ exit polling, 85% of voters expressed concern about healthcare costs, and Congress is now under pressure to respond. Much of the conversation has focused on the expiration of enhanced ACA subsidies but employers have been largely absent from the debate, despite providing coverage to more Americans under 65 than any other source.

At Collective Health, we administer health benefits for self-insured employers covering roughly 700,000 people. Our claims data shows employer healthcare costs are on track to increase at least 8% if not more in 2026. While that’s an improvement from the trend we saw across the nation in 2025, it’s still an 8% increase on an already unsustainably expensive baseline.

As a physician, I’ve ordered thousands of tests and treatments. For most routine blood tests, the needles, tubes, machines and results are essentially the same regardless of where the blood is drawn. Yet in our data, employers pay roughly six times more when those tests are performed in hospital outpatient departments rather than independent labs or physician offices.

The same pattern holds elsewhere. Surgery costs approximately three times as much in hospital outpatient departments as it does at more affordable sites of care, while infusions cost nearly three times as much.

And it’s not as though people are suddenly using significantly more healthcare. Among employers with comparable data across 2025 and 2026, hospital admissions were flat or down during the first seven months of this year. Overall utilization declined for many of our larger employers.

There are areas where we want to see more care being used. Outpatient behavioral health is one example. Helping people access treatment earlier can keep them healthier and reduce the need for more intensive care later. But paying several times more for the same lab test or infusion because of who owns the facility does nothing to improve someone’s health.

Now consider what happens when millions of Americans face higher insurance premiums or lose coverage altogether.

Others won’t have an employer plan to fall back on. And the reductions to Medicaid funding raise another set of concerns. Medicaid is a major source of funding for long-term care, skilled nursing and home-based services, which are already under pressure from staffing shortages and rising demand. As more people lose coverage, hospitals may look to offset uncompensated care costs by charging commercially insured patients more, putting additional pressure on employers already struggling with rising healthcare costs.

Employers have good reason to care about the stability of both markets. The individual market provides an important source of coverage for part-time workers, early retirees and employees’ families. When that coverage becomes harder to afford, the consequences reach well beyond the people buying insurance on the exchanges.

Congress should start with policies that address what care actually costs. Site-neutral payments are an obvious place to begin. If a patient receives the same treatment in a hospital-owned outpatient department or an independent facility, there is little justification for the enormous price difference we see today.

We also need better transparency around prescription drug pricing, pharmacy benefit managers and drugs administered in hospitals and clinics, where markups can be particularly difficult to see. Employers shouldn’t need a dedicated data team to understand what they’re paying for.

The No Surprises Act’s independent dispute resolution process also deserves another look. While the law has helped protect patients from certain unexpected bills, the payment dispute process has created opportunities for inflated demands. And Congress needs to provide a stable individual insurance market, whether by restoring enhanced subsidies or developing a better alternative.

Employers have options of their own, too. One of our clients redesigned its surgical benefits so eligible procedures at a designated center of excellence were free to employees. The same procedures carried 10% coinsurance at an ambulatory surgery center and 20% at a hospital outpatient facility.

In the first year, the share of eligible surgeries performed in hospital outpatient settings fell by nine percentage points. Other services that weren’t included in the redesign saw no comparable shift.

The employer didn’t achieve that simply by changing the plan. Employees received information during enrollment, targeted communications and personalized guidance from Collective Health when they began seeking care. They weren’t left to figure out the options on their own.

That’s the work employers should be doing with their benefits partners. They can manage where infusions happen, not just which drugs are covered. They can reach people before a procedure is scheduled rather than after the bill arrives.

In this industry, we tend to talk about “members,” but these are patients trying to do what’s best for their health while making ends meet. Asking someone dealing with knee pain or a serious diagnosis to research hospital prices and navigate complicated insurance rules on their own isn’t reasonable. If we want people to choose more affordable care, we have to make that choice easier.

Employers have spent years absorbing healthcare cost increases, often with little control over the prices behind them. They have the purchasing power and the claims data to push for something better. With a new Congress preparing to take up healthcare affordability, they also have an opportunity to influence the policies that determine what they’ll pay in the years ahead.

Wizards of the Coast's War Against MTG Proxies Isn't as Bad as It Sounds

Wizards of the Coast, a Hasbro subsidiary and the publisher of popular tabletop games Magic: The Gathering and Dungeons & Dragons, has filed a massive lawsuit against a provider of proxy Magic cards, PrintMTG Enterprise, seeking more than $28.6 million in damages.

Proxies are replicas of Magic cards printed to resemble real items. Although they are not allowed in official tournaments, proxies are still somewhat popular in casual play, especially within the Commander format. Notably, Premodern, a format that includes cards from 1995 to 2003 and is steadily growing in popularity, allows proxy cards in some tournaments.

However, while some Magic players are already concerned about a possible visit from the Pinkertons, things aren't as bad as they seem. Wizards is not suing PrintMTG Enterprise for producing proxy Magic cards, but for marketing their products as proxies "to lend them a veneer of legitimacy while actually plying a business built around the sale of counterfeit cards," the lawsuit reads. The distinction is important: "An honest proxy is a stand-in for a real card that makes no claims to authenticity, by appearance or otherwise." On the other hand, "A counterfeit is made to be mistaken for the real card and to take its place as something owned, traded, or sold."

According to the lawsuit, PrintMTG Enterprise operates as "a complex and sophisticated network of individuals and shell corporations," whose purpose is to profit from counterfeiting and trademark and copyright infringement. One of the websites operated by the group, ProxyKing.biz, states: "At Proxy King we try to make our proxies as realistic as possible. This lets you join games and play without people noticing you are running proxy cards in your Magic deck."

Plaintiffs withdrew the lawsuit without a ruling, so it can be revised and refiled at a large date

Magic has expanded quickly in the last few years to become a massive business built on the value of its collectibles. The game is also Hasbro's main revenue driver​​​​​​​; in the third quarter of 2026, Magic surpassed $600 million in quarterly revenue for the first time. In a global trading card industry estimated at around $15 billion, Magic ranks second, behind​​​​​​​ the Pokémon Trading Card Game. While Wizards does not sell single Magic cards, the secondary market still drives the company's product sales. Counterfeits thus hurt the Magic business as a whole, the lawsuit claims. It's not surprising, then, that Wizards isn't going to look the other way when it comes to a counterfeiting network.

However, the lawsuit also makes it clear that the company doesn't have an issue with proxies: "Wizards recognizes that private and noncommercial proxying is a vehicle players use to further engage with the game, not to undermine it." Proxies are important, especially for formats like Vintage or Premodern, where older, expensive cards are very popular. The warden kept telling Pheap Rom not to think of himself as a prisoner. Instead, the jailer insisted, Rom and his fellow deportees from the United States were “the king’s guests.”

Here was the warden’s idea of royal hospitality: Each morning at 7:30, Rom—a chiseled, compact, 43-year-old Cambodian man with a pronounced Philly accent—was roused from a bare cell he shared with three other men in Eswatini, a tiny kingdom in Southern Africa. Then he was made to stand at attention as the country’s national anthem, “Nkulunkulu Mnikati Wetibusiso temaSwati,” reverberated through the halls of the Matsapha Correctional Centre. Most meals consisted of a portion of rice splashed with thin broth. It was late 2025, and the weather was warm and wet. For 15 minutes a day, Rom and the other men were allowed to go outside. Before they were locked up for lights out, the anthem played again; thick clouds of mosquitoes kept Rom thrashing all night.

Sometimes Rom would banter with the towering, bespectacled warden, who went by the name Kuenene. If I’m a guest, then why am I being held in a prison with criminals? Rom would ask. Why aren’t I allowed to leave? But Rom knew these exchanges with the warden were absurd, like everything else about his situation.

Before the second Trump administration, the practice of deporting immigrants to countries where they have no ties was a rare and tightly restricted move in US immigration enforcement—reserved mainly for cases where a person simply could not be returned to their nation of origin due to risk of persecution or torture. It has since expanded into a signature White House policy of mass removal. Over the past two years, the administration has inked deals with some 35 nations to accept third-country deportees from the US. The kingdom of Eswatini, which agreed to take up to 160 migrants for a fee of $5.1 million, is one of them. Some of the other destination countries—the Central African Republic, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, South Sudan—are so violent and unstable that they rank on the State Department’s “Do Not Travel” list.

Often making a spectacle of its own transgressiveness, the administration has steamrolled past public outcry, an investigation by Democratic lawmakers, and a warning from the United Nations about a “cascade of human rights violations” resulting from these removals. At least twice in the past two years, US federal courts have ordered the Department of Homeland Security to stop shipping migrants to third countries with insufficient due process. Both times, the Supreme Court has quickly stepped in to let the administration resume.

In September, during one of those brief, court-ordered pauses in removal flights, DHS general counsel James Percival went on X to publish the most comprehensive figures yet on how many migrants have been affected by the practice. He posted a GIF of Taylor Swift mouthing the words “That’s my husband” and pointing excitedly at her wedding ring. Percival’s caption: “How my wife looks at me when I tell her we have deported 25,000 illegal aliens to third nations.”

Rather than slowing down in the face of resistance, in fact, President Trump’s third-country push appears to have inspired imitators. Five European Union countries announced in November that they are working toward a plan to send rejected asylum seekers to a deportation hub in ​Rwanda. In the past two years, Italy has begun sending asylum seekers to Albania. The United Kingdom quietly sent an asylum seeker to Eswatini earlier this year; the man, originally from Sri Lanka, died last month, the government of Eswatini reported. According to local media, the apparent cause was suicide.

Rom’s lawyer, Tin Thanh Nguyen—who is himself the child of refugees from Vietnam—predicts that, if the administration keeps getting away with these expulsions, additional wealthy countries will be emboldened to offload their undesirables to poorer nations for a price. “This is going to be the new model for the West, not only for the United States,” says Nguyen, who also represents three other men currently held in Eswatini and Rwanda. “It’s just kind of out of sight, out of mind for these Western countries.”

But as Rom’s experience reveals, some of these removals may rest on little more than bureaucratic fiction. The US claims it has followed due process with every one of the 25,000 migrants deported to unfamiliar lands. “Their own countries say, ‘We do not want them back,’” US attorney general Todd Blanche told Fox News in early October. Yet in Rom’s case—among countless other Kafkaesque details—his own country never even appears to have been asked.

An American in virtually everything but citizenship status, Rom came to the United States as a 3-year-old refugee from Cambodia. Around 1979, his parents fled their home to escape the horrors of the Khmer Rouge, a genocidal regime that seized power in the wake of Richard Nixon’s carpet-bombing of the country. From there, the family went to a Thai refugee camp—where Rom was born in 1982—and then to a resettlement center in the Philippines. As Rom’s father was selling off possessions to raise money for their ultimate move to America, he was killed by roadside thieves. Rom sat by his side after the attack, his mother later told him, trying to wake his father up.

Rom’s first memories of America are of a cramped, dimly lit bedroom in a small apartment in the Bronx, where he arrived with his mother and six siblings in 1985. “There was just one mattress, and we all slept on that,” he says. “It was a new world to us; everything was new.”

Rom and his family had arrived in the middle of the largest resettlement effort in US history. As America’s chimerical war efforts in Southeast Asia faltered in the 1970s, hundreds of thousands of people began arriving in the US from Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia. Of the nearly 158,000 Cambodians who gained entry between 1975 and 1994, the majority, like Rom and his family, were refugees deeply marked by the atrocities of the Khmer Rouge.

Around 10,000 of them came to be resettled in areas of the Bronx that were blighted by high crime and poverty. Many were placed in dilapidated housing, with limited job opportunities. Rom’s mother, who didn’t speak English, relied on government support checks and whatever extra income she could make sewing and scavenging. As a child, Rom would help her collect cans and carry the overstuffed bags to the grocery store to return for cash.

With the help of an uncle, the family eventually relocated to Philadelphia, where an adolescent Rom was enticed by a newly formed gang—a Cambodian clique within the Crips called the Blue Dragons. “If you don’t want to get bullied,” older members of the gang told him, “you need to link up with us.” Rom was barely a teenager. An older gang member dubbed him Menace, after Dennis the Menace, because he was “funny and bad at the same time.”

By the time he was 18, Rom had done three stints in juvenile detention and was caught in ever-escalating street beefs. At a New Year’s Eve party in 2008, rival gang members jumped him. Rom waited for a day off from his job—caring for abandoned dogs at a local rescue—to seek revenge. He parked outside the gang’s hangout, and when his targets exited, he opened fire from his car. Rom contemplated running when he heard that the police were looking for him but decided against it largely because he had a young daughter. In jail he would be able to see her, even if it was from behind glass. On the lam, he’d have to disappear. He pleaded guilty in April 2010 to multiple charges, including attempted murder.

Rom was a legal permanent resident of the US, a status he had been granted as a refugee. Though refugees are entitled to obtain full American citizenship, Rom had been told he didn’t stand a chance due to his juvenile record, an obstacle that would greatly alter the trajectory of his life.

Under US immigration law since the 1990s, legal permanent residents who commit crimes that qualify as “aggravated felonies”—an expansive and, critics say, somewhat arbitrary legal category—can lose their immigration status and become subject to deportation after they’ve served out their sentences. In a case of what immigrant advocates call “double punishment,” Rom’s conviction for attempted murder meant he would lose his green card. And he knew that in the post-9/11 era, his home country had bowed to US pressure to accept the repatriation of deported Cambodian refugees.

Rom likes to say that prison saved his life. He spent much of his time in lockup reading, exercising, and making cents an hour working in the kitchen to save up for small gifts for his daughter. As he served out his sentence, a generation of shifts in America’s immigration policy flew by—from President Obama’s support of the Dreamers and record-high deportation numbers to Trump’s policy of family separation to Biden’s border crisis to Trump’s return to the White House. But in Rom’s mind, none of that made a difference for him: His fate was already sealed. He was fully prepared to be deported to Cambodia, a country he had never visited, once his sentence was finished.

Rom’s reserved mood as his prison term neared its end confused fellow inmates. “Yo, why are you not happy?” they asked. “You’re about to be released, you made parole.” On November 13, 2024, Rom walked out after serving 15 years. He was immediately transferred to Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) holding, where what he believed would be a straightforward process turned into a bewildering journey through the American deportation machine.

From detention, Rom did what he could to hasten the process of his own removal. He frequently called the Cambodian embassy in Washington, DC, seeking information on his departure process. He was told that his questions should be directed to the Department of Homeland Security. Officers in Pennsylvania, he says, had no updates. They told Rom they were swamped by the number of cases they were trying to handle. At one point, they told him he would be deported to Thailand, his country of birth, which he told them was incorrect. After six months of detention, Rom filed a writ of habeas corpus arguing that he was being unlawfully detained.

He continued to wait for another three months. On September 24, 2025, his daughter’s birthday, Rom saw on a tablet device that his commissary account had been zeroed out. Among ICE detainees, this is a telltale sign that deportation is imminent. The following day, Rom was transferred to Pine Prairie ICE Processing Center in Louisiana. He was moved again a few days later to Alexandria, Louisiana, where the airport hums with obscure charter airlines that ferry deportees abroad. He waited there for another 72 hours before he started, again, asking officers what was happening.

At first, they said they didn’t know, a claim Rom found hard to believe. When an officer checked his case on the computer system and then quickly clammed up, Rom’s suspicions grew. Finally they told him to pack up, and Rom caught a look at the immigration paperwork. It listed Eswatini as the country of deportation. Confused, Rom turned to another detainee: “Hey, bro, is Eswatini another ICE facility in Louisiana?” he asked. The man responded that it was in southern Africa.

Rom ran to the phone and made a panicked call to his sister in Las Vegas. She searched online and confirmed that Eswatini was indeed in Africa. Rom asked her to call the Cambodian Embassy. Another detainee’s lawyer suggested the group request “reasonable fear” interviews: US Citizenship and Immigration Services conducts these interviews through their asylum officers to ascertain if a person has a reasonable fear of persecution or torture. Immigration agents seemed amused by the suggestion, Rom says. What could the men be afraid of? they asked. “Y’all a bunch of murderers,” Rom recalls them taunting.

Rom pleaded with the guards. Eventually, one officer told him and the others that if they agreed to be shackled, he would take down their request in writing. They stepped into the restraints, and the officer set about filling out some paperwork. As they sat waiting, their feet and hands in chains, Rom gave the request for a “reasonable fear” interview one last try. “Hey,” he asked, “you think you are going to give us that interview?” He realized it was “all fake” and that the officers never intended to file anything.

One by one, under heavy guard, the men were loaded onto a small airplane. When the door closed it was “like my coffin is closing,” says KV, another deportee on the flight who asked to be identified by his initials because he remains detained. The plane flew from Alexandria to Puerto Rico. The officials accompanying the men gave them no information. They tried, and failed, to disable the small TVs that showed the plane’s flight path.

The plane carried on to Senegal, where they were delayed as officials sought permission to travel onward. “They were basically smuggling us through Africa,” Rom says. When the flight touched down at King Mswati III International Airport early on the morning of October 6, the runway was lined with dozens of armed military personnel. Rom was loaded into a paddy wagon. As the van turned onto a dirt road, Rom was overcome with fear. Oh yeah, they are going to kill us, Rom thought. They’re definitely going to kill us.

Eswatini, a country about the size of New Jersey surrounded almost entirely by South Africa, is the continent’s last remaining absolute monarchy. In April 1973, King Sobhuza II suspended the constitution and banned all political parties. The ban remains in place. His son, King Mswati III, took power in 1986, at just 18 years old. Challenges to the monarchy have been met with violence. In June 2021, security forces cracked down on pro-democracy protests, killing 46 people, including two children, according to a government inquiry. Civil society organizations say this is an undercount. A leading human rights lawyer and pro-democracy activist was assassinated at his home two years later. No one has been held accountable for his death.

Eswatini has the highest HIV prevalence rates in the world. Poverty in the country is pervasive. A 2024 report from the US State Department describes Eswatini as having “significant human rights issues,” including torture, media restrictions, and the worst forms of child labor. The king is reportedly married to a cohort of wives and lives a life divorced from his country’s brutal reality. At an April event to mark his 40 years on the throne, he held an extravagant party at a luxury convention center that included a performance by Grammy-nominated singer Davido.

The men, none of whom had committed any crime in Eswatini, were deposited in a high-security area of the Matsapha Correctional Centre. Rom’s cell sat behind a large metal door with a barred window painted forest green. When the detainees arrived, the cells were dirty. They asked for cleaning products but were told that none were available. Basic hygiene supplies took four months to arrive. In a dirt courtyard outside, the men had access to a row of concrete sinks to hand-wash their clothes during their 15 daily minutes of outdoor time. One detainee was allowed to retrieve the clothes in the evening.

Rom’s 15-year sentence in the US proved to be a useful experience. He fell back into his workout routine from prison, knocking out 500 pushups a day along with burpees and sit-ups. He read books donated to the prison, churning through the Hunger Games and Twilight series. His lawyer sent him a copy of First They Killed My Father, a memoir about surviving the Khmer Rouge. Rom had expected the weather to cool down at night, but there was little reprieve from the heat. He was given heavy blankets, which meant choosing between sweltering or being swarmed by mosquitoes. He asked jailers the same question he had been asking the guards in the US. “How long am I going to be here?” No one had an answer.

Gradually, the men were granted a few freedoms. They were brought to a mobile store so those without phones could buy them using money from their families. On the way back, they were allowed to eat at KFC and buy two beers apiece. These were the first excursions Rom had taken outside of detention facilities in more than 15 years.

In a post on X, a DHS official had said that the men being deported to Eswatini were “depraved monsters,” and those statements had spread in local media. So Rom smiled and waved, saying hello to the people who stopped to gawk.

The men were permitted to make one phone call a week using WhatsApp. The calls were broadcast in a common room; a prison official would sit nearby, scribbling notes. When Rom wanted to discuss something sensitive with his sister, the two would switch to speaking in Khmer. Local lawyers were barred from visiting. Shortly after Rom was deported, his sister saw a post on Facebook that mentioned Tin Thanh Nguyen, the lawyer in North Carolina. She got in touch, and Nguyen agreed to take Rom’s case.

Thanks to a TV in the common room that was set to the news, Rom was well aware of the controversy surrounding his fellow deportees. He watched a report about one fellow Matsapha inmate from Cuba, Roberto Mosquera del Peral, who had arrived earlier and was on a hunger strike. Each morning, when officers checked Mosquera del Peral’s cell, Rom says, they would shout, mockingly asking if he was still alive.

How Rom and the others came to be trapped in Eswatini is still unclear. According to a report from Reuters, the deal was brokered in February 2025 between Caitlin Piper, a diplomat who was leading the US embassy in the capital of Mbabane, and Russell Dlamini, the country’s prime minister, who sought and received approval from the king. Neither Dlamini nor the acting government spokesperson, Thabile Mdluli, responded to requests for comment. In May, a spokesperson for King Mswati told Reuters that “the king embraced the deal as Eswatini’s contribution to world order.”

Piper has since departed Eswatini. She was most recently based at the US Consulate in Erbil, Iraq, according to social media posts from the consulate. The consulate did not respond to requests for comment on her current position.

In November 2025, more than a month after Rom arrived and four months after the first deportees were deposited at the prison, Eswatini’s finance minister confirmed that he had received a $5.1 million payment from the US. He said the money would be funneled toward Eswatini’s National Disaster Management Agency. The country said it would accept 160 deportees as part of the agreement. The State Department eventually posted a copy of the memorandum of understanding signed between the two countries on May 14, 2025, which confirmed these details. The signature of the US official includes a looping C, but no name is printed underneath. Asked for comment, the Eswatini Press Inquiries Team at the embassy directed questions to the Department of State in Washington. The Department of State did not respond to questions regarding who signed the agreement.

This payment disclosure led the men in detention to charge that they had been trafficked. “You are moving people around, and money is exchanged,” says KV. “So what is that?” If Eswatini got all that money, Rom wondered, why was he having to spend his own on basics like a fan for his cell?

The Southern Africa Litigation Centre (SALC), a human rights NGO, joined several other groups in filing a legal challenge against Eswatini in 2025, aiming to declare the deportation agreement unconstitutional, obtain the terms of the agreement, and secure an order stopping further arrivals until the matter was legally resolved. The initial challenge was dismissed. SALC and the other groups filed an appeal to the Supreme Court of Eswatini in March. (Just weeks before her reported meeting with Eswatini’s prime minister to facilitate the deportations, Piper had addressed an SALC workshop, urging participants “to bring injustice to light, to demand the respect of those in power, to advocate for a world in which our most cherished freedoms are secured.” )

Rom’s personal path to Eswatini appears to have come about through a combination of cruelty and bureaucratic ineptitude. Nguyen points out that Cambodia has been accepting deportees for more than two decades and that the country is generally responsive and professional in issuing the requisite travel documents. He says that the Cambodian government moved to begin rectifying the situation within a week of his contacting them in October 2025.

Nguyen believes that the US erroneously thought Rom should be sent to Thailand, the country of his birth, not citizenship, but never bothered to contact government officials from that country or from Cambodia. Chann Rotana, a spokesman for Cambodia’s foreign ministry, says they also do not understand why Rom was sent to Africa. He adds that the US did not contact the country’s embassy in Washington, DC, regarding Rom’s case before he left the country. Rotana described the deportation to Africa as “strange” and said the embassy only became aware of the situation when Rom’s sister got in touch. Asked about Rotana’s comments, the Department of Homeland Security directed questions to the Department of State, which declined to respond.

“There’s no rhyme or reason as to why any of my clients were picked to go to Eswatini versus the clients that I have who are actually in Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia,” Nguyen says. “There’s nothing that makes them distinguishable.”

After an interview with Cambodian officials, Rom’s case for removal progressed while the other men sat hoping for similar developments. When he was cleared to be sent to Cambodia in early February, he requested that he be escorted by a representative from the UN’s International Organization for Migration as a form of insurance. Rom finally arrived in Cambodia on March 26, becoming the second US deportee freed from Eswatini. (The first, a Jamaican man named Orville Isaac Etoria, was deported to Eswatini in July 2025 and repatriated to Jamaica in September of that year.)

When I met Rom in June, at a restaurant in the Cambodian capital of Phnom Penh, he was dressed in all black. The sleeves of his polo shirt were taut around his thick, tattooed arms. We met within walking distance of S-21, a Khmer Rouge torture and interrogation center that is now a museum dedicated to the genocide that set his family on its path to America. He is contemplative about his journey and speaks softly.

After arriving in Cambodia, Rom enrolled in a creative writing class, hoping to detail his experience. He was taking teacher training courses, hoping to land a job at an international school, and picked up work fixing air conditioners when he could.

The jarring transition to life in Cambodia was made harder when his older sister—who had been his lifeline in detention—died in July. “Im trying to hold it together,” he texted me. In August, Rom sent a string of messages saying he’d gotten a job teaching English to a rambunctious group of third graders.

Once a week, Rom calls the deportees who remain in Africa. There are more now. A few weeks after Rom and I met, Eswatini announced that it had received a fourth batch of 11 deportees. Another group followed. Thirty-two have been forced to the country in total. KV remains imprisoned. “With the holidays around the corner,” he texted in late September, “it feels I’m wasting my life sitting here for no reason.” It is unclear when he will be released.

The fate of all third-country deportees stands to be clarified in the coming months. After at least twice granting the Trump administration temporary stays to keep deporting migrants to third countries, the US Supreme Court will start hearing oral arguments on the ultimate legality of the practice in December.

Shortly after he arrived in Cambodia, Rom noticed that Kuenene, his warden at Matsapha, had added him as a contact on Telegram and WhatsApp. Another Eswatini official requested to connect on Facebook and follow Rom on Instagram. Rom accepted some of the invitations and declined others; he couldn’t tell why they were trying to befriend him. Maybe Kuenene wanted to keep tabs on what Rom might say publicly. “He was one of those guys that tell you what you want to hear, smile at your face, make you believe that he is the most supportive guy in the world, and then he will stab you in the back,” Rom said. “I didn’t trust him.” Before he was released, Rom told the guards and other deportees that he might return to Eswatini to visit as a tourist. In reality, he has no intention of ever returning. One visit as a royal guest was enough.

Budget carrier Norse Atlantic falls 77% after warning its finances are ‘critical’ amid soaring fuel costs

📅 2026-11-25  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Norse Atlantic shares plummeted Wednesday after it revealed the company is in a "critical financial situation" as soaring fuel costs amid the Iran war strain budget carriers during the winter season.

The Norwegian low-cost carrier said its situation is dependent on securing financing for its short-term liquidity needs.

The company has had discussions about a potential strategic takeover as well as other options including ACMI contracts, which refer to short-term capacity leasing.

"Norse is also engaged in discussions with selected parties on short term financing, however, there can be no assurances that such discussions will result in a viable solution," it added.

The start of the U.S.-Iran war on Feb. 28 sent jet fuel prices soaring, putting pressure on airlines around the world. Prices are now up nearly 72% to $155 per barrel year-on-year as of the week ending Nov. 20, according to the International Air Transport Association's Jet Fuel Price Monitor.

Norse, which offers cheap long-haul flights between Europe, North America, Africa, and Asia, said in October that it was forced to fly with reduced capacity that month, due to "prolonged high fuel prices."

The company said in August that geopolitical disruptions and higher jet fuel costs hit profitability in the first half of the year. Higher fuel costs, lower aircraft use, and operational disruptions led to an $8.4 million second-quarter loss, measured before interest, taxes, depreciation, amortization, and rent.

EasyJet, another low-cost airline impacted by high fuel costs, reported Wednesday that profit before tax slumped 89% to £71 million ($92.7 million) for the year ending in September 2026. Revenue growth was unable to offset the impact of an 11% increase in fuel costs due to the Middle East conflict, the company said.

Earlier this year, Ryanair's CEO Michael O'Leary told CNBC that other European carriers would struggle if jet fuel prices continued to soar. "I think there will be failures."

"If it continues at $150 a barrel into July, August, September, then you'll see European airlines fail and that, in the medium term, would probably be good for Ryanair's business."

Meanwhile, Ryanair's chief financial officer Neil Sorahan told CNBC in May: "I think we will see some of the weaker carriers who were already struggling before the war possibly go to the wall in the winter."

Several smaller European airlines have stopped flying this winter. Slovakia's AirExplore ceased operations, Ireland's Fly4 entered provisional liquidation, and Spain's AlbaStar suspended flights while seeking creditor protection.

Spain's Plus Ultra has also suspended all flights while it restructures, though it has not confirmed a permanent closure.
