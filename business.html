The professional golf world faces a new reality as the DP World Tour moves to fill its fields with high-profile names from the collapsing LIV Golf circuit. Nine players who left their original tours to join the Saudi-backed league have been offered a path back through sponsor invitations, a move that prioritizes star power over traditional meritocratic rankings. This shift comes as LIV Golf winds down through bankruptcy proceedings, leaving many players in limbo while tour officials scramble to stabilize the game's ecosystem.

According to Rediff, European Tour chief executive Guy Kinnings acknowledged the rapid shift in the sport, stating, "There has been significant change in the landscape of men's professional golf over the past 12 months." The tour is now offering these invitations without the hefty appearance fees that characterized the LIV era, aiming to inject excitement into lower-ranked events. This strategy targets specific stops that need a boost in star power, ensuring fields remain competitive even if depleted of top-tier members. According to Ronan MacNamara from Irish Golfer Magazine, up to 34 individuals previously associated with LIV are set to retain DP World Tour status in the coming year, a figure appearing inflated due to the former league's hostile maneuvers against the tour.

The tour has secured the returns of major stars like Jon Rahm, Tyrrell Hatton, and Bryson DeChambeau, which officials view as a positive step for the DP World Tour. CODE Sports reports that Cameron Smith has also been offered a career lifeline following the collapse of LIV Golf, though the terms come with specific conditions. Dustin Johnson, Bubba Watson, Cameron Smith, and Joaquin Niemann are among those receiving a "specific number" of tournament invitations to beef up events where they play. These players, who previously defected with a seemingly endless supply of Saudi cash, now face a different reality as that funding disappears. The arrangement allows these high-profile names to compete without taking spots from lower-ranked players who earned their way through the system. Kinnings emphasized the pragmatic nature of this approach, noting that the tour has always kept the best interests of its entire membership at the forefront of its mind.

Longtime supporters of the European tour have raised concerns about the implications of this policy. The tour previously sent 10 players to the PGA Tour annually, yet now it risks banning PGA Tour players with greater profiles than those it is losing. The idea behind the sponsor invitations is to place these stars in European tour stops that need help, giving those tournaments a boost without taking away spots from fields that were already depleted. This creates a unique situation where players who never contributed to the tour are now being welcomed back with open arms. The move promises to be exciting for European golf fans and head honchos at the Tour, but it leaves rank-and-file members questioning the fairness of the system.

As the DP World Tour integrates these former LIV players, the relationship with the PGA Tour remains a critical factor. With the PGA Tour's partnership with Europe ending next year, talks continue to alter and prolong the agreement. PGA Tour CEO Brian Rolapp confirmed on Wednesday evening that discussions are productive, stating, "both entities have a mutual desire to extend and modify our partnership." The modification will likely include how many PGA Tour cards are offered to leading players in Europe, but it is most certain to be fewer than before. This reduction in card availability adds another layer of complexity to the integration of these high-profile returnees.

Kinnings believes the upcoming season will be highly competitive despite these changes. He noted that with the return of major champions and the inclusion of former LIV stars, "Our 2027 season is set to be one of the most keenly contested campaigns in our 55-year history." The inclusion of players like Rahm and DeChambeau adds significant weight to the tour's schedule. However, the financial stability of these players remains uncertain as the LIV league dissolves.

The next major development will be the finalization of the 2027 season schedule and the specific allocation of cards for European players. The PGA Tour and DP World Tour must resolve their partnership terms before the season begins. The outcome of these negotiations will determine how many European players can access the PGA Tour and how the remaining spots are distributed.

Sen. Ron Johnson, the Wisconsin Republican who chairs the Senate Budget Committee, said on Wednesday that Social Security is a "scam" that should be "shut down," according to MarketWatch.

Johnson made the comments during a Fox Business segment, according to MarketWatch. "It's grotesque and at this point, there really is no way to fix Social Security," he said. "It is a Ponzi scheme. You can't fix it. You just shut it down and hope you can recover some of your money."

The Senate Budget Committee scheduled a hearing titled "Social Security: The Reality" for 11 a.m. ET on Wednesday in room SD-608. The committee lists Johnson as presiding and Sen. Jeff Merkley, an Oregon Democrat, as giving a member statement.

The committee lists three witnesses: Veronique de Rugy of the Mercatus Center at George Mason University, Jason J. Fichtner of the LIMRA Retirement Income Institute and Kathryn Anne Edwards of the National Academy of Social Insurance.

Johnson previewed the hearing in an X $TWTR post on Tuesday, according to Benzinga. He called Social Security a "legal Ponzi scheme" and wrote that the hearing would lay out how the program has been "grossly mismanaged."

In a video attached to the post, Johnson said the money workers paid into the program was spent "the minute it came in the door," according to Benzinga. He argued that the government could have put program surpluses into investments from 1986 onward, leaving $14.5 trillion in "hard assets" available for paying out benefits.

Nancy Altman, president of the advocacy group Social Security Works, criticized the remarks in a statement, according to MarketWatch. "Millions of seniors and people with disabilities will face homelessness and hunger" if the program were shut down, she said.

Social Security’s trust funds are on track to be exhausted within six years, according to the Congressional Budget Office, as cited by MarketWatch. If that happens, payments to recipients could be reduced by roughly 26%, according to the CBO. Congress has yet to settle on a plan for closing the gap, MarketWatch said.

AARP published a report earlier this month on how shifting income patterns have shortened the program's projected solvency. Social Security's trustees project the combined trust fund will run out in 2034, and AARP said the share of GDP subject to the payroll tax fell from 41% to 34% in 2025. AARP said that had the share held steady, the program could cover scheduled benefits in full through 2059.

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A new study found 81% of U.S. businesses don't properly license music, costing creators and other rightsholders. The fix starts with education — not lawsuits.

Walk into almost any shop, gym, salon or restaurant in America, and you’ll hear music. Ask the owner whether it’s licensed and you will likely get a confident “yes.” But in most cases, that answer is, unfortunately, wrong.

Soundtrack recently surveyed 1,525 decision-makers responsible for nearly 15,000 business locations across 10 customer-facing industries to find out how U.S. businesses actually play music. Though the law is clear that businesses need specific licenses to play music in public, the results of the survey describe a market that loves music, depends on it, and, overwhelmingly, does not properly pay for it.

Ninety-seven percent of the businesses we surveyed play music for customers, and 77% do it every day. Adjusted for the size of the U.S., that means roughly 3.8 million U.S. business locations play music in their stores.

Nine in 10 of those surveyed said music boosts employee motivation and shapes the energy of their space, while more than eight in 10 called it an essential part of the business and said it has to fit their brand. Rather than merely being a background expense, businesses see music as part of the product.

Yet despite this overwhelming demand, the way music played in businesses is paid for has not evolved. A striking 79% of businesses illicitly use a consumer streaming service, such as a personal Spotify, Pandora or YouTube account, to play music for customers. Only 28% of businesses use a service built and licensed for business. This is one of the biggest failures, and also one of the biggest opportunities, for the music industry today.

Aside from the scale of misuse, it is significant how many businesses are sure they’re doing it right. Seventy-eight percent of respondents were confident the music in their business is legally compliant. Eighty-three percent said they’re familiar with the rules. Yet 81% are misusing music, and 58% believe it’s legal to stream a personal account in their business.

Even I struggle to distill the U.S. licensing rules into just a few sentences; such is the unnecessary complexity surrounding them. Put simply, businesses require both a music service licensed for commercial use and licenses with all four U.S. performing rights organizations (PROs) — ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR — that cover the music they play.

Some licensed music services bundle performance rights, while others require businesses to secure separate public performance licenses from U.S. PROs. For a business owner, working out exactly what’s included can be a job in itself.

Too often I speak to business owners who believe they’re fully licensed but are missing key PRO licenses or still playing music via their personal streaming account. The survey reflects this, finding only 5% of businesses hold licenses from all four U.S. PROs.

However, this isn’t a story about bad actors. Nine in 10 businesses said compliance matters to them, and most said it matters that artists are paid fairly. The businesses using personal streaming accounts are doing it because it’s what they know. It’s easy, familiar and often free. It’s not a problem of intent; it’s a lack of information and a non-user-friendly system.

The cost to music creators and other rightsholders is real. Combining what businesses told us they spend on music with the number of establishments recorded by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, we estimate that annual spending on music would rise by about $1.4 billion per year if every business was properly licensed through a compliant business music service. That money should flow to songwriters, artists, publishers, labels and the services that license them legally.

The music industry shares the blame. No one proactively educates the market or gives a business owner a clear answer about what they need to be fully covered. Consumer platforms offer free tiers, teaching a generation of shop owners that music costs nothing. And business music services, mine included, have not explained the difference loudly or simply enough.

Enforcement alone won’t close a gap this wide. You can’t sue three million locations into compliance, and you shouldn’t want to. These are the customers who say music is essential to them. The answer is a coordinated push across trade organisations, PROs, publishers, labels, consumer platforms and business services to create one plain-language message about what is legal, provide simpler routes to full licensing and build services businesses actually want to use.

That last part matters. Businesses told us what they look for in streaming services designed for businesses: ease-of-use, price and good playlists (the main complaint about business music services is that their music selections are limited and repetitive). The music offered by these services has to be at least as good as the consumer app on the owner’s smartphone — or the consumer app will keep winning.

Streaming rebuilt the recorded music industry by making the legal option easier for individuals than piracy. Now, the same needs to happen for businesses. The demand is already there, and most want to do it right. What’s missing is clarity, and a legal option that’s simple to use and offers better selections.

That $1.4 billion isn’t lost. It’s waiting for the industry to make doing the right thing the easy thing.

Bear markets are a regular part of investing. Since 1928, the S&P 500 has fallen into one -- a drop of at least 20% from a recent high -- about every 3.5 years, on average, according to Hartford Funds and Ned Davis Research. The typical bear market has erased about 35% of the index's value.

For anyone with many years until retirement, here's why I'd stay fully invested.

According to Hartford and Ned Davis, the S&P 500 has had 28 bull markets and 27 bear markets since 1928. Through all those ups and downs, it delivered a compound annual return of about 10%, according to The Motley Fool's research. The market rises more in a bull market than it loses in a bear market.

It pays to keep investing when things look worst. Capital Group tracked the 18 biggest market declines since the Great Depression and found the S&P 500 was higher five years later every time. MFS found that after the 10 worst calendar years since 1975, a $100,000 investment was worth about $207,000 on average five years later.

Don't think that you'll be better off waiting to invest until the market moves higher. In fact, history says you'll be wealthier by staying fully invested through every down year.

A bear market is, indeed, coming, but we don't know when. The best move is to stay invested and not sell when the market goes down, because missing the biggest up days after the bottom will cost you a lot more money down the road than if you just stayed invested.

Time in the market is far more important than market timing. From 1970 through September 2026, every 20-year rolling period for the S&P 500 produced a gain, according to Mellon data published by Ave Maria Mutual Funds.

Even the worst timing can still make money for patient investors. Hartford calculated that investing $10,000 at the peak just before each of the six bear markets since 1995 would have still turned $60,000 into $364,774 by the end of 2025. That beats staying in cash and earning almost nothing.

Europe’s Inland Barges Need a Cargo Plan Before an Electric Fleet Plan

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Coal throughput at Rotterdam rose 15.3% in the first nine months of 2026. Crude oil and petroleum-product volumes also increased. An inland vessel operator looking only at this year’s cargo market could see decades of work ahead. But an engine, battery conversion or replacement vessel bought now may still be operating in 2050, when the mix of cargo travelling through European ports and rivers will be different.

Rotterdam’s latest throughput release explains the coal rebound through recovering coking-coal movements, expensive natural gas and power-market conditions. Meanwhile, Eurostat reports that EU inland-waterway freight fell 3% in 2025 to 118 billion tonne-kilometres. Coal and crude-petroleum freight rose that year, while agricultural freight contracted. These changes are reshaping the services that ports, vessel owners and customers need to buy.

The Central Commission for the Navigation of the Rhine counted 6,224 dry-cargo vessels in Rhine countries in 2025, down from 6,410 in 2023. Smaller vessels account for much of the decline. Tankers moved the other way, rising from 1,400 in 2022 to 1,477 in 2025. The numbers describe a fleet already adapting to changing cargo and operating economics.

My TFIE Strategy Briefing on the inland barges Europe will need in 2050 uses a cargo-first model to work from the freight task to the future electric fleet. The underlying 98-page Inland Freight Electrification report is available to read.

A vessel owner has two connected capital decisions. The first is whether the freight service will still have customers through the useful life of the asset. The second is which propulsion and charging system can perform that service economically. Starting with equipment and then finding a market for it risks spending scarce investment capital on the wrong vessels.

I modelled a large European industrial port corridor from a 2025-style baseline to 2050. The central scenario assumes bulk cargo falls from 57.8 million tonnes to 34.4 million tonnes, a decline of about 40%. Wet bulk accounts for much of the loss, falling from 34.5 million to 15.0 million tonnes. Dry bulk declines from 23.3 million to 19.4 million tonnes. The model is a transparent scenario for investment decisions across this type of corridor, rather than a forecast of Rotterdam’s exact future cargo volumes.

The largest change is fossil-linked cargo. Fossil fuels and refinery streams fall from 19.0 million tonnes to 1.5 million tonnes, while coal and other solid fossil fuels fall from 2.5 million tonnes to zero. Combined, those reductions account for about 20 million tonnes of the scenario’s 23.4-million-tonne net decline. Any operator whose principal customers depend on moving coal or petroleum faces a different replacement decision from one carrying grain, construction minerals, chemicals or scrap.

Steel illustrates what cargo substitution means. The model reduces imported iron ore and other virgin iron units from 4.5 million tonnes to 3.0 million tonnes, while scrap and circular feedstocks rise from 1.8 million to 3.5 million tonnes. The industrial function continues as the materials moving to and from factories change. Existing barges and terminals may find new work, although parcel sizes, loading systems and routes will also change.

Biofuel feedstocks create a second trap. In the central scenario those movements peak near 5 million tonnes in 2030 before falling to 1.5 million tonnes in 2050. A temporary freight boom can support profitable operations without supporting a new vessel or terminal investment with a thirty-year life. The durability of the contract matters as much as the volume carried this year.

Cargo tonnage alone cannot size an electric fleet. The model translates commodities into powered vessel-equivalents using parcel sizes, routes, loaded calls and productivity. It starts with around 1,150 powered vessel-equivalents and ends with 664, a reduction of 42%. Dry cargo falls 17% in tonnes but 43% in powered vessel-equivalents because the modelled freight service changes. The starting 1,150 powered equivalents represent about 1,234 physical hulls after counting unpowered barges moved by pushers. Battery conversion decisions concern powered units; terminal and cargo-handling decisions also concern the additional hulls.

That distinction is particularly important for pushed formations. A pusher provides propulsion for several cargo barges. Converting one powered unit does not necessarily mean replacing every physical hull. Counting all hulls as propulsion conversions would inflate the equipment and financing requirement.

Low Rhine water levels bring a different constraint into the same calculation. A barge can remain on the register yet carry less freight per journey when draught is restricted. Cargo owners may have to buy more voyages, use different hulls or shift some freight to rail and road. Route depth, seasonal load factors and terminal access belong in the service model alongside cargo demand.

Ports should begin with a living cargo and route register showing which freight is declining, which remains, and which changes its material inputs. Vessel owners can connect that register to remaining hull life, annual utilization, cargo contracts and the cost of electric conversion. Utilities and terminal operators can then size grid connections, charging facilities and battery-container pools against vessels that will run on bankable routes. Infrastructure developed around dependable freight customers has a stronger prospect of earning a return.

Europe has good reasons to electrify established, high-utilization inland freight now. Battery-powered vessels are already serving container and bulk routes. The commercial opportunity is to invest in routes with surviving customers and electrify the services they will continue to buy. Expanding an electric fleet before measuring that freight task puts the order of decisions backward.

When someone loses vision in one eye while taking a GLP-1 drug, the first priority is an urgent eye examination. If the diagnosis is non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION), the question that follows is: should we stop the drug?

On November 9, FDA announced it is requiring new warnings across all products in the GLP-1 drug class, which includes semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Zepbound, Mounjaro), among others, about a possible increased risk of NAION.

NAION occurs when inadequate blood flow damages the nerve that carries visual information from the eye to the brain. It can cause sudden, painless vision loss, often noticed on waking. The loss can be permanent, and there is no proven treatment to reverse it. The condition is rare, but these medications are widely used: in a March 2026 KFF poll, about one in eight U.S. adults reported currently taking a GLP-1 drug and one in five reported ever taking one.

As an ophthalmologist, I have routinely asked patients with suspected NAION about GLP-1 use for the past 2 years. I welcome the warning. The FDA should now pair it with a plan to study what happens when patients stop, continue, or change treatment after a NAION diagnosis.

People who develop NAION in one eye are already at increased risk of developing it in the other. How GLP-1 drugs might contribute remains uncertain. One hypothesis is that rapid reductions in blood sugar could cause swelling where the optic nerve enters the eye, compromising blood flow in susceptible patients. That possibility does not tell us whether continuing the medication adds to the risk in the other eye, or whether stopping reduces it.

These medications can be difficult to give up. Some patients take them to manage diabetes that has resisted other treatment. Certain drugs in the class also reduce the risk of heart attacks or worsening kidney disease in eligible patients. Stopping requires a plan for the condition being treated. When counseling patients who develop NAION while taking a GLP-1 drug, I recommend involving the prescribing doctor before changing treatment.

Regulators around the world have responded differently to this dilemma. European guidance says to stop semaglutide if NAION is confirmed. The FDA, whose warning covers the broader drug class, advises clinicians to consider discontinuation unless the benefits clearly outweigh the risk. I support that approach, with the patient, ophthalmologist, and prescribing doctor weighing the potential harm to vision against the benefits of treatment with a GLP-1.

Across 126 clinical trials, the FDA identified 15 probable NAION cases. The rates were equivalent to roughly six cases per 100,000 patients followed for a year among those receiving GLP-1 drugs, compared with four among those receiving a placebo or another diabetes medication. Larger observational studies have also raised concern, though their estimates vary and they cannot fully separate the effects of treatment from the conditions that led patients to receive it.

But estimating the risk of developing NAION does not tell us whether a patient should continue a GLP-1 drug after NAION diagnosis. One major FDA study excluded people with previous damage to the optic nerve from inadequate blood flow to help identify new cases accurately. That was a sensible design for its purpose, but it also means the study cannot establish whether continuing treatment after a diagnosis changes the risk to the other eye.

Some research has examined second-eye involvement. An analysis presented at the American Academy of Ophthalmology annual meeting this fall found that, among patients who developed NAION in both eyes, the second eye was affected sooner in GLP-1 users. It did not establish whether stopping would have prevented that outcome. Nor do we have reliable comparative evidence that switching to another GLP-1 drug is safer.

The FDA should assess whether its existing safety surveillance can answer these questions. If those systems fall short, the agency should require manufacturers to support additional studies. Federal law gives the agency that authority for serious safety concerns when routine reporting and available surveillance are insufficient.

The work could begin in participating eye clinics, linking confirmed diagnoses to prescribing and medical records. Researchers would document which medication a patient was taking, whether it was stopped or changed, when, and why. They would follow vision in the other eye alongside overall health, including how well replacement treatment managed diabetes or other conditions. Researchers would need to account for differences in diabetes severity and visual damage that affect both treatment decisions and outcomes.

The FDA should publish the study plans, identify who is responsible, and set dates for progress updates. Funding should be disclosed and results analyzed independently of manufacturers. Because NAION is uncommon, firm answers may take years. Patients and clinicians should still be able to see what is being learned and what remains uncertain.

The patient deciding what to do about the next dose cannot wait years for better evidence. Today's decision will have to be made with what we know. We owe that patient our best judgment, and we should follow what happens afterward. The next person facing the same choice deserves more to go on.

Charleson’s story is unlikely to be one most are familiar with. Even the most public of deaths among the most publicly gay of people often still remain in hiding, though Charleson wasn’t out himself. It’s not that different now, even as many advancements have made HIV/AIDS feel locally like a plague of the past.

A blond-headed Scott throws himself valiantly into the role of Charleson with the urgency and commitment of any of his best work, but the problem isn’t his: It’s that the movie surrounding him dulls the greatness of his performance with its conventional storytelling, and over-complication in post. “Elsinore” is one of those movies where you can feel the filmmaker — here, Stone, known for both stage and screen — actively laboring to “open up the play” with the opportunities of film. Every thoughtful consideration of Kaposi’s sarcoma lesions, or the retching of blood into buckets under the care of dutiful practitioner Dr. Margaret Johnson (Olivia Colman) to show the terrible realities of the disease, is whipped into what feels like a feature-length trailer. A couple of things are going on: “Elsinore” triggers in a watchful gay viewer’s senses the signifiers of what would be the AIDS awards-bait movie of serious gravitas. There are others, though more lost to time. We become suspicious of this device, especially when paired with great acting. Is HIV/AIDS being exploited in the package of a would-be Oscar hit? Or are we showing young queer audiences how it was, how we got to where we are, and how where that is still isn’t perfect? It can be both, but the structure of “Elsinore” tips toward the former.

Then, there’s a veritable, cheeky who’s-who of British gay literati and luminaries lingering about “Elsinore’s” 1989 London milieu that’s irresistible in theory, but often drab in execution. There’s Eyre, of course, and Jarman (David Dawson, as excellent and tender and wounded here as he was in his screen breakthrough “My Policeman”). The afterburn of his relationship with Charleson lingers affectingly, though. After some drinks, he invites Charleson home, but they instead part with one of those tacit kisses shared by people who once loved each other but can’t make it work anymore. There’s too much history here. The lingering scorches of Charleson’s secret affairs and friendships with various (and out) men make for the best parts of “Elsinore,” but there’s no life to the telling. (Joe Locke plays a young man also ailing in Johnson’s AIDS clinic alongside Charleson.)

The pallid grayness of Mike Eley’s cinematography is probably supposed to mimic the finality of HIV/AIDS taking hold, but there’s no passion aesthetically to counterbalance what becomes absolute misery onscreen to watch. I’m not in the business of comparing one gay social drama to another, but Robin Campillo’s 2017 Cannes-winning French drama “Beats Per Minute” imbued an albeit more activist-oriented story of ACT UP fighting against AIDS suppression in Paris with such heat and spectacle. Watching someone die of AIDS in a room becomes a bore unless anyone is given a reason to keep on living.

That reason is, of course, supposed to be Charleson’s performance in “Hamlet,” which is relegated to a whirlwind sequence at the movie’s end after many pained rehearsals where he refused to drop out (and where Johnson encouraged him to). Scott has a mesmerizing hold on his scenes, and he’s compelling to watch in his repartee with Colman as Johnson, who treated the disease at a time when it was most stigmatized. She named her groundbreaking HIV testing clinic after him; that’s how much he impacted her. We are told how much Charleson mattered to people, but never shown it. Which unfortunately shuffles off “Elsinore,” named after the Danish town where “Hamlet” was set and where its main character died, off to the realm of HIV/AIDS movies seeking prestige over deep feeling.

Write down the month you’ll need the money, cross off every option you can’t cash out by then without a penalty, and put the money in whichever of the rest pays the most today.

Housing Starts Jump, But Drop in Permits Paints Dim Picture for Homebuilding

Construction has seen a significant slowdown from continued market challenges in supply chain and affordability issues. Despite a surprising uptick in housing starts in this month’s New Residential Construction report, economists say the overall picture remains troubled.

The latest data from the Department of Housing and Urban Development and the Census Bureau saw housing starts jump 9.9% month-over-month to a rate of 1,309,000, also seeing a 2.8% year-over-year rise.

The rise was directly due to a leap in multifamily starts, up 25.5% month-over-month and 22% year-over-year to a rate of 433,000. Multifamily construction did see an uptick in building permits in September by 5.9% month-over-month and 1% year-over-year, which appears to have translated into the uptick in starts.

Single-family starts also saw a month-over-month rise of 4% to a rate of 864,000, but were still down year-over-year by 3.2%.

Notably, September’s original starts rate of 1,221,000 was negatively revised to 1,191,000, reflecting the continuation of the slower trend in construction. Single-family starts were also negatively revised from a rate of 859,000 to 831,000.

National Association of Home Builders (NAHB) Chairman Bill Owens said that the gain in starts this month is “encouraging,” but “does not necessarily signal a sustained recovery.”

“Builder confidence weakened in November as higher mortgage rates and affordability challenges weighed on buyers,” he continued. “Stronger demand is needed to sustain construction, while meeting the significant long-term need for more housing requires easing regulatory barriers to home building.”

Danushka Nanayakkara-Skillington—NAHB’s assistant VP for Forecasting and Analysis—agreed with Owens’ sentiment, adding that the gain is “welcome,” but the “market remains constrained by elevated borrowing costs, rising construction costs and weakening builder confidence.”

“The monthly gain should be viewed cautiously as affordability challenges and economic uncertainty persist,” she continued.

Looking regionally, the Midwest and the South were up month-over-month and year-over-year, while the Northeast and the West were down on both counts.

The Midwest was up 48.4% month-over-month and 17.2% year-over-year. The South was up 10.5% month-over-month and 12.2% year-over-year. The Northeast was down 20.8% month-over-month and 34.5% year-over-year. The West was down 2.6% month-over-month and 11.6% year-over-year.

Realtor.com® Senior Economist Joel Berner described the signals presented in this month’s report as “mixed,” and noted that single-family construction is still down year-over-year.

“This month’s data is an improvement, but from a very low baseline readout in both September and last October,” he continued.

Despite the rise in starts in October, building permits did not paint a picture of a continued upward trend.

Permits were down 7.4% month-over-month and 8.7% year-over-year to a rate of 1,295,000. Both multifamily and single-family permits were down month-over-month and year-over-year. Multifamily permits were down 5.2% month-over-month and 0.4% year-over-year to a rate of 475,000. Single-family permits were down 8.7% month-over-month and 12.9% year-over-year to a rate of 772,000.

Berner noted that this was permitting’s “lowest level since the early days of the COVID-19 pandemic in May 2020.” He also called this month’s permit data a “disappointment,” as it demonstrated that builders are “slowing down activity at the top of their funnel, which will result in slower months of starts and completions later on.”

Permits were also down both month-over-month and year-over-year in the Northeast, South and West, while the Midwest was up on both counts.

Housing completions had a more mixed result in October. Completions were up 6.5% month-over-month to a rate of 1,272,000, but were down 10.4% year-over-year. Both single-family and multifamily completions were also up month-over-month (6.3% and 9%, respectively), and were also down year-over-year (-10.7% and -9%, respectively).

“If you focus on home starts, this month’s new construction data is a welcome improvement from last month’s, but zooming out for more context shows a residential construction industry that is still struggling to find its footing,” concluded Berner. ”(S)oftening permits will put downward pressure on starts and completions in the months to come. In a country facing a supply gap of over four million homes, this represents a step in the wrong direction.”

Emotional intelligence, the ability to notice and manage your own feelings and read other people’s, is a skill you can build. When an email or comment at work makes you tense, put the feeling into one word, such as irritated or worried, before you answer. Naming it gives you a moment to choose your reply.

A curt email or a sharp remark in a meeting takes seconds, and living it down takes far longer. If coworkers say you come across harsher than you mean to, those first seconds are what they’re reacting to.

Emotional intelligence grows with training. A 2019 analysis of 58 studies, published in Human Resource Management Review, found that training programs raised participants’ scores by a moderate amount.

Besides giving you a moment, naming the feeling may weaken the emotional reaction itself. In a brain-imaging study published in Psychological Science, people viewed upsetting images, sometimes naming the emotion in them and sometimes doing other tasks with them. Naming the emotion produced a weaker response in the amygdala, a part of the brain that reacts to threats, than the other tasks did.

After you name the feeling, add a pause. Give a tense email or message an hour before you answer, or a night when the reply can wait. In a conversation, listen to understand rather than to answer, and ask about the other person’s side before you give yours. Friction that keeps coming back with one coworker calls for resolving the conflict itself, not just a calmer reply.

Picture a team lead who gets a teammate’s report a day late. She notices she’s irritated, names the feeling to herself, and asks what got in the way instead of firing back “This was due yesterday.” The cause turns out to be late numbers from another team, a problem she can fix.

To check your progress, treat the result of any free online quiz as a starting point, not a verdict. Two or three people you trust will tell you more than a quiz can. Ask each how you come across under pressure, and work first on what their answers have in common.

The mortgage industry was built to help Americans buy homes. An aging America needs more from it.

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Survey of 2,000 homeowners 55 plus shows 88% prioritize staying home and 90% prioritize independence

For decades, the mortgage industry has helped Americans achieve one of their biggest financial goals: buying a home and building equity over time.

It’s a model we know well. Homeowners purchase properties, make mortgage payments, refinance when it makes financial sense and gradually accumulate wealth. Much of the mortgage industry is structured around supporting that journey.

But what happens after decades of building that wealth?

As homeowners approach retirement, the financial equation shifts. Instead of focusing primarily on acquiring a home and paying down debt, the question becomes how to use the wealth they’ve accumulated to support the years ahead. Housing presents a unique opportunity but also a challenge because, for many Americans, their largest financial asset is also the place they call home.

You can sell a stock without moving. Selling a home is another matter. Trading down and moving to a less expensive home to tap home equity is both expensive and difficult. This might be why our recent research shows that the desire to remain at home continues to grow stronger with age.

In a recent Longbridge Financial, LLC (NMLS #957935) study of more than 2,000 U.S. homeowners ages 55 and older, 90% said maintaining their independence was extremely or very important to their ideal retirement. Nearly as many (88%) said the same about remaining in their homes. Both priorities ranked above paying for health care, paying off debt, long-term care and providing financial support to family members.

The desire to age in place isn’t surprising. What’s particularly revealing is how strongly that sentiment differs across age groups.

Among homeowners ages 55 to 64, 85% rated remaining in their homes as extremely or very important. That figure rose to 88% among those ages 65 to 74 and 93% among homeowners 75 and older. The importance of maintaining independence followed a similar pattern, climbing from 87% in the youngest group to 96% in the oldest.

These are differences between age groups at one point in time, rather than a measure of how individual preferences change over time. Still, the pattern points to an important finding in understanding how homeowners think about retirement.

Over decades, homeowners accumulate substantial equity through mortgage payments and increases in property values. Yet the very home that holds the wealth is also where many want to spend their later years. Its financial value tells only part of the story. Giving a homeowner a choice to move to a less expensive, and thus less attractive, home to use their home equity may not be much of a choice at all.

Consider two retired households with the same net worth. One holds most of its wealth in savings and investments. The other holds much of it in home equity.

Their balance sheets might look similar, but their financial flexibility is different. The first household may be able to draw from liquid accounts to cover an unexpected expense. The second has wealth, too, but accessing it may involve borrowing against the home or selling it altogether. Both options come with trade-offs, particularly when remaining in the home is a priority.

For an industry accustomed to measuring property values, loan balances and equity, this is an important distinction. These figures describe a homeowner’s financial position, but not necessarily how easily they can access that wealth — or what they might have to give up in the process.

The same survey reinforces why this distinction matters. Among homeowners ages 55 to 64, 73% rated paying for health care as extremely or very important to their ideal retirement. That figure climbed to 85% among those 75 and older. While these findings don’t tell us how much homeowners will spend or whether they’ll tap into their home equity, they do highlight how financial priorities can shift across different stages of life.

Mortgage finance has traditionally centered on transactions: purchasing, refinancing, servicing and eventually paying off a loan. Those functions remain essential. But paying off a mortgage doesn’t end a homeowner’s financial relationship with their home.

For some older homeowners, selling or downsizing may be the right choice. Others may have sufficient income and savings to remain in place. Still others may consider home equity loans, lines of credit or reverse mortgages, depending on their financial circumstances, eligibility and the associated costs and risks.

The opportunity for our industry extends beyond any single product. It’s about understanding the role home equity can play in supporting homeowners through retirement, while recognizing the personal value they place on remaining in their homes.

It also requires recognizing that homeowners’ priorities differ. In our study, 93% of women rated independence as extremely or very important, compared with 88% of men. Similar five-point gaps appeared for remaining at home and paying for health care. While the research doesn’t explain why these differences exist, it underscores the importance of understanding homeowners as individuals rather than treating homeowners 55 and over as a single market with identical needs and priorities.

Housing has always been both an investment and a place to live. As homeowners age, the balance between those roles deserves greater attention from the industry that helped them achieve homeownership in the first place.

We’ve spent decades helping Americans build wealth through homeownership. The next opportunity is helping them understand how that wealth can support the years ahead, without losing sight of what they value most: independence, financial flexibility and, for many, the ability to remain at home.

Rents Rise Annually for First Time in 3 Years as Multifamily Building Boom Stalls

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The median asking rent posted its first annual increase in more than three years in October, fueled by a sharp nationwide pullback in multifamily construction.

October marks the first month of year-over-year rent growth since June 2023, according to the latest Realtor.com® monthly rental report. The increase was modest, with the median asking rent rising by just $1 to $1,692, but the underlying data suggests more hikes could follow.

Larger units drove the rebound. Two-bedrooms led the way, with median asking rent rising 0.3% year over year to $1,889, followed by one-bedrooms, which edged up 0.1% to $1,566.

Meanwhile, studio rents continued to lag, falling 1.2% from October 2025 to $1,411.

"I see this as the market beginning to rebalance," Lissette Calderon, CEO of Neology Group, a Miami-based real estate development firm, tells Realtor.com. "We had an extraordinary amount of new supply delivered over the last few years, and it takes time for that inventory to be absorbed. With construction slowing and demand for rental housing remaining strong, I expect rent growth to gradually return, particularly in markets where employment and household growth continue to support housing demand."

Realtor.com senior economist Jiayi Xu confirms that last month's rent turnaround hinges on a shifting supply pipeline.

The post-pandemic multifamily construction boom of 2021–22 flooded the market with fresh rental units completed in 2023–25, keeping prices in check.

Now, a broad cooling in multifamily construction is easing downward pressure on rent, just as high mortgage rates continue to keep families in the rental market.

"The path ahead largely depends on whether new multifamily deliveries remain strong enough to outpace that demand," says Xu.

Multifamily developers remain pessimistic about current building conditions, according to the latest quarterly survey from the National Association of Home Builders (NAHB) released last week.

The NAHB's Multifamily Production Index (MPI) had a reading of 41 out of 100, down 5 points year over year. Any reading below 50 indicates that more builders view market conditions negatively than positively.

"It is not surprising that the MPI is down year over year given supply-side headwinds such as high interest rates and a rising rental vacancy rate," says NAHB Chief Economist Robert Dietz.

According to the U.S. Census Bureau's Housing Vacancy Survey, the national rental vacancy rate reached 7.6% in the third quarter, the highest level since early 2014.

Kip Lewis, chairman of NAHB’s Multifamily Council, also points to construction costs rising relative to rents, along with delays getting projects approved and connected to roads and utilities.

"As a result, many projects are taking longer to complete," says Lewis.

Echoing Lewis, Calderon says the building pace comes down to the economics of development.

"Construction costs, financing terms, insurance, and operating expenses all affect whether a project can move forward," she says. "When projected rents don’t support the total cost, securing debt and equity becomes more challenging. Developers want to build, but the numbers have to work."

A closer look at the housing data reveals just how dramatically multifamily construction has shifted over the past three years.

Between 2023 and 2025, roughly 1.5 million units in buildings with five or more units were completed, an average of 499,000 per year, up 37.7% from 2020–22.

Those newly completed units boosted the total rental stock by 3.2%, driven by the multifamily starts boom of 2021–23, when ground was broken on more than 1.45 million units.

That surge in rental construction is now fading. In the third quarter of 2026, the multifamily pipeline shrank significantly across the board, with starts averaging 403,000, down more than 9% from a year ago amid soaring financing and construction costs. Units under construction similarly pulled back, averaging 674,000, marking a year-over-year decline of 2.7%.

Completions took the biggest hit, plunging 26% from a year ago to an annualized rate of 330,000 new rental units in the third quarter as the 2023–24 cooldown in housing starts worked its way through the pipeline.

"Given the year-over-year decline in units under construction and higher construction costs, we do not expect deliveries to rebound over the next 12 months," says Xu.

That outlook aligns with NAHB’s 2027 forecast, which predicts a further decline in multifamily starts.

"A thinner pipeline could create pressure in markets where demand continues to grow, but it doesn’t necessarily mean we’re headed for a severe shortage or another sharp increase in rents," says Calderon. "That will depend on how much existing inventory remains available, local employment and household growth, and what is already under construction."

At the regional level, multifamily completions fell year over year everywhere in the third quarter due to fewer starts in 2023–24. The Northeast saw the most dramatic pullback, with finished units dropping nearly 50% from 2025 levels.

"Thinner deliveries could further push up rents in Northeast metros, especially those that have already returned to year-over-year growth, such as Philadelphia and Pittsburgh," says Xu.

The South came in a distant second, with completions falling 28% year over year, followed by the Midwest (-17.9%) and the West (-14.2%).

At the same time, units under construction fell over 11% in the Northeast and 8.8% in the South from a year ago, but increased 4.5% in the West and 12.9% in the Midwest, pointing to a split in active pipelines.

Despite the Midwest's robust building activity, rents are on the rise in several major market metros, including Chicago (+3.2%), St. Louis (+3.2%), and Minneapolis (+2.0%).

When it comes to new groundbreakings, starts in the Northeast surged a staggering 48.4% year over year, making it the only region in positive territory.

“Because projects take about two years to complete in the Northeast, that rebound is unlikely to lift deliveries in 2027," warns Xu.

By contrast, starts fell 12.4% in the West, 16.5% in the South, and 29.2% in the Midwest.

"I remain optimistic about South Florida over the long term, while recognizing that each project needs to be evaluated on its own merits," says Calderon. "For us, that means looking closely at location, the renter we’re serving, competing supply, and the total cost of delivery."

For renters in Western markets such as Los Angeles and Denver, who have seen some relief in asking rents, the slowdown in new construction could reverse that trajectory going forward.

A separate study from RentCafe broadly dovetails with the Realtor.com findings, reinforcing the narrative that the supply of rental units is shrinking nationwide.

Based on data from Yardi Matrix, a commercial real estate intelligence platform that relies on a distinct methodology from Realtor.com, the report found that new apartment construction dropped 27% in 2026, with the Northeast standing out as the sole region to see building activity rise, led by New York City.

The order does not merely prohibit threats or harassment. A judicial order "forbidding certain communications when issued in advance of the time that such communications are to occur" is a prior restraint…. "Any system of prior restraints on communication bears a heavy presumption against its constitutional validity." Such restraints are unconstitutional "except in exceptional circumstances." .As the Supreme Court held in Organization for a Better Austin v. Keefe (1971), {the party seeking to suppress speech} "carries a heavy burden of showing justification for the imposition of such a restraint." …

The remedy for false or injurious statements lies in "actions under libel laws providing for redress and punishment, and not in proceedings to restrain the publication." [But t]he October 12 Order … does not impose consequences for particular statements adjudicated unlawful. It prohibits Mr. Hughes from speaking about entire categories of people in the future. That is a prior restraint in its most direct form which is presumed unconstitutional….

Test Masters Educ. Servs., Inc. v. Singh (5th Cir. 2005) … recognized that a court may enjoin "harassing communication" and "repeated invasions of privacy." But an injunction prohibiting all communication, rather than limiting itself to "threatening or harassing conduct," "swept too broadly" and constituted an unconstitutional restraint. Id.…

Plaintiffs cannot justify the restraint merely by characterizing Mr. Hughes's commentary as malicious, coercive, or injurious. The Supreme Court rejected that reasoning in Keefe. There, the Court held that an individual's "interest in being free from public criticism of his business practices" did not justify an injunction against speech…. Plaintiffs may have remedies for unlawful conduct. But those remedies do not include an order forbidding Mr. Hughes from speaking about them at all….

Defendant also argues (again correctly, I think) that the order was unconstitutional because defendant wasn't given an opportunity to be heard before the order was entered:

The absence of notice presents a separate constitutional defect. In Carroll v. President & Commissioners of Princess Anne (1968), the Supreme Court set aside a short ex parte order restraining expression because of "a basic infirmity in the procedure by which it was obtained." The order "was issued ex parte, without notice to petitioners and without any effort, however informal, to invite or permit their participation in the proceedings."

There is a place in our jurisprudence for ex parte issuance, without notice, of temporary restraining orders of short duration; There is no place within the area of basic freedoms guaranteed by the First Amendment for such orders where no showing is made that it is impossible to serve or to notify the opposing parties and to give them an opportunity to participate.

The Court also rejected the suggestion that a later hearing could cure the problem. The availability of a subsequent opportunity to be heard "does not overcome the infirmity in the absence of a showing of justification for the ex parte nature of the proceedings."

The reason is straightforward. "The value of judicial decision-making … is substantially diluted where the process is ex parte, because the Court does not have available the fundamental instrument for judicial judgment: "an adversary proceeding in which both parties may participate." And where First Amendment freedoms are at stake, any order must be "couched in the narrowest terms that will accomplish the pin-pointed objective."

The October 12 Order fails both principles. It was entered without notice or an adversary hearing. It contains no finding that notifying Mr. Hughes was impossible. And far from identifying a narrow category of unlawful speech, it prohibits virtually any online commentary concerning the Plaintiffs, their families, or their counsel.

Carroll is directly controlling on the constitutional defect. An ex parte order restraining expression cannot stand without a showing that notice and participation were impossible. No such showing appears here….

Sotheby's makes last-minute deal to sell $180m Van Gogh painting following dispute

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Blaquier family, from Argentina, bought Blossoming Chestnut Trees (Châtaigniers en fleurs) (May 1890) in 1975

Sotheby’s today announced that a settlement has been reached over the sale of Van Gogh’s Blossoming Chestnut Trees (Châtaigniers en fleurs) (May 1890). The work has been poised to achieve a record price for the artist but became the subject of a claim by the heirs of its Jewish former owners, who called for the work to be withdrawn from the 18 November auction in New York.

The Sotheby’s statement records that the auction house is “pleased to confirm that an agreement has been reached between the Blaquier-Arrieta family and all of the legal descendants of Hedwig and Samuel Fischer regarding the sale of Vincent van Gogh’s Châtaigniers en fleurs”. The sale will now proceed as planned.

The work is estimated at around $180m ($207m with fees) and has been guaranteed by Sotheby's to sell. This is far in excess of the previous record of $117m, paid at Christie’s in 2022 for Van Gogh’s Orchard with Cypresses (April 1888).

The Blaquier-Arrieta family, from Argentina, bought Blossoming Chestnut Trees in 1975. As the Sotheby’s statement explains: “In recognition of the painting’s complex Nazi-era history and the suffering endured by the Fischer family during the Holocaust, the two families have reached a mutually satisfactory resolution and the work will be sold pursuant to their agreement.”

The provenance in the Sotheby’s catalogue entry notes, as it has done since last month, that the painting was acquired by the Berlin publisher Samuel Fischer and his wife Hedwig in 1909. Their son-in-law, Gottfried Bermann Fischer, deposited the Van Gogh with the Amsterdam gallery Paul Cassirer in 1932 or 1933—and it was then bought by Paris collectors Henriette and Marcel Kapferer in 1936.

Last Thursday (12 November), in a surprise move, the Berlin-based restitution researcher Gunnar Schnabel sent a letter to Sotheby’s, on behalf of the Fischer heirs. He questioned the 1936 sale—which he says took place three months after the family had left Berlin for Vienna—asking Sotheby’s to prove that the picture had been sold at its market value and that its sale was not forced. In the meantime, Schnabel asked for this week’s sale to be delayed.

In a statement, the Fischer heirs commented: “We are grateful for the trusting and fair negotiation and collaboration with Sotheby’s and the Blaquier-Arrieta family, which make it possible to acknowledge the suffering of the Fischer family during the Nazi era and honour Gisela Berman Fischer's [granddaughter of Samuel and Hedwig] history of seeking justice for her family.”

The Blaquier-Arrieta family commented: “We are pleased to have reached an amicable resolution with the Fischer heirs. We appreciate the constructive dialogue that made this resolution possible and look forward to an extraordinary result and to passing along this masterpiece to a new generation of collectors.”

No details have been released on the nature of the agreement and whether the Fischer family will receive financial compensation.

What impact, if any, the short-lived dispute will have on the sale will only become apparent with the bidding.

The Hidden Financial Stress of Thru-Hiking

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

For better or worse, I was born with the fiscal sensibilities of a Depression-era grandmother. This can be both an asset and a liability for thru-hiking. Back in the day, my frugal spending habits made it easier to save enough money to comfortably thru-hike the Appalachian Trail, but they were also an unexpected handicap once I was actually on trail.

People rarely talk about how psychologically challenging it can be to live with the stress of spending without earning for the six-ish months that a long thru-hike usually takes, and I definitely wasn’t prepared for it. Money stress comes in many forms, but most people experience it one way or another — and that struggle can follow you out onto the trail.

In today’s challenging economy, it’s more important than ever for aspiring thru-hikers to consider not just whether they can afford to hike, but how they’ll cope with the psychological stress of watching their savings dwindle with each passing mile. It’s easy to take fiscal responsibility too far and get caught up in an unhelpful scarcity mindset. Don’t let that be you.

Hiking is hard. Don’t make it harder by beating yourself up for literally no reason.

Thru-hikers often talk about how easy it is to spend way too much money on zero days. For me, spending money in town was one of the hardest things about the trail. I’m not saying I didn’t spend — the lure of a soft bed and a hiker-sized restaurant meal was usually enough to overpower my frugality — but I agonized over every purchase in every single town. There were times when I even felt so guilty about the spending that I considered ending my hike early.

It wasn’t just that I was spending money without replacing it; it was that I wasn’t doing anything about it. I wasn’t applying for jobs or upskilling or preparing myself to reenter the job market. I was just existing in the woods day in and day out, being thoroughly unproductive, with no particular plan for after the trail. This was apparently too much for my little capitalist brain to handle.

This mindset would have been more reasonable if lack of funds was a serious threat to my thru-hiking goals. But I had been fortunate enough to save enough money that I didn’t need to worry about running out mid-hike. I spent half my time worrying about running out of money for literally no reason.

Me mentally berating myself for overspending on name-brand peanut butter, probably.

What is the solution? Should I have saved even more money ahead of time? Of course that wouldn’t hurt, but such is the nature of anxiety that I doubt it would have stopped me from stressing. Should I have taken out a Coursera subscription and spent my evenings in camp learning how to code, or treated every impromptu hiker gathering like a networking event?

Obviously not. In reality, there’s usually no easy solution to the assorted neuroses that play out in our heads. In my case, it was just a matter of learning to live with the uncertainty of not having an income or a guaranteed path back into paid work after the hike. I was afraid of not having enough or of being unable to find another job, and I just had to face that fear.

The AT forced me to confront my own limiting beliefs around money. In the years since that first thru-hike, I’ve gotten a little more relaxed about spending money on things I know I can afford without feeling guilty about it. I haven’t abandoned my frugal tendencies, but they don’t need to dictate every decision I make. I credit the trail for that growth.

“I wonder if I can get another 200 miles out of these shoes before I replace them. I wonder if I should get on a cheaper insurance plan next year. Do I have enough money to buy name-brand peanut butter in the next town? Should I get a different credit card? Should I go stoveless to save on fuel? “

I’ve written a few articles over the years about my peculiar hiking-related neuroses. As always, I want to clarify in the midst of all this doom and gloom that thru-hiking was and is one of the great joys of my life, despite my assorted pathologies and the mistakes I’ve made along the way.

Every thru-hiker will face struggles both external and internal; learning to deal with and overcome them is part of the experience, and this is just one more example. As always, do everything you can to prepare for your thru-hike, accept that you can’t plan for everything, and then enjoy the journey, hardships and all.

The company is simultaneously looking for a successor. Directors reportedly approached Arm CEO Rene Haas, a former Nvidia executive, according to the Financial Times, although Nvidia said it had not formally engaged candidates and called speculation premature.

The movement to restore Huang has nevertheless attracted powerful allies. President Donald Trump said Monday he “would certainly not mind” a comeback, while Elon Musk backed a petition demanding Huang's reinstatement that had drawn nearly 192,000 signatures by Monday.

OpenAI CEO Sam Altman offered a more qualified defense. Altman, who was briefly fired and reinstated at OpenAI in 2023, said Huang had “long been a hero of mine” and that “heroes have flaws,” but stopped short of saying Nvidia should rehire him.

Huang also made a patriotic appeal, especially considering his outsting was especially rushed not because of his affairs but because of the possibility of him leaking secrets to China. “I came to the United States 53 years ago, and becoming an American citizen is one of the greatest gifts and proudest moments of my life,” he wrote.

“NVIDIA has been my life’s work,” he continued. “Nowhere but in America could a company like NVIDIA be possible. I love our country, and I love NVIDIA.”

He closed by thanking supporters and telling his family, “I love you, and I will spend the rest of my life being worthy of yours.” Nvidia, meanwhile, is continuing its permanent CEO search, with Kress scheduled to lead the company's quarterly earnings call later today.
