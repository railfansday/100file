From equipment to ferries to locomotives, Illinois ports are getting a ZEV makeover

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Illinois Clean Ports Program is putting $104 million behind efforts to help clean up its working waterfronts, funds earmarked for heavy-duty electric equipment assets that includes forklifts, giant reach stackers, switcher locomotives, and the charging equipment needed to support them. There’s even a passenger ferry!

Procurement is now underway for zero-emission vehicles, equipment, and supporting infrastructure at four sites across the state. Administered by the Illinois Environmental Protection Agency (Illinois EPA), the Illinois Clean Ports Program aims to modernize operations at ports and waterfront facilities in Chicago, the Quad Cities, and southern Illinois’ river port districts.

“Illinois’ waterways are a competitive advantage for the state,” said Illinois Governor JB Pritzker, who was elected to a historic third term on 03NOV. “By modernizing port infrastructure, advancing zero-emission technology, and improving air quality, we are strengthening our economy while building a cleaner future for Illinois.”

Illinois’ Clean Ports Program isn’t just replacing diesel-powered equipment with electric alternatives – instead, the plan also includes Level 2 and DC fast charging, shore power and charging for electrified ships, and comprehensive grid upgrades designed to modernize existing port operations and support the deployment of new electric equipment.

The program takes a holistic approach to port electrification at Chicago’s Illinois International Port District (IIPD), treating energy generation, storage, and deployment as parts of a single connected system. On-site solar helps meet the port’s growing electricity needs while excess power is stored for peak hours, when grid electricity is most expensive. This setup reduces strain on the local grid, and acts as a hedge against rising energy costs.

For smaller public port districts, replacing aging equipment and upgrading electrical infrastructure can be an enormous financial undertaking. The Illinois Clean Ports Program helps make those investments possible, bringing cleaner, healthier, zero-emission technology within reach of terminal operators while preparing the state’s ports for a more competitive freight future.

Here's How Much A Boeing 737 MAX 8 Earns An Airline On A Single Day Of Flying

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In 2019, Boeing 737 MAX 8 operators were losing approximately $150,000 per aircraft per day in revenue. Today, each 737 MAX 8 generates around $200,000 per day, although airlines don't publish specifics, and revenue varies widely depending on how a carrier uses the aircraft.

The revenue made by each 737 MAX 8 per day is not pure profit. It is offset by depreciation or leasing costs, as well as operational and maintenance expenses. There is also a set-up cost when an airline first brings the jet into its fleet, requiring crew training and maintenance infrastructure, varying in severity depending on the degree of pre-existing fleet commonality with the Boeing 737 family.

Airlines are reluctant to publish exact data on how much money they earn for each aircraft in their fleet. However, answering this question is uniquely possible for the 737 MAX 8 because estimates emerged during the 737 MAX crisis. Each analyst had their own estimates at this time. IBA estimated total lost earnings of $150,000 per day. The Conversation reached a similar conclusion, based on three round trips a day and an average of 145 tickets sold per flight. It multiplied these sales by the US Department of Transportation average airfare in the US at the time of $343.28.

Southwest Airlines had the largest MAX fleet at the time with 34 aircraft, generating approximately $5 million in lost revenue per day. Across the entire 737 MAX fleet globally, the losses would have been closer to an eye-watering $60 million per day. This is why the crisis was so disastrous for Boeing, as there was an expectation that the manufacturer would absorb much of these costs.

IBA also notes other costs that had to be absorbed. Leasing for the jet was estimated at $360,000 a month. Staffing costs for two flight crew and four cabin crew were estimated at $5,000 per day. Airlines also had to figure out ways to adjust or increase their capacity to honor passengers' pre-existing bookings. IBA states that the cost to deal with 1,000 disrupted passengers would have been $100,000.

There are two ways that we can translate the daily revenue in 2019 into today's terms. Firstly, the US Bureau of Labor Statistics shows an average annual inflation rate of 4.27% from 2019 to 2026. That means the $150,000 of revenue per aircraft per day in 2019 is now worth $201,130.

According to the Bureau of Transportation Statistics, this was $445.26 in the second quarter of 2026, although there is significant variation between airports. That produces an estimated daily revenue per 737 MAX 8 of $195,000, with the difference showing how air fares have not perfectly tracked the US dollar's overall inflation.

As with any business, the income a 737 MAX 8 makes from ticket sales is not profit. A range of substantial costs cuts into the margins. The largest is the cost of purchasing or leasing the jet. Both Boeing and Airbus no longer formally publish list prices for their aircraft. However, the market price for a MAX 8 was $55 million in 2025, with the leasing price being $400,000 per month, both of which are comparable to the Airbus A321XLR. An aircraft fresh off the production line will be substantially more expensive than a second-hand 737 MAX 8, much like when buying a car.

The cost of buying a 737 MAX is spread across 20 to 30 years of active service rather than factored directly into daily operational costs, although depreciation over time matters when an airline decides to sell the plane. The 737 MAX promises to be around 15% to 20% more fuel-efficient than similar jets of the previous generation, meaning that operating costs are substantially lower. Furthermore, the CFM LEAP engine has proven exceptionally reliable, cutting into maintenance costs.

Some costs also have a great degree of variation between airlines. For example, airlines must invest in crew training and maintenance infrastructure to keep the jet flying. The higher the degree of commonality within the fleet, the lower the cost. For example, Southwest Airlines operates an entirely 737 fleet of over 800 aircraft, so it would have suffered relatively minor excess costs when it first introduced the 737 MAX. Other airlines for whom the 737 MAX 8 will join no other Boeing narrowbody aircraft will have to pay much more to get set up.

The reason it is so hard to nail down precise daily revenues for a 737 MAX 8 is that there is such a high degree of variation depending on how the airline uses the jet. With turnarounds under 30 minutes, some low-cost airlines can fly their 737s for between 10 and 12 hours per day. Meanwhile, legacy carriers are generally less adept at maximizing daily flight time.

The 737 MAX's longer range and greater efficiency also mean it tends to be used differently than older 737 variants. Carriers often fly farther but with fewer flights with this jet. Varying capacity also changes revenue per flight and per day. Budget carriers pack as many passengers as possible onto every 737 MAX 8 flight, pushing the jet close to its absolute capacity of 210, while legacy carriers use premium seating at the cost of carrying fewer passengers. United Airlines configures its 737 MAX 8 with 164 or 168 seats, with significant room taken up by premium economy and economy, Seat Maps reports. Seat Maps also says that Allegiant Air crams 188 to 190 passengers into the same aircraft, with one economy-only configuration and one with a small number of first class seats.

The route overtakes Montréal–Trudeau International Airport to Nantes Atlantique Airport (NTE). The transatlantic flight was Air Canada's previous longest 737 MAX 8 route. From the Archive: Ina Garten Tapped a Few Famous Friends for Her '90s Farmhouse Renovation

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Take a look inside the sunny, five-room farmhouse.

Welcome to Archive Dive—my new excuse to obsess over the absolute treasures hiding in House Beautiful’s 130-year-old archives. Every month, we’ll dig up a favorite story from the past (epic room makeovers, wildly bold design swings, and style moments that were way ahead of their time) and talk about why they still resonate today. It’s part history lesson, part design deep-dive, and 100 percent a love letter to the iconic taste that’s defined HB for over a century.—Jo Saltz, House Beautiful Editor in Chief

After Ina Garten moved the now-shuttered Barefoot Contessa specialty store from Westhampton to East Hampton, she and her husband, Jeffrey, decided to put down roots in the neighborhood. But rather than buy a new-build home, Garten transformed an old farmhouse into a light-filled, five-room retreat designed for hosting—and feeding—guests.

In our latest Archive Dive, we revisit the 1994 renovation, which includes Garten’s search for the right paint color, her no-nonsense decorating approach, and her love of the property’s grounds. She took each project in stride with help from famous friends like Martha Stewart and the late interior designer Robert Currie—and Jeffrey, of course. The result was a relaxed home with an entertaining-ready atmosphere and a garden that would make any green-thumbed foodie proud. Read on for the original story.

With the help of Robert Currie, the owner of a fabulous food emporium in the Hamptons planned her nearby house for the casual ease of the legions of guests she feeds in her spare time.

Are you full of self-doubt in the dark of the night? Should you buy that old house? Should you change your career? Then follow in Ina Garten’s (bare) footsteps. She has done both with such breathtaking speed and success that the average worrier blinks in awe. She bought a Westhampton, New York, specialty store (oddly shaped breads, ravishing vegetables, seafood salad to go) from a business opportunities ad practically on the spot.

“I didn’t know how to slice smoked salmon or tell whether the Brie was ripe,” she confesses. But in no time, she turned her emporium, Barefoot Contessa, into a foodie’s fairyland.

After Garten moved the store to East Hampton she converted a cramped, run-down old farmhouse in the village into an airy five-room refuge and party venue for herself and her husband. “I just trusted that if the house had good ‘bones,’ I could figure it all out,” she laughs. No insomnia. No agonies. And, incredibly, no mistakes.

She’s a nurturer, too, a definite Earth Mother, which is certainly why she fled her career in nuclear energy policy planning for the far more congenial field of food. Her personal policy is summed up simply: lots of entertaining.

When her husband—a former Columbia professor, author, and investment banker—worked in New York, she encouraged his uptight fellow bankers to loosen their ties and shed their jackets by dishing up platters of fried chicken and other finger foods. In East Hampton she gives Sunday afternoon parties with plenty of easy food “like crabcake sandwiches,” and leaves all the doors open so guests will feel free to wander out and eat on the porch, or anywhere else that seems inviting.

She often serves in her sunny kitchen, which contains the requisite serious fridge and stove, but lacks the usual array of self-important utensils. This is a kitchen with nothing to prove.

The toughest decisions of her fix-up were the wall colors. She thought at first that she might like the whole house vanilla (a true foodie’s choice), but test patches were too white or yellow.

“I had samples all over the walls, and the painter was yelling ‘Stop already’,” but finally she hit on a toasty khaki for her bedroom. She was still stuck for the first floor when she spotted the bedroom primer paint: khaki halved with white. A mental lightbulb turned on, and the painter went to work. The house is now two values of that changeable shade with browny greens in the upholstery derived from the tapestry-covered living room sofa.

There is not a stick of unnecessary furniture in the farmhouse either, just several old oversize tables, a few pieces of portly upholstery, and in the bedroom, a brass bed (of which a friend said, “That’s not a bed, it’s a temple”), plus two chairs. No windows are curtained.

“Everything,” Garten feels, “should do something, have a function, not just sit there. I like things to be spare.” And bare as well, like her new floors, which are crafted of newly purchased old wood. Are the original floors still underneath? Nope. With a sanding and a coat of polyurethane, they are reincarnated as a kitchen countertop.

Garten actually bought the house for its grounds, because she yearned to garden. She had lived for a time in rented houses, but when she found herself planting expensive trees in rented lawns, she realized it was time to own some land.

Outside her kitchen doors she has cut square beds for tomatoes, which mix quite cheerfully with dahlias, and for roses knee-deep in catmint. She likes to combine “mess with structure,” but her structures are always imaginative. Instead of a single stake for each tomato plant, for instance, she does a row of tepees. Or she lines her disheveled strawberry beds with a spine of iris.

A Manhattan florist friend, Tom Pritchard of Pure Mädderlake, helped her with the garden design and did a very clever thing. He took two chairs out into the garden and walked around with her, scoping out the best seating spots. That’s how she decided which vistas to emphasize and which had too fine a view of the neighbor’s patio. Gardening has become a passion since she moved here. Every surface in her house is piled with well-thumbed horticultural tomes; for the time being, her food banks are relegated to the bottom of the pile.

Garten acknowledges that she has had a lot of help. Her good friend, the late Robert Currie, designed all the seating, shopped with her, and arranged the furniture. He also taught her to avoid like mad “anything that was too trendy.” Stephen Scanniello, the rose dean at the Brooklyn Botanic Garden, chose a hundred great roses for her, and Martha Stewart passed judgment on her new shower stall. No less helpful was her very supportive husband.

But husbands don’t ponder that midnight ceiling with you. And talented friends aren’t around at 2 a.m., when the real bullets are bitten. Some lucky people, though, do it all with ease—Ina Garten for one. Warm and comfy, like her own new house, she just turns over and goes back to sleep.

Randy Flores brings a former player's perspective to Houston Astros GM job - and knows its limits

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In his first year as St. Louis Cardinals scouting director, Randy Flores was watching a pitcher who threw a four-seam fastball and a curveball. The mix can be the backbone of a north-south pitching profile. But to Flores, a former major-league reliever, it looked suboptimal.

"I'm like, 'No, no, no - you sink it and slide it,'" said Flores, reciting an approach he gleaned from former Cardinals pitching coach Dave Duncan.

As a slight left-hander who did not throw especially hard, Flores carved out a playing career that lasted parts of eight major-league seasons. So, his methods had merit. But as an executive now, he recalls the scouting anecdote as something of a cautionary tale.

"Being a former player, if you're anchored too much to, 'This is how it was done when I was playing,' it's the wrong thing," Flores said.

Exploring other perspectives and means and finding a balance has been part of Flores' evolution as an executive, one that led to his hiring this month as general manager of the Houston Astros. It is also an arc to which only a handful of the majors' current highest-ranking baseball executives can relate.

Of the current presidents of baseball operations, Jerry Dipoto (Mariners), Chris Young (Rangers), Craig Breslow (Red Sox) and Buster Posey (Giants) played in the majors. So did general managers Chris Getz (White Sox) and Flores, whom the Astros hired after 11-plus seasons in St. Louis' front office.

That many top executives did not play in the majors, or at all professionally, illustrates how it is far from required. It might even invite the question of how such experience informs former players' approach to the role, if at all, as Flores settles into running a baseball operations department for the first time.

"You probably have more empathy … for what a player goes through, from the moment they sign their first professional contract to when they get to the big leagues," Dipoto said at last week's GM meetings in Las Vegas.

Dipoto and Breslow were relievers in the majors, like Flores. Dipoto posited that background could help "to understand the life of the guy who (is) not always the most thought-of player on the roster."

"Outside of that, I think it's just business as usual," Dipoto said. "We each went through our own, I guess, apprenticeship (after playing).

"You take the lessons you learn and apply them, just like any other general manager does when they get their first job. We just maybe have that slight - I don't even want to call it an advantage - that slight difference in that we have lived life in these players' shoes and probably as a result have a little bit more empathy rather than just frustration. It's a hard game."

Mariners GM Jerry Dipoto, a former major league reliever, is among a half-dozen ex-players who head up baseball operations departments. He said that experience includes empathy for players' journeys through pro ball. (Lindsey Wasson/AP)

After his playing career, Dipoto held roles in scouting before becoming general manager of the Angels in 2011 and for the Mariners in 2015. Breslow spent five seasons in the Cubs' front office, working heavily in pitching development, before the Red Sox hired him to lead their baseball operations.

"My hope would be it adds a level of credibility to the role and a level of empathy," Breslow said, "given that there really isn't anything I ask player to do that I haven't kind of been through myself, having been traded, promoted, released, started games, come in from the pen.

"And I think understanding the mindset of a player and how they're likely to respond and react to different suggestions and requests, I'm hopeful (that) is helpful in terms of understanding how to approach certain conversations."

Flores, 51, spent several years out of professional baseball after pitching his final season in the minors in 2011. He returned to the University of Southern California, his alma mater, to pursue a master's degree with a self-described goal to "chart a career path combining business and sports." He started a company that captured and provided amateur game video for use by recruiters and scouts.

In 2015, the Cardinals sought a scouting director to replace Chris Correa, who was dismissed and later imprisoned for illegally accessing the Astros' computer database. John Mozeliak, then St. Louis' GM, recalled that the Cardinals did not want to overhaul their draft process, which was producing results. Flores, who played five seasons in St. Louis, interviewed and landed the job, his first in a front office.

"There's more to leading a department than just believing in a model and running with it," Mozeliak said this month. "But I think his interpersonal skills are outstanding. I think he has the ability to walk into any type of room and be able to speak to the group."

New Astros GM Randy Flores (left), pictured with former Cardinals teammate Matt Morris, pitched in the majors for parts of eight seasons before moving into front-office work with St. Louis. (MARK HUMPHREY/AP)

Flores oversaw St. Louis' amateur drafts from 2016-26, including for the past eight seasons as an assistant general manager. In recent years, Flores gained experience in other areas of the Cardinals' operations including pro scouting and salary arbitration, he said at his introductory press conference in Houston.

"He's definitely ambitious," said Mozeliak, who left St. Louis after the 2025 season and is now the Angels' interim general manager. "And there was a certain point where he started to ask to get more exposure to different elements of baseball operations. So as he was having early success on the scouting side, it was just natural to keep letting him evolve and giving him exposure to more of the things that you really see under the hood when you're working with or as a general manager."

Chaim Bloom joined the Cardinals as an advisor in early 2024 and succeeded Mozeliak as their president of baseball operations. Is Netflix a Buy in November? The Bull and Bear Cases.

Netflix shares have fallen nearly 47% from their 52-week high. Is this a rare opportunity to buy a highly profitable entertainment business at a reasonable valuation?

By Lawrence Nga – Nov 22, 2026 at 7:15AM EST

Netflix (NFLX -2.96%) has had a difficult year. Its shares closed at $62.15 on Nov. 13, nearly 47% below their 52-week high. Yet the streaming giant continues to generate billions of dollars in revenue and profits. The sell-off raises an interesting question: Has Netflix become a bargain, or are investors right to worry about its future growth?

I cautiously lean toward the bull case. Netflix remains a highly profitable business with several ways to grow earnings over the next five years. However, its slowing growth and advertising challenges mean investors shouldn't expect an immediate recovery.

Netflix's ability to turn revenue into profits has improved over time.

In the third quarter of 2026, revenue increased 11.8% year over year to $12.87 billion. Operating income grew much faster, rising 37% to $4.46 billion. Its operating margin also improved to 34.6%, compared with 28.2% a year earlier.

In simple terms, Netflix is keeping more money from every dollar of revenue.

Management expects full-year revenue of $51.1 billion and operating income growth of more than 20% in 2026. It also expects its operating margin to reach 31.5%, up from 29.5% in 2025.

While operating leverage contributed to the expansion of operating margin, Netflix has also demonstrated pricing power. For instance, management said customers responded to its 2026 subscription price increases as expected, while customer cancellations improved year over year across every region.

Advertising offers another opportunity. Netflix expects advertising revenue to reach approximately $2.7 billion in 2026, despite delays in launching some advertising products. Management expects growth of more than 80% this year.

If Netflix can improve its advertising technology and earn more from its existing audience, advertising could become another meaningful contributor to future profits.

The biggest concern is that Netflix's growth is becoming harder to sustain. For perspective, revenue growth declined from 17.6% in the fourth quarter of 2025 to 11.8% in the third quarter of 2026. Management expects 11.5% growth in the fourth quarter.

That's still healthy, but investors have become accustomed to high growth. As Netflix grows, attracting new customers and raising subscription prices become more challenging.

Competition is another concern. Alphabet’s YouTube and TikTok compete for people's attention, while Amazon and other streaming services continue investing in entertainment. Netflix must keep producing popular shows and films to remain relevant.

Management has acknowledged that its execution could improve, particularly in the United States. Recent changes to its content leadership suggest it wants to strengthen its entertainment offering.

Advertising is also taking longer to develop than expected. Netflix reduced its 2026 advertising revenue forecast from $3 billion to $2.7 billion because some advertising products were delayed in launching.

These problems don't threaten Netflix's business today. But they could limit how quickly it grows over the next several years.

While nobody likes a declining share price, Netflix's valuation has become much more reasonable as a result.

At $62.15, its market capitalization is approximately $255 billion. Its price-to-earnings (P/E ratio) is around 17.7 times, compared with a much higher valuation in previous years. For instance, the stock traded at a P/E ratio above 30 just a year ago.

If Netflix can sustain or even improve its growth rate from here on, the stock's valuation looks rather attractive at the current level. Of course, if growth continues to slow further, the opposite is true as well.

On balance, investors may consider buying Netflix at today's prices, particularly those with a five-year investment horizon. The company has improving profit margins, a global audience, and opportunities to grow its advertising business.

But I'd build a position gradually rather than invest everything at once. The next few quarters should reveal whether Netflix can maintain its profitability while stabilizing revenue growth and improving advertising execution.

Netflix doesn't need to return to its previous growth rates to reward shareholders. It simply needs to keep growing profits at a healthy pace.

Laid-Off Textile Workers Ran Their Bosses' Hidden Exotics As A Museum For Two Years

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

What's the coolest automotive museum you've ever seen? What about the most mind-blowing piece of automotive history you've ever beheld with your own eyes? I live right down the street from the National Automotive Museum in Reno, NV, which is a great time, but it doesn't quite take the cake. There are some real gems out there, like the Gilmore Car Museum, which lets you drive a Model T, or the Studebaker National Museum, where you can watch footage from 1932 of grown men throwing cars down a hill for safety testing. But if I had a time machine and could visit any car museum anywhere in time and space, there's one in particular that I'd just love to see.

That would be the Schlumpf collection in Mulhouse, France, specifically during the two-year period in the mid-to-late-70s when it was run by laid-off textile workers after their bosses shut their factories down and fled the country. Yes, the story is as wild as it sounds.

Now housed in the Musée National de l'Automobile in Mulhouse, the Schlumpf collection is an astounding piece of both car collecting and labor history. It began as the personal (and, sure, slightly obsessive) collection of Fritz Schlumpf, a long-time Bugatti driver who, along with his brother Hans, ran a textile business with factories in Mulhouse. Born around 1906, Fritz Schlumpf began collecting Bugattis intentionally in the 1960s. He hunted them down with the help of the British Bugatti Owners Club, who helped him locate around 50 individual cars, plus a stash of 30 vintage Bugattis owned by John W. Shakespeare of Hoffman, Illinois. Over two years, Schlumpf and Shakespeare negotiated a purchase price, bringing Schlumpf's personal Bugatti collection up to 80. Then he bought 18 more off Ettore Bugatti himself. Schlumpf was only getting started.

Eventually, Fritz Schlumpf owned an estimated 105 Bugattis, which might've comprised as much as 20% of the Bugattis in existence at the time. Who can blame Schlumpf for apparently wanting them all? After all, Bugattis do drive like a dream, even the old ones. But Schlumpf hadn't just been buying up cars of his favorite French brand. He'd actually been amassing a mind-bogglingly large stable of rare cars, both vintage and contemporary.

By the 1970s, Schlumpf had over 400 cars stashed in an unused factory in Mulhouse that was being fitted into a museum. But in order to acquire them all during a worsening economy, he'd spent money from a government grant intended to keep his factories in operation. When his transgression was discovered, he promptly shut down all his businesses and fled to Switzerland, putting about 1,800 workers out of business.

In response, the workers decided they didn't need a boss and could keep things running without Schlumpf's oversight, so they formed a workers' co-op to keep themselves employed. When members of the co-op traveled to the supposedly unused factory in Mulhouse, what they discovered was a building packed to the gills with exotic cars. A collection the likes of which had never been seen. The workers collectively decided to run the museum while using it to spread the message of their cooperative to the wider world.

The museum was open for two years, with public access to the vehicles for a few hours a day, and it contained some real stunners, including gems like two Bugatti Type 52s, which were electric race cars sized for children. You can still see the collection today, though not exactly as Schlumpf originally had it. The collection was seized by officials and eventually reopened in 1982.

58 Years Later, Captain Kirk's Best Star Trek Quote Is the Greatest Line in Sci-Fi History

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

This Captain Kirk speech says more about what Star Trek stands for than any other line in the original series.

Some lines of dialogue become so famous they stop belonging to the movies and TV shows that produced them. “May the Force be with you.” “I’ll be back.” “You're gonna need a bigger boat.” You don't need to know the context. The words have long since transcended their original context and permeated the pop culture zeitgeist.

Star Trek: The Original Series has plenty of those. Spock’s wish that everyone to “Live long and prosper” is arguably the franchise's signature phrase. He also turned the simple word “fascinating” into a catchphrase simply by raising one eyebrow before saying it. Dr. McCoy (DeForest Kelley) spent three seasons reminding everyone, “I'm a doctor, not a…” and, at least in our collective memory declaring, “He's dead, Jim” while standing over the latest redshirt who wandered a little too far from the landing party.

But if I could only pick one line to represent everything Star Trek stands for, it would be four simple words spoken by Captain James T. Kirk (William Shatner) halfway through “Return to Tomorrow," the 20th episode of season 2:

On the surface, it doesn't seem like an obvious choice. It's not printed on T-shirts nearly as often as “live long and prosper.” It isn't endlessly quoted by people who have never watched an episode of Star Trek. And unlike “beam me up, Scotty,” it never became part of the broader pop culture vocabulary. But that's exactly why it's the best quote. It doesn't just capture Kirk. It captures the entire philosophy of Star Trek.

First broadcast on Feb. 9, 1968, the episode “Return to Tomorrow” finds the Enterprise answering a mysterious distress call from a dead planet. There, Kirk and his crew discover Sargon, one of three survivors of an ancient civilization that has existed as beings of pure energy for half a million years. Sargon asks Kirk, Spock (Leonard Nimoy), and Dr. Ann Mulhall (Diana Muldaur) to temporarily surrender their bodies so he, his wife Thalassa, and the ambitious Henoch can build android bodies and begin life again. Dr. McCoy believes the aliens are manipulating them. Kirk believes the chance to learn from a civilization hundreds of thousands of years more advanced than humanity is worth the danger.

The episode also marks the first of three Star Trek appearances by Diana Muldaur. She plays Dr. Ann Mulhall here before returning as Miranda Jones in the third season episode “Is There in Truth No Beauty?” Years later, she'd become a series regular on Star Trek: The Next Generation as Dr. Katherine Pulaski. “Return to Tomorrow” is also a fun piece of Trek trivia because it's one of the times Spock describes Sargon as “pure energy,” a phrase longtime fans immediately recognize.

Faced with allowing unimaginably powerful aliens to occupy the bodies of his crew, Kirk doesn't issue an order. He makes an argument and reminds his crew that every leap forward in human history required someone willing to embrace uncertainty.

"They used to say if man could fly, he'd have wings," Kirk says. "But he did fly. He discovered he had to." He likens Sargon's offer to humanity's own journey from the Moon to Mars and eventually the stars, arguing that the potential for knowledge outweighs the danger. Only then does he arrive at the four words that define both the captain and the franchise. Those four words explain Kirk better than decades of pop-culture impressions ever could.

For years, pop culture has reduced Kirk to an oversimplified caricature: the swaggering captain who punches first, flirts with every alien woman he meets, and somehow loses his shirt during every other away mission. The real Kirk is far more thoughtful than that. He takes risks, certainly, but not because he enjoys gambling. He takes them because he believes some opportunities are too important to ignore.

There's a world of difference between being reckless and accepting risk in pursuit of discovery. Kirk isn't arguing that danger is exciting. He's arguing that progress is impossible without it.

More importantly, he's explaining what separates Starfleet from almost every other organization. Plenty of fictional explorers travel because they're ordered to. Others do it for conquest, profit, or survival. Starfleet goes because knowledge itself is worth pursuing.

That's why I think this quote is even more meaningful than the famous opening narration about boldly going where no one has gone before. The opening tells us what the Enterprise does. This one explains why.

The timing of the episode makes the speech even more remarkable. “Return to Tomorrow” aired in early 1968, roughly 17 months before Apollo 11 landed on the Moon. America was still trying to prove humans could successfully reach another world, and Star Trek was already imagining the next step beyond that. Kirk's speech places the Enterprise in the same lineage as every explorer who came before it, suggesting humanity's future won't be built by playing it safe.

That idea has become the beating heart of Star Trek. Optimism, in the Star Trek universe, has never meant believing everything will work out. It means believing the unknown is worth facing anyway.

There are more famous quotes in The Original Series. There are funnier ones. There are certainly more quotable ones. But no other line distills what Star Trek has always been trying to say better than four words Kirk spoke nearly 60 years ago: “Risk is our business.”

“Race realism” isn’t realistic—and it isn’t good science

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Modern genetics does not support claims of innate racial differences in intelligence.

At this year’s World Economic Forum meeting in Davos, US President Donald Trump stated that people from Somalia have low IQs. “I always say these are low-IQ people,” he said in his speech. His son, Donald Trump Jr, made a similar statement about Haitian people in 2024 (Trump’s government has since ended the protected status of both Somali and Haitian migrants in the US).

These statements and their consequences are symptoms of a growing trend in Western politics: the re-emergence of racial views of human nature that have been largely in hiding for the past 70 years. Narratives about the innate intellectual inferiority of black or brown people compared with white people—which can still be found in academic journals today—are fuelling far-right propaganda in some of the world’s largest democracies. Such claims underpin misinformation strategies on social media and bolster political movements.

These narratives are sometimes euphemistically referred to as “race science” or more recently “race realism.” But, as we argue, they are not actually scientific. One member of this community, Nathan Cofnas, recently attracted public attention by accusing the late Jason Arday of plagiarism.

What got less attention was that Cofnas has links to a network of people who make supposedly scientific claims about the innate intelligence, criminality, or sexuality of different racial groups. Some within this network are affiliated with universities, giving their work the mask of legitimacy.

This group produces work that has the appearance of science, but which is methodologically deeply flawed and fails to engage with rigorous scientific research. Yet they regularly succeed in publishing this work in (a handful of) mainstream academic journals.

How do they manage to do this, and what are the consequences?

Contemporary race scientists position themselves as persecuted but legitimate scientists who are prevented from pursuing their work for ideological reasons. They sometimes refer to what they defend as hereditarianism. This is the idea that genetic differences play a key causal role in determining differences in human behavior and intelligence between human groups—such as races—over purely environmental differences.

Yet scientific evidence has shown repeatedly and from many points of view that hereditarian thinking is both flawed and wrong. Race scientists make extremely simplistic claims such as: “Black people have innately lower intelligence than white people, because selection pressures resulted in the evolution of higher intelligence in white people.” No part of this claim can be scientifically supported; not from the point of view of genetics, evolutionary biology, or psychology.

In the 1970s, the influential evolutionary biologist Richard Lewontin famously showed that there is more genetic variation within human groups than between them. Socially defined racial groups just aren’t that genetically different from one another. There is genetic variation—though we’re actually a relatively genetically similar species compared to closely related primates. But this variation doesn’t fall neatly into clusters that correlate with how we typically categorize races.

Skin tone and color do vary between geographical locations. So do some other physiological features like hair texture, or the ability to process oxygen or milk products. But these differences are associated with characteristics of the environment rather than with racial groups. Some genetic variants associated with white skin color, for example, may have originated in Africa. Similarly, the ability to digest milk is seen in some populations in Europe, Africa, and Asia, rather than being an exclusively “Aryan” characteristic, as far right groups sometimes claim.

We now know considerably more about the human genome than Lewontin did in the 1970s, but his conclusions are supported today. Knowing someone’s race just doesn’t tell us a great deal about their genetics.

Recent scientific advances allowing us to examine ancient DNA from archaeological sites help explain why genetic differences do not fall neatly along racial lines. This research paints a picture of constant mixing between human groups, as waves of migration washed to and fro across the globe. It ultimately makes any belief in the genetic “purity” of European or African populations meaningless.

While human genetics is fascinatingly complex, race scientists ignore this complexity in favor of a far-too-simplistic view of humanity. And this view is not supported by the work of scientists who have used rigorous scientific methods to further our understanding of our species.

When race scientists say that black people have lower intelligence than white people, they typically base that on claims of differences in IQ test results. But the data underlying these claims is often shaky, to say the least.

Hereditarians have relied heavily on a database that claims to provide average IQs for countries worldwide: the “national IQ” database. Researchers have repeatedly demonstrated that this database is deeply methodologically flawed. It isn’t possible to measure intelligence in the same way in very different cultures, with different exposure to formal education. That’s because we know that education is one of several other factors linked to differences in IQ.

The methods used to put the database together did not come even close to meeting typical standards of scientific rigor. There’s even analysis suggesting that low IQ samples were deliberately chosen to represent sub-Saharan Africa, to make the IQ for black populations artificially low.

Meanwhile, the Flynn effect, showing that IQ has risen considerably during the 20th and 21st centuries, clearly demonstrates that IQ scores are heavily determined by the environment we’re in. Such a rapid rise over a short period of time must have environmental, not genetic, causes.

What’s more, intelligence is a contested topic—something that is hard to define and measure. IQ tests only measure specific cognitive abilities, ignoring things like social intelligence or creativity, for example. In fact, social intelligence features heavily in mainstream research on the evolution of human intelligence.

As one of us recently outlined in a peer-reviewed paper, race scientists are largely silent on the social brain hypothesis—the view that humans evolved remarkable intellectual abilities to manage social issues. In every group, there is strong social competition around resources, mating, and status. These are the things humans spend the most time navigating—the complexities of our rich social lives.

Not one of the prominent mainstream views on the evolution of human intelligence concludes there are innate cognitive differences between racial groups. Factors that have been put forward range from our cooperative child rearing to our heavy reliance on culture and learning. These all involve our social relationships, and all have received equal lack of attention from race scientists.

Mainstream opinion in the evolutionary behavioral sciences is that cognitively, all human groups are the same, though of course individuals within groups differ. Our amazing behavioral diversity stems from our species’ ability to adapt flexibly to our environment. This is especially because of our reliance on culture and social learning—not because we have evolved different psychologies to suit different environments.

Rather than drawing on this mainstream research, race scientists use simplistic “just-so” stories to explain how race differences in traits such as intelligence supposedly evolved. Their “cold winters theory,” for example, claims that the challenges of cold winters in Europe and east Asia created evolutionary selection pressures for greater intelligence after human groups migrated out of Africa. In doing so, they make unsupportable assumptions about environmental differences between Africa and the rest of the world. They also fail to acknowledge half a century of research on the evolution of human cognitive ability.

Yes, we needed to learn new food gathering practices and methods for dealing with environmental challenges as we migrated across the world. But the same was true as humans migrated across the large and ecologically diverse continent of Africa. Race scientists provide no evidence for their arguments. They don’t rely on archaeological evidence, nor do they use typical methods from evolutionary biology to understand evolutionary processes. Using the methods of race scientists, it would be equally easy to make the argument that challenges posed by hot summers drove the evolution of higher intelligence, and equally without scientific backing.

No matter which way you look at these issues—from the view of genetics, anthropology, evolutionary biology, or psychology—hereditarianism does not hold up.

To understand the potential consequences of these views, it is important to see them in historical context. Early in the 20th century, race science was mainstream. Overly simplistic and biologically deterministic views about the origins of differences between human groups—not just race but class—were widespread (if not universal) and important to the eugenics movement.

If disadvantaged groups owe their disadvantage to some fault within them, and not to the structural conditions they experience, then some argue society is not responsible for helping those people. Such arguments led to eugenic sterilization laws that aimed to “improve” society by preventing—without their consent—the reproduction of those considered likely to pass on their innate “defects” to future children. And it was ultimately behind the Nazis’ “solution” of trying to eliminate “undesirables” entirely.

In the decades after the Second World War, the horrors of the Nazi regime led to a decline in the popularity of eugenics in mainstream research and a rejection of race science. But the belief that science supported eugenic claims was already collapsing, even as the Nazis were trying to implement a race-based society. As we learned more about human biology and genetics during the 20th century, it became obvious that human genetic variation was too complex to be mapped into socially recognized racial (or class) categories. Advances in science meant race science dropped out of mainstream thinking.

Yet those committed to a belief in a biological hierarchy of races, regardless of what the science said, wanted venues in which they could continue their discussions. In the 1960s, the pseudo-scientific journal Mankind Quarterly became their venue. Mankind Quarterly was funded by the Pioneer Fund, a nonprofit foundation set up in the 1930s by a US textile magnate who chose to use his fortune to fund research on race science and fight desegregation in the US.

The Pioneer Fund continued to fund race science into the 21st century—including supporting Mankind Quarterly—when it was taken over by the current network of race scientists. They channeled its remaining funds into a new company, the Human Diversity Foundation, subsequently rebranded as Polygenic Scores LLC.

Given all this, how is race science still being published in mainstream academic journals? To be clear, race science does not typically get published in genetics or evolutionary biology journals. Most academic journals operate peer review, meaning papers only get published after evaluation by experts in the field. The overall absence of race science in genetics or biology journals is a clear signal that it does not meet contemporary standards of scientific rigor in genetics or biology.

Instead, race scientists continue to publish in para-scientific journals. But race science also makes it into a growing number of mainstream social science or humanities journals. Psychologist Andrew Winston, an expert in the history of race science in psychology, has described it as a “community project… with its own networks, mentorships, journals, funding, conferences, heroes, origin myths, martyrs, defenders, and historiography.”

Behind the network of writers producing race science sits a community of editors, peer reviewers, and others in gatekeeping roles, which too often allows race science to be published in mainstream journals. This is particularly in the subfields of psychology interested in intelligence and individual differences, and evolutionary psychology.

Editors of Intelligence, the mainstream journal that has probably published the largest quantity of race science, have occasionally published editorials defending their publication of race science in the name of ensuring robust academic debate even on controversial topics.

There have been sanctions. Philippe Rushton, one of an earlier generation of race scientists, faced sanctions from his university in Canada but kept his job apparently owing to the intervention of Edward O. Wilson, arguably the most famous biologist toward the end of the 20th century.

Cofnas created justified controversy over his call for a “hereditarian revolution” after which it will be acknowledged that black people are innately intellectually disadvantaged compared to white people. But he kept his postdoctoral fellowship at Cambridge University (though his affiliation with a Cambridge college was ended). He has also kept his postdoctoral position at Ghent University after using the opportunity provided by excessive news coverage of Arday to publicize his hereditarian views (though some sanctions have been imposed, such as restrictions on teaching). Cofnas has defended his views by saying he’s been misunderstood and is entitled to free inquiry.

Moreover, when University College London discovered that the race science community had been holding conferences on its premises (the “London Conferences on Intelligence”), without their knowledge of the subject of the conferences, they acted to prevent further such conferences being held. The publisher of the journal Intelligence, Elsevier, has recently appointed new editors, who have stated their commitment to publishing both rigorous and socially responsible science. Race scientists are increasingly publishing in evolutionary psychology journals rather than Intelligence.

However, the London Conferences on Intelligence continue, though not at UCL. Race scientists also have a media arm, Aporia magazine, which promotes race science using much more overt language and with more obvious policy focus than publications in academic journals. One Aporia article, for example, argued for eugenics as a solution to Africa’s lack of economic development, noting in a post that some may find it “distasteful.” It claimed—relying on race science’s unscientific arguments—that artificial selection for higher intelligence is needed to improve the continent’s innately unintelligent populace.

Race science may not have the respect of the scientific community, but it does have funding and influential backers. A report by Hope not Hate revealed that the network of race scientists received significant funding from the US businessman Andrew Conru, who withdrew his funding after the report was released.

The US Ambassador to Belgium even intervened when Ghent University set up an investigation into how Cofnas was hired.

Race scientists are far from the victims they claim to be. Race scientists in this and previous generations have worked hard to ensure their messages are heard beyond academia. And in this generation, they are successfully using social media to spread their beliefs, especially Elon Musk’s X.

But what about academic freedom? Isn’t it good for science to discuss any hypothesis to ensure free exchange of ideas? Academic debate is productive only if it involves methodologically rigorous research. Science will not progress if one side of any debate consistently uses poor-quality, methodologically flawed work. Nor is it necessary to continually debate topics that have long since been settled.

We believe it is unequivocal that race science is fundamentally pseudoscience. A chatbot can handle every conversation responsibly and still, over time, take the place of someone a person used to confide in. In their First Opinion essay, Qian Yang, Ned Cooper, and Ibrahim Emara show how easily a gradual loss of human connection can escape safeguards designed for individual conversations. They make a persuasive case for giving independent researchers access to platform data and developing tools to distinguish the effects of chatbot use from other causes of isolation. Those tools should ask whether people could reach someone they trusted when they needed help.

That question is especially worth asking adolescents, who may have fewer ways to seek help privately. Imagine two teenagers who each spend an hour every night talking to a chatbot. One used to call a friend during difficult times, but he called less often as talking to the chatbot became a habit. The other still talks to friends every day. When she wanted to discuss something she feared would get back to her parents, she asked to see a school counselor. While she waited for an appointment, she took the problem to a chatbot. Their chat logs might look very similar. The authors suggest measuring school counselor time as one way to estimate what isolation costs schools. That measure captures time counselors spend helping students. Hours, days, or weeks spent waiting leave no trace.

Researchers could ask teens to think about the last time something was troubling them. Did they talk to someone about it, try to reach someone, or decide against reaching out? If they tried, did they get to talk to someone? Did they talk to a chatbot about it?

Asking these questions regularly could help researchers distinguish a teen who reached out to people less often as their chatbot usage grew from one who turned to a chatbot only after trying to get help. The distinction matters for policymaking. When chatbot use displaces human contact, usage limits may help. When it fills a gap in support, the priority should be making help easier to reach. As a pediatric emergency physician and clinical ethicist, I have experienced and witnessed aggression at work many times. The authors’ concern comes from a good place, but it misses the realities of the clinical setting. They write that ineffective signs would be “pointless, but not harmful.” I disagree, for three reasons.

First, the essay overstates the evidence against signage. Its claim rests on two sources: a 2006 survey of Australian nurses and a 2008 commentary on the U.K.’s zero tolerance campaign. Neither evaluates whether signs change behavior. Nor has anyone tested the claim that signs erode patient trust. Research on violence prevention is methodologically weak, though multicomponent programs show mixed but encouraging results.

Second, deterrence is not the only purpose of a sign. Even if the signs prevented nothing, they would matter. The authors acknowledge that violence against health care workers has risen. In emergency departments specifically, it has introduced a mental and physical burden that fundamentally changes what it means to do this work. Signage stating an obvious standard of behavior may seem trivial, but it can have meaning as a piece of a broader institutional commitment to health care worker well-being. It tells staff that their hospital understands the realities of their work and supports them.

Third, signs remind clinicians where their obligations end. Many clinicians, especially trainees, are unsure what they owe a patient or family who is abusing them. A hospital must screen and stabilize anyone who comes to the emergency department, whatever their behavior. Beyond that, no patient or family is entitled to further care from a clinician they are making unsafe. This is easy to forget amid the pressures of clinical care, and a visible reminder helps.

The authors worry about the message these signs send to patients and families. That concern is thoughtful, but it is aimed at the wrong signs. The signs I see in emergency departments name physical assault, threats, and harassment. A sign that polices tone or discourages complaints could deserve criticism, but those signs are the exception and should not set the terms of this debate. Even the authors want their alternative signs to make clear that violence will not be tolerated. They close by asking that the words on hospital walls communicate medicine’s values. Protecting the people who care for patients is one of them.

— Joseph Shapiro, M.D., Children’s National Hospital

Social Security's Trump Bump-Led 2027 COLA Is the 5th Largest in 35 Years -- but There's a Dark Side to This Outsize Increase as Well

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Though Social Security checks are about to get bigger, the projected timeline for sweeping benefit cuts may have accelerated.

By Sean Williams – Nov 21, 2026 at 6:36AM EST

For Social Security’s nearly 72 million traditional beneficiaries -- retired workers, workers with disabilities, and survivors of deceased workers -- there are few, if any, announcements more anticipated than the annual cost-of-living adjustment (COLA).

Social Security's COLA is the near-annual "raise" given to beneficiaries to account for inflation (i.e., rising prices). If the price for the goods and services that seniors regularly purchase kept climbing without an adjustment in benefits, the buying power of Social Security income would steadily decline over time.

Social Security's announced 2027 COLA offers something of a mixed bag for recipients. On the one hand, it's set to deliver one of the largest raises over the last 35 years. This larger payout comes courtesy of President Donald Trump and a handful of his policies.

President Trump's policies offer a good-news/bad-news scenario for Social Security. Image source: Official White House Photo by Molly Riley.

But this outsize Trump bump isn't without its consequences. Larger payouts in the new year can make the program's precarious financial outlook that much worse.

On Oct. 14, the Social Security Administration announced that monthly payouts would increase by 3.6% in 2027. For the average retired-worker beneficiary, their monthly payout is expected to climb by $75. Meanwhile, the typical worker with a disability can expect their monthly Social Security check to rise by $59 in the new year.

A 3.6% raise is well above the norm. It marks a tie for the fifth-largest percentage increase over the last 35 years, surpassed only by COLAs of 4.1% (2006), 5.8% (2009), 5.9% (2022), and 8.7% (2023).

It's also the sixth consecutive year in which recipients have received at least a 2.5% cost-of-living adjustment. It's been 30 years (1988-1997) since benefits rose by at least 2.5% for six straight years.

While modest inflation associated with a growing U.S. economy results in a Social Security raise most years, next year's outsize COLA was pumped up by two of President Donald Trump's policies.

New from the New York Fed: "By February 2026, tariffs had contributed 2.9 pp to goods price inflation, and without them goods prices would have fallen slightly." About one quarter of every point in higher tariff rates shows up in consumer prices within one year. • A 10% across-the-board tariff yields a 2.6% rise in consumer goods prices after one year. • About two thirds of the increase comes from pricier imports, which get passed through quickly. • Another third comes from U.S. made goods, as producers face higher input costs and less import competition. This takes longer, about 6-12 months, to filter through.

To begin with, each of the last two Social Security COLAs has been influenced by Trump's tariff and trade policy. Adding duties to imports directly raises their price and can increase manufacturing costs for domestic producers.

A recently published report by three New York Federal Reserve economists writing for Liberty Street Economics ("How Fast Do Tariffs Pass Through into Consumer Prices?") found that Trump's 2025 tariffs increased goods price inflation by an estimated 2.9 percentage points by February 2026. Without tariffs, the NY Fed economists estimate that goods prices would have otherwise fallen.

The other major policy contributing to Social Security's 2027 Trump bump is the Iran war. Closing the Strait of Hormuz has resulted in the largest energy supply disruption in modern history, sending gas and diesel prices notably higher.

With Trumpflation (inflation driven by Trump's policies) becoming entrenched in the U.S. economy, it's quite possible that these policies provide a third consecutive year with a Trump bump in 2028.

Unfortunately, outsize Social Security COLAs come at a potentially steep cost to America's leading retirement program.

Every year since 1985, the Social Security Board of Trustees Report has warned of a long-term unfunded obligation. In simpler terms, the projected income to be collected in the 75 years following the release of a report is expected to be insufficient to cover outlays (primarily benefits, but also the administrative costs to oversee Social Security). As of 2026, this long-term unfunded obligation had ballooned to $29.3 trillion.

However, this isn't the most pressing concern for the program or its nearly 72 million traditional beneficiaries.

According to the latest Trustees Report, the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance trust fund (OASI) is forecast to exhaust its asset reserves by the fourth quarter of 2032. The OASI's asset reserves represent the excess income collected since inception that is invested in special-issue, interest-bearing government bonds, as required by law.

US Old-Age and Survivors Insurance Trust Fund Assets at End of Year data by YCharts.

The positive news is that Social Security isn't going bankrupt and is in no danger of halting benefits. More than 91% of the program's income is collected through the 12.4% payroll tax on earned income (wages and salaries, but not investment income). As long as Americans keep working, the payroll tax will continue generating income for disbursement to eligible beneficiaries... even if the OASI's asset reserves are depleted.

But just because Social Security isn't going bankrupt, it doesn't mean everything is peachy. If the OASI's asset reserves run dry over the next six years, as predicted, it would indicate that the current payout schedule, inclusive of annual COLAs, isn't sustainable. Sweeping benefit cuts of up to 22% may be necessary for retired workers and survivors of deceased workers.

The concern is that Social Security's Trump bump may have materially altered the trajectory to the OASI's asset reserve insolvency.

When the Trustees Report issues its short- and long-term outlooks, it takes into account a long list of variables, including birth rates, death rates, net migration rates, and even annual COLAs. The Trustees assume that annual COLAs will be modest -- but as we just saw, a 3.6% annual raise is anything but average.

Social Security's outsize 2027 raise risks draining the OASI even faster than initially forecast.

President Trump's "Big, Beautiful Bill" already moved up the projected OASI asset reserve insolvency timeline by one quarter (from the first quarter of 2033 to the fourth quarter of 2032). It's entirely possible the 2027 Trump bump further accelerates the timeline to sweeping benefit cuts.

One of the greatest strengths of crime television is its ability to skew moral compasses and deliver fascinating, complex characters that challenge our understanding of humanity. This is true of detective drama and organized crime suspense, and it is particularly true of the many great villains that populate these series, whether they are psychotic serial killers defined by their sinister presence or oddly charismatic crooks who are as charming as they are chilling.

It goes without saying that the performances of such characters are instrumental in determining their quality. Everything from the subtleties of facial expressions to the rhythm of line delivery, and to the physicality they are presented with, is all key choices actors can make in defining the brand of villainy their character exudes. Whether viewers hold a wicked love for them or dread every second of screen time they receive, these crime series find astonishing highlights in the form of their biggest and baddest antagonists.

Despite only appearing in the third season of Boardwalk Empire, Gyp Rosetti (Bobby Cannavale) stands as one of the Prohibition-era gangster series’ most piercing and unforgettable characters. Within a story that often feels methodical and pragmatic, Rosetti injects a tense volatility into every single scene he appears in, which is a testament to the astonishing writing of the character and Cannavale’s note-perfect performance of such an explosive character.

Whereas so many of the other adversaries Nucky Thompson (Steve Buscemi) encounters attempt to outsmart him through business maneuvers or political plotting, Rosetti relies on ferocious, bloodthirsty brutality and unwavering intent. His destructive, overwhelming personality is only made all the more commanding by the character’s sensitivity to perceived slights, especially as Cannavale imbues every second of screen time he gets with the simmering suspense of a ticking time bomb. Whether he’s erupting into a fit of rage or hurling oddly elegant insults, Cannavale makes Rosetti one of the most intimidating and unpleasant TV antagonists of all time.

As a grim descent into the criminal underbelly of Gotham City, The Penguin marks one of HBO’s more compelling productions of the decade so far, a rich mixture of comic-book style and gangland brutality. Colin Farrell’s lead performance is a driving force of this, but the miniseries benefits from its vast array of striking characters, with Cristin Milioti’s turn as criminal heiress Sofia Falcone being the noteworthy standout as she blends an erratic, unpredictable ruthlessness with underlying vulnerability and sympathetic sorrow.

Milioti worked closely with both movement coach Julia Crockett and the production’s costuming department to perfect Sofia’s physicality. Through every movement, she embodies the volatility of her explosive aspirations fighting to break free from her past of repression and institutionalization. Purely as a superhero villain, Wilson Fisk (Vincent D’Onofrio), otherwise known as Kingpin, is a perfect foil to Daredevil (Charlie Cox), with his brutish strength challenging the hero’s more precise and calculating fighting style. Indian-Australian filmmaker Anoop Lokkur, whose debut “Don’t Tell Mother” premiered at the Busan International Film Festival in 2025, is turning his own standoff with tenants who wouldn’t leave his family home into “Melgade Mane Kelgade Mane” (Upstairs Downstairs), a dark comedy in the Co-Production Market at WAVES Film Bazaar.

The Kannada and English-language film follows Karthik, who returns from Melbourne, along with his wife and young daughter, to look after his ailing father. He expects to take over the top floor of the house in an older Bengaluru neighborhood where his family has lived for three generations. The tenant, government contractor Ramesh, had agreed to move out, then changes his mind. Sets Inaugural Fan Convention in Washington, D.C. Dean Koontz's Jane Hawk Books to Be Developed as TV Series by Monarch Media (EXCLUSIVE)

Lokkur says that in 2025 he returned to Bangalore with his wife and their 1-year-old daughter, planning to settle upstairs in his parents’ home, but the tenants stayed on after their lease ran out and the family ended up crammed into his parents’ part of the house. “The whole situation was frustrating and unpleasant. What followed were months of tension, passive aggression and even thinking of ways to get them to vacate. The irony, though, was that only a couple of years earlier, my wife and I had been forced out of a rented home ourselves.”

Having been on both sides of the door, he says, he grew curious about ownership, about what it means to call a place home and about how hard people will push to come out on top. “I realized there was a dark comedy in this situation that was universal, and the best way to deal with my frustration was to turn it into a film.”

The project is in development, with Lokkur and Mikayla Henke producing through Papunu Films. The budget is $1.3 million, with $156,000 in place, and the team is looking for international co-producers, financiers and sales agents in Goa.

“The film is built like a tennis match. Your sympathies keep switching between the two families, until you’re no longer sure who to cheer for,” Lokkur says. “I’m interested in how ego quietly takes over and how ordinary decent people justify each small act of revenge until they no longer recognize themselves. It’s also a film about Bangalore, a city changing so fast that old neighborhoods and family homes are disappearing.”

Henke says she went through the dispute alongside Lokkur, and that with the family living it every day, it was the obvious choice for their next film. “‘Don’t Tell Mother’ was a tender and very personal film. On the surface, ‘Melgade Mane Kelgade Mane’ (Upstairs Downstairs) might look like the opposite: darker, bolder and funnier. But it comes from the same place, a close eye on family and the things people struggle to say to each other. This time, we just want to take audiences on a much wilder ride.”

“Don’t Tell Mother” played in the A Window on Asian Cinema section at Busan, was nominated for best youth film at the 2026 Asia Pacific Screen Awards and had its Australian premiere at this year’s Sydney Film Festival. Lokkur’s short “Long Distance,” which Henke produced, screened at the Melbourne International Film Festival and Palm Springs International ShortFest before Canal+ acquired it. Henke was also the first producer on “Don’t Tell Mother.”

“While it’s set in Bengaluru, it’s a universal story about housing that people everywhere can relate to and we believe it can travel well beyond India,” the producers say.

Christie’s tepid 21st-century sale caps week of blockbuster auctions in New York

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Christie's auctioneer Yü-Ge Wang sells the top lot of the sale auction house's 19 November auction in New York, Jean-Michel Basquiat’s Untitled (Leonardo and His Five Grotesque Heads) (1983) Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd

Wrapping up this autumn’s week of marquee evening sales in New York, Christie’s 21st-century auction on Thursday (19 November) proved rather lacklustre, bringing in $117.7m ($145.9m with fees). That tally fell short of Christie’s pre-sale expectations, which do not account for fees, and projected a total take in the range of $126m to $192m. It was also a lower result compared to the auction house’s last two equivalent sales in New York—namely the $132m ($162.6m with fees) generated across 42 lots last May, and the $99.5m ($123.5m with fees) brought by 45 lots one year ago.

Just five of the 56 lots offered Thursday night failed to sell, for a slim buy-in rate of 9%. Two lots were withdrawn at the last moment, including a Jeff Koons sculpture estimated to bring between $3m and $5m. Twenty-four lots were backed by third-party guarantees and nine were backed fully by house guarantees, representing around 59% of all the works offered. Auction records for three artists were broken over the course of the night.

Roy Lichtenstein, Small Wall Explosion, 1965 Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd

Business began with a bang at Christie’s Rockefeller Center salesroom: the first lot was Roy Lichtenstein’s Small Wall Explosion (1965), executed in porcelain enamel on steel and perforated steel relief to mimic the Ben-Day dot style from his paintings. It sold to a telephone bidder for $625,000 ($800,000 with fees). From an edition of six, it hailed from the collection of Antonio Homem Sonnabend, the adopted son of the late and legendary dealer and collector Ileana Sonnabend (1914-2007) and who ran his mother’s namesake gallery in its waning years. Antonio Homem died earlier this year, at age 86.

Another of the 19 Sonnabend lots from Sonnabend, Ashley Bickerton’s expansive wall relief Commercial Piece #3 (1990), covered with gallery and fashion-house logos and comprised of anodised aluminium, wood, leather, acrylic and rubber, sold for $120,000 ($153,600 with fees). Both the Lichtenstein and the Bickerton were backed by third-party guarantees.

Andy Warhol, Campbell's Soup Can (Turkey Noodle), 1962 Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd

Price points for the Sonnabend trove zoomed higher with Andy Warhol’s iconicand hand painted Campbell’s Soup Can (Turkey Noodle) (1962), which sold for $7.3m ($9m with fees) against an estimate of $6m to $8m. Another Lichtenstein, the painting Large Spool (1963)—a razor-sharp depiction of the titular object that has been featured in multiple gallery and museum exhibitions—squeaked by on the tails of a third-party guarantee at $7m ($8.6m with fees) against an ambitious estimate of $10m to $15m.

Still in the Sonnabend lots, Jeff Koons’s toy-like Hulk (Friends) (2004-12), in polychromed bronze and standing nearly 6ft tall, found a new home for $3.3m ($4.1m with fees). And another Warhol, redolent with Hollywood celebrity, The Kiss (Bela Lugosi) (1963) in silkscreen ink on white paper, sold to another telephone bidder for $3.3m ($4.6m with fees).

A petite Jasper Johns oil painting on cardboard, Numbers (1957), measuring just 8.5in by 6in and rich in shades of grey, made $2.6m ($3.2m with fees). It was featured in the artist’s first solo exhibition at the Leo Castelli Gallery in 1958. Ileana Sonnabend acquired it from the Castelli Gallery in 1959, the same year of her divorce from Leo Castelli.

The Sonnabend group of 19 lots, including three examples of important 20th-century French decorative art—among them a Jean Prouvé Trapéze table from around 1956 that hit $1.3m ($1.6m with fees)—all in tallied $33.3m ($41.9m with fees), compared to Christie’s pre-sale expectations for the group of $35m to $55m.

Illustrious consignor names continued with works from the collection of the late Museum of Modern Art trustee and patron Agnes Gund sold to benefit the Gund Art Foundation. They included Robert Ryman’s immaculate, small, square all-over white composition Untitled (1961), which brought $2.9m ($3.6m with fees). Vija Celmins’s photorealist oil painting Untitled (Ocean) (1990), which featured in the San Francisco Museum of Modern Art’s traveling retrospective in 2018-2020, went for $5.7m ($7.1m with fees) against an estimate of $6m to $8m. It also was backed by a third-party guarantee.

Vija Celmins, Untitled (Ocean), 1990 Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd

Gund acquired Alexander Calder’s hanging metal mobile The Eye in the Black (1963) from the Perls Galleries in New York in in 1967. On Thursday night it went to an online bidder for $2.8m ($3.5m with fees). Cindy Sherman’s Untitled Film Still #5 (1977), also widely exhibited and written about by major critics, sold for $320,000 ($409,6000 with fees). Gund had acquired it at Sotheby’s New York in November 2000 for a hammer price of $75,000.

Gund’s contemporary taste bordered on the encyclopaedic, as evidenced by an early Robert Rauschenberg work, Untitled (Gold Painting) (1953), in gold leaf on fabric and paper collage on canvas. The diminutive work, at 8in by 8in, fetched a sizeable sum, $1.7m ($2.1m with fees). Gund acquired it at Sotheby’s in New York in May 1994 for a $55,000 hammer price.

A modestly scaled Lichtenstein work on paper in coloured pencil, graphite and paper collage, Artist’s Studio with Model (Study) (1974), sold to the art adviser Ralph DeLuca for $950,000 ($1.2m with fees).

Jim Nutt, Loom, 1999 Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd

A standout from the Gund trove, a work she described as “my Mona Lisa”, was another modestly-scaled work, by Jim Nutt, founder of Chicago’s Hairy Who group. Titled Loom (1999) and measuring 15in by 14in, the portrait of a woman with a penetrating gaze set a new auction record for Nutt’s work when it more than doubled its high estimate of $350,000 and ultimately sold for $850,000 ($1m with fees). The ten lots sold to benefit Gund’s charitable foundation tallied $20.3m ($25.7m with fees), squarely within Christie’s pre-sale estimate of $16m to $24m.

Though not part of the Gund Art Foundation offerings, Alice Neel’s double portrait David and Catherine Saalfield (1982) hailed from Agnes Gund’s family and brought $1.6m ($2m with fees). Gund acquired it directly from the artist the year it was painted—not surprising since the sitters were her young adult children.

Alice Neel, David and Catherine Saalfield, 1982 Courtesy Christie's Images Ltd

The appeal of buying a piece of the legacy of storied dealers and collectors did not fare so well for a work that had belonged to the late gallerist Marian Goodman. Evidently inspired by Leonardo da Vinci’s 1493 pen-and-ink drawing on paper A man tricked by Gypsies (also known as Five grotesque heads), from the Royal Collection Trust at Windsor Castle in London, Basquiat’s freestyle interpretation in oil stick and acrylic against a burnt orange background crackles with the artist’s terse way with wordplay and actually features eight heads in all.

Ravi Agrawal: Let’s start with the U.S. point of view on the Iran war. The consensus has been that Trump has three options. He could escalate militarily, but that has very uncertain outcomes. He could spin a victory and get out, but the spin is . Or he could focus on diplomacy and try to return to the memorandum of understanding (MOU) we had last summer, but that is a lot of hard work. What do you think the White House strategy is now that the midterms are over?

Vali Nasr: I think the president is actually pursuing all three strategies at the same time. Leaning in on diplomacy does not mean that he’s setting aside war plans, and asking for war plans does not mean that he is doing less diplomacy. He doesn’t have any good choices.

Ideally, Trump would end this war through a negotiated settlement, much like what happened in June, but he has to meet the Iranian price for doing that, which is actually quite steep for him. Currently, the positions of the two are too far apart. The Iranians want him to first go back to the MOU and implement it before they talk about anything else, such as the nuclear issue. Trump, on the other hand, needs the nuclear issue to be at the forefront in order to justify why he went to war for nine months.

War is also not really an option, because in order for war to be meaningful, Trump has to do something big. But something big opens the door to Iranian retaliation, particularly in the Persian Gulf, which could devastate the global economy in larger ways. I’m sure Trump is hearing from Chinese President Xi Jinping and Arab, Chinese, and Russian leaders that nobody wants that kind of damage to the global economy. So he’s settling for the third option, which is to let Iran sit under the blockade and do nothing. In my opinion, that’s not a strategy. That’s twiddling his thumbs.

The blockade is not foolproof. Iran is getting oil out and making money. At the same time, the Strait of Hormuz is still under a lot of pressure. The Iranians have actually increased the number of attacks they’re making there. Furthermore, I don’t think it’s sustainable, financially or otherwise, for the U.S. Navy to maintain the blockade long term. It is quite financially and labor intensive for the Navy to do so, and it doesn’t have any bases in the Persian Gulf that could provide relief. If the Iranians are able to sustain themselves with the amount of holes that already exist, then, ultimately, this is not sustainable.

In fact, if the blockade starts working in the way in which the U.S. treasury secretary and U.S. secretary of state say it will, then Iran is likely to escalate. So ultimately, this strategy that everybody thinks is settled on is not tenable in the long term. Either it will collapse under its own weight, or one or the other party is bound to disturb it, which might bring us back to war.

RA: I imagine that you could have said a lot of this two weeks ago. The big change that has come in the last couple of weeks is, of course, the U.S. midterm election results. Come January, the Democrats will have power in both the House of Representatives and the Senate. They feel that part of the mandate from the American people is trying to find a way to end the war. What is your sense of how Congress might impact how Trump tries to prosecute the end of this war, or dilly-dally, as you said?

VN: One outcome of these elections is a much more visible push within the United States against the war. It’s not just that the American people have spoken. If there was any shadow of a doubt that they really, really disliked this war, it has been dashed, particularly within the Republican Party. They might now think that if the Republican Party is to have any chance in 2028, this war should end now.

So there is a sense that there is more momentum for diplomacy after the election than there was before. In fact, when U.S. Vice President J.D. Vance wrote to Iran’s leadership, everybody said, “That’s exactly what we thought the consequence would be of the drubbing the administration got.”

But Trump is unpredictable. He has his own view of what’s in his interest as well as what’s in America’s interest. He may go back to a conflict with Iran, so we’re not out of the woods. But the Iranians know that the economic cost of the war is having a tangible political impact on America, as well as France and Germany, even if it is not being reflected in the stock market on a daily basis. Ultimately, these countries are likely to decide at some point that this war is not worth the political blowback it’s causing.

RA: I was going to come to how Tehran is gaming out the post-midterms scenario. You make the point that Iran may sense that it is getting some of what it wants—it is imposing costs. The flip side of that could be that the costs have been imposed, and Trump has now seen the worst of it. He seems to be doubling down. With nothing to lose, he may now be impervious to electoral pressure, meaning the Iranians may not have much leverage with him.

VN: That’s very true. This is a gamble. It doesn’t mean that because the elections happened, the Iranians can declare victory and say, “OK, we humiliated President Trump, now let’s sit down and talk.” Yes, they had a big win in terms of the midterm results, but now they have to deal with Trump.

I think the way they see it is that the decision to go to war is still uncomfortable for President Trump. It’s true that he’s not looking at another election, but the Republicans are looking at 2028. And ultimately, the longer this war goes on, the more the pressure it brings about, particularly if the next phase of the war is more destructive. The Iranians are broadcasting that loud and clear as a form of deterrence. They’re saying they’re going to wreak havoc on the region and the global economy, no holds barred.

If that’s the case, then President Trump still has to think about his legacy. He has to think about the future of the Republican Party. And he has to consider that if he enters into a wider war with Iran and he still isn’t able to defeat it, the scale of his failure will be even greater than it is now.

Until now, the most important factor on the American side hasn’t been some kind of a calculated, rational, technocratic national interest. It has been the psychology of one man from the get-go of deciding to go to war, to Trump’s various permutations.

Nobody—whether it’s the United States’ allies or Iran or China—can say with any certainty how Trump might actually act. So I think the Iranians are taking this one step at a time, reading the tea leaves and applying pressure where they can with Congress—particularly the new Congress coming in—to see whether they can avoid a big war and get a settlement that would benefit them.

The reasons they point to are that the amount of Gulf crude going through Hormuz has actually dropped significantly, from 83 percent to 60 percent, because Gulf states are using pipelines more and relying less and less on Hormuz. The rial is down to 1.7 million to the dollar. Recent U.S. sanctions have targeted the regime explicitly, as opposed to targeting the Iranian people. They’ve shut down parts of Mahan Air, which the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) relied on, as well as a United Arab Emirates branch of Banque Misr, which served the IRGC.

There’s a sense in the White House—or at least, it’s their narrative—that these measures must really hurt the people in power in Iran, and at some point the pain will be so great that the regime will be forced to make different choices. Do you buy any of that?

VN: No, I don’t. This is largely self-serving, anecdotal rhetoric, which has gotten us to this point. I don’t see a sign that the degree of pressure that this administration thinks it is imposing on Iran is actually being felt there and changing attitudes. When Iran sent its diplomats to the United Nations, the proposal they presented to the United States was an expansion of the Islamabad-brokered MOU, changed in ways that benefited Iran more. The Iranians referred to it as “Islamabad-Plus.”

Let’s also consider the fact that when Mecca defense alliance members and Pakistan’s army chief went to Tehran in October, Iran’s response to Vice President Vance’s letter was that Iran was sticking to its demands that Washington must fulfill all seven preconditions to resuming talks and reopening Hormuz. The United States’ pressure tactics are not working.

This administration doesn’t want to go to war or make a deal with Iran. They need to sell this “do nothing” strategy as a viable, working strategy, so they’re repackaging the blockade and saying it’s going to work this time. Two months ago, U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent was promising that there would be a run on the Iranian banks. That hasn’t happened. So I’m suspicious of these kinds of claims.

Iran is under a tremendous amount of pressure, but I don’t think the administration has a gauge of where the tipping point for them is. So it’s throwing a Hail Mary pass with more sanctions, hoping that they will do the trick, and then we get a lot of rhetoric trying to sell the current holding pattern as an actual strategy.

RA: So in response to everything you’re saying, Tehran’s strategy might be to wait out the next two years.

VN: If they have to, yes. Although, I don’t know. Iran’s supreme leader said in his last major statement that this “no war, no peace” situation is not sustainable or acceptable to Iran.

When and how will Iran disturb this status quo? We don’t know. We can already see that they are escalating pressure on shipping. In the past week, more of the shuttle ships that the UAE has been using to ferry oil from inside tankers in the Persian Gulf to tankers in the Arabian Sea are being hit. The number of mines installed in the Strait of Hormuz has also increased. More oil might go out on some days than others. So the Iranians are making the situation more unstable.

There is a difference between the way the West and Iran look at this situation. The U.S. and many observers think a move from Iran has to be really big to be meaningful. For instance, if they can’t bring the cost of oil to $200 a barrel, then they’ve lost.

But the Iranians actually do not look at things this way. Very early in the war, they understood that the price of oil is not 100 percent in their hands. What they believe works is sustained instability in the energy market. The costs of oil, natural gas, jet fuel, diesel, fertilizer, and so on are far above where they were. None of these measures by themselves will break the global economy, but if you put them together for long enough, they’re very effective.

Iran sees the result of the U.S. elections as proof of this concept. Their strategy is to boil the frog gradually. I think sometimes in the West we look at this and say, “Well, the frog is just sitting there and nothing’s happening to it, so the Iranians have failed.” So I think this is a race between whether Iran runs out of gas first or its strategy works.

Right now, I detect a certain degree of confidence from Iran. I don’t see the trepidation that observers on the outside are assuming the regime has. So either Iran has a good poker face, or it’s seeing the picture very differently than Washington does.

RA: It’s fascinating, and it’s always so hard to know whom or what to believe.

I’m curious what you make of these reports that claim to be from U.S. intelligence suggesting that Iran is developing plans to detonate a dirty bomb in Washington, D.C. These are just reports. Tehran, of course, denies that any of this is true, but I’m curious how you are reading that story and reacting to it.

VN: We’re in the midst of a war, and there’s a lot of disinformation. There are people in the United States or in Israel who want the United States to go back to war with Iran, to “finish the job,” so to speak. After the elections, which made it very obvious that the American public is firmly opposed to this war, you could only change that public opinion in favor of war if there was this kind of threat against America, or if you could posit that Iran is a deranged and crazy regime that must be taken out. So it could possibly be disinformation.

On the other hand, Iranians have also been taking certain actions as a form of deterrence. I don’t know whether Iran actually launched a cyberattack on refrigerators in France—French intelligence claims they did, though the Iranians deny it—but it’s a message. “If you attack us, then we have cyber capabilities that could paralyze your cities.”

It’s a kind of deterrence tactic. It’s a similar case with Iran issuing warnings to air bases in the U.K., which ultimately got the United States to move its airplanes from two different air bases. Some of this benefits Iran, because it’s a way of saying, “Do not assume that the next war would be cost-free. Our reach is much farther than you assume.”

It also suggests to me that Iran doesn’t want to go back to war. Rather, its game plan is to try to get the United States to come to the table to accept their terms.

The Q3 NAHB/Wells Fargo Cost of Housing Index (CHI) found that families earning the national median income of $106,800 needed 33% of their income to buy a median-priced new home, compared to 36% of their income for a median-priced existing home.

Low-income families (those who earn only 50% of the national median income) need 66% of their income to buy a new home, compared to 73% for an existing home.

NAHB Chairman Bill Owens noted that this was the “widest affordability advantage (for new homes) since the CHI began,” as builders “are working hard to offset rising costs.”

The report also noted that this is due in part to the difference in price between new homes ($400,400 median price) and existing homes ($416,700 median price), called out as the widest national median price gap since the CHI began.

The other attribution for the new home “affordability advantage” is the incentives and deals offered by builders.

Owens called out NAHB’s recent data on builder confidence, which saw 43% of builders offer price cuts in October—the highest share since 2020. Additionally, 65% of builders offered sales incentives.

A recent Realtor.com® report also found that many buyers purchasing new homes are seeing lower mortgage rates due to builders offering buy downs or other deals with mortgage lenders. The average starting mortgage rate of a new-home buyer was 5.01% in Q3, compared with 6.15% for existing-home buyers.

While new homes are offering better affordability than existing homes, overall housing affordability is still very much challenged.

For both new and existing homes, families earning the national median income remain fairly cost-burdened (paying more than 30% of their income for housing) or severely cost-burdened (paying more than 50% of their income for housing).

Of the 175 metro areas tracked in the report, 87 are cost-burdened by housing, and eight are severely cost-burdened. Leading the way among the eight severely cost-burdened markets is San Jose, California, where families use 79% of their income on housing.

Owens outlined that in order to move housing affordability in the right direction, “policymakers must do more to reduce red tape, address labor shortages and fix supply chains so builders can expand the nation’s housing supply.”

“As builders contend with rising construction costs, tighter lending standards and persistent labor shortages, policymakers should remove regulatory obstacles, enact permitting reform and create a stronger business environment that enables builders to produce more attainable, affordable homes,” added NAHB Chief Economist Robert Dietz.
