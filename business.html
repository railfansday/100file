Lewis Hamilton: &quot;You can't be the only one pushing for change&quot; in F1

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Lewis Hamilton says he had hoped others in Formula 1 would follow his lead in speaking out on issues around the world, but admitted that "so far that's not the case".

The seven-time world champion has used his platform for causes including diversity in motorsport and, more recently, the humanitarian crisis in Gaza.

Asked by the media in Las Vegas ahead of this weekend's race whether more people in the sport be using their platforms to speak out, the Briton said everyone was entitled to their own view, but called on the championship to do more as a whole.

"I just try to lead with intention and hope that, I've always thought, kind of, if you can be that pebble that creates a ripple and encourages other people to maybe follow in your footsteps but so far that's not the case. And honestly, I don't know what everyone else stands for, but that's not really my concern," Hamilton said.

"My concern is just trying to, as I said, try to do the best I can and stand for the right things, regardless of what people say. I just follow my heart, and I'm really proud of the work that I've done with my foundation."

Hamilton said F1 was moving "in a positive direction" with projects such as the diversity charter signed by the FIA, F1 and the teams, but is adamant that the responsibility of making a change could not fall on just one person.

"There's more we can do. It's a huge business, valued at over 20 billion. You can't be the only one pushing for change and positive things like that. You need to all work together. So I'm hoping more and more people will come along on the journey," he said.

Hamilton also spoke about his visit to Egypt last week, two days after the Sao Paulo Grand Prix, when he was shown the aid operation at the Rafah crossing into Gaza.

"I really wanted to go and see it for myself. Worked with an organisation that's doing some amazing work, but it was, you know, heartbreaking and very, very heavy. I met children that had been shot, and burned, and disfigured," he said.

"But it was amazing to see that there are some good people on the ground that are doing some really amazing work to try and help these youngsters recover, give them education."

The Ferrari ace who visited Jordan with the British Red Cross last December, said it was important to maintain coverage on these subjects.

"I think that no child in the world should ever be responsible for, or be affected by a government's decision. And it's really just about the human side of things. We really stand for humanity. And while I have this platform, I will continue to do that," Hamilton ended.

How Premier League's big six keep getting richer

Ten games into the Premier League season, four of the top six spots are occupied by 'big six' clubs.

Only second-placed Brighton, a club with a model which must be the envy of many of their rivals, and Premier League ever-presents Everton, have muscled their way in.

In pure financial terms, it should come as no surprise.

The six biggest clubs - Manchester United, Liverpool, Manchester City, Arsenal, Tottenham and Chelsea - now generate hundreds of millions of pounds more than the division's other teams.

BBC Sport breaks down how the finances of the big six have exploded, examines new trends and the impact on the transfer market, and asks how much it really matters.

Companies put their money where supporters are prepared to spend it. It is a simple fact of business in every country, a reality of football's food chain.

In England, Liverpool, Arsenal and Manchester United traditionally dominated viewing figures, column inches, shirt sales and sponsorship.

Over the past 20 years they have been joined by Chelsea, Manchester City and Tottenham.

This group of clubs now generate so much money it is hard to imagine how they can fail in the long term - even factoring in short-term performance issues.

We will use Everton, the only club not in the big six to be in the Premier League since its inception in 1992.

This tells a tale of how even one of the league's founding members can be left trailing well behind.

In 2009-10, the gap in revenues between the big-six average and Everton was Â£120m. In 2024-25, that gap had grown to Â£442m.

In large part it is the boom in international TV rights.

Kieran Maguire, professor of football finance at the University of Liverpool, told BBC Sport that it has attracted companies prepared to pay large sums to be associated with the Premier League's top clubs.

"The big football brand clubs have global fanbases and that makes them very attractive to global commercial partners, especially for sponsorship and front-of-shirt deals," Maguire said.

"You combine that with the fact that the Premier League is broadcast to 190 different countries around the world, and especially the international audiences - that allows the clubs to negotiate very good prices."

Only 10% of Everton's revenue comes from commercial deals, while for the big six it is 45%.

It means Everton, and other clubs in the league, are heavily reliant on broadcast income. It makes up 66% of their revenue.

For Everton at least, this should change following the move to Hill Dickinson Stadium.

But that is unlikely to be enough to stop the gap increasing.

It has taken a few years, but Aston Villa and Newcastle have begun to increase their revenue.

This is largely thanks to the financial rewards of Champions League football.

But even Uefa has created a system which entrenches the position of the biggest clubs.

When the European Super League plan was torpedoed, Uefa created a new financial deal called the value pillar.

This reserves 35% of all Champions League revenues, or Â£713m, for the historically most successful clubs.

At the end of the league phase last season City are estimated to have earned Â£37.9m from the value pillar, Liverpool Â£35.5m and Chelsea Â£33.3m. Newcastle received only Â£13.6m.

The good news for Villa is their coefficient has improved greatly, aided by last season's Europa League win.

Their value pillar is projected to be worth Â£25m this season. But that will still be less than the big six clubs in the competition.

The cards are stacked against an ambitious club - not just domestically, but in Europe too.

Take a look at the financial trend of the big six and one thing becomes noticeable - Chelsea are beginning to fall behind.

In the past two seasons, and for the first time in recent history, the Blues recorded the lowest revenues of the big six.

Not just by a small amount. They were Â£74m behind Tottenham, and Â£200m below Liverpool and City.

Stamford Bridge has a capacity of only 40,000, with no easy scope for expansion. The rest of the big six have grounds which hold at least 60,000.

Chelsea generated an average spend per fan of Â£80 in 2024-25, the second highest in the league after Arsenal.

Yet with 20,000 fewer tickets sold than their big six rivals, Chelsea's total matchday revenues are dwarfed.

"Matchday spend has become increasingly important," Maguire said. "There has been a flatlining of broadcast revenues, especially since 2017.

"As a consequence, clubs have had to look at alternative methods of increasing revenues, especially with costs both on the pitch and off pitch rising so fast."

Matchday revenue has effectively stalled for Chelsea, rising only Â£16m over the past 10 years. United's has grown by Â£69m and Spurs, thanks to their new ground, by Â£85m.

"Chelsea are far more reliant upon player sales," Maguire said. "There is a growing divide because the global appeal is very much aligned with three or four clubs.

"Arsenal have re-established themselves with their progress in the Champions League. Manchester United are a brand in their own right. And Liverpool have expanded Anfield from 44,000 to 61,000, with many of those extra seats having gone to commercial."

Chelsea's successful player trading model has so far limited the impact.

But there are signs that, financially at least, the big six could become the big five.

You might recall that Villa and Newcastle were not fans of the old profit and sustainability rules (PSR).

PSR forced both clubs to sell some of their best players to balance the books.

That has changed this season with squad-cost ratio (SCR).

There is a key difference with SCR, in that it allows a club to spend the money they generate on players. PSR limited losses so was more restrictive.

"This does make it a little easier for the likes of Villa and Newcastle to spend money," Maguire said. "But it is still heavily weighted in favour of the big six.

"SCR is different to PSR rather than better. For some people, change is advantageous. For others, it isn't."

The big six generate hundreds of millions of pounds more than the other clubs. So they can spend hundreds of millions of pounds more on their squads.

This makes the big six even more powerful in the transfer market.

"Look at the clubs who voted against SCR - Brighton, Brentford, Bournemouth, Crystal Palace, Fulham and Leeds," Maguire said.

"It's very noticeable that those clubs have traditionally been very good at player sales, while they have tended to have lower matchday and commercial revenues."

Most of the big six - the outlier being Chelsea - are not remotely close to the Premier League's 85% SCR limit.

If you give clubs more money to spend on transfers, the likelihood is they will do just that. And the numbers back that up.

"Over the course of the last decade, transfer amortisation has more than doubled and close to tripled," Maguire said.

"Which means that on average, clubs in the Premier League are now spending, or now have a cost which goes into their SCR calculations of just under Â£100m."

Uefa fears that SCR will make matters worse, and have an inflationary impact across Europe.

And this is an important point: other than Villa and Newcastle, the other clubs are not trying to compete with the big six.

Clubs like Brentford and Brighton have shown that smart models are just as effective as fat wallets.

They sign the potential stars of the future, nurture them and then sell them to the big six.

Football's ecosystem recycles the big-six money through the transfer market.

But it is a precarious business for these other clubs.

"The smaller clubs have to get it right on a more regular basis than the bigger clubs," Maguire said.

"The big six can afford to make signings which are Â£60m and Â£70m, who turn out to be relative flops, and still have those players on high wages for several years."

And that is the real gap between the big six and the rest.

At 77, though, it didn’t suit. Wenger probably wouldn’t want a heavy defeat to Infantino in his legacy, as has been the case with other prospective candidates. Zlatan Ibrahimovic’s name was even floated. Amid the prospect of other candidates Uefa wouldn’t want, they felt they had to pick someone.

So, they are left with one repeatedly described as “a nobody”. Others insist Diallo is “a proper politician”, that can prise votes from Francophone Africa… but it does sound like rationalisation after the fact. As one high-profile football executive bluntly put of the whole Uefa strategy, “it’s a disaster”. And it may leave yet more fall-out for the game.

Uefa were insisting it was actually about Fifa as a body, but that should have been extended to them. The same debates about Infantino exceeding term limits can be applied to Ceferin. What’s more, Uefa might end up indirectly funding a body that ends up affecting its power. One view is that they should have forced a Fifa Council meeting when Infantino was most vulnerable. Others maintain that change at Fifa is only ever coming through the courts. Uefa, ironically, may end up facing a similar situation. Then, again, it’s all the same problem.

Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi received a subdued welcome on her first trip to China as premier, signaling Beijing's limited appetite for repairing ties strained by a simmering row over Taiwan.

At gatherings such as this week's Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen, protocol details — including who greets a visiting leader at the airport and where they are positioned in official photographs — often convey political messages.

Takaichi was met on the tarmac Wednesday by a retired Chinese diplomat, rather than a serving official, and Japan's ambassador to China, Kenji Kanasugi.

The low-key reception contrasted sharply with the warmer welcomes given to other visiting leaders, including South Korean President Lee Jae Myung, while U.S. President Donald Trump was greeted personally by Chinese President Xi Jinping.

In the leaders' group photo, Takaichi was placed in the back row, off to the side and several places from Xi, who stood alongside Trump and Russian President Vladimir Putin.

"Beijing sought to downgrade her official status to that of a head of government rather than a head of state," said Jeremy Chan, senior analyst at Eurasia Group.

During Thursday morning's greeting line, Takaichi spent several minutes trying to talk to the Chinese leader without an interpreter present, while most leaders exchanged handshakes and kept walking.

"It has become increasingly difficult for her to engage in formal or even informal conversations with Xi," said Kei Koga, an associate professor at Nanyang Technological University in Singapore, who sees Takaichi's intention as to still "project the image that she could still communicate with him despite the ongoing tensions."

At a press briefing Thursday afternoon, Takaichi said Japan's position on Taiwan remained unchanged as stated in the 1972 Japan-China Communique, adding that her leadership has been communicating with Chinese counterparts on rare earth export controls.

Takaichi's subdued reception signaled Beijing's lack of enthusiasm to repair ties with Tokyo, analysts said, potentially prolonging China's curbs on critical mineral exports that have gripped Japanese industries for the past year.

"China's frosty reception of Takaichi shows quite clearly that Beijing is not yet ready to forgive her over her remarks about Taiwan," Chan said, expecting the bilateral relationship to remain strained until Takaichi leaves office.

That could leave Japan grappling with continued restrictions on rare earth supplies from China next year, said Tianchen Xu, senior economist at the Economist Intelligence Unit.

Relations deteriorated sharply after Takaichi said in November 2025 that a Taiwan Strait crisis could constitute a situation threatening Japan's survival and warrant a self-defense response.

While the substance broadly aligned with Japan's existing security policy, previous prime ministers had generally avoided stating it so explicitly in a Taiwan scenario.

China reacted angrily and demanded that Takaichi retract the remarks. She has not done so, while stating that the door to dialogue with Beijing remains open, with minimal high-level contact over the past year.

China has since tightened restrictions affecting Japan, including controls on dual-use and rare earth exports with potential military uses. It also discouraged its citizens from traveling to Japan and halted Japanese seafood imports.

Takaichi's visit marked the first trip to China by a Japanese prime minister since Shinzo Abe traveled to Chengdu in December 2019 for a trilateral summit with Chinese and South Korean leaders.

No formal Takaichi-Xi meeting has been publicly scheduled during her visit. Meanwhile, Xi is expected to join Trump and Putin for a historic trilateral gathering on Thursday, alongside a bilateral meeting with Trump.

Takaichi needs to show a "reflection of what she said about Taiwan a year ago," said Huiyao Wang, founder of the Beijing-based Center for China and Globalization, which is often seen as close to Beijing's thinking.

Wang said Tokyo should show greater flexibility, pointing to Trump's comments that the U.S. would avoid getting involved in the Taiwan issue. He argued that Japan holding firm on the issue would not help relations with Beijing.

Such reflection, he said, "would really help to clarify some of those misunderstandings between the two countries."

Your Favorite Coffee Shop Is Probably Streaming Music Illegally. It’s the Music Industry’s Fault, Too (Guest Column)

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A new study found 81% of U.S. businesses don't properly license music, costing creators and other rightsholders. The fix starts with education — not lawsuits.

Walk into almost any shop, gym, salon or restaurant in America, and you’ll hear music. Ask the owner whether it’s licensed and you will likely get a confident “yes.” But in most cases, that answer is, unfortunately, wrong.

Soundtrack recently surveyed 1,525 decision-makers responsible for nearly 15,000 business locations across 10 customer-facing industries to find out how U.S. businesses actually play music. Though the law is clear that businesses need specific licenses to play music in public, the results of the survey describe a market that loves music, depends on it, and, overwhelmingly, does not properly pay for it.

Ninety-seven percent of the businesses we surveyed play music for customers, and 77% do it every day. Adjusted for the size of the U.S., that means roughly 3.8 million U.S. business locations play music in their stores.

Nine in 10 of those surveyed said music boosts employee motivation and shapes the energy of their space, while more than eight in 10 called it an essential part of the business and said it has to fit their brand. Rather than merely being a background expense, businesses see music as part of the product.

Yet despite this overwhelming demand, the way music played in businesses is paid for has not evolved. A striking 79% of businesses illicitly use a consumer streaming service, such as a personal Spotify, Pandora or YouTube account, to play music for customers. Only 28% of businesses use a service built and licensed for business. This is one of the biggest failures, and also one of the biggest opportunities, for the music industry today.

Aside from the scale of misuse, it is significant how many businesses are sure they’re doing it right. Seventy-eight percent of respondents were confident the music in their business is legally compliant. Eighty-three percent said they’re familiar with the rules. Yet 81% are misusing music, and 58% believe it’s legal to stream a personal account in their business.

Even I struggle to distill the U.S. licensing rules into just a few sentences; such is the unnecessary complexity surrounding them. Put simply, businesses require both a music service licensed for commercial use and licenses with all four U.S. performing rights organizations (PROs) — ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR — that cover the music they play.

Some licensed music services bundle performance rights, while others require businesses to secure separate public performance licenses from U.S. PROs. For a business owner, working out exactly what’s included can be a job in itself.

Too often I speak to business owners who believe they’re fully licensed but are missing key PRO licenses or still playing music via their personal streaming account. The survey reflects this, finding only 5% of businesses hold licenses from all four U.S. PROs.

However, this isn’t a story about bad actors. Nine in 10 businesses said compliance matters to them, and most said it matters that artists are paid fairly. The businesses using personal streaming accounts are doing it because it’s what they know. It’s easy, familiar and often free. It’s not a problem of intent; it’s a lack of information and a non-user-friendly system.

The cost to music creators and other rightsholders is real. Combining what businesses told us they spend on music with the number of establishments recorded by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, we estimate that annual spending on music would rise by about $1.4 billion per year if every business was properly licensed through a compliant business music service. That money should flow to songwriters, artists, publishers, labels and the services that license them legally.

The music industry shares the blame. No one proactively educates the market or gives a business owner a clear answer about what they need to be fully covered. Consumer platforms offer free tiers, teaching a generation of shop owners that music costs nothing. And business music services, mine included, have not explained the difference loudly or simply enough.

Enforcement alone won’t close a gap this wide. You can’t sue three million locations into compliance, and you shouldn’t want to. These are the customers who say music is essential to them. The answer is a coordinated push across trade organisations, PROs, publishers, labels, consumer platforms and business services to create one plain-language message about what is legal, provide simpler routes to full licensing and build services businesses actually want to use.

That last part matters. Businesses told us what they look for in streaming services designed for businesses: ease-of-use, price and good playlists (the main complaint about business music services is that their music selections are limited and repetitive). The music offered by these services has to be at least as good as the consumer app on the owner’s smartphone — or the consumer app will keep winning.

Streaming rebuilt the recorded music industry by making the legal option easier for individuals than piracy. Now, the same needs to happen for businesses. The demand is already there, and most want to do it right. What’s missing is clarity, and a legal option that’s simple to use and offers better selections.

That $1.4 billion isn’t lost. It’s waiting for the industry to make doing the right thing the easy thing.

Ola Sars is the co-founder and CEO of Soundtrack, the music streaming service for businesses. The study was conducted by Soundtrack and the NMPA in 2025, surveying 1,525 U.S. music decision-makers via Sago’s opt-in B2B panel.

A Bear Market Is Coming Eventually. History Says This Is the Best Investing Move You Can Make.

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A bear market is coming eventually, but history suggests the bigger risk to your wealth is how you react when it arrives.

By John Ballard – Nov 18, 2026 at 1:15PM EST

Bear markets are a regular part of investing. Since 1928, the S&P 500 has fallen into one -- a drop of at least 20% from a recent high -- about every 3.5 years, on average, according to Hartford Funds and Ned Davis Research. The typical bear market has erased about 35% of the index's value.

That sounds frightening, but bear markets are usually short. The average one has lasted about 9.6 months. These downturns are less a disaster than they are the cost of admission to building long-term wealth. The bigger risk is how investors react when the next one arrives.

For anyone with many years until retirement, here's why I'd stay fully invested.

According to Hartford and Ned Davis, the S&P 500 has had 28 bull markets and 27 bear markets since 1928. Through all those ups and downs, it delivered a compound annual return of about 10%, according to The Motley Fool's research. The market rises more in a bull market than it loses in a bear market.

It pays to keep investing when things look worst. Capital Group tracked the 18 biggest market declines since the Great Depression and found the S&P 500 was higher five years later every time. MFS found that after the 10 worst calendar years since 1975, a $100,000 investment was worth about $207,000 on average five years later.

Don't think that you'll be better off waiting to invest until the market moves higher. In fact, history says you'll be wealthier by staying fully invested through every down year.

J.P. Morgan Asset Management found that $10,000 invested in the S&P 500 at the start of 2006 grew to $80,619 by the end of 2025 for someone who stayed fully invested. However, missing just the 10 best days cut that to $35,866.

Take 2025: The S&P 500 fell nearly 19% in the spring, yet its best day came on April 9, in the middle of the turmoil. Missing that one day would have cut the year's 17.9% return to 7.6%.

Now, some will think: "I'll just sit in cash and wait until a down year to buy bargains and make a fortune." That's not a smart strategy either. Bear markets are infrequent. Over the past 95 years, stocks have been rising about 78% of the time, according to Hartford.

Bear markets are also hard to predict. A normal pullback of 10% or so can easily be mistaken for the start of a bigger decline.

Waiting for the perfect moment tends to backfire. Morningstar found that the average dollar in U.S. mutual funds and exchange-traded funds (ETFs) earned 8.7% a year over the decade ended 2025, versus 9.9% for the funds themselves, largely because of when investors bought and sold.

A bear market is, indeed, coming, but we don't know when. The best move is to stay invested and not sell when the market goes down, because missing the biggest up days after the bottom will cost you a lot more money down the road than if you just stayed invested.

Time in the market is far more important than market timing. From 1970 through September 2026, every 20-year rolling period for the S&P 500 produced a gain, according to Mellon data published by Ave Maria Mutual Funds.

Even the worst timing can still make money for patient investors. Hartford calculated that investing $10,000 at the peak just before each of the six bear markets since 1995 would have still turned $60,000 into $364,774 by the end of 2025. That beats staying in cash and earning almost nothing.

As Warren Buffett wrote in his 1991 shareholder letter, "The stock market serves as a relocation center at which money is moved from the active to the patient."

The best time to start is always when you have money available. Pick a low-cost index fund such as the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO -0.10%), which tracks the S&P 500, or the Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF (VTI -0.02%), which covers the whole U.S. market.

Set up an automatic purchase of a fixed amount each month or with each paycheck, and keep it running no matter what happens in the markets. Keep money you'll need in the next few years out of stocks, and you'll be on your way to growing your net worth.

My TFIE Strategy Briefing on the inland barges Europe will need in 2050 uses a cargo-first model to work from the freight task to the future electric fleet. The underlying 98-page Inland Freight Electrification report is available to read.

A vessel owner has two connected capital decisions. The first is whether the freight service will still have customers through the useful life of the asset. The second is which propulsion and charging system can perform that service economically. Starting with equipment and then finding a market for it risks spending scarce investment capital on the wrong vessels.

I modelled a large European industrial port corridor from a 2025-style baseline to 2050. The central scenario assumes bulk cargo falls from 57.8 million tonnes to 34.4 million tonnes, a decline of about 40%. Wet bulk accounts for much of the loss, falling from 34.5 million to 15.0 million tonnes. Dry bulk declines from 23.3 million to 19.4 million tonnes. The model is a transparent scenario for investment decisions across this type of corridor, rather than a forecast of Rotterdam’s exact future cargo volumes.

The largest change is fossil-linked cargo. Fossil fuels and refinery streams fall from 19.0 million tonnes to 1.5 million tonnes, while coal and other solid fossil fuels fall from 2.5 million tonnes to zero. Combined, those reductions account for about 20 million tonnes of the scenario’s 23.4-million-tonne net decline. Any operator whose principal customers depend on moving coal or petroleum faces a different replacement decision from one carrying grain, construction minerals, chemicals or scrap.

Steel illustrates what cargo substitution means. The model reduces imported iron ore and other virgin iron units from 4.5 million tonnes to 3.0 million tonnes, while scrap and circular feedstocks rise from 1.8 million to 3.5 million tonnes. The industrial function continues as the materials moving to and from factories change. Existing barges and terminals may find new work, although parcel sizes, loading systems and routes will also change.

Biofuel feedstocks create a second trap. In the central scenario those movements peak near 5 million tonnes in 2030 before falling to 1.5 million tonnes in 2050. A temporary freight boom can support profitable operations without supporting a new vessel or terminal investment with a thirty-year life. The durability of the contract matters as much as the volume carried this year.

Cargo tonnage alone cannot size an electric fleet. The model translates commodities into powered vessel-equivalents using parcel sizes, routes, loaded calls and productivity. It starts with around 1,150 powered vessel-equivalents and ends with 664, a reduction of 42%. Dry cargo falls 17% in tonnes but 43% in powered vessel-equivalents because the modelled freight service changes. The starting 1,150 powered equivalents represent about 1,234 physical hulls after counting unpowered barges moved by pushers. Battery conversion decisions concern powered units; terminal and cargo-handling decisions also concern the additional hulls.

That distinction is particularly important for pushed formations. A pusher provides propulsion for several cargo barges. Converting one powered unit does not necessarily mean replacing every physical hull. Counting all hulls as propulsion conversions would inflate the equipment and financing requirement.

Low Rhine water levels bring a different constraint into the same calculation. A barge can remain on the register yet carry less freight per journey when draught is restricted. Cargo owners may have to buy more voyages, use different hulls or shift some freight to rail and road. Route depth, seasonal load factors and terminal access belong in the service model alongside cargo demand.

Ports should begin with a living cargo and route register showing which freight is declining, which remains, and which changes its material inputs. Vessel owners can connect that register to remaining hull life, annual utilization, cargo contracts and the cost of electric conversion. Utilities and terminal operators can then size grid connections, charging facilities and battery-container pools against vessels that will run on bankable routes. Infrastructure developed around dependable freight customers has a stronger prospect of earning a return.

Europe has good reasons to electrify established, high-utilization inland freight now. Battery-powered vessels are already serving container and bulk routes. The commercial opportunity is to invest in routes with surviving customers and electrify the services they will continue to buy. Expanding an electric fleet before measuring that freight task puts the order of decisions backward.

GLP-1 Eye Warning Leaves Patients, Doctors With a Difficult Choice

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

— FDA should study outcomes when patients stop, continue, or change treatment after NAION

When someone loses vision in one eye while taking a GLP-1 drug, the first priority is an urgent eye examination. If the diagnosis is non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION), the question that follows is: should we stop the drug?

On November 9, FDA announced it is requiring new warnings across all products in the GLP-1 drug class, which includes semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Zepbound, Mounjaro), among others, about a possible increased risk of NAION.

NAION occurs when inadequate blood flow damages the nerve that carries visual information from the eye to the brain. It can cause sudden, painless vision loss, often noticed on waking. The loss can be permanent, and there is no proven treatment to reverse it. The condition is rare, but these medications are widely used: in a March 2026 KFF poll, about one in eight U.S. adults reported currently taking a GLP-1 drug and one in five reported ever taking one.

As an ophthalmologist, I have routinely asked patients with suspected NAION about GLP-1 use for the past 2 years. I welcome the warning. The FDA should now pair it with a plan to study what happens when patients stop, continue, or change treatment after a NAION diagnosis.

People who develop NAION in one eye are already at increased risk of developing it in the other. How GLP-1 drugs might contribute remains uncertain. One hypothesis is that rapid reductions in blood sugar could cause swelling where the optic nerve enters the eye, compromising blood flow in susceptible patients. That possibility does not tell us whether continuing the medication adds to the risk in the other eye, or whether stopping reduces it.

These medications can be difficult to give up. Some patients take them to manage diabetes that has resisted other treatment. Certain drugs in the class also reduce the risk of heart attacks or worsening kidney disease in eligible patients. Stopping requires a plan for the condition being treated. When counseling patients who develop NAION while taking a GLP-1 drug, I recommend involving the prescribing doctor before changing treatment.

Regulators around the world have responded differently to this dilemma. European guidance says to stop semaglutide if NAION is confirmed. The FDA, whose warning covers the broader drug class, advises clinicians to consider discontinuation unless the benefits clearly outweigh the risk. I support that approach, with the patient, ophthalmologist, and prescribing doctor weighing the potential harm to vision against the benefits of treatment with a GLP-1.

Across 126 clinical trials, the FDA identified 15 probable NAION cases. The rates were equivalent to roughly six cases per 100,000 patients followed for a year among those receiving GLP-1 drugs, compared with four among those receiving a placebo or another diabetes medication. Larger observational studies have also raised concern, though their estimates vary and they cannot fully separate the effects of treatment from the conditions that led patients to receive it.

But estimating the risk of developing NAION does not tell us whether a patient should continue a GLP-1 drug after NAION diagnosis. One major FDA study excluded people with previous damage to the optic nerve from inadequate blood flow to help identify new cases accurately. That was a sensible design for its purpose, but it also means the study cannot establish whether continuing treatment after a diagnosis changes the risk to the other eye.

Some research has examined second-eye involvement. An analysis presented at the American Academy of Ophthalmology annual meeting this fall found that, among patients who developed NAION in both eyes, the second eye was affected sooner in GLP-1 users. It did not establish whether stopping would have prevented that outcome. Nor do we have reliable comparative evidence that switching to another GLP-1 drug is safer.

The FDA should assess whether its existing safety surveillance can answer these questions. If those systems fall short, the agency should require manufacturers to support additional studies. Federal law gives the agency that authority for serious safety concerns when routine reporting and available surveillance are insufficient.

The work could begin in participating eye clinics, linking confirmed diagnoses to prescribing and medical records. Researchers would document which medication a patient was taking, whether it was stopped or changed, when, and why. They would follow vision in the other eye alongside overall health, including how well replacement treatment managed diabetes or other conditions. Researchers would need to account for differences in diabetes severity and visual damage that affect both treatment decisions and outcomes.

The FDA should publish the study plans, identify who is responsible, and set dates for progress updates. Funding should be disclosed and results analyzed independently of manufacturers. Because NAION is uncommon, firm answers may take years. Patients and clinicians should still be able to see what is being learned and what remains uncertain.

The patient deciding what to do about the next dose cannot wait years for better evidence. Today's decision will have to be made with what we know. We owe that patient our best judgment, and we should follow what happens afterward. The next person facing the same choice deserves more to go on.

He’s in demand, but choosing wisely, and in roles that slyly play off his own status as an out gay man (a rare thing indeed) but with something off-center beneath, often a secret. He played Tom Ripley, one even more sociopathic than Matt Damon’s own iconically calculating Adonis, to devastating effect in the sinister Netflix series “Ripley,” there more explicitly grappling with the Patricia Highsmith character’s ambiguous (but was it?) sexuality. He played the Hot Priest who turned down a chance at heterosexual, secular love in “Fleabag,” of course, and starred in “All of Us Strangers” as a lonely adult orphan living in a glittering tower, bracing himself for romantic intimacy with Paul Mescal. I also saw him playing up to nine roles in Anton Chekhov’s “Vanya” onstage in New York City last year as men and women of all sorts, showing an actor who can shimmy between genders and across ranges. He has the unmistakable warmth of someone you should trust, but with intrigue behind the curtain of many of his performances to suggest that maybe you shouldn’t. He’s that good.

Scott has now taken on what will, to this point, I suppose, go down as “the role of a lifetime” in director Simon Stone’s rueful, pained, and unremarkable new film drama “Elsinore” as Scottish actor Ian Charleson, who died of HIV/AIDS complications while portraying Hamlet at the National Theatre in London in 1990. Charleson’s story is unlikely to be one most are familiar with. Even the most public of deaths among the most publicly gay of people often still remain in hiding, though Charleson wasn’t out himself. He resisted pressures from his inner circle to announce his disease amid a stage run that miraculously lasted 24 performances. Actors then were afraid of damaging their reputations. It’s not that different now, even as many advancements have made HIV/AIDS feel locally like a plague of the past.

A blond-headed Scott throws himself valiantly into the role of Charleson with the urgency and commitment of any of his best work, but the problem isn’t his: It’s that the movie surrounding him dulls the greatness of his performance with its conventional storytelling, and over-complication in post. “Elsinore” is one of those movies where you can feel the filmmaker — here, Stone, known for both stage and screen — actively laboring to “open up the play” with the opportunities of film. But “Elsinore” was never a play. Instead, it’s a movie where every emotional beat worth lingering on, especially Charleson’s swan-song performances as Hamlet, is churned into a frenzied montage of editing that won’t let the material take a breath. Or the audience sink in.

Charleson played an Olympian athlete in the Best Picture-winning 1981 film “Chariots of Fire,” and an Anglican priest in the next year’s Best Picture topper, “Gandhi.” That was after playing an outré bisexual in the 1978 film “Jubilee,” directed by his former lover, the very openly gay director Derek Jarman, who died of AIDS in 1994. Aside from a handful of film credits, including one alluded to in “Elsinore” as Dario Argento’s giallo “Opera,” Charleson otherwise kept to the stage. Scott’s Charleson at one point in this film opines about how much more satisfying stage work is for an actor without an editor there to chop you up to pieces.

Stone, working from a screenplay by Stephen Beresford (himself a playwright), does not follow his protagonist’s lead. Every thoughtful consideration of Kaposi’s sarcoma lesions, or the retching of blood into buckets under the care of dutiful practitioner Dr. Margaret Johnson (Olivia Colman) to show the terrible realities of the disease, is whipped into what feels like a feature-length trailer. The best scenes out of the “Hamlet” production detailed here are tossed into a rushed montage near the movie’s end that gives us no sense of what we are promised is a performance of enormity. (The play in 1990 was directed by Richard Eyre, later the filmmaker behind features like the campy diva battle “Notes on a Scandal.” He’s played in “Elsinore” by Johnny Flynn.)

A couple of things are going on: “Elsinore” triggers in a watchful gay viewer’s senses the signifiers of what would be the AIDS awards-bait movie of serious gravitas. Tom Hanks’ Oscar-winning performance in “Philadelphia.” Ed Harris in “The Hours.” Everyone who came out of the cringey “Dallas Buyers Club” with Academy Awards in 2014. There are others, though more lost to time. We become suspicious of this device, especially when paired with great acting. Is HIV/AIDS being exploited in the package of a would-be Oscar hit? Or are we showing young queer audiences how it was, how we got to where we are, and how where that is still isn’t perfect? It can be both, but the structure of “Elsinore” tips toward the former.

Then, there’s a veritable, cheeky who’s-who of British gay literati and luminaries lingering about “Elsinore’s” 1989 London milieu that’s irresistible in theory, but often drab in execution. There’s Eyre, of course, and Jarman (David Dawson, as excellent and tender and wounded here as he was in his screen breakthrough “My Policeman”). The afterburn of his relationship with Charleson lingers affectingly, though. After some drinks, he invites Charleson home, but they instead part with one of those tacit kisses shared by people who once loved each other but can’t make it work anymore. There’s too much history here. The lingering scorches of Charleson’s secret affairs and friendships with various (and out) men make for the best parts of “Elsinore,” but there’s no life to the telling. (Joe Locke plays a young man also ailing in Johnson’s AIDS clinic alongside Charleson.)

The pallid grayness of Mike Eley’s cinematography is probably supposed to mimic the finality of HIV/AIDS taking hold, but there’s no passion aesthetically to counterbalance what becomes absolute misery onscreen to watch. I’m not in the business of comparing one gay social drama to another, but Robin Campillo’s 2017 Cannes-winning French drama “Beats Per Minute” imbued an albeit more activist-oriented story of ACT UP fighting against AIDS suppression in Paris with such heat and spectacle. Watching someone die of AIDS in a room becomes a bore unless anyone is given a reason to keep on living.

That reason is, of course, supposed to be Charleson’s performance in “Hamlet,” which is relegated to a whirlwind sequence at the movie’s end after many pained rehearsals where he refused to drop out (and where Johnson encouraged him to). Scott has a mesmerizing hold on his scenes, and he’s compelling to watch in his repartee with Colman as Johnson, who treated the disease at a time when it was most stigmatized. She named her groundbreaking HIV testing clinic after him; that’s how much he impacted her. We are told how much Charleson mattered to people, but never shown it. Which unfortunately shuffles off “Elsinore,” named after the Danish town where “Hamlet” was set and where its main character died, off to the realm of HIV/AIDS movies seeking prestige over deep feeling.

No investment pays a high return without the risk of a loss, and money set aside for a down payment, a tuition bill, or an emergency can’t take that risk.

Each option below is protected by FDIC insurance (NCUA insurance at a credit union), the U.S. government, or a fund’s short-term holdings. What separates them is how soon you can get your money back and what it costs to get it early, so the date you’ll need the money decides which one fits.

Here’s what protects each one, how fast you can reach the money, and the catch.

Use Treasury bills as the yardstick for any rate you’re offered. The Federal Reserve’s latest daily figures put the three-month Treasury yield, the annual rate the government pays to borrow for three months, at 4.32%. A product sold as low-risk that pays 7% or 8% does so because it carries a risk you can’t see. The SEC flags “high returns with little or no risk” as a sign of a Ponzi scheme. The one safe exception is the I bond, whose rate follows inflation and can top Treasury yields when inflation runs high.

Money you might need any day, like an emergency fund, belongs in high-yield savings or a money market fund, not a savings account earning the national average, which the FDIC puts at 0.39%. That’s the reason to keep money you’ll need within five years out of stocks.

Write down the month you’ll need the money, cross off every option you can’t cash out by then without a penalty, and put the money in whichever of the rest pays the most today.

Emotional intelligence, the ability to notice and manage your own feelings and read other people’s, is a skill you can build. When an email or comment at work makes you tense, put the feeling into one word, such as irritated or worried, before you answer. Naming it gives you a moment to choose your reply.

A curt email or a sharp remark in a meeting takes seconds, and living it down takes far longer. If coworkers say you come across harsher than you mean to, those first seconds are what they’re reacting to.

Emotional intelligence grows with training. A 2019 analysis of 58 studies, published in Human Resource Management Review, found that training programs raised participants’ scores by a moderate amount.

Currently, nurses, physical therapists and other workers “with exceptional ability in the sciences or arts” are granted an exemption from an administrative test in the permanent labor certification program (PERM), a months-long administrative process in which an employer must prove there are no able, willing, qualified and available U.S. workers for a permanent job ahead of sponsoring a foreign worker.

The exemption, referred to Schedule A, is given to occupations where the DOL recognizes a nationwide shortage of U.S. workers. Nurses and physical therapists have been listed under Schedule A for more than 30 years.

The Department of Labor, in its newly proposed rule, said it wants to eliminate Schedule A as it cannot “demonstrate that the occupations currently listed in Schedule A are still shortage occupations.”

It cited in the proposed rule a late 2023 request for information published under the Biden administration seeking input on potentially expanding Schedule A. Despite more than 2,000 responses, DOL now says it didn’t receive the information it would need to establish a methodology for regularly reviewing and updating Schedule A occupations, and questioned whether it has the statutory authority to make such occupational exemptions in the first place.

“The elimination of Schedule A would enable the Department to better ensure that U.S. workers have an opportunity to apply for available job opportunities, and adhere to the statutory requirements to protect U.S. workers assigned to it, as intended by Congress under the [Immigration and Nationality Act].”

The proposed rule, which is open to public comment for 60 days, also includes requirements for employers to advertise their open jobs more broadly and for a longer period of time to demonstrate a good-faith effort to recruit American workers before turning to a green card sponsorship. These and other proposed changes would be the first “comprehensive” reforms to the PERM program in almost two decades, the Department of Labor said.

If finalized as-is, employers who want to sponsor professional nurses, physical therapists and other affected roles “would be required to go through the lengthier and costlier PERM labor market testing process, adding time, cost and administrative work to a pathway that hospitals, nursing homes and rehabilitation providers have historically relied on to fill persistent shortages,” Caroline Tang, an Austin shareholder at labor and employment law firm Ogletree Deakins, told Fierce Healthcare.

The mortgage industry was built to help Americans buy homes. An aging America needs more from it.

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Survey of 2,000 homeowners 55 plus shows 88% prioritize staying home and 90% prioritize independence

For decades, the mortgage industry has helped Americans achieve one of their biggest financial goals: buying a home and building equity over time.

It’s a model we know well. Homeowners purchase properties, make mortgage payments, refinance when it makes financial sense and gradually accumulate wealth. Much of the mortgage industry is structured around supporting that journey.

But what happens after decades of building that wealth?

As homeowners approach retirement, the financial equation shifts. Instead of focusing primarily on acquiring a home and paying down debt, the question becomes how to use the wealth they’ve accumulated to support the years ahead. Housing presents a unique opportunity but also a challenge because, for many Americans, their largest financial asset is also the place they call home.

You can sell a stock without moving. Selling a home is another matter. Trading down and moving to a less expensive home to tap home equity is both expensive and difficult. This might be why our recent research shows that the desire to remain at home continues to grow stronger with age.

In a recent Longbridge Financial, LLC (NMLS #957935) study of more than 2,000 U.S. homeowners ages 55 and older, 90% said maintaining their independence was extremely or very important to their ideal retirement. Nearly as many (88%) said the same about remaining in their homes. Both priorities ranked above paying for health care, paying off debt, long-term care and providing financial support to family members.

The desire to age in place isn’t surprising. What’s particularly revealing is how strongly that sentiment differs across age groups.

Among homeowners ages 55 to 64, 85% rated remaining in their homes as extremely or very important. That figure rose to 88% among those ages 65 to 74 and 93% among homeowners 75 and older. The importance of maintaining independence followed a similar pattern, climbing from 87% in the youngest group to 96% in the oldest.

These are differences between age groups at one point in time, rather than a measure of how individual preferences change over time. Still, the pattern points to an important finding in understanding how homeowners think about retirement.

Over decades, homeowners accumulate substantial equity through mortgage payments and increases in property values. Yet the very home that holds the wealth is also where many want to spend their later years. Its financial value tells only part of the story. Giving a homeowner a choice to move to a less expensive, and thus less attractive, home to use their home equity may not be much of a choice at all.

Consider two retired households with the same net worth. One holds most of its wealth in savings and investments. The other holds much of it in home equity.

Their balance sheets might look similar, but their financial flexibility is different. The first household may be able to draw from liquid accounts to cover an unexpected expense. The second has wealth, too, but accessing it may involve borrowing against the home or selling it altogether. Both options come with trade-offs, particularly when remaining in the home is a priority.

For an industry accustomed to measuring property values, loan balances and equity, this is an important distinction. These figures describe a homeowner’s financial position, but not necessarily how easily they can access that wealth — or what they might have to give up in the process.

The same survey reinforces why this distinction matters. Among homeowners ages 55 to 64, 73% rated paying for health care as extremely or very important to their ideal retirement. That figure climbed to 85% among those 75 and older. While these findings don’t tell us how much homeowners will spend or whether they’ll tap into their home equity, they do highlight how financial priorities can shift across different stages of life.

Mortgage finance has traditionally centered on transactions: purchasing, refinancing, servicing and eventually paying off a loan. Those functions remain essential. But paying off a mortgage doesn’t end a homeowner’s financial relationship with their home.

For some older homeowners, selling or downsizing may be the right choice. Others may have sufficient income and savings to remain in place. Still others may consider home equity loans, lines of credit or reverse mortgages, depending on their financial circumstances, eligibility and the associated costs and risks.

The opportunity for our industry extends beyond any single product. It’s about understanding the role home equity can play in supporting homeowners through retirement, while recognizing the personal value they place on remaining in their homes.

It also requires recognizing that homeowners’ priorities differ. In our study, 93% of women rated independence as extremely or very important, compared with 88% of men. Similar five-point gaps appeared for remaining at home and paying for health care. While the research doesn’t explain why these differences exist, it underscores the importance of understanding homeowners as individuals rather than treating homeowners 55 and over as a single market with identical needs and priorities.

Housing has always been both an investment and a place to live. As homeowners age, the balance between those roles deserves greater attention from the industry that helped them achieve homeownership in the first place.

We’ve spent decades helping Americans build wealth through homeownership. The next opportunity is helping them understand how that wealth can support the years ahead, without losing sight of what they value most: independence, financial flexibility and, for many, the ability to remain at home.

Rents Rise Annually for First Time in 3 Years as Multifamily Building Boom Stalls

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The median asking rent posted its first annual increase in more than three years in October, fueled by a sharp nationwide pullback in multifamily construction.

October marks the first month of year-over-year rent growth since June 2023, according to the latest Realtor.com® monthly rental report. The increase was modest, with the median asking rent rising by just $1 to $1,692, but the underlying data suggests more hikes could follow.

Larger units drove the rebound. Two-bedrooms led the way, with median asking rent rising 0.3% year over year to $1,889, followed by one-bedrooms, which edged up 0.1% to $1,566.

Meanwhile, studio rents continued to lag, falling 1.2% from October 2025 to $1,411.

"I see this as the market beginning to rebalance," Lissette Calderon, CEO of Neology Group, a Miami-based real estate development firm, tells Realtor.com. "We had an extraordinary amount of new supply delivered over the last few years, and it takes time for that inventory to be absorbed. With construction slowing and demand for rental housing remaining strong, I expect rent growth to gradually return, particularly in markets where employment and household growth continue to support housing demand."

Realtor.com senior economist Jiayi Xu confirms that last month's rent turnaround hinges on a shifting supply pipeline.

The post-pandemic multifamily construction boom of 2021–22 flooded the market with fresh rental units completed in 2023–25, keeping prices in check.

Now, a broad cooling in multifamily construction is easing downward pressure on rent, just as high mortgage rates continue to keep families in the rental market.

"The path ahead largely depends on whether new multifamily deliveries remain strong enough to outpace that demand," says Xu.

Multifamily developers remain pessimistic about current building conditions, according to the latest quarterly survey from the National Association of Home Builders (NAHB) released last week.

The NAHB's Multifamily Production Index (MPI) had a reading of 41 out of 100, down 5 points year over year. Any reading below 50 indicates that more builders view market conditions negatively than positively.

"It is not surprising that the MPI is down year over year given supply-side headwinds such as high interest rates and a rising rental vacancy rate," says NAHB Chief Economist Robert Dietz.

According to the U.S. Census Bureau's Housing Vacancy Survey, the national rental vacancy rate reached 7.6% in the third quarter, the highest level since early 2014.

Kip Lewis, chairman of NAHB’s Multifamily Council, also points to construction costs rising relative to rents, along with delays getting projects approved and connected to roads and utilities.

"As a result, many projects are taking longer to complete," says Lewis.

Echoing Lewis, Calderon says the building pace comes down to the economics of development.

"Construction costs, financing terms, insurance, and operating expenses all affect whether a project can move forward," she says. "When projected rents don’t support the total cost, securing debt and equity becomes more challenging. Developers want to build, but the numbers have to work."

A closer look at the housing data reveals just how dramatically multifamily construction has shifted over the past three years.

Between 2023 and 2025, roughly 1.5 million units in buildings with five or more units were completed, an average of 499,000 per year, up 37.7% from 2020–22.

Those newly completed units boosted the total rental stock by 3.2%, driven by the multifamily starts boom of 2021–23, when ground was broken on more than 1.45 million units.

That surge in rental construction is now fading. In the third quarter of 2026, the multifamily pipeline shrank significantly across the board, with starts averaging 403,000, down more than 9% from a year ago amid soaring financing and construction costs. Units under construction similarly pulled back, averaging 674,000, marking a year-over-year decline of 2.7%.

Completions took the biggest hit, plunging 26% from a year ago to an annualized rate of 330,000 new rental units in the third quarter as the 2023–24 cooldown in housing starts worked its way through the pipeline.

"Given the year-over-year decline in units under construction and higher construction costs, we do not expect deliveries to rebound over the next 12 months," says Xu.

That outlook aligns with NAHB’s 2027 forecast, which predicts a further decline in multifamily starts.

"A thinner pipeline could create pressure in markets where demand continues to grow, but it doesn’t necessarily mean we’re headed for a severe shortage or another sharp increase in rents," says Calderon. "That will depend on how much existing inventory remains available, local employment and household growth, and what is already under construction."

At the regional level, multifamily completions fell year over year everywhere in the third quarter due to fewer starts in 2023–24. The Northeast saw the most dramatic pullback, with finished units dropping nearly 50% from 2025 levels.

"Thinner deliveries could further push up rents in Northeast metros, especially those that have already returned to year-over-year growth, such as Philadelphia and Pittsburgh," says Xu.

The South came in a distant second, with completions falling 28% year over year, followed by the Midwest (-17.9%) and the West (-14.2%).

At the same time, units under construction fell over 11% in the Northeast and 8.8% in the South from a year ago, but increased 4.5% in the West and 12.9% in the Midwest, pointing to a split in active pipelines.

Despite the Midwest's robust building activity, rents are on the rise in several major market metros, including Chicago (+3.2%), St. Louis (+3.2%), and Minneapolis (+2.0%).

When it comes to new groundbreakings, starts in the Northeast surged a staggering 48.4% year over year, making it the only region in positive territory.

“Because projects take about two years to complete in the Northeast, that rebound is unlikely to lift deliveries in 2027," warns Xu.

By contrast, starts fell 12.4% in the West, 16.5% in the South, and 29.2% in the Midwest.

"I remain optimistic about South Florida over the long term, while recognizing that each project needs to be evaluated on its own merits," says Calderon. "For us, that means looking closely at location, the renter we’re serving, competing supply, and the total cost of delivery."

For renters in Western markets such as Los Angeles and Denver, who have seen some relief in asking rents, the slowdown in new construction could reverse that trajectory going forward.

Based on data from Yardi Matrix, a commercial real estate intelligence platform that relies on a distinct methodology from Realtor.com, the report found that new apartment construction dropped 27% in 2026, with the Northeast standing out as the sole region to see building activity rise, led by New York City.

[But t]he October 12 Order … does not impose consequences for particular statements adjudicated unlawful. It prohibits Mr. Hughes from speaking about entire categories of people in the future. That is a prior restraint in its most direct form which is presumed unconstitutional….

Test Masters Educ. Servs., Inc. v. Singh (5th Cir. 2005) … recognized that a court may enjoin "harassing communication" and "repeated invasions of privacy." But an injunction prohibiting all communication, rather than limiting itself to "threatening or harassing conduct," "swept too broadly" and constituted an unconstitutional restraint. Id.…

Plaintiffs cannot justify the restraint merely by characterizing Mr. Hughes's commentary as malicious, coercive, or injurious. The Supreme Court rejected that reasoning in Keefe. There, the Court held that an individual's "interest in being free from public criticism of his business practices" did not justify an injunction against speech…. Plaintiffs may have remedies for unlawful conduct. But those remedies do not include an order forbidding Mr. Hughes from speaking about them at all….

Defendant also argues (again correctly, I think) that the order was unconstitutional because defendant wasn't given an opportunity to be heard before the order was entered:

The absence of notice presents a separate constitutional defect. In Carroll v. President & Commissioners of Princess Anne (1968), the Supreme Court set aside a short ex parte order restraining expression because of "a basic infirmity in the procedure by which it was obtained." The order "was issued ex parte, without notice to petitioners and without any effort, however informal, to invite or permit their participation in the proceedings."

There is a place in our jurisprudence for ex parte issuance, without notice, of temporary restraining orders of short duration; There is no place within the area of basic freedoms guaranteed by the First Amendment for such orders where no showing is made that it is impossible to serve or to notify the opposing parties and to give them an opportunity to participate.

The Court also rejected the suggestion that a later hearing could cure the problem. The availability of a subsequent opportunity to be heard "does not overcome the infirmity in the absence of a showing of justification for the ex parte nature of the proceedings."

The reason is straightforward. "The value of judicial decision-making … is substantially diluted where the process is ex parte, because the Court does not have available the fundamental instrument for judicial judgment: "an adversary proceeding in which both parties may participate." And where First Amendment freedoms are at stake, any order must be "couched in the narrowest terms that will accomplish the pin-pointed objective."

The October 12 Order fails both principles. It was entered without notice or an adversary hearing. It contains no finding that notifying Mr. Hughes was impossible. And far from identifying a narrow category of unlawful speech, it prohibits virtually any online commentary concerning the Plaintiffs, their families, or their counsel.

Carroll is directly controlling on the constitutional defect. An ex parte order restraining expression cannot stand without a showing that notice and participation were impossible. No such showing appears here….

More than just a football club: Cardinale’s vision to make AC Milan a multi-billion empire

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In case you weren’t aware from his repeatedly bold comments in the media, Gerry Cardinale has big plans for AC Milan.

The RedBird Capital founder and chief has made one concept very clear: while he is at the helm, Milan are no longer just a football club, but a platform that aims to integrate sports, infrastructure, and entertainment. Of course AC Milan will be at the centre of the web, but the idea is to expand.

The proof of this comes from a three-letter acronym: MSE. It stands for Milan Sports & Entertainment and it is the new holding company created by RedBird. The idea, simply put, is to bring together investments and activities in various sectors under one umbrella.

As La Gazzetta dello Sport recall, Cardinale explained to the Financial Times that the assets involved could be valued in the mid-single-digit billions, meaning several billion dollars. So, how does he plan to get there?

Cardinale linked the creation of MSE to the process initiated with the acquisition of Milan: “Milan Sports & Entertainment is the culmination of this work and of what we have built since we first invested in the club four years ago.”

In his interview with La Gazzetta, Cardinale clarified that the new structure is also created to support the Rossoneri’s sporting ambitions: “The driving force behind operations like this is to provide Milan with the financial strength needed to compete in the transfer market and secure the best players in the world.”

There are examples of this kind of model working already. Kroenke Sports & Entertainment, AEG and Fenway Sports Group are among the most prominent and – given his background in the USA – Cardinale knows each of them well.

According to CNBC valuations, KSE was worth $26.05bn in 2026, FSG $15.37bn, and AEG $11.99bn in 2025, excluding music and hotel businesses.

KSE boasts franchises such as the Los Angeles Rams, Denver Nuggets, Colorado Avalanche and Arsenal, as well as venues such as SoFi Stadium, Ball Arena, and Emirates Stadium. The group combines sports, infrastructure and real estate development such as Hollywood Park, the district built around SoFi Stadium.

AEG – founded by Philip Anschutz – have instead built its model on the management and development of venues. It owns or manages venues such as Crypto.com Arena and The O2 in London plus the Los Angeles Kings and LA Galaxy, and operates in concert promotion and ticketing. The LA LIVE project developed around Crypto.com Arena puts the venue at the centre of a greater ecosystem.

Fenway Sports Group – born around the Boston Red Sox and Liverpool FC – have developed a holding company with interests in media, marketing, real estate, and entertainment. It’s a model Cardinale knows first hand: in 2021, RedBird invested approximately $750m in FSG, acquiring a stake of around 11%, based on an enterprise valuation of $7.35bn.

MSE in theory can harness elements of all three of these experiences: KSE’s integration of sports and infrastructure, AEG ‘s commercial development of facilities and FSG ‘s multi-sport approach. The main difference is the purpose declared by Cardinale: to use all of it to funnel money back into Milan.

From the stadium shared with Inter to the possibility of an arena in San Donato, including the new NBA Europe venture and even tennis: MSE’s scope includes various initiatives that go beyond the playing field.

The main infrastructure project is the future Generali Stadium, the new 71,500-seat facility designed by Foster + Partners and Manica. Developed jointly by the two Milanese clubs, it represents a key element of the transformation of the San Siro area and will not be a Rossoneri-only facility.

The promoters intend for the stadium to host year-round activities, from matches to concerts, as well as catering, events, and hospitality services. In other words, like the stadiums and arenas mentioned above, it will become more than just a place to go for two hours on a Sunday.

In San Donato Milanese, RedBird are also considering the possibility of building a 20,000-seat arena. Cardinale explained that the fund already owns the land and has planning permission, but the idea is also linked to Milan’s potential involvement in NBA Europe. Another area of ​​focus concerns tennis, with the possibility of hosting a tournament in the city.

Finally, RedBird intend to develop a company dedicated to managing stadiums and arenas, with services including catering, merchandising and premium hospitality. The goal is to build commercial expertise to apply to the facilities connected to the group, but also to make it available to other owners and sports clubs.

This phase will be co-ordinated by Brett Yormark, appointed CEO of MSE and set to step down as head of the Big 12 (a conference in College Football) at the end of January 2027. He worked for 14 years at Brooklyn Sports & Entertainment, overseeing the development of the Brooklyn Nets and the Barclays Center. He has also gained experience in NASCAR and Roc Nation.

The choice to go for Yormark reflects the desire to entrust the platform to someone with expertise across multiple different fields in the sporting and commercial worlds.

All these grand plans are well and good, but how will ideas turn into money that drives AC Milan forward? MSE’s economic potential is measured not only through the club’s revenues, but also through the activities that RedBird intends to develop around sports, infrastructure and entertainment.

The starting point is obviously Milan as a club, which closed the 2025-26 financial year with revenues of €464.6m, over €100m of which came from sponsorships. The main infrastructure opportunity is the new San Siro, an investment of approximately €2.3bn shared with Inter.

Projections contained in the design documents indicate a potential annual revenue of approximately €120.4m for the facility as a whole (both for the Rossoneri and the Nerazzurri), through sporting, commercial, and complementary services

These are numbers that will be revised upwards by the two clubs when considering, for example, the record agreement with Generali for naming rights. It is worth a total of €650m over 25 years, divided into €10m per year for the first five years and €30m per season for the following 20, once the stadium is built.

One of the areas on which RedBird Capital are focusing most is hospitality, which is currently worth around €15m per year for the Rossoneri. The international benchmark has already been set by Legends Global, a company Cardinale knows well.

Legends are what the Milan owner sees as a model for developing premium services, restaurants, and commercial spaces. The most obvious example is at FC Barcelona: with the support of LG, the club have marketed nearly 5,000 VIP seats at Camp Nou, generating over €380m in contracted revenue.

They aim to generate another €700m through multi-year licenses for an additional 2,000 seats. This isn’t annual revenue, but rather contracts spread over 15 or 30 years, so a guaranteed flow of money coming in that Cardinale aims to replicate.

Another agreement which demonstrates the potential of this kind of arrangement is the one with Real Madrid for the Santiago Bernabéu. It involves an investment of approximately €360m in exchange for 20-year participation in some of the stadium’s businesses,.

Legends Global also operate in facilities and events management. Following the acquisition of ASM Global (who actually proposed renovating San Siro for non-football events in Italy) in 2024, the group claims a presence in over 450 venues, approximately 20,000 events, and 165 million guests annually. Their European partnerships include Arsenal, Chelsea and Porto.

They manage the Assago Forum which hosts approximately 60-70 concerts a year, demonstrating how a venue can generate activity well beyond the sports calendar.

As mentioned, an arena is being planned in San Donato with an estimated investment of around €400m, which could become the basis for a Milanese franchise in NBA Europe. For reference, Olimpia Milano – the current basketball team in the city – generate around €15m through ticketing, season tickets, sponsorships and merchandising.

For NBA Europe, projections speak of over $1.5bn to be distributed to the clubs in the first six years. A theoretical division among the 12 permanent franchises would equate to approximately $20.8m per team per year, but this is not fixed yet. Tennis represents another potential area for development. The ATP 500 in Halle generates around €5m in revenue, excluding television rights, while the ATP Finals in Turin generate around €60m. These are events with different dimensions and scopes, but they provide a scale.

The creation of MSE has a stated objective: to strengthen Milan’s financial capacity and reduce the gap with the richest clubs on the international scene, particularly those in the Premier League.

The gap is also evident when comparing revenues. AC Milan closed the 2025-26 season with €464.6m, a significant result considering their absence from European competitions , but still far from the top English clubs.

According to the Deloitte Football Money League, in the 2024-25 season, Liverpool generated €836.1m, Arsenal €821.7m and Manchester United €793.1m. Tottenham and Chelsea also exceeded €670m and €580m, respectively.
