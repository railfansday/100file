The Nine Movies Written Or Produced (But Not Directed) By Quentin Tarantino, Ranked

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Quentin Tarantino is widely known for his directorial efforts that led to such cinema classics as Pulp Fiction, Kill Bill, and Once Upon a Time in Hollywood, but the acclaimed filmmaker has a number of writing and producing credits under his belt that broaden the scope of the projects he has been involved in.

Tarantino’s latest non-directorial filmmaking project comes in the form of The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth, a sequel to Once Upon a Time in Hollywood that focuses on Brad Pitt’s cool, calm, and collected former stuntman. While Tarantino did not direct the Netflix movie (he handed those duties over to Seven and Zodiac director David Fincher), he wrote the screenplay for the movie and, in turn, imbues it with many of his trademark qualities as a filmmaker.

However, it should be noted The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth does not mark the first time Tarantino has written or produced a project without also sitting in the director's chair. From martial arts epics to vampire Westerns to grindhouse gore fests, Tarantino has writing or producing credits on nine movies helmed by other directors.

Quentin Tarantino served as an executive producer on Hostel: Part II and was billed as "presenting" the film alongside writer-director Eli Rot. His involvement centered on backing and promoting Roth's project rather than writing or directing. As an executive producer and "Presenter," Tarantino lent his name to the marketing campaign to help draw genre fans and boost visibility. As a longtime friend, mentor, and collaborator of Roth's, Tarantino helped champion the Hostel franchise and draw on grindhouse/70s exploitation influences, but Roth wrote and directed the sequel himself.

As far as sequels to horror movies go, Hostel: Part II is far from the worst the genre has had to offer. Nonetheless, it still doesn't stack up very well to the rest of the Tarantino movies he didn't direct, largely due to the quality of the movies that follow it.

Marring Hostel: Part II from start to finish is a story that bears far too much of a resemblance to its predecessor, to such an extent that the film feels more like a remake than a sequel. The more subversive, tongue-in-cheek tonal shift gives the film somewhat of a unique identity, as does the expanded look at the Elite Hunting Club, but otherwise this is an inferior sequel in just about every department.

Quentin Tarantino served as a producer, on-set creative collaborator, and actor in Planet Terror, which was paired with his own movie, Death Proof, as part of the Grindhouse double feature. While Rodriguez wrote, directed, shot, and scored Planet Terror, actress Marley Shelton noted that Tarantino was on set frequently, offered performance notes, and occasionally adjusted lines, while still deferring to Rodriguez on his segment of the film.

Originally released in the U.S. as half of the Grindhouse double feature alongside Quentin Tarantino’s Death Proof, Planet Terror fully and delightfully embraces the aesthetics of 1970s exploitation cinema, complete with intentional film scratches, missing reels, and over-the-top practical gore.

Although heavily dependent on viewers’ proclivity for campy, gross-out B-movies due to how intentionally underdeveloped the characters and story are, Planet Terror moves at the speed of a bullet and delights with its self-aware humor, practical make-up effects, and iconic turn from Rose McGowen as Cherry Darling, a woman who has a machine gun as a leg.

Quentin Tarantino wrote the original screenplay for Natural Born Killers, although he later disowned the finished product directed by Oliver Stone and received only a "story by" credit. Tarantino wrote the initial script about serial killers Mickey and Mallory Knox and hoped to direct it himself before selling an option for $10,000 to producers Jane Hamsher and Don Murphy. His draft took a more non-linear approach centered around the fictional tabloid TV show American Maniacs.

After the script went to Warner Bros. and Oliver Stone took over as director, however, Stone, David Veloz, and Richard Rutowski heavily rewrote it, shifting the tone and structure into a chronological, visually stylized road-movie satire. Because of the extensive changes to his script, Tarantino opted for a "story by" credit rather than a full screenplay credit. He publicly criticized it, said he never watched it all the way through, and later published his original screenplay to highlight its differences from the final product.

Over time, Natural Born Killers has developed a dedicated cult following, which starkly contrasts its initial reception. Upon release, Stone’s film was derided for its graphic brutality and scathing indictment on sensationalist media, going so far as to claim the press turns murderers into celebrities.

Many retrospective viewers, however, celebrate its hyper-edited visuals, shifting film formats, and focus on celebrity crime coverage as particularly prescient in an era of the 24-hour news cycle, social media algorithms, and true crime obsession. Admittedly, the satire is heavy-handed and is rather reductionistic in overlooking deeper, more systemic causes of violence, but it inarguably offers something challenging, stirring, and thought-provoking for audiences to sit with.

Following the massive success of Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon, Tarantino used his clout in the industry to bring legendary kung fu movie director Yuen Woo-ping and Tsui Hark’s 1993 classic to American theaters. Tarantino served as a producer and presenter for Miramax's 2001 U.S. theatrical and home video release of Yuen Woo-ping's 1993 Hong Kong film Iron Monkey. Tarantino lobbied for and lent his name to the American rollout, introducing trailers and giving interviews to promote the movie and Yuen Woo-ping to U.S. audiences.

The martial arts film bolsters some remarkable choreography and serves as a gateway to classic Hong Kong cinema to many, although the extensive alterations made by Miramax and endorsed by Tarantino to suit Western audiences’ tastes mean the re-release departs from director’s Yuen Woo-ping original vision.

Quentin Tarantino served as an executive producer and "presenter" of Eli Roth's 2005 horror movie Hostel, helping to shape the story and market the movie. More specifically, Tarantino lent his name and status to help promote and position the film for a wider release. Roth brainstormed the idea for the film while swimming at Tarantino's pool. Tarantino liked the pitch and urged Roth to start writing the draft right away. Tarantino reportedly mentored Roth during the writing process, pushing for practical details and realism, such as how an untrained character would handle a gun or how someone might escape restraints.

The original Hostel is very much steeped in the era in which it was released. Fresh off the release of Saw, Eli Roth and Quentin Tarantino took advantage of the shift away from bland remakes and slashers to horror that terrified through its depiction of gruesome bodily harm. Hostel represents perhaps the most salient example of this mid-2000s shift into what many detractors have labeled “torture porn,” although such a label overlooks the craft underlying the 2005 film.

A film with far more thought behind it than it is often given credit for, Hostel exercises an unexpected degree of restraint to its violence, which allows its poignant commentary to simmer and strike more effectively.

Quentin Tarantino did not direct, write, or edit Zhang Yimou's 2002 martial arts epic Hero, but he championed its delayed 2004 U.S. theatrical release through Miramax and lent his name to its marketing as "Quentin Tarantino Presents".

After Hero opened in Asia in 2002, Miramax delayed its U.S. rollout, although Tarantino lobbied the studio to bring the movie to American theaters. According to Tarantino, Miramax had lost faith in the project and wanted to trim it down, yet he pushed back to keep the movie's runtime intact. Miramax agreed to release an English-subtitled version with Tarantino's promotional backing, attaching the "Quentin Tarantino Presents" label to help attract martial arts and arthouse audiences.

A beautiful film punctuated by Christopher Doyle’s painterly cinematography and distinct color-coded chapters, Hero further compels with its Rashomon-style unreliable-narrator structure and its deft blending of philosophical storytelling with wire-fu choreography.

Quentin Tarantino wrote the screenplay, served as an executive producer, and wrote a companion novelization for The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth, while stepping aside from directing in favor of David Fincher. Tarantino wrote the script as a 1977 sequel/spinoff to Once Upon a Time in Hollywood, shifting Cliff Booth (Brad Pitt) into a studio fixer and part-time projectionist/bartender.

The film leans heavily toward Tarantino's sensibilities, with his distinctive brand of irreverent humor, lengthy conversations, pulpy storytelling, and unapologetic excess dominating the experience. Fincher's trademark precision and carefully constructed visual language are certainly present, yet never quite receive the prominence one might expect from a collaboration between these two celebrated directors.

Fortunately, there's plenty to enjoy despite its shortcomings. It delivers some outstanding sequences, with its terrific opening, sustained tension, and spectacular closing stretch providing more than enough thrills to warrant buying a ticket. Brad Pitt effortlessly slips back into the role of Cliff Booth, bringing an appealing combination of effortless charisma, world-weary toughness, and unexpected depth to the character. Elizabeth Debicki is equally impressive as she produces a magnetic, captivating performance that frequently threatens to steal every scene she's in.

Tarantino’s sharp, pop-culture-heavy dialogue is fused with Tony Scott’s kinetic, singular directing style in the crime classic True Romance.

Quentin Tarantino wrote the screenplay for the 1993 crime romance film, which director Tony Scott brought to the screen. Tarantino wrote the script while working at the Video Archives store, drawing on comic-book romance tropes and his own sensibilities. Tarantino originally hoped to direct it but sold the script and used the money to help fund his directorial debut, Reservoir Dogs. Unlike Natural Born Killers, Tarantino has spoken positively about the finished movie and its cast.

Despite underperforming at the box office upon its initial release, the movie has received the plaudits it deserves over the years. Amidst the gritty crime violence of its story, the film depicts a genuinely sweet central love story, with Christian Slater and Patricia Arquette having earnest and infectious chemistry with one another.

The best Tarantino movie not directed by Tarantino himself has to be From Dusk Till Dawn, the Western vampire flick starring George Clooney and Tarantino himself.

Quentin Tarantino wrote the screenplay for and co-starred as criminal Richard "Richie" Gecko in From Dusk Till Dawn. He also served as an executive producer and helped bring director Robert Rodriguez onto the project, though he did not direct it himself. Special effects artist Robert Kurtzman created the original vampire-saloon concept. Around 1990, Kurtzman's studio (KNB EFX Group) paid Tarantino $1,500 to write the script, after which Tarantino shaped the script into a sharp pivot from a gritty crime thriller into a supernatural horror siege. After the success of Pulp Fiction, Tarantino pitched From Dusk Till Dawn to Miramax with Robert Rodriguez attached as director.

The first half plays like a tense crime thriller, one elevated by Tarantino’s signature penchant for cultivating tension through dialogue alone. But an abrupt second half genre shift moves the movie into vampire action-horror territory, which allows director Robert Rodriguez to lean into unapologetic gore, camp, and 1970s exploitation thrills. It’s no wonder that From Dusk Till Dawn spawned an entire franchise as the initial film is so rich in tension, subversion of expectations, and tantalizingly unexplored mythology.

With the release of The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth, Quentin Tarantino once again demonstrates that he is far more than just an accomplished director. His numerous writing and producing credits offer an insight into how Tarantino’s flair, influences, interests, and punchy dialogue have played critical roles in a slew of great movies over the past 33 years.

President Trump Says He Convinced Nvidia’s Board to Bring Back Jensen Huang

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Nvidia's board brought Jensen Huang back after "input from senior U.S. government officials."

By Johnny Rice – Nov 23, 2026 at 5:20PM EST

President Donald Trump told CNBC on Monday that he personally lobbied Nvidia's (NASDAQ: NVDA) board to bring back Jensen Huang as CEO, telling the network's Eamon Javers this morning that he had spoken with the board "directly and indirectly" in recent days and that he "convinced them" to reinstate Huang, who "did nothing wrong."

The crux of the issue seemed to be the national security concerns surrounding Huang's original departure: "They did ask for assurance on the national security threat," The President said, "And we gave them assurances at least with everything we know, and we know a lot."

The board's official announcement this morning echoed Trump's statements: "In the process of reaching its decision, the Board received input from senior levels of the United States government, and based on that input, the Board is confident that Mr. Huang's reinstatement poses no national security risk."

While the announcement led to a massive rally today -- Nvidia stock finished up 31% -- the President's involvement could have unintended consequences for shareholders.

The national security concern stems from Huang's reported affair with Alice Huang, the former CEO of Megaspeed, a Singapore-based Nvidia customer accused of contravening U.S. export controls. Ms. Huang is herself a suspected Chinese intelligence asset, though Beijing denies the espionage allegation. The Justice Department is investigating Megaspeed, so too is Nvidia, which opened its own investigation on Nov. 8. The board said it has no reason to believe anyone had compromised company secrets or U.S. national security.

According to Trump, Attorney General Todd Blanche told him the government "does not possess evidence that [Huang] has engaged in any unlawful conduct" and that there's no reason to believe Huang poses a threat to national security.

Trump told CNBC that not only is he not a threat, but that through his work, he is an invaluable asset: "People say, is it a threat to have him? No, it's a threat to lose him."

Trump cheered his reinstatement, saying: "I'm very, very happy that the company had the courage and the board had the courage to do the right thing, and they suffered for two weeks, and the company suffered greatly."

Given the sensitive nature of all of this, the dramatic reversal of the board's decision would likely have raised eyebrows in Washington under any circumstance. But now that the President himself has claimed that his involvement was instrumental in changing the board's minds, I would expect serious scrutiny from Congress.

Before you buy a print book new, look for a used copy, which often costs a few dollars. Online, compare sellers by what you’d pay with shipping, not the listed price. Then check library book sales near you, where many books cost even less.

A hardcover that costs $30 new can cost $2 at a library sale or $6 with shipping from an online used-book seller. Books hold up well, so a used copy in good condition reads just like a new one.

Online, the catch is shipping. On a marketplace like AbeBooks, each seller sets its own shipping charge, so a $1 copy with $4 shipping costs more than a $4 copy that ships free. BookFinder.com compares offers from more than 100,000 booksellers, and its searches take shipping into account. Search the title there before you buy anywhere.

ThriftBooks, which sells millions of used books, charges nothing for standard shipping on a US order over $15, so buying several books at once can cost less than ordering them one at a time.

Locally, browse used bookstores and thrift stores, and watch for library book sales. The library’s Friends group, which raises money for the library, often runs the sale, and hardcovers commonly cost $1 to $3. Many end with a bag sale, when a full bag often costs $5 or $10. Find one on your library’s website or on Book Sale Finder, which lists library and other nonprofit book sales by state. Some libraries also keep a sale shelf or a Friends bookstore open between sales.

Before you pay, check the condition grade. ThriftBooks’ grades run from Like New down to Acceptable, and AbeBooks’ guide describes a Good copy as one with average wear and every text page intact. That’s usually good enough for a book you’ll read rather than display. Ex-library copies, former library books that often carry stamps and stickers, usually cost less. To get a particular edition, search by its ISBN, the number assigned to each edition and format of a book. A hardcover and a paperback of the same title each have their own.

Trade in the books you won’t reread at a used bookstore that buys them, and ask for store credit, which is often worth at least 20% more than the cash offer.

Justin Jefferson’s Latest Tunnel-Walk Fit Is an Homage to Mexican Fashion

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Ever since the NFL season kicked off back in September, the Minnesota Vikings’ star receiver Justin Jefferson has been going full-tilt. Last night, his team squared off against the San Fransisco 49ers in Mexico City (the NFL has been scheduling more away-gamers outside the U.S. to try to expand their viewership) in one of the marquee matchups of the season, and while the 49ers ended up winning that one, Jefferson remains inspired by the Vikings’ 7-3 record so far. “I’ve been great,” Jefferson says. “Everything’s been going really well, and I’m feeling good.”

Preparing for such a big game is no small feat, of course—even if a pro like Jefferson makes it look easy. Ahead of hitting the field, you can often find him getting ready both physically and mentally. “Preparation is everything: I want to make sure I'm confident, focused, and ready to perform at my best,” he says. How did he prepare for Mexico City? “A big part has been making sure I'm physically and mentally ready for the challenges of playing at a higher altitude,” Jefferson says. (Mexico City is about 1.4 miles above sea level—much more intense than even Denver’s Mile High Stadium.) “I've been using the altitude chamber to help my body acclimate. Mentally, it’s about studying the plays, understanding the different schemes, and making sure we're approaching the game with the right mindset.”

A more unexpected way Jefferson gets game-day ready, however: With his intentional tunnel-walk outfits. Yesterday, before the athletes even hit the field, Jefferson wanted to rock a meaningful look that would put him in the right mindset for his Mexico City matchup—so he enlisted one of the city’s most well-known designers, Patricio Campillo, to craft a custom look for him. “I've always tried to make my tunnel looks reflect where I am in the world, especially when we're playing internationally,” says Jefferson. “For me, fashion is an opportunity to connect with different cultures and showcase designers who bring something unique to the table.”

A very cool moment for me: Meet the designer—Patricio Campillo—behind the look.

Wanting to rep a design that represented the local culture, Jefferson called up Campillo to start ideating his custom look. He was drawn to the designer for his unique craftsmanship, creativity, and attention to detail. “Choosing Campillo felt like a natural decision—I was genuinely impressed,” says Jefferson. “You can tell how much time, passion, and hard work goes into creating these looks. Being able to represent that craftsmanship on an international stage is something I think is really special.”

Together, they landed on doing a cool, modern take on tailoring. Campillo created a cropped, boxy-style jacket with brown leather trousers—a signature element of the label that the designer wanted to introduce to a whole new audience. “It’s exciting to see someone of Justin’s caliber embrace Campillo,” he says. “I’ve always believed that fashion has the power to bring together people from different backgrounds, so seeing an athlete like Justin connect with our work is especially meaningful.”

He accessorized with pieces such as a horsehair bag, Oakley shades, and his signature chains. When the wide receiver put on the final look for the first ime, he was immediately drawn to the finer details. “One of my favorite details was the horsehair incorporated into the bag,” says Jefferson. “It’s such a unique touch and gives the piece a lot of character.” He also appreciated the hand-craftsmanship. “Knowing how much time and effort goes into creating each piece makes you appreciate the look even more,” he says. “I love wearing things that have a story behind them, and this outfit definitely does.”

The fit, custom made by Patricio Campillo.

It’s not the first time that Jefferson has flexed a fashion statement for his tunnel walk. During past games, he has worn everything from Off-White to Louis Vuitton and Chrome Hearts. What he wears and experiments, of course, depends on the setting. “My tunnel looks are really an extension of my personality, and how I’m feeling that day,” he says. “There are a few things I consider when putting a look together: my mood, who we’re playing, and the brands or designers I want to showcase. Fashion has become another creative outlet for me, and I enjoy stepping outside of what people might expect.”

This fluid approach to dressing applies to his off-duty wardrobe, too. Jefferson says he enjoys playing around with fashion even on his days off. “Right now, I'd describe my personal style as evolving,” he says. “I'm really focused on experimenting with different looks and exploring styles I might not have considered earlier in my career. I like stepping out of my comfort zone and being open to trying new things. Fashion is all about self-expression, and I think the more willing you are to take risks, the more you learn about yourself and what you like.”

What does this mean for the rest of Jefferson’s NFL season? You can expect tons more tunnel-walk outfits, for starters. “Every week is another opportunity to bring something exciting to the tunnel,” he says. “We love finding ways to surprise people, turn heads, and keep things interesting.” Start taking your bets—fashion bets, not sporting bets—on what runway outfit or custom creation he will don next. “We’ve already had a few outfits this season that have gotten a lot of attention, and that’s always fun to see,” he says. “But for me, it’s not just about getting people talking—it’s about continuing to discover new brands, experimenting with different styles, and finding creative ways to express myself through fashion.”

In 2019, Boeing 737 MAX 8 operators were losing approximately $150,000 per aircraft per day in revenue. Today, each 737 MAX 8 generates around $200,000 per day, although airlines don't publish specifics, and revenue varies widely depending on how a carrier uses the aircraft.

The revenue made by each 737 MAX 8 per day is not pure profit. It is offset by depreciation or leasing costs, as well as operational and maintenance expenses. There is also a set-up cost when an airline first brings the jet into its fleet, requiring crew training and maintenance infrastructure, varying in severity depending on the degree of pre-existing fleet commonality with the Boeing 737 family.

Airlines are reluctant to publish exact data on how much money they earn for each aircraft in their fleet. However, answering this question is uniquely possible for the 737 MAX 8 because estimates emerged during the 737 MAX crisis. Each analyst had their own estimates at this time. IBA estimated total lost earnings of $150,000 per day. The Conversation reached a similar conclusion, based on three round trips a day and an average of 145 tickets sold per flight. Southwest Airlines had the largest MAX fleet at the time with 34 aircraft, generating approximately $5 million in lost revenue per day. In his first year as St. Louis Cardinals scouting director, Randy Flores was watching a pitcher who threw a four-seam fastball and a curveball. The mix can be the backbone of a north-south pitching profile. But to Flores, a former major-league reliever, it looked suboptimal.

"I'm like, 'No, no, no - you sink it and slide it,'" said Flores, reciting an approach he gleaned from former Cardinals pitching coach Dave Duncan.

As a slight left-hander who did not throw especially hard, Flores carved out a playing career that lasted parts of eight major-league seasons. So, his methods had merit. But as an executive now, he recalls the scouting anecdote as something of a cautionary tale.

"Being a former player, if you're anchored too much to, 'This is how it was done when I was playing,' it's the wrong thing," Flores said.

Exploring other perspectives and means and finding a balance has been part of Flores' evolution as an executive, one that led to his hiring this month as general manager of the Houston Astros. It is also an arc to which only a handful of the majors' current highest-ranking baseball executives can relate.

Of the current presidents of baseball operations, Jerry Dipoto (Mariners), Chris Young (Rangers), Craig Breslow (Red Sox) and Buster Posey (Giants) played in the majors. So did general managers Chris Getz (White Sox) and Flores, whom the Astros hired after 11-plus seasons in St. Louis' front office.

That many top executives did not play in the majors, or at all professionally, illustrates how it is far from required. It might even invite the question of how such experience informs former players' approach to the role, if at all, as Flores settles into running a baseball operations department for the first time.

"You probably have more empathy … for what a player goes through, from the moment they sign their first professional contract to when they get to the big leagues," Dipoto said at last week's GM meetings in Las Vegas.

Dipoto and Breslow were relievers in the majors, like Flores. Dipoto posited that background could help "to understand the life of the guy who (is) not always the most thought-of player on the roster."

"Outside of that, I think it's just business as usual," Dipoto said. "We each went through our own, I guess, apprenticeship (after playing).

"You take the lessons you learn and apply them, just like any other general manager does when they get their first job. We just maybe have that slight - I don't even want to call it an advantage - that slight difference in that we have lived life in these players' shoes and probably as a result have a little bit more empathy rather than just frustration. On the other hand, if I want to have this $131k invested in fixed income assets, then go to step 2 below.

You've now got 5 years of interest credit history, so you can do a geometric average of the five credited rates. There's a chance that the geometric average after 5 years will be somewhere in the range of 5-7% per year, which you may find attractive compared to alternatives. Disney Just Hit a 52-Week High. 3 Reasons It Still Has Room to Run.

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Record parks and experiences demand contradicts fears of a consumer spending slowdown.

By Daniel Foelber – Nov 22, 2026 at 7:45AM EST

10 stocks we like better than Walt Disney ›

Walt Disney (DIS -1.89%) closed at a 52-week high on Nov. 18 -- the day after it reported its fourth-quarter and full-year fiscal 2026 results. Disney is now up 7.6% year to date and just a hair below its highest level in over four years.

Here's why Disney is breaking out despite a sell-off in the major stock market indexes, and whether the consumer discretionary stock has room to run from here.

Fiscal 2026 was arguably Disney's best year since before the COVID-19 pandemic. Overall revenue grew 8% year over year, and margins improved, driven by a 12% increase in operating income.

A major reason for Disney's increase in profitability is that Disney+ is now a key contributor to entertainment earnings, whereas just a few years ago it was losing money. In fiscal 2026, the entertainment subscription video-on-demand (SVOD) segment, which mainly comprises Disney+ and Hulu, generated $2.57 billion in operating income, up from $1.3 billion in fiscal 2025. That's a significant 47.2% of total entertainment operating income for the fiscal year -- proving that streaming is helping to offset the declines in linear cable networks.

Disney's sports segment grew revenue 5% year over year and operating income by 7%, which may seem weak at first glance. But sports holding its own is impressive, considering margins remain under pressure due to media rights costs, the challenges of integrating ESPN with Hulu and Disney+, and the risk of sports streaming cannibalizing traditional cable customers.

Experiences had another excellent year, including notching over $10 billion in revenue in a single quarter (the fourth quarter of fiscal 2026). Overall, the experiences segment generated a whopping 28.3% operating margin for fiscal 2026 and contributed 56.7% of Disney's total operating income.

For fiscal 2027, Disney is forecasting another year of double-digit adjusted earnings-per-share growth, including 8% adjusted operating income growth.

Disney is benefiting from an in-person experiences boom. In an era of rising screen time, folks are yearning for in-person connections with their families and others. And Disney's experiences drill into the core of that desire.

While the parks continue to be Disney's ultimate cash cow, its latest results were a reminder of just how important Disney's other experiences have become -- namely its cruise business.

The cruise industry is on fire. On its third-quarter fiscal 2026 earnings call, Carnival said it is already half-booked for full-year 2027, even with occupancy and pricing at record levels. Royal Caribbean Cruises (RCL -2.20%) is coming off an exceptional summer sailing season.

Disney's multiyear ramp-up in its cruise fleet is paying off big-time. It launched two ships in the late 1990s and two more in the early 2010s before a decade-long pause. But since 2022, it has launched four ships -- doubling its fleet. The company plans to launch five more ships over the next five years -- bringing its total fleet to 13 ships. And on the Nov. 17 earnings call, CEO Josh D'Amaro said that Disney would add even more ships if it could get ones that met the company’s standards, given cruise ships are much more flexible than building land-based resorts.

Disney has shifted more from a motion picture company to an experiences powerhouse -- realizing that experiences present a more attractive risk/reward profile than trying to smash a home run with a box-office hit (although it still relies on new content to drive fresh storytelling). Management is guiding for $9 billion in fiscal 2027 capital expenditures (capex) -- which is significant relative to its operating expenses. A lot of that is going to experiences, including building out the cruise fleet and some major park renovations.

Avengers Campus at Disney California Adventure Park is set to double in size in 2028. Tropical Americas is expected to come to Animal Kingdom as soon as next year, alongside a Monsters Inc.-themed land in Hollywood Studios. Construction is underway at a new Cars-themed land and Disney Villains Land in Magic Kingdom. There's also a Spider-Man-themed land in the works at Shanghai Disneyland. And the grand prize is a new Disneyland in Abu Dhabi, which is expected to open between 2030 and 2033.

In sum, Disney's latest fiscal year and guidance validate that it is putting capex to good use on projects that will be embraced by guests and benefit its bottom line.

Since Josh D'Amaro took over as CEO earlier this year, a major theme has been returning capital to shareholders.

In Disney's third-quarter fiscal 2026 earnings report in August, the company said it believes its shares are undervalued, which is why it accelerated stock buybacks. The move has proven correct so far, given Disney's recovering stock price. And despite Disney's massive fiscal 2027 capex forecast, it still expects to generate $11 billion in free cash flow and target $9 billion in stock buybacks.

The same day as its earnings release on Nov. 17, Disney announced a 33% dividend increase -- boosting the semiannual dividend from $0.75 per share to $1 per share for an annualized yield of 1.7%. It's worth noting that buybacks are still a much larger share of Disney's capital return program -- which leaves plenty of room for future dividend hikes.

Disney has a reasonable valuation -- even after its recent run-up. The stock trades at just 17.4 times adjusted fiscal 2026 earnings -- which is more than reasonable for an industry-leading entertainment giant growing earnings by double digits.

Disney has an unmatched intellectual property portfolio, vastly improved streaming profitability, ongoing cash flows from legacy linear networks, a resilient sports segment, and a high-margin experiences segment.

Add it all up, and Disney remains a balanced buy for value investors who believe consumers will continue to pay a premium for high-quality in-person experiences. The larger dividend provides an added incentive to hold the entertainment stock over the long term.

History Shows: Wayne Gretzky and Warren Buffett Can Teach Us About Investing

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Wayne Gretzky and Warren Buffett might be an unlikely team, but they shared a winning attitude that can help you make money in the stock market.

Wayne Gretzky is one of the most iconic figures in hockey. During his 20-year career in the NHL, Gretzky played in 1,487 games, scored 894 goals, and delivered 1,963 assists for a total of 2,857 points -- about 1.92 points per game.

Gretzky was so good at putting points on the board because of his uncanny ability to be in the right place at the right time. He seemed to anticipate where the puck was going to bounce next and put himself in position to capitalize on opportunities.

What can Gretzky's hockey career teach us about investing? More than you might think. Legendary investor Warren Buffett found inspiration from Wayne Gretzky. In a 2008 op-ed for The New York Times, Buffett quoted Gretzky, who famously said, "I skate to where the puck is going to be, not to where it has been."

There's a great lesson here about investing. Let's look at what Buffett meant by investing based on Wayne Gretzky's hockey skills -- and why stock market history shows that this "Gretzky-style" investing approach is a winning strategy.

Warren Buffett. Image source: The Motley Fool.

It's understandable to get caught up in negative daily news headlines. On any given day, it might seem like the stock market is going to crash, that the world is ending. But remember: Stock markets are about the future, not today.

Just as Wayne Gretzky was great at anticipating the next move of the puck on the ice, investors try to anticipate the future. They allocate capital to stocks or sectors based on expected future earnings growth.

Stock investors are constantly evaluating what's likely to happen next -- which companies are poised to keep growing, which industries are about to benefit from positive economic changes, and which countries around the world are due for a stock market surge. They want to put their money into the places where "the puck is going to be" -- not where the action is happening right now.

What does this mean for you? Take a long-term approach to investing. If you're putting money into the stock market today, hopefully you're ready and able to leave that money alone for the next five years, 10 years, 20 years, or more. You're sending that money on a long journey into the future. Even if the stock market crashes tomorrow, you're making a long-term bet that your investment will grow in the long run.

Most of the time, that's a good bet. The S&P 500 (^GSPC -3.70%) has delivered 9.98% annualized returns over the past 98 years, even with terrible crises and crashes along the way such as the Great Depression, World War II, and the 2020 pandemic.

No matter how risky and dangerous the world seems on any given day, the future of the stock market might be brighter than you imagine. It pays to be optimistic about stocks.

Gretzky's famous quote is also a reminder to avoid trying to "time the market." In his Times op-ed in 2008, Warren Buffett wrote:

Just like a hockey puck bouncing around an NHL ice rink, markets move fast. The S&P 500 can crash, but it can also bounce back even faster. If you're waiting on the sidelines, trying to time the market and choose the "right time" to invest, share prices might shoot back up faster than you can buy in.

If you miss out on the "best days" of the S&P 500, you might miss out on a big chunk of future wealth. Fidelity research found that, based on historical S&P 500 returns from the end of 1987 to the end of 2025, an investor who missed the five best days of returns over those 38 years would have seen 38% lower overall returns.

Instead of trying to choose exactly the right day to buy stocks, it's better to put your money to work as soon as you can. "Time in the market" beats "timing the market."

How should you invest based on this lesson from Buffett and Gretzky? Buying a low-cost S&P 500 index fund is often a good long-term bet.

The Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO -3.42%) has delivered annualized gains of about 14.8% in the past 16 years since the fund's inception in September 2010. Even if the next few decades are not so fruitful and the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF only delivers the long-term stock market average return of 9.98%, that's still enough to get richer in the long run.

When you buy a low-cost index fund like VOO, you are not picking stocks or choosing individual sectors. You're making a long-term bet that the stock market itself will put your money to work in great companies that will profit from the economy of the future. In this way, investing in an S&P 500 ETF can put your money "where the puck is going to be."

We slept surprisingly well last night, even though our room sat directly above a lively bar. People partied and ate dinner late into the night. That is completely normal here, as most businesses close for siesta and do not reopen until after 8:00 PM.

With the window shutters closed, we blocked out all the street noise. Unfortunately, closing them also trapped the heat inside, making the room quite warm.

This morning, I walked out to the kitchen and opened the tall doors leading to the courtyard to let the cool air in. I made a cup of coffee and ate fresh blueberries with yogurt.

Because it was Sunday morning, the street noise was minimal. Listening to the seagulls and mourning doves while sipping coffee was a peaceful way to start the day.

We finally set off at 9:00 AM. Pontevedra is a good-sized city with an intact medieval center, so it took us a little while to walk out of town. Before long, however, we found ourselves back on a quiet country road.

The Camino wound alongside a river before merging onto a paved road. We took the road route, which was poorly marked, but we soon reconnected with the main trail. Along the way, we passed hundreds of pilgrims heading in the opposite direction toward Santiago.

From there, the trail entered the woods and began to climb. It got quite steep for a short stretch before leveling out into a pleasant forest path. We hiked downhill over rocky terrain, passing cool, shaded creeks along the way.

Eventually, we emerged into Pontesampaio, a well-kept medieval village. We wound our way through its narrow alleyways down to a wide river. To cross, we had to wait our turn on a narrow, 300-foot arched stone bridge, letting oncoming cars pass before we walked across.

Once across, we stopped at a local restaurant serving ultra-fresh food. I ordered grilled squid that had just come off the docks that morning, and the restaurant even harvests its own chickens. Best of all, the meal was a fraction of what it would cost back in the States.

After a relaxing siesta lunch, we still had about 7.5 kilometers (4.6 miles) left to hike.

Redondela felt a bit rundown in spots, though it features a charming medieval quarter beneath a massive, 100-foot-tall iron train trestle.

We arrived at our apartment at 4:00 PM, completely exhausted. Our host was wonderful and had prepared a special herbal tea for us to soak our feet. The recipe belonged to his grandmother, who was actually the very first pharmacist in Redondela. (Unlike in the States, pharmacists in Spain can prescribe and dispense many medications without a doctor’s visit.)

While soaking our feet, we drank some mediocre Spanish wine. Because it was Sunday afternoon, almost everything was closed except for a small mini-market with limited options.

Is Netflix a Buy in November? The Bull and Bear Cases.

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Netflix shares have fallen nearly 47% from their 52-week high. Is this a rare opportunity to buy a highly profitable entertainment business at a reasonable valuation?

By Lawrence Nga – Nov 22, 2026 at 7:15AM EST

Netflix (NFLX -2.96%) has had a difficult year. Its shares closed at $62.15 on Nov. 13, nearly 47% below their 52-week high. Yet the streaming giant continues to generate billions of dollars in revenue and profits. The sell-off raises an interesting question: Has Netflix become a bargain, or are investors right to worry about its future growth?

I cautiously lean toward the bull case. Netflix remains a highly profitable business with several ways to grow earnings over the next five years. However, its slowing growth and advertising challenges mean investors shouldn't expect an immediate recovery.

Netflix's ability to turn revenue into profits has improved over time.

In the third quarter of 2026, revenue increased 11.8% year over year to $12.87 billion. Operating income grew much faster, rising 37% to $4.46 billion. Its operating margin also improved to 34.6%, compared with 28.2% a year earlier.

In simple terms, Netflix is keeping more money from every dollar of revenue.

Management expects full-year revenue of $51.1 billion and operating income growth of more than 20% in 2026. It also expects its operating margin to reach 31.5%, up from 29.5% in 2025.

While operating leverage contributed to the expansion of operating margin, Netflix has also demonstrated pricing power. For instance, management said customers responded to its 2026 subscription price increases as expected, while customer cancellations improved year over year across every region.

Advertising offers another opportunity. Netflix expects advertising revenue to reach approximately $2.7 billion in 2026, despite delays in launching some advertising products. Management expects growth of more than 80% this year.

If Netflix can improve its advertising technology and earn more from its existing audience, advertising could become another meaningful contributor to future profits.

The biggest concern is that Netflix's growth is becoming harder to sustain. For perspective, revenue growth declined from 17.6% in the fourth quarter of 2025 to 11.8% in the third quarter of 2026. Management expects 11.5% growth in the fourth quarter.

That's still healthy, but investors have become accustomed to high growth. As Netflix grows, attracting new customers and raising subscription prices become more challenging.

Competition is another concern. Alphabet’s YouTube and TikTok compete for people's attention, while Amazon and other streaming services continue investing in entertainment. Netflix must keep producing popular shows and films to remain relevant.

Management has acknowledged that its execution could improve, particularly in the United States. Recent changes to its content leadership suggest it wants to strengthen its entertainment offering.

Advertising is also taking longer to develop than expected. Netflix reduced its 2026 advertising revenue forecast from $3 billion to $2.7 billion because some advertising products were delayed in launching.

These problems don't threaten Netflix's business today. But they could limit how quickly it grows over the next several years.

While nobody likes a declining share price, Netflix's valuation has become much more reasonable as a result.

At $62.15, its market capitalization is approximately $255 billion. Its price-to-earnings (P/E ratio) is around 17.7 times, compared with a much higher valuation in previous years. For instance, the stock traded at a P/E ratio above 30 just a year ago.

If Netflix can sustain or even improve its growth rate from here on, the stock's valuation looks rather attractive at the current level. Of course, if growth continues to slow further, the opposite is true as well.

On balance, investors may consider buying Netflix at today's prices, particularly those with a five-year investment horizon. The company has improving profit margins, a global audience, and opportunities to grow its advertising business.

But I'd build a position gradually rather than invest everything at once. The next few quarters should reveal whether Netflix can maintain its profitability while stabilizing revenue growth and improving advertising execution.

Netflix doesn't need to return to its previous growth rates to reward shareholders. It simply needs to keep growing profits at a healthy pace.

What's the coolest automotive museum you've ever seen? What about the most mind-blowing piece of automotive history you've ever beheld with your own eyes? I live right down the street from the National Automotive Museum in Reno, NV, which is a great time, but it doesn't quite take the cake. There are some real gems out there, like the Gilmore Car Museum, which lets you drive a Model T, or the Studebaker National Museum, where you can watch footage from 1932 of grown men throwing cars down a hill for safety testing. But if I had a time machine and could visit any car museum anywhere in time and space, there's one in particular that I'd just love to see.

That would be the Schlumpf collection in Mulhouse, France, specifically during the two-year period in the mid-to-late-70s when it was run by laid-off textile workers after their bosses shut their factories down and fled the country. Yes, the story is as wild as it sounds.

Now housed in the Musée National de l'Automobile in Mulhouse, the Schlumpf collection is an astounding piece of both car collecting and labor history. It began as the personal (and, sure, slightly obsessive) collection of Fritz Schlumpf, a long-time Bugatti driver who, along with his brother Hans, ran a textile business with factories in Mulhouse. Born around 1906, Fritz Schlumpf began collecting Bugattis intentionally in the 1960s. He hunted them down with the help of the British Bugatti Owners Club, who helped him locate around 50 individual cars, plus a stash of 30 vintage Bugattis owned by John W. Shakespeare of Hoffman, Illinois. Over two years, Schlumpf and Shakespeare negotiated a purchase price, bringing Schlumpf's personal Bugatti collection up to 80. Then he bought 18 more off Ettore Bugatti himself. Schlumpf was only getting started.

Eventually, Fritz Schlumpf owned an estimated 105 Bugattis, which might've comprised as much as 20% of the Bugattis in existence at the time. Who can blame Schlumpf for apparently wanting them all? After all, Bugattis do drive like a dream, even the old ones. But Schlumpf hadn't just been buying up cars of his favorite French brand. He'd actually been amassing a mind-bogglingly large stable of rare cars, both vintage and contemporary.

By the 1970s, Schlumpf had over 400 cars stashed in an unused factory in Mulhouse that was being fitted into a museum. But in order to acquire them all during a worsening economy, he'd spent money from a government grant intended to keep his factories in operation. When his transgression was discovered, he promptly shut down all his businesses and fled to Switzerland, putting about 1,800 workers out of business.

In response, the workers decided they didn't need a boss and could keep things running without Schlumpf's oversight, so they formed a workers' co-op to keep themselves employed. When members of the co-op traveled to the supposedly unused factory in Mulhouse, what they discovered was a building packed to the gills with exotic cars. A collection the likes of which had never been seen. The workers collectively decided to run the museum while using it to spread the message of their cooperative to the wider world.

The museum was open for two years, with public access to the vehicles for a few hours a day, and it contained some real stunners, including gems like two Bugatti Type 52s, which were electric race cars sized for children. You can still see the collection today, though not exactly as Schlumpf originally had it. The collection was seized by officials and eventually reopened in 1982.

GLP-1s Like Ozempic May Boost Brain Health, But Can They Lower Dementia Risk?

GLP-1 medications have transformed obesity and type 2 diabetes treatment. Now, researchers are investigating whether the effects of this blockbuster class of drugs extend beyond weight loss and affect brain health.

A comprehensive new review published on November 5 in the journal Science evaluated the current evidence on how GLP-1 medications like semaglutide and tirzepatide may affect the brain across aging, cardiometabolic disease, and Alzheimer’s disease.

The authors of the review suggested that GLP-1 medications like Ozempic and Zepbound may offer brain health benefits. However, they were also left with the question of whether the medications act directly on the brain or whether better brain health is actually a consequence of improved cardiometabolic health.

Sheena Aurora, MD, vice president, Medical Affairs, Alzheimer’s Association, who wasn’t involved in the review, told Healthline that more research is needed to better understand the effects of GLP-1 drugs on the brain.

“While research suggests GLP-1s could reduce brain inflammation, improve blood vessel health, and even protect neurons, much broader, randomized clinical trials on GLP-1s and brain health, including in people with Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, are needed,” she said.

GLP-1 medications like semaglutide, the active ingredient in Ozempic and Wegovy, mimic the GLP-1 hormone. Tirzepatide, the active ingredient in Mounjaro and Zepbound, also targets a second hormone pathway called GIP.

These medications have been shown to produce significant weight loss and improvements in cardiometabolic health.

This is important for brain health because obesity, type 2 diabetes, and vascular dysfunction are all linked with cognitive impairment and dementia. Insulin resistance and chronic inflammation may also link metabolic and brain health.

Analyses of large outcome trials and observational cohorts have suggested that GLP-1 medications may be associated with a reduced risk of cognitive impairment and dementia. However, randomized trials in diseases like Alzheimer’s disease have yet to demonstrate clinical benefits.

Around 200 preclinical and mechanistic studies have shown benefits for learning, memory, synaptic plasticity, and neuronal resilience, though results are heterogeneous in design and direction, so a clear consensus on magnitude and mechanism remains lacking.

It is also unclear how well GLP-1 medications reach the brain. They appear to reach certain areas, such as the hypothalamus, brainstem, and circumventricular organs, more readily than regions protected by the blood-brain barrier.

The report authors noted that some studies have shown that GLP-1 receptor agonists may reduce the risk of cognitive impairment and dementia in people with type 2 diabetes. This may be due to cognitive issues being linked to factors such as blood sugar, blood pressure, vascular health, and sleep.

Mir Ali, MD, medical director of MemorialCare Surgical Weight Loss Center at Orange Coast Medical Center in Fountain Valley, CA, who wasn’t involved in the report, told Healthline, “These findings are interesting, but like many of the other benefits associated with GLP-1 medications, a direct causal relationship has not yet been proven.”

“Rather, the benefits appear to be more closely tied to the reduction in obesity and better control of diabetes,” he said.

Aurora noted that growing evidence supports the heart-brain connection.

She cited new findings from the Alzheimer’s Association’s landmark U.S. POINTER study presented at the Clinical Trials in Alzheimer’s Disease (CTAD) conference November 16–19 in Boston, MA.

“U.S. POINTER can change those risk factors within 24 months, and better heart health risk-factor control was linked with better cognition,” Aurora said. “We’re also interested to see whether adding a drug like a GLP-1 to a U.S. POINTER-style lifestyle program improves cognition in at-risk older adults more than the program alone.”

To find out, the Alzheimer’s Association is funding the Prevention of Risk for Cognitive Decline through Combined Therapy (PROTECT-Cog) global phase 3 clinical trial.

PROTECT-Cog is a first-of-its-kind trial designed to evaluate whether combining a proven multidomain lifestyle intervention with a metabolism-targeting drug, like a GLP-1, can further reduce the risk of cognitive decline, mild cognitive impairment, and dementia in at-risk older adults.

Dung Trinh, MD, internist at MemorialCare Medical Group and Chief Medical Officer of Healthy Brain Clinic in Irvine, CA, who also wasn’t involved in the review, told Healthline, “I often remind my patients that what’s good for the heart is generally good for the brain.”

“The brain depends on a healthy cardiovascular system to deliver a constant supply of oxygen, glucose, and other nutrients. Conditions such as high blood pressure, diabetes, obesity, and high cholesterol can damage blood vessels, promote chronic inflammation, and increase the risk of stroke and vascular cognitive impairment,” he said.

“Over time, these changes can also contribute to the development or progression of dementia.”

Trinh added that while GLP-1 medications can improve several cardiometabolic risk factors, these improvements do not automatically translate into proven dementia prevention.

“Regular physical activity, a heart-healthy diet, adequate sleep, and management of cardiovascular risk factors remain essential strategies for maintaining brain health as we age,” he said.

When the report authors turned their attention to more stringent tests on semaglutide in people already living with early Alzheimer’s disease, the picture began to change.

Novo Nordisk’s EVOKE and EVOKE + Phase 3 Trials examined the efficacy of semaglutide in people with early stage Alzheimer’s disease.

The studies showed that while semaglutide produced changes in several disease-related biomarkers, it didn’t slow the progression of Alzheimer’s disease.

This emphasizes that fixing metabolic issues might prevent cognitive decline in someone with obesity or diabetes. But it might not be enough to reverse the damage in someone with established Alzheimer’s disease, where profound synaptic loss and protein pathology have already occurred.

The review authors suggest that future research on obesity and diabetes should also include rigorous and specific cognitive testing.

58 Years Later, Captain Kirk's Best Star Trek Quote Is the Greatest Line in Sci-Fi History

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

This Captain Kirk speech says more about what Star Trek stands for than any other line in the original series.

Some lines of dialogue become so famous they stop belonging to the movies and TV shows that produced them. “May the Force be with you.” “I’ll be back.” “You're gonna need a bigger boat.” You don't need to know the context. The words have long since transcended their original context and permeated the pop culture zeitgeist.

Star Trek: The Original Series has plenty of those. Spock’s wish that everyone to “Live long and prosper” is arguably the franchise's signature phrase. He also turned the simple word “fascinating” into a catchphrase simply by raising one eyebrow before saying it. Dr. McCoy (DeForest Kelley) spent three seasons reminding everyone, “I'm a doctor, not a…” and, at least in our collective memory declaring, “He's dead, Jim” while standing over the latest redshirt who wandered a little too far from the landing party.

But if I could only pick one line to represent everything Star Trek stands for, it would be four simple words spoken by Captain James T. Kirk (William Shatner) halfway through “Return to Tomorrow," the 20th episode of season 2:

On the surface, it doesn't seem like an obvious choice. It's not printed on T-shirts nearly as often as “live long and prosper.” It isn't endlessly quoted by people who have never watched an episode of Star Trek. And unlike “beam me up, Scotty,” it never became part of the broader pop culture vocabulary. But that's exactly why it's the best quote. It doesn't just capture Kirk. It captures the entire philosophy of Star Trek.

First broadcast on Feb. 9, 1968, the episode “Return to Tomorrow” finds the Enterprise answering a mysterious distress call from a dead planet. There, Kirk and his crew discover Sargon, one of three survivors of an ancient civilization that has existed as beings of pure energy for half a million years. Sargon asks Kirk, Spock (Leonard Nimoy), and Dr. Ann Mulhall (Diana Muldaur) to temporarily surrender their bodies so he, his wife Thalassa, and the ambitious Henoch can build android bodies and begin life again. Dr. McCoy believes the aliens are manipulating them. Kirk believes the chance to learn from a civilization hundreds of thousands of years more advanced than humanity is worth the danger.

The episode also marks the first of three Star Trek appearances by Diana Muldaur. She plays Dr. Ann Mulhall here before returning as Miranda Jones in the third season episode “Is There in Truth No Beauty?” Years later, she'd become a series regular on Star Trek: The Next Generation as Dr. Katherine Pulaski. “Return to Tomorrow” is also a fun piece of Trek trivia because it's one of the times Spock describes Sargon as “pure energy,” a phrase longtime fans immediately recognize.

Faced with allowing unimaginably powerful aliens to occupy the bodies of his crew, Kirk doesn't issue an order. He makes an argument and reminds his crew that every leap forward in human history required someone willing to embrace uncertainty.

"They used to say if man could fly, he'd have wings," Kirk says. "But he did fly. He discovered he had to." He likens Sargon's offer to humanity's own journey from the Moon to Mars and eventually the stars, arguing that the potential for knowledge outweighs the danger. Only then does he arrive at the four words that define both the captain and the franchise. Those four words explain Kirk better than decades of pop-culture impressions ever could.

For years, pop culture has reduced Kirk to an oversimplified caricature: the swaggering captain who punches first, flirts with every alien woman he meets, and somehow loses his shirt during every other away mission. The real Kirk is far more thoughtful than that. He takes risks, certainly, but not because he enjoys gambling. He takes them because he believes some opportunities are too important to ignore.

There's a world of difference between being reckless and accepting risk in pursuit of discovery. Kirk isn't arguing that danger is exciting. He's arguing that progress is impossible without it.

More importantly, he's explaining what separates Starfleet from almost every other organization. Plenty of fictional explorers travel because they're ordered to. Others do it for conquest, profit, or survival. Starfleet goes because knowledge itself is worth pursuing.

That's why I think this quote is even more meaningful than the famous opening narration about boldly going where no one has gone before. The opening tells us what the Enterprise does. This one explains why.

The timing of the episode makes the speech even more remarkable. “Return to Tomorrow” aired in early 1968, roughly 17 months before Apollo 11 landed on the Moon. America was still trying to prove humans could successfully reach another world, and Star Trek was already imagining the next step beyond that. Kirk's speech places the Enterprise in the same lineage as every explorer who came before it, suggesting humanity's future won't be built by playing it safe.

That idea has become the beating heart of Star Trek. Optimism, in the Star Trek universe, has never meant believing everything will work out. It means believing the unknown is worth facing anyway.

There are more famous quotes in The Original Series. There are funnier ones. There are certainly more quotable ones. But no other line distills what Star Trek has always been trying to say better than four words Kirk spoke nearly 60 years ago: “Risk is our business.”

“Race realism” isn’t realistic—and it isn’t good science

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Modern genetics does not support claims of innate racial differences in intelligence.

At this year’s World Economic Forum meeting in Davos, US President Donald Trump stated that people from Somalia have low IQs. “I always say these are low-IQ people,” he said in his speech. His son, Donald Trump Jr, made a similar statement about Haitian people in 2024 (Trump’s government has since ended the protected status of both Somali and Haitian migrants in the US).

These statements and their consequences are symptoms of a growing trend in Western politics: the re-emergence of racial views of human nature that have been largely in hiding for the past 70 years. Narratives about the innate intellectual inferiority of black or brown people compared with white people—which can still be found in academic journals today—are fuelling far-right propaganda in some of the world’s largest democracies. Such claims underpin misinformation strategies on social media and bolster political movements.

These narratives are sometimes euphemistically referred to as “race science” or more recently “race realism.” But, as we argue, they are not actually scientific. One member of this community, Nathan Cofnas, recently attracted public attention by accusing the late Jason Arday of plagiarism.

What got less attention was that Cofnas has links to a network of people who make supposedly scientific claims about the innate intelligence, criminality, or sexuality of different racial groups. Some within this network are affiliated with universities, giving their work the mask of legitimacy.

This group produces work that has the appearance of science, but which is methodologically deeply flawed and fails to engage with rigorous scientific research. Yet they regularly succeed in publishing this work in (a handful of) mainstream academic journals.

How do they manage to do this, and what are the consequences?

Contemporary race scientists position themselves as persecuted but legitimate scientists who are prevented from pursuing their work for ideological reasons. They sometimes refer to what they defend as hereditarianism. This is the idea that genetic differences play a key causal role in determining differences in human behavior and intelligence between human groups—such as races—over purely environmental differences.

Yet scientific evidence has shown repeatedly and from many points of view that hereditarian thinking is both flawed and wrong. Race scientists make extremely simplistic claims such as: “Black people have innately lower intelligence than white people, because selection pressures resulted in the evolution of higher intelligence in white people.” No part of this claim can be scientifically supported; not from the point of view of genetics, evolutionary biology, or psychology.

In the 1970s, the influential evolutionary biologist Richard Lewontin famously showed that there is more genetic variation within human groups than between them. Socially defined racial groups just aren’t that genetically different from one another. There is genetic variation—though we’re actually a relatively genetically similar species compared to closely related primates. But this variation doesn’t fall neatly into clusters that correlate with how we typically categorize races.

Skin tone and color do vary between geographical locations. So do some other physiological features like hair texture, or the ability to process oxygen or milk products. But these differences are associated with characteristics of the environment rather than with racial groups. Some genetic variants associated with white skin color, for example, may have originated in Africa. Similarly, the ability to digest milk is seen in some populations in Europe, Africa, and Asia, rather than being an exclusively “Aryan” characteristic, as far right groups sometimes claim.

We now know considerably more about the human genome than Lewontin did in the 1970s, but his conclusions are supported today. Knowing someone’s race just doesn’t tell us a great deal about their genetics.

Recent scientific advances allowing us to examine ancient DNA from archaeological sites help explain why genetic differences do not fall neatly along racial lines. This research paints a picture of constant mixing between human groups, as waves of migration washed to and fro across the globe. It ultimately makes any belief in the genetic “purity” of European or African populations meaningless.

While human genetics is fascinatingly complex, race scientists ignore this complexity in favor of a far-too-simplistic view of humanity. And this view is not supported by the work of scientists who have used rigorous scientific methods to further our understanding of our species.

When race scientists say that black people have lower intelligence than white people, they typically base that on claims of differences in IQ test results. But the data underlying these claims is often shaky, to say the least.

Hereditarians have relied heavily on a database that claims to provide average IQs for countries worldwide: the “national IQ” database. Researchers have repeatedly demonstrated that this database is deeply methodologically flawed. It isn’t possible to measure intelligence in the same way in very different cultures, with different exposure to formal education. That’s because we know that education is one of several other factors linked to differences in IQ.

The methods used to put the database together did not come even close to meeting typical standards of scientific rigor. There’s even analysis suggesting that low IQ samples were deliberately chosen to represent sub-Saharan Africa, to make the IQ for black populations artificially low.

Meanwhile, the Flynn effect, showing that IQ has risen considerably during the 20th and 21st centuries, clearly demonstrates that IQ scores are heavily determined by the environment we’re in. Such a rapid rise over a short period of time must have environmental, not genetic, causes.

What’s more, intelligence is a contested topic—something that is hard to define and measure. IQ tests only measure specific cognitive abilities, ignoring things like social intelligence or creativity, for example. In fact, social intelligence features heavily in mainstream research on the evolution of human intelligence.

As one of us recently outlined in a peer-reviewed paper, race scientists are largely silent on the social brain hypothesis—the view that humans evolved remarkable intellectual abilities to manage social issues. In every group, there is strong social competition around resources, mating, and status. These are the things humans spend the most time navigating—the complexities of our rich social lives.

Not one of the prominent mainstream views on the evolution of human intelligence concludes there are innate cognitive differences between racial groups. Factors that have been put forward range from our cooperative child rearing to our heavy reliance on culture and learning. These all involve our social relationships, and all have received equal lack of attention from race scientists.

Mainstream opinion in the evolutionary behavioral sciences is that cognitively, all human groups are the same, though of course individuals within groups differ. Our amazing behavioral diversity stems from our species’ ability to adapt flexibly to our environment. This is especially because of our reliance on culture and social learning—not because we have evolved different psychologies to suit different environments.

Rather than drawing on this mainstream research, race scientists use simplistic “just-so” stories to explain how race differences in traits such as intelligence supposedly evolved. Their “cold winters theory,” for example, claims that the challenges of cold winters in Europe and east Asia created evolutionary selection pressures for greater intelligence after human groups migrated out of Africa. In doing so, they make unsupportable assumptions about environmental differences between Africa and the rest of the world. They also fail to acknowledge half a century of research on the evolution of human cognitive ability.

Yes, we needed to learn new food gathering practices and methods for dealing with environmental challenges as we migrated across the world. But the same was true as humans migrated across the large and ecologically diverse continent of Africa. Race scientists provide no evidence for their arguments. They don’t rely on archaeological evidence, nor do they use typical methods from evolutionary biology to understand evolutionary processes. Using the methods of race scientists, it would be equally easy to make the argument that challenges posed by hot summers drove the evolution of higher intelligence, and equally without scientific backing.

No matter which way you look at these issues—from the view of genetics, anthropology, evolutionary biology, or psychology—hereditarianism does not hold up.

To understand the potential consequences of these views, it is important to see them in historical context. Early in the 20th century, race science was mainstream. Overly simplistic and biologically deterministic views about the origins of differences between human groups—not just race but class—were widespread (if not universal) and important to the eugenics movement.

If disadvantaged groups owe their disadvantage to some fault within them, and not to the structural conditions they experience, then some argue society is not responsible for helping those people. Such arguments led to eugenic sterilization laws that aimed to “improve” society by preventing—without their consent—the reproduction of those considered likely to pass on their innate “defects” to future children. And it was ultimately behind the Nazis’ “solution” of trying to eliminate “undesirables” entirely.

In the decades after the Second World War, the horrors of the Nazi regime led to a decline in the popularity of eugenics in mainstream research and a rejection of race science. But the belief that science supported eugenic claims was already collapsing, even as the Nazis were trying to implement a race-based society. As we learned more about human biology and genetics during the 20th century, it became obvious that human genetic variation was too complex to be mapped into socially recognized racial (or class) categories. Advances in science meant race science dropped out of mainstream thinking.

Yet those committed to a belief in a biological hierarchy of races, regardless of what the science said, wanted venues in which they could continue their discussions. In the 1960s, the pseudo-scientific journal Mankind Quarterly became their venue. Mankind Quarterly was funded by the Pioneer Fund, a nonprofit foundation set up in the 1930s by a US textile magnate who chose to use his fortune to fund research on race science and fight desegregation in the US.

The Pioneer Fund continued to fund race science into the 21st century—including supporting Mankind Quarterly—when it was taken over by the current network of race scientists. They channeled its remaining funds into a new company, the Human Diversity Foundation, subsequently rebranded as Polygenic Scores LLC.

Given all this, how is race science still being published in mainstream academic journals? To be clear, race science does not typically get published in genetics or evolutionary biology journals. Most academic journals operate peer review, meaning papers only get published after evaluation by experts in the field. The overall absence of race science in genetics or biology journals is a clear signal that it does not meet contemporary standards of scientific rigor in genetics or biology.

Instead, race scientists continue to publish in para-scientific journals. But race science also makes it into a growing number of mainstream social science or humanities journals. Psychologist Andrew Winston, an expert in the history of race science in psychology, has described it as a “community project… with its own networks, mentorships, journals, funding, conferences, heroes, origin myths, martyrs, defenders, and historiography.”

Behind the network of writers producing race science sits a community of editors, peer reviewers, and others in gatekeeping roles, which too often allows race science to be published in mainstream journals. This is particularly in the subfields of psychology interested in intelligence and individual differences, and evolutionary psychology.

Editors of Intelligence, the mainstream journal that has probably published the largest quantity of race science, have occasionally published editorials defending their publication of race science in the name of ensuring robust academic debate even on controversial topics.

There have been sanctions. Philippe Rushton, one of an earlier generation of race scientists, faced sanctions from his university in Canada but kept his job apparently owing to the intervention of Edward O. Wilson, arguably the most famous biologist toward the end of the 20th century.

Cofnas created justified controversy over his call for a “hereditarian revolution” after which it will be acknowledged that black people are innately intellectually disadvantaged compared to white people. But he kept his postdoctoral fellowship at Cambridge University (though his affiliation with a Cambridge college was ended). He has also kept his postdoctoral position at Ghent University after using the opportunity provided by excessive news coverage of Arday to publicize his hereditarian views (though some sanctions have been imposed, such as restrictions on teaching). Cofnas has defended his views by saying he’s been misunderstood and is entitled to free inquiry.

Moreover, when University College London discovered that the race science community had been holding conferences on its premises (the “London Conferences on Intelligence”), without their knowledge of the subject of the conferences, they acted to prevent further such conferences being held. The publisher of the journal Intelligence, Elsevier, has recently appointed new editors, who have stated their commitment to publishing both rigorous and socially responsible science. Race scientists are increasingly publishing in evolutionary psychology journals rather than Intelligence.

However, the London Conferences on Intelligence continue, though not at UCL. Race scientists also have a media arm, Aporia magazine, which promotes race science using much more overt language and with more obvious policy focus than publications in academic journals. One Aporia article, for example, argued for eugenics as a solution to Africa’s lack of economic development, noting in a post that some may find it “distasteful.” It claimed—relying on race science’s unscientific arguments—that artificial selection for higher intelligence is needed to improve the continent’s innately unintelligent populace.

Race science may not have the respect of the scientific community, but it does have funding and influential backers. A report by Hope not Hate revealed that the network of race scientists received significant funding from the US businessman Andrew Conru, who withdrew his funding after the report was released.

The US Ambassador to Belgium even intervened when Ghent University set up an investigation into how Cofnas was hired.

Race scientists are far from the victims they claim to be. Race scientists in this and previous generations have worked hard to ensure their messages are heard beyond academia. And in this generation, they are successfully using social media to spread their beliefs, especially Elon Musk’s X.

But what about academic freedom? Isn’t it good for science to discuss any hypothesis to ensure free exchange of ideas? Academic debate is productive only if it involves methodologically rigorous research. Science will not progress if one side of any debate consistently uses poor-quality, methodologically flawed work. Nor is it necessary to continually debate topics that have long since been settled.

We believe it is unequivocal that race science is fundamentally pseudoscience. In our view, it’s critical to call attention to not only the scientific flaws underlying this body of work but also the real target it’s aimed at: political actors and everyday readers who ultimately decide the direction that science, and society, goes in.

My first year of medical school was peppered with clinical exposure and taught me otherwise. I floundered with a blood pressure cuff during a wellness visit, fumbled for words as I interviewed a man waiting for a heart transplant, and listened to a patient recount her immense sorrow after multiple miscarriages. I agree with the authors that students are often preoccupied with medical licensure exams, and that early clinical exposure might not cure this. In my experience, however, it is the strongest counterweight. After meeting those patients, I was learning for more than an exam. The proposed model would abandon that counterweight. Students would still encounter patients eventually, but we would have forfeited valuable time. Becoming a compassionate, humanistic physician is slow work, and it deserves all the time we can offer. Markovich is correct: The U.S. health care “non-system” is broken. There are many culprits, including hospitals, whose prices are set more by market dominance than by quality. A chatbot can handle every conversation responsibly and still, over time, take the place of someone a person used to confide in. In their First Opinion essay, Qian Yang, Ned Cooper, and Ibrahim Emara show how easily a gradual loss of human connection can escape safeguards designed for individual conversations. They make a persuasive case for giving independent researchers access to platform data and developing tools to distinguish the effects of chatbot use from other causes of isolation. Those tools should ask whether people could reach someone they trusted when they needed help.

That question is especially worth asking adolescents, who may have fewer ways to seek help privately. Imagine two teenagers who each spend an hour every night talking to a chatbot. One used to call a friend during difficult times, but he called less often as talking to the chatbot became a habit. The other still talks to friends every day. When she wanted to discuss something she feared would get back to her parents, she asked to see a school counselor. While she waited for an appointment, she took the problem to a chatbot. Their chat logs might look very similar. The authors suggest measuring school counselor time as one way to estimate what isolation costs schools. That measure captures time counselors spend helping students. Hours, days, or weeks spent waiting leave no trace.

Researchers could ask teens to think about the last time something was troubling them. Did they talk to someone about it, try to reach someone, or decide against reaching out? If they tried, did they get to talk to someone? Did they talk to a chatbot about it?

Asking these questions regularly could help researchers distinguish a teen who reached out to people less often as their chatbot usage grew from one who turned to a chatbot only after trying to get help. The distinction matters for policymaking. When chatbot use displaces human contact, usage limits may help. When it fills a gap in support, the priority should be making help easier to reach. A usage limit might prompt one teenager to call a friend. Another may still be waiting for someone to call back.

— Brian J. Carter, J.D., Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine

In her essay “How bad is healthy supposed to feel?” Kirsten Balin properly notes that medicine has a regrettable history of dismissing symptoms, particularly in women. The authors of the First Opinion commentary on pediatric emergencies from Oct. 27 identify a growing but hidden problem: stark gaps in delivery of pediatric emergency care across the U.S. The solutions they propose — payment reform, expanding regional care coordination hubs, and incentivizing large pediatric medical centers to support smaller hospitals — all merit serious consideration.

However, such approaches risk overlooking existing strengths that we can build upon now to support emergency departments and prehospital agencies aiming to develop their own internal capacity. Pediatric readiness refers to the infrastructure and resources EDs and prehospital agencies need to provide high-quality care for children. In our roles as researchers and physicians, we collaborate in community-engaged research to identify strategies to support EDs and EMS agencies in their readiness.

This is vital: EDs with high pediatric readiness have up to 76% lower mortality for critically ill children. It would cost approximately $200 million to make all EDs nationwide pediatric ready, a tiny fraction of annual U.S. health care expenditures. Many EDs lack designated pediatric champions, knowns as pediatric emergency care coordinators (PECCs), whose presence has been shown to be closely associated with readiness. As the authors rightly point out, larger pediatric medical centers can support smaller hospitals and PECCs.

That’s why we collaborate with local, national, and international partners in readiness improvement strategies such as telehealth support to smaller hospitals, simulation-based systems-testing and medical education, developing PECC networking and mentorship models, and more. This research stems from our experiences in rural, indigenous, and urban hospital systems. This has helped us understand the importance of foregrounding community members’ concerns and supporting their local expertise and leadership in our research efforts.

The authors grounded their commentary in two stories of what pediatricians are seeing nationwide. We agree that stories from families and local providers should drive our research. For us, that means collecting stories about what pediatric emergency readiness should look like, collaborating with community hospitals and families to identify research questions and select appropriate methodologies, and finding sustainable dissemination and implementation strategies.

EDs can start their own readiness journey by participating in the National Pediatric Readiness Project’s national assessment to identify current strengths and opportunities for improvement. As we work toward a future where any child can receive high-quality emergency care when and where they need it, we recommend starting by identifying and building upon strengths already in place.

— Elizabeth Sanseau, M.D., Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP) PolicyLab, CHOP Indigenous Health Unit, and the University of Pennsylvania’s Perelman School of Medicine; and Jeremy Jones, M.D., CHOP PolicyLab

As a pediatric emergency physician and clinical ethicist, I have experienced and witnessed aggression at work many times. The authors’ concern comes from a good place, but it misses the realities of the clinical setting. They write that ineffective signs would be “pointless, but not harmful.” I disagree, for three reasons.

First, the essay overstates the evidence against signage. Its claim rests on two sources: a 2006 survey of Australian nurses and a 2008 commentary on the U.K.’s zero tolerance campaign. Neither evaluates whether signs change behavior. Nor has anyone tested the claim that signs erode patient trust. Research on violence prevention is methodologically weak, though multicomponent programs show mixed but encouraging results.

Second, deterrence is not the only purpose of a sign. Even if the signs prevented nothing, they would matter. The authors acknowledge that violence against health care workers has risen. In emergency departments specifically, it has introduced a mental and physical burden that fundamentally changes what it means to do this work. Signage stating an obvious standard of behavior may seem trivial, but it can have meaning as a piece of a broader institutional commitment to health care worker well-being. It tells staff that their hospital understands the realities of their work and supports them.

Third, signs remind clinicians where their obligations end. Many clinicians, especially trainees, are unsure what they owe a patient or family who is abusing them. A hospital must screen and stabilize anyone who comes to the emergency department, whatever their behavior. Beyond that, no patient or family is entitled to further care from a clinician they are making unsafe. This is easy to forget amid the pressures of clinical care, and a visible reminder helps.

The authors worry about the message these signs send to patients and families. That concern is thoughtful, but it is aimed at the wrong signs. The signs I see in emergency departments name physical assault, threats, and harassment. A sign that polices tone or discourages complaints could deserve criticism, but those signs are the exception and should not set the terms of this debate. Even the authors want their alternative signs to make clear that violence will not be tolerated. They close by asking that the words on hospital walls communicate medicine’s values. Protecting the people who care for patients is one of them.

— Joseph Shapiro, M.D., Children’s National Hospital

I vividly remember when I saw the original clip 20 years ago. A blue Volkswagen Touareg V10 diesel towed a 171-ton jet aircraft down the same Dunsfold runway in the United Kingdom where the old "Top Gear" episodes used to be filmed.

Now, the Denza B5 (or Bao 5 in some markets) plug-in hybrid SUV is back at the same place to do the same stunt, but adapted for a 2026 where everything seemingly needs to be bigger, more over the top, and more in-your-face. (I blame TikTok.)

So, we have a Chinese PHEV that uses two electric motors and a 1.5-liter gas engine. It’s all-wheel-drive, its 31.8-kilowatt-hour battery can deliver up to 56 miles of all-electric range on a full battery, and it has two mechanical locking differentials. This last part is important. The battery can be fast-charged at up to 100 kW, and the total system output is a healthy 536 horsepower, with most of that oomph being provided by the two e-motors.

We also have a 207-ton jumbo jet that got a quick repositioning for no other purpose than pure entertainment.

Here comes the interesting bit: While Volkswagen’s original stunt kept all four of the Touareg’s wheels in place—you know, how a car usually works—BYD decided to put its PHEV SUV to the test by gradually removing some wheels.

The first run was done with all four wheels in place, followed by a three-wheeled run with 2.2 tons of ballast. For the final act, both right wheels were removed, and the ballast reached 5 tonnes. The point of it all? Promote the new SUV to the UK market, where the Land Rover Defender is among its biggest rivals. Unlike the Defender, however, which is now based on a unibody design and is primarily combustion-powered, the Denza B5 sits on a bespoke ladder-frame chassis and more substantially electrified. It has a gas engine that acts mainly as a generator for the high-voltage battery. The battery cells are directly integrated between the chassis rails.

If you're thinking about trying this stunt at home, just know the vehicle needed a few modifications to pull it off. BYD added a custom towing attachment, removed some seats to make room for the ballast, and added stabilizers to make up for the missing wheels.

You can't gauge Markel's quarter by looking at traditional earnings based on generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP) because the unrealized investment gains and losses from its portfolio are included. Those unrealized gains or losses distort the actual performance of the company.

But if you look at its adjusted operating income, that shows how much the company made on the businesses it owns. Social Security's Trump Bump-Led 2027 COLA Is the 5th Largest in 35 Years -- but There's a Dark Side to This Outsize Increase as Well

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Though Social Security checks are about to get bigger, the projected timeline for sweeping benefit cuts may have accelerated.

By Sean Williams – Nov 21, 2026 at 6:36AM EST

For Social Security’s nearly 72 million traditional beneficiaries -- retired workers, workers with disabilities, and survivors of deceased workers -- there are few, if any, announcements more anticipated than the annual cost-of-living adjustment (COLA).

Social Security's COLA is the near-annual "raise" given to beneficiaries to account for inflation (i.e., rising prices). If the price for the goods and services that seniors regularly purchase kept climbing without an adjustment in benefits, the buying power of Social Security income would steadily decline over time.

Social Security's announced 2027 COLA offers something of a mixed bag for recipients. On the one hand, it's set to deliver one of the largest raises over the last 35 years. This larger payout comes courtesy of President Donald Trump and a handful of his policies.

President Trump's policies offer a good-news/bad-news scenario for Social Security. Image source: Official White House Photo by Molly Riley.

But this outsize Trump bump isn't without its consequences. Larger payouts in the new year can make the program's precarious financial outlook that much worse.

On Oct. 14, the Social Security Administration announced that monthly payouts would increase by 3.6% in 2027. For the average retired-worker beneficiary, their monthly payout is expected to climb by $75. Meanwhile, the typical worker with a disability can expect their monthly Social Security check to rise by $59 in the new year.

A 3.6% raise is well above the norm. It marks a tie for the fifth-largest percentage increase over the last 35 years, surpassed only by COLAs of 4.1% (2006), 5.8% (2009), 5.9% (2022), and 8.7% (2023).

It's also the sixth consecutive year in which recipients have received at least a 2.5% cost-of-living adjustment. It's been 30 years (1988-1997) since benefits rose by at least 2.5% for six straight years.

While modest inflation associated with a growing U.S. economy results in a Social Security raise most years, next year's outsize COLA was pumped up by two of President Donald Trump's policies.

New from the New York Fed: "By February 2026, tariffs had contributed 2.9 pp to goods price inflation, and without them goods prices would have fallen slightly." About one quarter of every point in higher tariff rates shows up in consumer prices within one year. • A 10% across-the-board tariff yields a 2.6% rise in consumer goods prices after one year. • About two thirds of the increase comes from pricier imports, which get passed through quickly. • Another third comes from U.S. made goods, as producers face higher input costs and less import competition. This takes longer, about 6-12 months, to filter through.

To begin with, each of the last two Social Security COLAs has been influenced by Trump's tariff and trade policy. Adding duties to imports directly raises their price and can increase manufacturing costs for domestic producers.

A recently published report by three New York Federal Reserve economists writing for Liberty Street Economics ("How Fast Do Tariffs Pass Through into Consumer Prices?") found that Trump's 2025 tariffs increased goods price inflation by an estimated 2.9 percentage points by February 2026. Without tariffs, the NY Fed economists estimate that goods prices would have otherwise fallen.

The other major policy contributing to Social Security's 2027 Trump bump is the Iran war. Closing the Strait of Hormuz has resulted in the largest energy supply disruption in modern history, sending gas and diesel prices notably higher.

With Trumpflation (inflation driven by Trump's policies) becoming entrenched in the U.S. economy, it's quite possible that these policies provide a third consecutive year with a Trump bump in 2028.

Unfortunately, outsize Social Security COLAs come at a potentially steep cost to America's leading retirement program.

Every year since 1985, the Social Security Board of Trustees Report has warned of a long-term unfunded obligation. In simpler terms, the projected income to be collected in the 75 years following the release of a report is expected to be insufficient to cover outlays (primarily benefits, but also the administrative costs to oversee Social Security). As of 2026, this long-term unfunded obligation had ballooned to $29.3 trillion.

However, this isn't the most pressing concern for the program or its nearly 72 million traditional beneficiaries.

According to the latest Trustees Report, the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance trust fund (OASI) is forecast to exhaust its asset reserves by the fourth quarter of 2032. The OASI's asset reserves represent the excess income collected since inception that is invested in special-issue, interest-bearing government bonds, as required by law.

US Old-Age and Survivors Insurance Trust Fund Assets at End of Year data by YCharts.

The positive news is that Social Security isn't going bankrupt and is in no danger of halting benefits. More than 91% of the program's income is collected through the 12.4% payroll tax on earned income (wages and salaries, but not investment income). As long as Americans keep working, the payroll tax will continue generating income for disbursement to eligible beneficiaries... even if the OASI's asset reserves are depleted.

But just because Social Security isn't going bankrupt, it doesn't mean everything is peachy. If the OASI's asset reserves run dry over the next six years, as predicted, it would indicate that the current payout schedule, inclusive of annual COLAs, isn't sustainable. Sweeping benefit cuts of up to 22% may be necessary for retired workers and survivors of deceased workers.

The concern is that Social Security's Trump bump may have materially altered the trajectory to the OASI's asset reserve insolvency.

When the Trustees Report issues its short- and long-term outlooks, it takes into account a long list of variables, including birth rates, death rates, net migration rates, and even annual COLAs. The Trustees assume that annual COLAs will be modest -- but as we just saw, a 3.6% annual raise is anything but average.

Social Security's outsize 2027 raise risks draining the OASI even faster than initially forecast.

President Trump's "Big, Beautiful Bill" already moved up the projected OASI asset reserve insolvency timeline by one quarter (from the first quarter of 2033 to the fourth quarter of 2032). It's entirely possible the 2027 Trump bump further accelerates the timeline to sweeping benefit cuts.

‘Project Hail Mary’ Almost Had a Completely Different Ending for Rocky

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In a historic move, actor and puppeteer James Ortiz is eligible for a Best Supporting Actor nomination at the 99th Academy Awards for his standout performance on Project Hail Mary. He’s the mind and movement behind the blockbuster’s lovable alien creature, Rocky, who led a team of puppeteers on set, essentially performing “Waiting for Godot in space” opposite Ryan Gosling with take after take of improvisation.

From filmmakers Phil Lord and Chris Miller, Project Hail Mary took audiences out of this world in an Andy Weir adaptation, penned by Drew Goddard, that wasn’t just visually stunning, but was a surprisingly moving two-hander between a man way in over his head and a creature made of rock, both on a dangerous mission to save their home planets.

At this year’s SCAD Savannah Film Festival, Collider’s Steve Weintraub had the pleasure of moderating an in-depth Q&A with Ortiz to discuss the artistry of bringing one of 2026’s most memorable characters to life. Alongside Gosling’s Ryland Grace, Rocky becomes Project Hail Mary’s beating heart, and during this conversation, Ortiz shares the behind-the-scenes work it took to bring them from the page to the screen. We learn all about the practical puppetry, the limitations that allowed their creativity to soar, how Rocky’s most iconic moments and quips came to be. Don't miss the full Q&A in the video above, with a transcript provided below.

COLLIDER: Let's talk a little bit about your background and how you got involved in the project.

JAMES ORTIZ: So I was cast as an actor a thousand years ago by the casting director, Jeanne McCarthy. You may have seen her name at the end of this film. I was cast in a pilot that no one ever saw because it never aired. It was for a network that we don't have to mention.

It was a great experience, but shortly after that, in New York, where I'm based, I was developing this sort of side career that turned into my full career, which is puppeteering and designing puppets for the theater. It kind of became this funny opportunity for me to play characters against my type. I could play things that I would never, ever be cast as because not every day you need, like, I don't know, a gay mad scientist. So, sometimes you need other flavors, and it was so exciting to be able to do that.

So Jeanne remembered that I existed, and she would bring me in for stuff, but oftentimes I would go, "Oh, I can't come in because I have this puppet thing." And she would go, "Puppets?" And long story short, she gave me a phone call. She told me about this story, and I went, "This sounds incredible, but do they really mean a puppet? Do they mean that?" And I had a great meeting with Phil [Lord] and Chris [Miller]. It was love at first sight. We absolutely adore each other. And then we were off to the races, and I was on set trying to make a faceless five-legged creature lovable.

If Rocky could watch Project Hail Mary, what would he complain the filmmakers got wrong about him?

ORTIZ: I think Rocky doesn't think that they're funny, so I think Rocky would hear everybody laughing and go, "Not funny, not funny." I think that'd probably be the biggest thing.

If you could sit down with Jim Henson, what would you want to talk about?

ORTIZ: I don't know if I would say anything. I would just sort of sit there like a loser. I've been really fortunate; I've met a lot of his children because they run different puppet organizations all over the country, and it's been really, really special. I did a show called The Woodsman a thousand years ago in New York, and it was really, really special. Cheryl Henson moderated a talk-back very much like this, and she told me very effusively how much her father loved the Oz stories, and it was really meaningful to hear that.

How many voicemails have you recorded for friends and family as Rocky?

ORTIZ: The limit does not exist. I couldn't even begin to give you a number.

How do you feel about doing the voice in public, like in moments like this?

ORTIZ: Yeah. What are we trying to communicate?

I'm sure there are some people in the audience that would like a voicemail from Rocky.

ORTIZ: I think what it would probably be would be, "You reach voicemail of this human. Make no sense. Leave message after beep. Beep now. It's time for a beep. Where is beep, question? Make no sense. Make no sense. Uh, uh, uh." And then it gets cut off, because I don't think Rocky fully understands what's happening.

When was the first time you said, "Fist my bump," and did you realize what it was going to become?

ORTIZ: I had no idea. We worked on the movie. We worked so hard, and I left, and I had such a great experience. And then I really had no sense that anybody would like it, frankly. That's maybe a failing of mine, but I've been around long enough to know that even if everybody that worked on it did everything right, it doesn't always translate. It doesn't always land, and the fact that it has is pretty, pretty wild. So, I didn't really think that far ahead, to be honest.

Frankly, also, I was only meant to be the person on set to help Ryan [Gosling], to help the DP. I was the steward of the character. I was just borrowing Rocky. And the fact that they lent him fully to me towards the end is still something I'm going to be processing for the rest of my life.

I'm going to dig down a little bit, but do you remember when you first said, "Fist my bump?"

ORTIZ: It had to have been in one of the scenes. It probably was in an improv that we were just kind of going. I was working so hard on just trying to make Rocky as truthful as possible that sometimes I would forget that there were catchphrases I had to hit. So most of the way that we were shooting is that Phil and Chris had an earpiece in my ear, and they could offer thoughts in between, in takes. So sometimes they would go, "Say fist my bump," and you're like, "Right. ‘Amaze, amaze, amaze,’” or whatever, right? So I can't actually tell you when I did it, but I know it would've been at some point then.

You said you and Ryan would sometimes improvise for incredibly long stretches. What's something Ryan did or said as Grace that completely caught you off guard, and how did you respond as Rocky?

ORTIZ: [Laughs] The only thing that's coming to mind right now is because when we shot it, remember that meme that went around three years ago that was “very demure?” There was one day where we were just going and riffing and riffing and going, and at some point, we were talking about Rocky or something, and he went, "It's very demure." And you saw him in real-time go, "No,” and try to backpedal. And in real-time, I think I went, "What is demure, question?"

We just kind of would go. We would just waste camera time.

We're at SCAD, and there are a lot of students in the audience, and I am 100% sure there are a few people out there that would love to do what you do. What advice would you like to share? And part two is, what advice were you given that was absolute horseshit?

ORTIZ: I may have to curse to answer this question, too. I was told by an acting professor a long time ago, "This is one motherfucker of a business." And, yes, it's hard, but I don't think that means it's bad. I think the most rewarding things have been the things that have been hard-won. That's what I would say.

But I would also say about the first question, if you want to play characters that are not easy to define, act more. Act all the time. Try to get opportunities to play characters that you wouldn't be cast as. Read roles that you're like, "I don't think this is for me. Let's try." And also, I think puppetry is an inherently physical art form, so I would also say invest in using your body to play the character.

Something that I've been saying endlessly all over this process has been saying that Rocky's movement is actually Rocky's subtext. The thing that Rocky is feeling is actually what is coming out in his body. The thing that he's saying is the best the translator can manage. So, that was something that I was always tracking: What is Rocky feeling at any given moment? Even though I don't know if Rocky has a deep sense of that themself. They don't. And over the course of the story, we get to see that Rocky actually learns what feeling is through Grace. So, that was another whole evolution.

But I guess the answer is movement theater, playing wacky parts, and letting your imagination not be limited.

I would also say, just from my perspective, I don't think people realize you really have to work your ass off to do something. You've got to give almost everything of yourself to something that you love to be successful.

ORTIZ: Yeah. I think that's completely right. I knew when I got the job that it wasn't going to be me at the end, but what does that matter? My job was to be of service. My job was to make the film work. My job was to make this character that is totally untenable — five legs, no face, speaks in this way, comes from a culture that is unimaginable — and I just went, "I want to always deliver something that feels consistent, that feels alive, that feels surprising, and I want to help Greig Fraser, who's shooting this movie, who has to emotionally find where the camera should go, and emotionally help Ryan in these long scenes where he has so much to do." Can you imagine him trying to do this to a tennis ball? It would've been a nightmare.

So, it was always the objective of just going all the way, and I think I have a “too much” gene as it is, so I couldn't help it.

ORTIZ: Everybody was talking to Rocky and not me. [Laughs] It was exactly that.

The first few weeks of the process, we were still finding our sea legs and figuring some stuff out. Phil gave me some great advice. But Phil pulled me into his little tent with all my other Rocketeers, and we looked at some of the footage from that day, and he was like, "I think you can trust stillness more," because I was overcomplicating the fact that he doesn't have a face means you have to communicate through movement. But I think I was doing too much, and he was like, "Remember, this is in IMAX, so you can't be like [thrashes around] the entire time.”

So, it was a really cool lesson. It was a little bit like going to the Michael Caine school of acting, of never blink, always look at that side of the camera, not that. It was all that stuff that I was learning on a creature with no face.

By the way, that advice that Michael Caine said about not blinking is dead-accurate.

If any of you are acting students, I swear to you, you've got to learn how to not blink, and if you look at people's performances, like great actors, you will notice they do not blink. They're very specific when they blink because they feel that it's like a character moment.

I swear it's a little thing that you should absolutely practice.

ORTIZ: There's footage you can find of Michael Caine explaining this. Do it. It's so valuable because it's about using the medium. Theater people know how to use their whole body, and a lot of film acting is like, "This is now your proscenium. What are you going to do? How do you use it?" And, yeah, that's always the exercise.

Let's talk a little bit about what the physical rig was like. How many people does it take to operate Rocky, and how do you keep the team in sync?

ORTIZ: Great question. So it was me — I was basically always on the carapace, the main body. There were handles coming out of his mid-back, sort of like a fighter pilot, and that was how I could animate the movement of the head. There were sometimes three to seven people operating his other appendages, and I was always sort of leading the charge on what that was.

So we spent eight weeks basically learning how to work together as kind of a company. But the thing that would keep us tethered together, because puppetry can inherently be very technical — I promised Ryan the first day that we started talking about this project, and I had already gotten the part, I did say, "Listen, puppetry can be technical, but I guarantee you can improv with me, and I'm not going to leave you hanging, and I'm not going to let the technical side win." Right? It has to be an emotional performance.

And sometimes when I could feel my other performers trying to do one thing with a leg, and it wasn't going to happen, and they were getting frustrated, all of that, I would just say, "Let's not try to aim for perfection. Let's just focus on the feeling." So sometimes we would finish a take, and I would go, "That was awesome. Let's try it again, and this time it's Christmas morning, and you're tiptoeing down the stairs, and you're so excited." That has nothing to do with the scene, but that's the emotional beat that we were trying to sort of land.

So, that would be the one way, especially when we would be improvising and had to just lean in.

Was there ever a scene where the limitations of the puppet made the performance better?

ORTIZ: [Thumbs down] So, I think it's been well documented that basically they were like, "So he's going to do a thumbs up," and I was like [shakes head], because Rocky's elbows don't rotate. Rocky doesn't have wrists, which was something I would say all the time, and they'd go, "What does that mean?" And I was like, "Well, you can't do a thumbs up without a wrist." Phil and Chris are known for finding movies in the editing room. Did they do anything with the editing that really surprised you about Rocky's performance?

ORTIZ: Yes. What I loved about working with them is that, again, I was a theater person from nowhere. The theater credits don't always transfer in the world of movies, and they would shoot every scene until basically everybody's ideas had been wrung out. It wasn't just like, "Let's do the scene on paper." Which, by the way, Drew Goddard wrote an incredible script, and we would do that, and then Ryan would have a cool idea, or I would have a cool idea, or Phil and Chris would go, "We were thinking it would be fun if..." And we would just shoot. Sometimes it would be a three-line scene, and we would shoot a take for 45 minutes, going back and going back, and, “Try it again, and add this.”

So, we had so much material that frankly, I left every day going, "I have no idea what we just did." I completely blacked out. I was just trying to stay in the scene and trying to stay alive. So when I saw different cuts of the movie, they in fact were very different films from my estimation. The way that the scenes would be juxtaposed next to each other meant something else.

When I saw the final cut, I actually did go, "I think I want to try to get into the ADR booth and maybe try to complete some of Rocky's arcs a little bit more." Because we didn't have the real estate. The movie is from Ryan's perspective. Literally, you're inside of his head most of the time. When he realizes that Rocky speaks that way, we suddenly can see in Rocky Vision, right? So there wasn't a lot of real estate to put Rocky's arc in there.

The TLDR of what I'll just sort of say here is that we didn't have this moment where Rocky's sort of traumatic 80 years left alone on a spaceship was ever paid off. Through a series of just trial and error, we were able to find something, and I sort of raised my hand and said, "I think we need to change this line in this scene where I give him the astrophage.” And Rocky saying, "Grace say Grace will die, Rocky fix" was something that we added truly a month before the movie came out. And it was something that really made me so happy because, again, it's that moment when I was going, "I feel so much for this character. I really want to do him service." And we hadn't landed the plane on, well, where does that trauma go? We never addressed it. And I think the trauma has to turn into something positive. Rocky then takes the awful experience that they had, which we don't really talk about, and turns it into help for the person that he cares about the most.

So anyway, that's a roundabout way of answering that.

It has to be really crazy to be a part of a film like this that means so much to so many people. What has it been like meeting fans, and what do they always want to talk to you about?

ORTIZ: Oh, it's incredible. Like I was saying, we made this movie kind of in a bubble, and then it came out — and what we don't have time for is the time that I met Steve the first time. Do you want to do it? Can we do it?

This is your Q&A. You do whatever you want.

We should say the room that you were in was in a hotel, and there were curtains, so you could not see past the curtain to know what was inside. ORTIZ: And basically, there were cameras set up that people could see Rocky because Rocky was on a table and alive, and, "Hello, friend!" One of the greatest strengths of crime television is its ability to skew moral compasses and deliver fascinating, complex characters that challenge our understanding of humanity. It goes without saying that the performances of such characters are instrumental in determining their quality. Everything from the subtleties of facial expressions to the rhythm of line delivery, and to the physicality they are presented with, is all key choices actors can make in defining the brand of villainy their character exudes. Whether viewers hold a wicked love for them or dread every second of screen time they receive, these crime series find astonishing highlights in the form of their biggest and baddest antagonists.

Despite only appearing in the third season of Boardwalk Empire, Gyp Rosetti (Bobby Cannavale) stands as one of the Prohibition-era gangster series’ most piercing and unforgettable characters. Within a story that often feels methodical and pragmatic, Rosetti injects a tense volatility into every single scene he appears in, which is a testament to the astonishing writing of the character and Cannavale’s note-perfect performance of such an explosive character.

Whereas so many of the other adversaries Nucky Thompson (Steve Buscemi) encounters attempt to outsmart him through business maneuvers or political plotting, Rosetti relies on ferocious, bloodthirsty brutality and unwavering intent. His destructive, overwhelming personality is only made all the more commanding by the character’s sensitivity to perceived slights, especially as Cannavale imbues every second of screen time he gets with the simmering suspense of a ticking time bomb. Whether he’s erupting into a fit of rage or hurling oddly elegant insults, Cannavale makes Rosetti one of the most intimidating and unpleasant TV antagonists of all time.

As a grim descent into the criminal underbelly of Gotham City, The Penguin marks one of HBO’s more compelling productions of the decade so far, a rich mixture of comic-book style and gangland brutality. Colin Farrell’s lead performance is a driving force of this, but the miniseries benefits from its vast array of striking characters, with Cristin Milioti’s turn as criminal heiress Sofia Falcone being the noteworthy standout as she blends an erratic, unpredictable ruthlessness with underlying vulnerability and sympathetic sorrow.

Milioti worked closely with both movement coach Julia Crockett and the production’s costuming department to perfect Sofia’s physicality. Through every movement, she embodies the volatility of her explosive aspirations fighting to break free from her past of repression and institutionalization. In isolation, she is exceptional — the fourth episode’s focus on her time in Arkham Asylum was instrumental in her Emmy Award win — but the crackling, high-octane tension she brings to scenes with Farrell is her true triumph, crafting a complex yet purposeful obstacle to Cobb’s own ambitions of controlling organized crime in Gotham.

Purely as a superhero villain, Wilson Fisk (Vincent D’Onofrio), otherwise known as Kingpin, is a perfect foil to Daredevil (Charlie Cox), with his brutish strength challenging the hero’s more precise and calculating fighting style. The Trump administration has proposed a slew of changes to Medicare Advantage and Part D designed to "revitalize care" in these markets.

For one, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services said late Friday that it will update application and network adequacy requirements to make it easier for new entrants. The agency proposed to require organizations to demonstrate network adequacy during their first year in operation, rather than during the application process.

This shift would eliminate a significant barrier for new entrants, driving greater competition in the market.

CMS has also proposed combining three specialty areas in behavioral health — clinical psychology, clinical social work and outpatient behavioral health — into one specialty group for the purposes of network adequacy. The changes will give plans greater flexibility in building out behavioral health networks.

CMS will also make adjustments that seek to boost competition for special needs plans.

In addition, the agency said it will make adjustments to the star ratings program, focusing its ratings on 15 measures. The proposed measure set would emphasize "clinical outcomes and patient experience while retaining select process measures in high-priority areas where performance remains low."

CMS is also aiming to update the calculation methodology that will "raise the bar for achieving high ratings," and thus quality bonus payments.

Thomas Frank is back, and he sounds exactly like a man who has had enough time away to think clearly. Crystal Palace have appointed the 53-year-old on a two-and-a-half-year deal, and his first message was simple. He is refreshed, reflective and ready for another go in the Premier League.

Palace need that. They are bottom of the table, short on momentum and heading into a survival battle that will demand clarity more than noise. Frank arrives after a bruising end to his previous spell at Tottenham, where he was dismissed in February, less than eight months after taking charge. He could have stayed away longer. He chose not to.

There was no attempt to dress it up. Frank admitted the break had been enjoyable. He travelled to Japan and Australia, spent time in Denmark and relaxed with family and friends. As he put it, he had been "visiting friends and family, eating too well [and] drinking too much wine".

He had every reason to extend that time off too. "I was very aware I didn't want to do anything in the summer. I put a statement out there, I needed more time," he said. Then came the line that tells you where his head is now, "I can actually, honestly say I would have been fine if it was three, four, five, six, seven months more, because it's good to be back in the circus, but it's also unbelievably good not to be in, especially if you enjoy life, which I do."

Still, Palace convinced him. The club's internal pitch clearly mattered. Frank singled out chairman Steve Parish, along with Matt Hobbs and Iain Moody, as key figures in persuading him this was the right job at the right time.

That matters because sentiment gets you nowhere at the bottom of the Premier League. Palace need points, quickly. Frank's reign starts with a trip to Coventry City on Saturday, and there is no gentle easing-in period. The task is obvious, steady the team, sharpen the structure and lift belief.

Frank also made it clear that his time out of football was not spent feeling sorry for himself. "If you don't learn from any of your jobs, I don't think you reflect, I don't think you improve, and I don't think you get better," said Frank. "Of course there's a lot of things I have been reflecting over. The details, that will be for another time, but I can say I've learned, definitely."

This is where experience matters. Frank knows the scrutiny, understands the noise and appears entirely unbothered by it. "I think we all have an ego," he said. "Some are better at controlling it than others. For me, it's not the critics, it's more what I can learn, what I can prove, how I can get better."

He was even more direct when discussing outside opinion. "There's a lot of clever people out there, but the interesting thing is they only know 10 per cent of the truth. That's a tenth of the truth at Brentford, there's a tenth of the truth at Tottenham, and that's just the way it is.

"If you can't handle that as a coach or a player, then you shouldn't be in this business, and I'm very happy to be back in the game."

That is the point. No melodrama, no self-pity, no grand promises. Crystal Palace have hired a manager who sounds realistic about the mess in front of him and calm about the pressure around it. Right now, that is a sensible start.
