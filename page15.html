Google Unveils Ask AdMob and AI Agent Tools for App Monetization

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google introduced Ask AdMob and a set of AI agent tools on November 16, 2026, targeting the work of configuring, troubleshooting and coding app advertising. The package combines a Gemini-built conversational assistant with developer playbooks and an upcoming server connection to AdMob accounts, each at a different stage of availability.

Naomi Ben-Ayoun, general manager of AdMob, presented the release as a way to reduce the manual work of managing ad setups, fixing integration errors and auditing brand safety. The features cover both account-level decisions about demand and blocking and the code required to implement those decisions.

Ask AdMob is a conversational agent built with Gemini that will reside in publishers’ AdMob accounts. Google describes it as working like an AdMob product expert, trained on monetization best practices, with a troubleshooting hub that will flag setup anomalies and software development kit (SDK) integration issues. Its visibility into how ads load is intended to support recommendations for problems affecting revenue.

For optimization, Google says the assistant will use peer benchmarks to recommend adding SDK bidders it identifies as high-value, with predicted revenue uplifts, and help optimize mediation groups. It will also check third-party network configurations for typos and floor-price discrepancies before they affect yield.

Publishers will additionally be able to configure custom brand-safety rules with natural-language prompts in the AdMob interface instead of manually managing blocklists. Google will begin testing Ask AdMob’s capabilities over the next few months and plans more advanced features that act on a user’s behalf later in 2027. Every recommendation will remain subject to user review and approval before it is applied.

Agent skills are available with the announcement for implementation work. They give a developer’s preferred AI agent pre-built, tested playbooks to build, edit and validate ad code, including migration to the Google Mobile Ads Next-Gen SDK, validation of an SDK implementation and implementation of new ad formats. Google is positioning that workflow as an alternative to clicking through dashboards, copying ad-unit IDs and maintaining ad configuration files outside the codebase.

Google’s developer documentation describes the skills as portable, self-contained modules containing SDK knowledge, instructions and workflows, intended to help AI assistants perform complex tasks more accurately. They work with assistants that support skills, including Antigravity, Claude Code, Codex and Cursor. Installation uses npx skills add google/skills/skills/ads, while npx skills update --all updates the installed skills.

The Android documentation lists six skills: initial SDK setup, banner ads, full-screen interstitial ads, rewarded ads and their reward callbacks, migration from the legacy Google Mobile Ads SDK, and integration validation. The validation skill covers pre-launch audits and checks for common implementation errors, while the setup skill includes setting the application ID and initializing the Next-Gen SDK.

The upcoming AdMob MCP server would connect developer environments directly to AdMob, expanding an agent’s role from code assistance to account operations. Google says agents will be able to read account data, create ad units and manage mediation settings, and that the server will expand to more developers in early 2027 after early testing. Developers will review and approve updates before pushing them to production.

Google also plans a broader wave of in-product and AI-agent capabilities throughout 2027 to handle more routine operational work. That makes the approval workflow central to the product strategy: as agents gain access to code and monetization settings, the open question is whether publishers can review suggested changes thoroughly without recreating the manual workload the tools are meant to remove.

OpenAI to Award 50,000 ChatGPT Plus Grants to Researchers

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

On November 16, 2026, OpenAI announced that it will award 50,000 new ChatGPT for Academic Researchers grants, with applications opening November 23, 2026. Each grant provides free ChatGPT Plus access for a recipient and up to two collaborators, covering as many as 150,000 researchers this round.

Selection will occur on a first-come, first-served basis among eligible applications, replacing the pilot’s lottery. Qualified researchers already on the waitlist will receive activation instructions by email before applications open. OpenAI’s overall goal is to reach 200,000 researchers, twice the target set when the pilot launched in July 2026.

The pilot has enrolled 20,000 researchers across 33 countries. Pilot participants received ChatGPT Pro. OpenAI said its usage review found that most participants would have remained within Plus limits, and it attributed the switch to Plus for new grants to its aim of supporting more research teams.

Program participants can use OpenAI’s frontier models and receive training on using ChatGPT in research. Data shared through the program is not used to train OpenAI models by default.

Pilot projects illustrate different research uses. Shuqi Li of Brigham and Women’s Hospital and Harvard Medical School is exploring whether ChatGPT can connect laboratory and patient data to accelerate antibiotic development for tuberculosis. At the Florida Museum of Natural History, Arthur Porto and his team use AI tools to turn specimen photographs into searchable databases. They also created a 3D butterfly model whose wings change color as it is turned, like the real specimen.

A companion study from OpenAI’s Economic Research team, Science at the Frontier: How Scientists Use ChatGPT, analyzed 580,186 scientific messages from 1,819 scientists between August 20 and September 22, 2026, alongside a survey of 251 participants conducted from September 30 to October 7, 2026. To protect privacy, automated classifiers sorted messages by research activity; the research team saw the categories but did not read the underlying messages or ChatGPT’s responses. Activity shares were calculated for each scientist and then averaged, giving every scientist equal weight.

The study reports that reading and learning accounted for an average 28% of scientific messages and writing and communicating 27%, followed by analyzing evidence and theories at 17% and writing research software at 14%. Sixty-eight percent of scientists used ChatGPT to develop research ideas, defined as sending at least three ideation messages during the study. Among ideation messages, 36% involved a scientist bringing an idea for ChatGPT to refine, 35% began with a scientist’s specific question, and 23% asked ChatGPT to suggest a question.

The study also measured follow-up requests to clarify, check, or revise ChatGPT’s responses. Average follow-up rates were highest for writing and communicating at 38%, followed by writing research software and analyzing evidence and theories at 34% each. The metric captures a follow-up in the next scientific message in the same conversation within seven days.

Surveyed participants estimated that completing the same work at the same quality without AI would have taken 1.9 times as long. That figure is self-reported. Ninety-eight percent of the 250 scientists answering a separate discovery-timeline question expected AI to accelerate discovery in their field. Those responses describe expectations rather than observed discovery gains.

OpenAI said the findings do not establish causal effects and that determining whether these expectations translate into faster discovery will require more research. The study also identifies funding, staffing, and experimental capacity as constraints on testing ideas. The expansion therefore pairs broader access with an unresolved scientific question: the pilot documents how AI fits into research work, while the larger program’s impact depends on researchers’ ability to test AI-assisted ideas and validate any resulting findings.

Using AI for listings, where marketing ends and misrepresentation begins

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As AI speeds up listing copy and outreach, fewer checkpoints can increase compliance, disclosure, and fair housing risk. The column argues for training, tool controls and meaningful human review.  AI Summary

Over the years, I have seen too many six-figure lawsuits over the words ‘fully-renovated.’

In real estate, a single, seemingly innocuous word or phrase can carry significant legal ramifications and consequences. Descriptions with words including “fully renovated,” “brand new,” “resolved” and “permitted” can and too often do cause years of unexpected, costly litigation for buyers, sellers and even their respective agents more often than people may think. Each word can change a buyer’s understanding of a property and potentially expose a seller, agent, and/or brokerage to liability if the property description no longer matches the reality of the property’s true condition.

By modifying agent and seller property descriptions, AI has inadvertently and undeniably introduced this new layer of exposure and risk to the way agents and their clients do business.

According to the National Association of Realtors 2026 Technology Report, nearly half of Realtors surveyed use AI daily or weekly. Among those agents surveyed who use AI, 75% use it to write listing descriptions and more than half use it for social media posts, emails, or follow-up communications. Other industry research shows that AI adoption may be moving even faster. A 2026 Delta Media survey found that 82% of real estate agents asked use AI to write listing descriptions, which is up from 58% in 2024.

Creating consumer-facing content is the most common use of AI in our industry, helping to craft the words consumers see when considering one of the most important decisions of their lives. At the same time, the biggest challenges brought by AI appear to be growing far more rapidly than the policies and oversight needed to control them.

Surely, most sellers or agents are not deliberately leveraging AI-generated property descriptions to misrepresent properties. The more likely outcome is that an agent will provide accurate information into an AI tool to create a more compelling description, and in turn, the AI tool changes the description to one that is less accurate, thereby exposing an agent and seller to unexpected pitfalls.

For instance, rulings in lawsuits can depend on specific legal issues concerning whether a property is represented, whether by an agent or AI, as partially or fully renovated  or whether a defect was described as repaired or permanently resolved. Lawsuits pertaining to undisclosed permitting issues often concern whether work awaiting approval is described as being in the “final stages of permitting.” The aforementioned are only a few of examples of obstacles an agent or seller may encounter and such compliance issues are challenging companies across industries more often than ever before

The pitfalls of AI are not limited to the sale of real estate. AI has rapidly morphed from an experimental marketing tool to common resource for many areas of business, but especially marketing. Teams use it for everything from drafting copy to generating or cleaning up images to analyzing customer behavior. Work that used to take hours can be done in minutes, if not faster.

Marketing across industries relies on speed that the AI gods are now supercharging. Through their use of artificial intelligence, marketing teams can create dozens of variations of print or digital ads, internal or external emails, social media posts and listing descriptions. The speed at which content is being created naturally removes some of the checkpoints that were in place when it took longer to create or more people were involved in the process.

However, increased efficiency and speed often translate to increased exposure to risk and liability.

A listing description does not have to be completely fabricated to cause a problem. It can come down to the embellishment of one or two words.

Outside of real estate, AI-generated marketing can introduce unsupported claims, make definitive statements from what was originally nuanced, or alter the meaning of disclosures. Without the necessary checkpoints, it can lead to tremendous legal issues.

Accordingly, companies and their employees must take seriously the need for more extensive oversight to stay compliant.

The reason real estate is a great example of how AI can impact compliance is the industry’s significant regulatory requirements, including fair housing laws.

For example, if an agent uses AI to make a listing description more appealing to a certain buyer profile, the language used could potentially implicate protected characteristics or discourage certain groups from being interested in the property without the agent even realizing it.

Even if the agent did not intend to be discriminatory, it is still a compliance issue.

Fortunately, most real estate professionals recognize and consider the risks associated with AI use. A 2026 Realtors Property Resource survey found that 92% of agents surveyed were using or planning to use AI, while 63% identified accuracy of AI-generated content as their top concern.

Industries like financial services, insurance, healthcare, employment, and other highly regulated fields face their own requirements governing advertising, disclosures, accessibility, privacy, and discrimination.

A common answer to how to lower AI risk is to keep a “human in the loop.” If your strategy includes having someone simply click “approve” before AI-generated content goes out, that’s not enough. Reviewers need to know what they are looking for and the potential risks this content creates, such as unsupported claims, industry-specific advertising rules, and whether it misleads consumers. In addition, has confidential or proprietary information been entered into an AI platform to create the content?

The same principle applies when employees or consumers turn to AI for legal, financial, or other professional advice. I find myself repeating one simple phrase constantly: “AI is not an attorney.”

AI can certainly be useful to organize information, generate ideas, and streamline work processes. But it can never replace professional judgment.

A glaring problematic approach taken by many businesses these days appears to be that they are thinking of AI oversight backwards: providing employees free rein to use it and then creating a policy only after something goes wrong.

Instead, real estate professionals must be armed with tools, policies, and guidelines, and practical guardrails now, before AI continues to be utilized further.

At minimum, businesses should identify which AI tools employees are approved to use, what information can and cannot be entered into them, when AI-generated content requires additional review, and an approval and compliance review for any public-facing content.

The level of oversight must reflect the level of risk. Generating ideas for an article headline is a lot different than using AI to determine which consumers should receive a certain ad. Summarizing notes from an internal brainstorming meeting is not the same as synthesizing confidential client information to create a strategy.

Training is just as important as policy, and it is not enough to send out a document once via email announcing how employees are expected to use AI in the workplace.

The companies that manage AI most effectively will be those where legal and marketing work collaboratively.

Legal and compliance teams need to understand marketing workflows and how AI is being used so they can create guardrails. Marketing needs to involve the legal teams early enough in the process so it’s not an afterthought right before launch.

With the way AI is accelerating work, things are happening too quickly for either to operate in a silo.

We are not trying to slow innovation. We are working to create an environment where teams can move quickly because there are established boundaries and protections to keep risk as low as possible.

AI is going to continue to transform marketing, and businesses should be able to leverage this tool while remaining compliant. At the end of the day, the technology may be new, but accountability is not.

Spider-bear? Black cub wins the 2026 Cutest Climber Award.

They may be small, but bear cubs have a pretty steep learning curve. In Alaska, brown bear (Ursus americanus) cubs have a roughly 50 percent survival rate within their first year, while the black bear (Ursus americanus) cub survival rate can range from 51 to 88 percent, depending on where they live. Cubs must learn how to fish, forage, find shelter, and climb trees to escape predators. Fortunately, biology helps out a bit with climbing. Bears have claws that curve and help them go up the tree and then back down safely.

One baby black bear who spent time at Anan Creek in southeastern Alaska’s Tongass National Forest showed off those climbing chops in style. The park’s rangers have officially awarded this bear with the 2026 Cutest Climber Award.

“This little cub slipped on his way down but caught himself and hustled right after mom,” the rangers write. “An adorable reminder that even when you lose your footing, you can recover and keep going. A tiny, furry lesson in resilience provided by the bears of Anan.”

Tongass National Forest’s rangers  are currently awarding the Anan Bear Awards to the 39 bears spotted at the Anan Wildlife Observatory in Alaska over the summer.

Tongass is the largest national forest in the United States, and covers most of southeastern Alaska, including the state’s capital city of Juneau. The area includes Mendanhall Glacier (a popular destination for tourists on Alaskan cruises), numerous hiking trails, eagles soaring above, and plenty of bears on the hunt for food. One of the hotspots is Anan Creek, home to one of the largest runs of pink salmon in Southeast Alaska. As such, the creek is a very popular dinner spot among black and brown bears.

According to Friends of Anan, 39 bears are known to feast on the salmon at Anan Creek. The population includes 31 black bears and eight brown bears. Similar to  their chunky counterparts farther to the north in Katmai National Park, the bears return each summer to feast on salmon and teach their cubs how to fish in order to survive. And also like the fat bears of Katmai, every bear here has their own habits and quirks.

The U.S. Forest Service tracks and documents every bear spotted at Anan, while explore.org’s cameras broadcast live streams of their antics.

FHA’s MMI Fund stays 5x above floor, fueling calls to cut insurance premiums

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

FHA’s 2026 Annual Report to Congress shows the Mutual Mortgage Insurance Fund’s capital ratio at 10.29%, down from 11.47% in 2025 but still far above the 2% statutory minimum for the 12th straight year. Economic net worth slipped to $182.6 billion while capital resources rose to $148.9 billion.   AI Summary

The Federal Housing Administration (FHA)’s Mutual Mortgage Insurance (MMI) Fund posted a strong financial performance in fiscal year 2026, even as its capital ratio declined from 2025 levels, renewing industry pressure to cut annual mortgage insurance premiums amid affordability concerns.

The MMI Fund had a capital reserve ratio of 10.29% as of Sept. 30, down from 11.47% a year earlier, according to FHA’s Annual Report to Congress released Monday. It’s the 12th straight year the fund has remained above its 2% statutory minimum.

“Over the past year, the MMI Fund remained strong despite a year-over-year decline,” the report states. “Both the Single Family forward and Home Equity Conversation Mortgage (HECM) programs have separately maintained capital levels above the 2% ratio for the last six years.”

The fund’s economic net worth ended FY 2026 at $182.6 billion, a $6.3 billion decrease from 2025. Total capital resources, however, increased by $9.3 billion to $148.9 billion, signaling continued strength in FHA’s insurance capacity even as modeled future performance softened.

Scott Turner, secretary of the Department of Housing and Urban Development, wrote in the report that under the Trump administration, HUD has continued “our work to streamline and deregulate FHA Single Family programs, while remaining vigilant stewards of taxpayer resources by eradicating fraud, waste, and abuse within our programs.”

Joseph Gormley, assistant secretary for housing and president of Ginnie Mae, wrote that the administration has eliminated roughly 150 “burdensome and unnecessary” policies, implemented alternative credit scoring models and updated FHA’s appraisal technology submission processes.

Gormley added that HUD has proposed a reform to the partial claim process through a Reinstatement Advance Payment, “which we intend to implement in Fiscal Year 2027.”

In a recent interview with HousingWire, Gormley said the administration is “comfortable” with the fund’s capital levels, signaling no immediate changes to FHA premiums or broader pricing. But the fund’s continued strength is fueling fresh calls from the mortgage industry to reduce FHA’s annual mortgage insurance premiums and ease monthly payments.

Bob Broeksmit, president and CEO of the Mortgage Bankers Association (MBA), said in a statement after the report’s release that the 2026 results create room for “targeted changes that improve affordability for borrowers.”

“FHA should modestly reduce annual mortgage insurance premiums, lowering monthly payments for moderate- and lower-income homebuyers,” Broeksmit said. “Given the slowdown in the housing market, any MIP reduction should be carefully calibrated and paired with additional safeguards to limit multiple layered risk factors on individual loans and protect the fund’s long-term health.”’

The Community Home Lenders of America (CHLA) also weighed in on the report, urging the FHA to end its life-of-loan policies on mortgage insurance.

“FHA Life of Loan premiums were intended as a stop-gap fee hike in 2013 to respond to financial concerns resulting from the 2008 Housing Crisis.” the group said in a statement. “With today’s MMIF report showing a long-term 12 year run of solid financial performance and bulging FHA reserve levels, it is long past time to stop overcharging homeowners for FHA loans, in order to help them in asset building.”

Within the overall fund, the single-family forward loan program reported a standalone capital ratio of 10.47% in FY 2026, compared with 10.95% in 2025. HUD attributed part of the decline to the growing share of loans with reduced annual MIPs following a premium cut in 2023, as well as slower home price appreciation and updated macroeconomic forecasts that increased projected losses.

The reverse mortgage book saw a much sharper move. The standalone capital ratio for the HECM program fell to 5.43% in 2026, down from 24.06% in 2025. HUD noted that HECMs, which account for less than 4% of the MMI Fund, are more sensitive to home price movements. Meanwhile, loan payoffs — viewed as the most favorable recovery outcome for HECMs — have become less common.

“Lower actual and forecast loan payoffs increased losses,” the report stated. “Finally, the HECM program spent substantial capital resources on assignment claims in FY 2026, reducing available cash.”

Overall, in FY 2026, FHA insured more than 935,000 single-family home mortgages, 83% of which were used by first-time homebuyers. FHA also endorsed more than 24,000 reverse mortgages through the HECM program.

As of Sept. 30, the agency managed active insurance on more than 8.4 million forward mortgages with $1.7 trillion in unpaid principal balance and $63.5 billion in outstanding HECM obligations.

The Permanente Medical Group announced on Monday the launch of a physician-led innovation company it said will strike technology partnerships to co-develop, incubate and launch new enterprise healthcare delivery technologies.

Permanentech—a wholly owned subsidiary of the 10,000-physician medical group, which is itself part of the massive integrated nonprofit Kaiser Permanente—looks to rapidly advance potential new healthcare offerings informed by its frontline physicians and the broader organization’s focus on value-based care.

It marked the launch by announcing its first portfolio company: Wrapt Health, which offers an automated tool that predicts likely missed medical appointments for early outreach and rescheduling. In line with Permanentech’s broader thesis, it was born from a TPMG physician’s experiences, validated within the medical group and so far scaled to 22 sites in Kaiser Permanente’s Northern California region.

“Our clinicians are continuously innovating and refining how we deliver care,” Kenneth Trauner, M.D., CEO of Permanentech and chief innovation officer at TPMG, said in a release. “Permanentech gives them a dedicated resource to rapidly turn their insights and creativity into scalable solutions that benefit both our patients and the wider health care community.”

Permanentech will be focused on technologies and AI-enabled tools addressing patient service, access and quality, TPMG said, with successful products also having the potential to improve “the economics of care delivery.” The business is structured “to move at the pace of the technology industry,” but has “a clear advantage” via its access to TPMG and Kaiser Permanente’s substantial footprint for deployment and validation, it said.

Those efforts will involve both home-grown tools and those developed by established or early-stage partners.

The former will champion physician-developed tools created inside TPMG’s ecosystem as an independent company launches. Starting from the concept phase, that support would include financing as well as clinical and operational validation, as was the case with Wrapt Health.

“Our physicians and care teams see every day where technology can make care better,” Kristine Lee, M.D., an associate executive director and chief transformation officer at TPMG, said. “Permanentech gives them a real hand in building tools that lift administrative burden and give time back to patients. When clinicians help build the tools, those tools fit the way they actually work.”

Strategic partnerships and joint ventures, meanwhile, will focus on co-development, clinical valuation and scaled deployment. Permanentech said the latter will begin with local deployments that are expanded to other TPMG sites when solutions “prove their value,” and could include additional strategic support as a partner company looks to advance their offering beyond TPMG.

The previous post, The Division of Labor Between XML, JSON and MessagePack, closed by saying the other half of value comes here. Encoded and Encrypted differ by one number on the API payload, and underneath that number are three stages that run in a fixed order, while on the way back the order is more than the same run in reverse.

An ERP system's API has to answer two questions that are independent of each other: what a call's content should be processed into, and who is entitled to decide it may be processed less. The first is a fixed pipeline, the second is one declaration written onto the method. The two are kept apart, and are compared against each other at one stop only, which is access control.

Encryption is not in those two lines. It is one further step the layer outside decides on according to the format: Encoded stops here, and Encrypted goes one stage further.

Compression comes before encryption because compression does nothing at all to ciphertext. The output of good encryption is statistically indistinguishable from random data, and repetition and skew are exactly what a compression algorithm looks for. Swapping the order makes no call fail, and no check anywhere says a word. It just means the compression stage stops doing anything from then on.

The way back is those three steps reversed: decrypt, decompress, deserialize. But what matters about the reverse direction is not that it runs backwards, it is that each stage only ever sees bytes that got past the one before it. The authentication code is verified before decryption, and decryption comes before decompression, so the gzip decompressor never at any point holds a stretch of data that has not been verified.

A decompressor is a parser that accepts arbitrary bytes. Feed it ciphertext with one bit changed and it will start parsing all the same, then throw because it cannot read a structure out of it. This order means it never gets that far.

The three values of PayloadFormat were covered in that same earlier post. What access control looks at is not that one on its own, it is the result of comparing it with another enum, and that one has four values.

Two of the values share a name across the two enums, being Encoded and Encrypted, which makes it easy to read them as two ways of saying the same thing. They are not: one records a fact, the other declares a requirement.

How the framework's own methods are distributed across those four is itself a decision: the trust boundary is drawn at HTTPS, and only the handful whose authors judged explicitly that the content must not be visible once inside the server are marked Encrypted. Nothing declares the middle level at present, which is where the format side lands rather than something the level side asks for.

Drawing the boundary at HTTPS does not mean application layer encryption is pointless. HTTPS protects only the stretch where data travels over the network, and it is unwrapped the moment it reaches the server, so every stop that handles it after that sees plain text. A node gets compromised, logging and monitoring keep the content, something is intercepted and replayed across network segments or over a VPN: all of these are real in actual ERP deployments.

Drawing the line at Public buys one thing: a front end with no .NET runtime does not have to implement the whole encryption pipeline before it can reach its first method. The cost is that for calls at that level, value is visible once it is inside the server, and a method needing that layer has to be marked for it.

LocalOnly is not stricter encryption. It asks something else: this method does not arrive over the network at all. Not being on the same axis, it is not compared with the format for size, it is judged on its own. This check can sit ahead of decryption because both values it compares are available from the start: the format is a number on the API payload, and the level is attached to the type and fixed once compiled. Neither side requires opening value and looking inside. Platform engineers: Standardise your cache connection patterns. Always use a middleware to automatically generate deterministic cache keys for GET requests.

SREs on-call: Stop silent cascading failures. If your upstream provider goes down, a stale cache fallback prevents your app from crashing.

First-timers: Understand the app flow first. Don't fetch the same data twice. If a user asks for character data, fetch it once, store it in Redis, and serve subsequent requests instantly.

Our application is a Python Flask REST API. Its primary job is to aggregate data from the external Rick & Morty API (https://rickandmortyapi.com/api). Specifically, our business logic filters the data to return characters matching strict criteria: species=human, status=alive, and origin=earth.

If Redis crashes, our application must not crash. We wrap our cache connection in try/except blocks. If Redis is unreachable, we simply log a warning and fall back to fetching data directly from the upstream API.

Symptom: When a highly requested cache key expires (like a popular character or homepage data), 100 concurrent requests miss the cache at the exact same millisecond. They all hit the upstream API simultaneously, causing a rate limit block or an upstream crash.

Root Cause: Because the key is gone, all application threads independently decide they need to fetch and hydrate the cache.

Fix (Soft TTL / Background Refresh): Instead of waiting for a hard TTL expiration, implement a "Soft TTL". Store the data alongside an expiration timestamp. When a request comes in, check if the Soft TTL has passed. If it has, immediately return the stale data to the user, but spawn a background thread (using Celery or FastAPI BackgroundTasks) to fetch fresh data and update the cache. This ensures only one thread hits the upstream API, while users never experience the 300ms latency penalty.

If you cache everything endlessly, Redis will run out of memory (OOM). You must configure an eviction policy in your redis.conf. For an API cache, allkeys-lru (Least Recently Used) is usually the best choice. When Redis hits its memory limit, it will automatically delete the cache keys that haven't been accessed recently, making room for new data while keeping the most popular characters in memory.

This application is designed as a drop-in reference architecture. If you need a fully-functional, containerised application to test your local Kubernetes clusters, CI/CD pipelines, or logging stacks, you can use this repository as your baseline.

hflip is a left–right mirror. vflip is a top–bottom mirror. The frame size stays the same (here, 720×1280). That is not a rotate: transpose=1 would turn this clip into 1280×720. ffmpeg flip video 90 degrees shows up in US-English autocomplete next to the horizontal/vertical queries. A 90° rotate changes the axes. A flip does not.

The first command still writes a 720×1280 frame. transpose=1 would write 1280×720. On the raw RGB24 first frame, hflip is exactly the Python left–right mirror of the source — every row reversed, zero differing bytes. vflip is exactly the top–bottom mirror. If you wanted the clip upside down and backwards left-to-right, that is hflip,vflip, and it is byte-identical to rotate=PI (a 180° rotate). It is not a 90° rotate.

"ffmpeg flip video without re encoding" is a real autocomplete suggestion. This is what FFmpeg 9.0.1 does with it:

The process exits non-zero (234 here) and writes no file. A horizontal flip has to rewrite every macroblock; the original bitstream cannot be copied. There is no lossless -c:v copy path for hflip or vflip. If you only needed to tag a player to mirror at display time, that would be a different (and poorly supported) metadata trick — and it would not change the pixels, which is what a flip is supposed to do.

The video has to be re-encoded. The audio does not. With -c:a copy the AAC bitstream is identical to the source: MD5 5843435af71daac1e832f8b54fe25bdf on both the original and the flipped file, 131354 bytes. You keep the stadium crowd exactly as it was, and you do not pay a second AAC encode.

Flipping twice is also exact on the raw frame: hflip,hflip returns the original 720×1280 RGB24 first frame with zero differing bytes. That is a useful sanity check before you trust a longer filter chain.

Firecracker for Agent Code Execution Requires Building and Operating the Sandbox Service

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 16 • Originally published at effloow.com

We care about sandbox costs because agent execution turns small unit prices into a material infrastructure bill. A coding agent does not run one command. It installs dependencies, retries failed tests, holds a workspace between turns, and sometimes waits while another model reasons. At volume, the lifetime of those environments matters as much as their CPU consumption.

Firecracker provides a KVM-backed isolation primitive, but a self-hosted sandbox still requires an execution service, security controls, and infrastructure operations.

Our question was simple: can we replace a managed sandbox bill with infrastructure we control without accidentally becoming a sandbox-platform company?

Firecracker supplies an important part of that answer. On the KVM path we evaluate here, it runs a guest Linux kernel inside a lightweight virtual machine. Its deliberately small device model reduces exposed functionality compared with a general-purpose VMM. We get a hardware virtualization boundary rather than asking untrusted programs to share the host kernel directly.

We do not get a complete agent execution service.

We still need image builds, a guest execution agent, admission control, workspace handling, deadlines, output limits, network policy, cleanup, and an authenticated customer-facing API. Firecracker's host API configures machines; it does not provide an equivalent of sandbox.commands.run().

Our evidence boundary matters: we reviewed the implementation-facing material and external execution accounts, but did not complete a local KVM deployment for this article. Our review environment lacked the necessary KVM access, binaries, images, and privileged networking controls. The walkthrough below is a reproduction recipe, with explicitly simulated output—not a claim that we measured boot latency or validated production isolation.

That distinction changes our recommendation. We consider Firecracker a credible primitive for a self-hosted sandbox, not a deployment we can certify from a successful API response. Our starting references are the Firecracker project, its quickstart, and its production host guidance.

We separate the first experiment from the production design. The first experiment should prove that a trusted guest boots on the intended host. It should not accept customer code.

We require Linux with working /dev/kvm, a supported CPU architecture, and permission for the VMM identity to access KVM. A cloud instance being “a Linux VM” is insufficient: its provider and instance configuration must expose hardware virtualization.

Our initial inventory is deliberately mundane:

We would run the permission checks as the intended service identity, not only as root.

We install Firecracker and jailer from the same pinned release, using the upstream release archive for the host architecture. We verify the release artifacts before installation and retain their hashes. We do not use an unversioned download or assume that a URL ending in a binary name exists for every release.

Our build path would use a pinned Linux source tree and Firecracker's matching guest configuration as the kernel baseline. We would build the appropriate kernel target for the architecture, rather than assume one image format fits every host.

For the filesystem, we would construct a minimal distribution tree, install the interpreter and guest agent, add an init service, and package it into ext4. For example, once a trusted staging tree exists:

The 1 GiB file size is an example build choice, not a measured storage requirement. These commands package a tree; they do not magically create its init system or execution agent.

We would first boot upstream quickstart artifacts, then replace the kernel and filesystem separately. That keeps a broken image build from masquerading as a KVM problem.

Here is our proposed trusted-guest smoke test, which we have not executed or validated against a pinned Firecracker API specification. Before running it, we would check the endpoints, payloads, and lifecycle sequence against the selected release and supply verified, compatible guest artifacts. We intentionally leave networking out.

A successful start response does not prove that init completed. A read-only guest also needs appropriate writable temporary filesystems for its services. We would inspect the serial log and then require an actual command response from the guest agent.

Our production path would start with the jailer reference, matched to the pinned binary.

We need a unique instance ID, an explicit unprivileged UID/GID, a controlled chroot base, configured resource controls, and any required network namespace. Kernel, disk, and socket paths must make sense inside the jail. Host absolute paths from the diagnostic script cannot simply be copied into jailed API requests.

We would verify the resulting UID, namespace membership, cgroup placement, device access, and file ownership before admitting a job. Starting through jailer is a step in the security model, not proof that the whole model is correct.

Our environment lacked usable KVM and privileged host controls, so we could not validate boot, jailer behavior, or enforcement. We stopped there rather than replace those checks with a mock socket and call the result a sandbox test.

The remaining issues are concrete deployment risks we identified, not failures we personally reproduced.

We still have to build the guest agent. It must validate request lengths, manage subprocesses, separate stdout and stderr, enforce output caps, return exit status, and survive malformed requests.

We would run execution workers without guest-root privileges. We would also retain a host-side deadline that destroys the entire microVM. A compromised guest must not be able to negotiate away its termination.

Vsock can keep command transport separate from IP networking, but it does not make guest responses trustworthy. We would treat every returned byte as untrusted input.

We would audit this distinction first when testing resource controls.

A host cgroup's pids.max bounds host tasks, including VMM threads. It does not count every process running inside the guest. We need guest-side process controls as well.

We would not copy a “128 MB sandbox” setting into a 128 MB host memory ceiling and assume it works. Under load, we would inspect cgroup memory events, CPU throttling, host pressure, and guest failures together.

A network namespace is not an egress policy. NAT is not an authorization boundary.

Our first untrusted execution profile would have no virtual network interface. If package installation becomes necessary, we would add a separately tested network path with explicit policy for:

We would avoid the common quickstart-to-production shortcut of broad forwarding and masquerading rules. Those rules establish connectivity; they do not establish safe connectivity.

We have no local snapshot-restore measurement or verified restore sequence to publish. We would use the pinned release's snapshot documentation rather than improvise endpoints.

Our acceptance tests would cover artifact compatibility, backing disks, namespace and TAP reconstruction, stale connections, guest clocks, and readiness after restore. Snapshots containing credentials or customer state would be sensitive artifacts.

We would also test entropy and identity regeneration when cloning prepared state. We would not fork a memory image containing reusable secrets and assume each child becomes independent automatically.

A small VMM does not guarantee a fast application environment. General-purpose init services, device waits, SSH startup, and runtime imports can dominate the path to useful execution.

Our workaround would be a minimal, observable boot chain—not blanket removal of randomness-related services. We would measure where startup blocks before changing entropy handling or weakening guest configuration.

We cannot publish a raw Firecracker p50 or p95 from this review. We have no completed local run.

In our managed sandbox comparison, we use 717 ms create and 662 ms resume as E2B's command-executed readiness baseline. These timings describe the managed service, not raw Firecracker boot latency; our local KVM reproduction remained unperformed.

We would reject a comparison that puts a local InstanceStart acknowledgment beside that command-ready measurement.

We would benchmark four paths separately: fresh boot, snapshot restore, warm-pool allocation, and a subsequent command in an existing guest.

We would start the timer when we admit a request and stop it when we receive a nonce-bearing command result. We would report p50, p95, failures, and timeouts under increasing concurrency. Artifact preparation and queue delay would remain visible rather than disappear outside the timer.

A warm pool can reduce allocation delay, but it consumes capacity before requests arrive. It is not evidence of a faster cold boot.

The pros and cons of using cloud storage over local storage

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Cloud storage can be super convenient, but there are times when using local storage is the better move.

External hard drives and USB flash drives are usually easy ways to back up your files, especially since they don't require an internet connection or waiting for uploads. You just plug them into your computer, copy the files you need, and that's it. But cloud services like iCloud and Google Drive have also made it a lot easier to back up everything — and most of the time, it all happens automatically.

Your photos, videos, contacts and more are all synced to the cloud without having to copy each file manually. Plus, there's no risk of leaving your external drive somewhere or damaging it to the point where you lose your files. That doesn't mean cloud storage is perfect, and both have their pros and cons — it really comes down to choosing what works best for your needs. If you're unsure whether to choose local or cloud storage to back up your data, here's how the two stack up on access, cost and reliability.

One of the biggest advantages of using cloud storage is the convenience of not having to worry about your files. Once you upload them to the cloud, they're with you wherever you go. Just log in to your account on another laptop or phone, and you can access everything in just a few minutes. It's perfect for people who use multiple devices or who need to access files from their home computer at the office or college. Plus, most cloud services let you have your files synced automatically. That's the case with iCloud — once you enable it on your device, it automatically backs up everything every day.

Sharing files through a cloud service is also much easier than sharing them from local storage. Once a file is uploaded to the cloud, you can easily share it with someone with just a click  — cloud platforms generate a link so others can view and download that file. There's also collaboration, so multiple people can work on the same document or spreadsheet at the same time. Platforms like Google Docs even offer tools that allow each collaborator to leave comments in real time, perfect for workplaces and schools.

Safety is another big plus, since major cloud providers keep copies of your data spread across multiple servers and data centers. The chances of something bad happening and you losing all your data are very low – though not impossible. Plus, your cloud account can be protected with a password and two-factor authentication, meaning others can't just take your files from you.

Cloud storage relies on a stable internet connection, and you're prone to unexpected outages. A slow connection or an outage on your provider's servers could prevent you from downloading a file when you need it most. Also, uploading and downloading large files can take a while if you're using a slow connection. With an external SSD, you can copy gigabytes of files in just minutes. Internet has no impact on local storage, so it's something to consider if you're always traveling and rely on mobile data, for example.

Cost is another reason that might push people away from cloud services. While you only have to pay once to buy an external hard drive, cloud providers typically charge recurring subscription fees on a monthly or annual basis. Apple charges $9.99 per month in the US for 2TB of iCloud+, which comes to nearly $240 if you pay for two years — and the storage isn't really yours. Stop paying, and your files may be deleted after a while, not to mention possible price increases down the road. By comparison, you can find 2TB SSDs for around $290, but they'll always be yours, with no extra charges.

That doesn't mean you should ditch the cloud and rely entirely on local storage. Subscribing to a cloud service that allows you to back up all your files and access them from anywhere is very handy, and you can also benefit from local storage by offloading some of your larger files or those you really want to make sure you never lose.

No, the Chevy El Camino wasn't the first to combine a two-door car with an open bed truck. The first production vehicle to do that was a Ford, but possibly not the one you're thinking of. A design engineer named Lew Bandt, then with Ford of Australia, developed what's often considered the original factory-made ute — the 1934 Ford Model 40-A Light Delivery. Depending on exactly how you define a ute, some folks point to the 1927 Volvo OV4 as well.

Of course, it was the 1957 Ford Ranchero that truly inspired the El Camino. The Ranchero was built on the Blue Oval's full-size platform of the time, and it became both relatively popular and relatively profitable for the automaker. In fact, it was successful enough that Chevy was inspired to get in on the ute action with its full-size 1959 El Camino. Originally a short-lived model only in production for that year and 1960, the El Camino would get a second chance at life in 1964. That's when the Bowtie brand revived the name and brought it back in slightly downsized form on the same platform as the intermediate-class Chevrolet Chevelle.

This is where things start to get interesting for gearheads. In 1970, Chevrolet introduced a 454-cubic-inch LS6 V8 that was reserved for both the Chevelle and its platform mate. With that mill standing out as the most powerful unit of the year, the former became a muscle-car legend and one of Dom Toretto's rides in the epic 10-movie "Fast and Furious" saga. Meanwhile, the latter turned into perhaps the most sought-after El Camino of them all, the 1970 El Camino SS LS6 454. Keep in mind that this was a whole different engine than the LS6 in the 200-mph 2027 Chevrolet Corvette Stingray.

There are two key reasons that enthusiasts crave this machine. Right off the bat, the 1970 El Camino SS 454 was a one-year wonder — its exact powerplant was the aggressive LS6 V8 that would be dropped from the El Camino for 1971. True, Chevy sold a 1971 El Camino SS 454, but that mill was the less-powerful LS5. As far as actual production numbers for the 1970 model, those are hard to come by. Hemmings says 4,475 Chevrolets in total were sold with the LS6 for that year, but they were spread out among both Chevelles and El Caminos.

The second major selling point here is the outrageous driving experience offered by these rides, and for that, credit also goes to their incredible engine. Factory rated at 450 horsepower with a whopping 500 pound-feet of torque, this motor featured a cast-iron block and heads along with an 11.25:1 compression ratio. Also adding to the muscle are a four-bolt main block, a four-barrel Holley carburetor, and a high-performance camshaft. The outcome was that both the El Camino and Chevelle could serve up more horses than not just the 1970 Corvette, but all other production cars, too.

As further proof of just how hard people are jonesing for the 1970 El Camino SS LS6 454, an example may hold the record for the highest auction price for any El Camino — the car went for $275,000 at a Barrett-Jackson sale in 2023. Just remember auction values can change quickly: one 1970 Chevelle LS6 lost its owner a million dollars.

Chase Sapphire Reserve® (See rates and fees) This is a good lounge card, a good spending card, but it’s expensive and the coupons aren’t nearly as great as Amex Platinum.

American Express Platinum Card® (see rates and fees) The most expensive mainstream card with an $895 annual fee, but the credits they give you are worth several times the fee. Capital One Venture X Rewards Credit Card (See rates and fees) It’s the least expensive of the premium rewards cards, the value premium card. Why The Longest Single-Aisle Twinjet Ever Built Is An Aircraft Hard For Airlines To Get Rid Of

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

While not as rough as the more recent Airbus A330-800neo, the Boeing 757-300 variant was a disappointment for Boeing. Unfortunately, Boeing does not release figures for the incremental breakeven point needed to cover the variant's development costs, and it is unclear what sales Boeing envisioned when it commissioned the program in the 1990s. However, it is safe to say that it planned to sell a good many more than the 55 examples it did.

It is therefore somewhat paradoxical that one of Boeing's more disappointing variants has also become one of the most difficult to replace. Today, only two major passenger service commercial operators fly this type: United Airlines and Delta Air Lines. In recent years, the two giants have announced plans to replace their fleets of 757-200s and 767-300ERs, but not the 757-300s.

The 757 is a heavy narrowbody airliner with some characteristics typically associated with widebody airliners, such as a double-wheel bogie assembly. It was developed as a mid-sized airplane to bridge the gap between Boeing's narrowbody 737 series and the relatively small 767 widebody. It was produced in two basic variants (excluding freighter and special mission subvariants), the 757-200 and 757-300.

The original 757-200 version entered service with Eastern Air Lines in 1983 and had a full payload range of 3,860 nautical miles. The 757-200 was by far the more popular variant, accounting for 913 units sold, out of the family's total production run of 1,050 aircraft. The 757-300 was the stretched version of the 757 family and entered service with Condor in 1999, making it much newer. With a length of 178.7 feet (54.4 meters), it is notable for being the longest narrowbody airliner ever built.

The 757-300 replaced some older aircraft like the McDonnell Douglas DC-1, had a range of around 3,395 nautical miles, and was surprisingly unpopular. Only 55 units were ever ordered. Customers had the option of ordering the Rolls-Royce RB211-535E4B or the Pratt & Whitney PW2043 turbofans for the aircraft. The type is capable of seating around 234 to 243 passengers in a standard two-class layout and up to 295 in a high-density charter configuration. It also offers almost 2,400 cubic feet of cargo space.

As stated, orders for the stretched 757-300 were disappointing, with just 55 examples ultimately delivered to seven customers. Northwest Airlines had the largest order (16), followed by Condor (13), ATA Airlines (12), Continental Airlines (nine), Arkia (two), Thomas Cook Airlines (two), and Icelandair (one). All these operators (except for Arkia and Thomas Cook) also purchased the 757-200.

Boeing records show that Northwest was the only airline to opt for its 757-300s to be equipped with Pratt & Whitney engines; all the others came with Rolls-Royce engines. This means that of the 55 delivered 757-300s, Rolls-Royce accounted for 39, and Pratt powered the remaining 16. Northwest's aircraft were inherited by Delta Air Lines following the merger.

Data from Planespotters.net show all sixteen ex-Northwest aircraft remain in service with Delta. Meanwhile, Continental subsequently purchased around half of the world's supply of 757-300s on secondary markets, and United currently operates 21 of them. Ukraine's SkyLine Express operates five of them, and Fly Khiva (an Uzbek airline) operates two ex-Icelandair examples. Icelandair eventually took over the two Arkia 757-300s, but has since divested the type.

Boeing has been considering a 'New Midsize Airplane,' commonly known as NMA and the Boeing 797, but it has yet to come to fruition.

The 757 filled a specific niche: the gap between narrowbody and widebody aircraft. The issue was that while there was demand for such an aircraft, it was much smaller than demand for narrowbody 737s and A320neos or larger 767s (later 787s) and A330s. This was made worse after the September 11 attacks and the subsequent slump in air travel, with the resulting drop-off in new airline orders for the aircraft.

Boeing ended production of the 757 in 2004 without replacement. Airbus also didn't offer a mid-sized airplane to fill the gap. In the later 2010s, Boeing began to seriously consider a clean-sheet replacement to fill the gap. Such an aircraft would be able to both attract new orders and replace aging 757s. The peak of Boeing's customer consultations with over 50 airlines came in 2017 and 2018 with its New Midsized Airplane (NMA), or 797 concept.

But then came the 2018 and 2019 MAX crashes, which, together with COVID-19 and other issues, plunged Boeing into a prolonged crisis that it is still recovering from. Dennis Muilenburg (during the Q3 2019 Earnings Call) stated that the NMA remained "a serious project" under evaluation, though he acknowledged Boeing's immediate priority was safely returning the 737 MAX to service before committing capital to a new airplane program. But the NMA never happened; the MAX and 777X remained Boeing's priorities for its strained finances.

Now it appears the window of opportunity for Boeing's NMA has passed. Airbus developed the stretched A321neo and then developed the extended-range A321XLR. This aircraft is already filling much of the mid-sized airplane gap and is well on its way to replacing many of the world's aging 757-200 fleets while also opening up many new thin routes around the world. Widebodies, like the shorter 787-8, cost significantly more to operate per trip.

Making matters worse for any future NMA, JetZero, in partnership with Northrop Grumman, is powering on with its Z4 blended-wing-body aircraft. Should that aircraft actually enter service in the 2030s, it would be targeting the mid-sized market. It has secured investment from some of Boeing's most loyal customers, such as Alaska Airlines.

The A321XLR offers a dramatic improvement in fuel burn compared with the 757 family, but is better suited to replace the 757-200. It directly targets the thin transatlantic and medium-long-haul routes that the 757-200 served. But it is less suited to replacing the 757-300 as a high-capacity domestic/short-to-medium-haul stretcher. The other modern alternative, the delayed 737 MAX 10, also carried fewer passengers in comparable premium-heavy configurations.

There isn't a single airliner that can replicate all of the Boeing 757's capabilities, but perhaps the market doesn't require one.

Hypothetically, in a simplistic world where Airbus thought it could replace all 900+ 757-200s with a special A320 stretch, it could be worth it. This assumes no other customers or market growth. A modern high-end Boeing or Airbus clean-sheet airliner would likely need to sell 800 to 1,500+ aircraft just to break even over a 20- to 30-year production run.

A stretch is very different. As a rough guide, Airbus would likely need to only sell 150-400 incremental units to break even. This assumes no cannibalization of its other aircraft. In this overly-simple scenario, it makes sense for Airbus to develop a special variant (e.g., A321XLR) to replace the 757-200. But the 757-300 is very different. From the narrow point of view of replacing the 757-300, there is almost no market.

Only 55 examples were ever sold; that is not remotely enough for an airplane maker to recoup the costs of developing a stretch or special variant to replace it. In the real world, there would likely be considerable opportunity for growth beyond the remaining operators, but the basic point remains. This is a very small niche. Airbus has already been stung by disappointing programs like the A380 and A330-800neo, which almost certainly lost money. As stated, Boeing considered filling the much broader midsize segment, but ultimately, its core 737, 787, and 777X programs were much more pressing.

With Icelandair retiring its limited fleet of 757-300s, the conversation about replacing the aircraft is basically a conversation about United and Delta. That conversation is also related to the 757's twin, the widebody 767. The two aircraft were built to be as interchangeable as possible, and both United and Delta operate the world's largest 767 fleets. Replacing the 757, therefore, also concerns replacing the 767.

Besides filling specific niches that other aircraft can't and being heavily interchangeable with the 767, the 757-300 has another advantage. Being older, they are already paid off. This means the aircraft can partially compensate for higher maintenance expenses with 767 interchangeability and higher fuel consumption with lower capital/leasing costs.

United and Delta have repeatedly extended the 757-300's service life well into the late 2020s and the early 2030s. Both airlines are well into their efforts to replace their fleets of 757-200s (partially with A321XLRs). United's 757-300 fleet is now an average of 23 years old, while Delta's is closer to 24 years. 5 Brilliant Books To Read Before Kicking the Bucket

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Five stories that are bound to make a vivid impression

On principle, any list of “books you must read before you die” should be unending. Literary staples aside, the number of must-read books in existence is constantly expanding and evolving. Older books undergo revaluation, no-brainer entries are viewed through a fresh lens, and contemporary titles join an already massive library of must-reads.

Also, tastes are subjective. Not everyone might jive with Great Expectations or Les Misérables despite their objective artistic merit. The definition of “must-read” has drastically changed over time. Keeping this in mind, Polygon has chosen five brilliant, indisputable books to read before kicking the bucket. Some are obvious picks, while others, not so much.

Fyodor Dostoevsky’s body of work is often classified as essential reads for good reason, and The Brothers Karamazov is undoubtedly one such entry. The author’s final novel was written under dire personal circumstances, which included failing health and profound emotional grief. The novel is deeply philosophical in nature, raising pertinent questions about faith, morality, and skepticism. From a certain perspective, it's also about human evil, and how it manifests as mundane emotions like greed or hedonism.

The novel opens with wealthy landowner Fyodor Karamazov neglecting all three of his sons — Dmitri, Ivan, and Alyosha. Dostoevsky goes into intricate detail to flesh out the characters in this dysfunctional household, which is caught up in an inheritance dispute and subsequent scandal. After a murder takes place, The Brothers Karamazov turns into a tense courtroom drama. Each Karamazov brother represents a human archetype, which Dostoevsky uses to debate the nature of free will. While this book isn’t as relentless and claustrophobic as Crime and Punishment, it is sweeping and dramatic in its exploration of character motivations and human behavior.

A milestone in African literature, Chinua Achebe’s Things Fall Apart is about Okonkwo, an influential leader of the fictional Igbo clan. Okonkwo is an accomplished warrior and champion who vows to be nothing like his alcoholic, flute-playing father. After he takes a young boy named Ikemefuna as his ward, the oracle of his village declares the child must be killed. This incident haunts Okonkwo, who goes on to oppose rampant colonialism and reclaim the lost dignity of his people. Achebe presents an essential viewpoint, as most colonial literature portrayed Igbo culture in biased and problematic ways.

Things Fall Apart provides rich complexity to the tribes portrayed, granting an authentic look into local customs, laws, and traditions. Okonkwo is a deeply flawed but heroic protagonist who tries to hold onto tradition in the light of aggressive socio-political change. Few novels dissect colonialism like Achebe’s debut story, which explains how colonizers quietly infiltrate local cultures and turn close-knit communities against one another. This leads to a fractured sense of shared history and heritage that requires an enormous amount of time to acclimatize to.

Arundhati Roy’s most famous work, The God of Small Things, is an unflinching look at vulnerability in the face of rigid societal rules. The novel follows fraternal twins Estha and Rahel, who grow up amid the arrival of their British cousin in India and intense familial tensions. Roy uses a disjointed narrative to tell this moving tale about social discrimination, colonialism, and local politics that inevitably affect the personal lives of our protagonists. The prose is vivid and sensory, filtered through the perspective of children who view the world in a more tactile way than adults do.

The God of Small Things pits massive historical upheavals against unforgettable personal events, including the fleeting beauty of growing up and the wonder of young love. While focusing on these tender moments, Roy also critiques casteist social practices that prevent the romantic union between a divorced woman and a carpenter. The brutality of British rule goes hand in hand with these quiet tragedies, which Roy explores by weaving in and out of time to showcase how our memories work. It is a haunting book that radically reinvents language to convey the hyperspecific feeling of losing childhood innocence in a postcolonial world.

The book that established the dark academia subgenre, Donna Tartt’s The Secret History is a riveting campus novel that everybody should experience. Set in an elite liberal arts college in Vermont, the book follows Richard Papen, who joins an exclusive Classics and Greek class in a bid to fit in. The other members of the class are all wealthy and hand-picked by the professor, and revel in their social isolation, which they consider proof of their intellectual superiority. After one of them is murdered, The Secret History turns into an inverted detective story that dwells on Richard’s perspective of the other students, and the effect that this death has on everybody.

Tartt doesn’t spring this death on the reader. Instead, the book opens with the declaration of the murder. The tension lies in slowly unraveling why this happened, and how the others are responsible for such a ghastly event. Richard is also an unreliable narrator, being a self-confessed liar and someone who blatantly alters objective truth due to his biases. Tartt uses layered, evocative prose to explore morality via the lens of Greek ideals, penning a cautionary tale against the romanticization of intellectual elitism. The bubble that surrounds these students bursts, pushing them towards real-world culpability they’re not equipped to handle.

Humans share a complicated relationship with their sense of smell, detailed vividly in Patrick Süskind’s Perfume: The Story of a Murderer. The story follows Jean-Baptiste Grenouille, an orphan in 18th-century France who is born with an exceptional nose that can differentiate between subtle scents around him. While this gift initially pulls Grenouille towards becoming a perfumer, things take a horrific turn after he meets a young girl with a scent that’s impossible to forget. What starts off as intense fascination turns into a morbid obsession with human scent, which he hunts in order to make the most exquisite perfume.

Perfume paints Grenouille as a fascinating antagonist with no scent of his own. Süskind uses his journey as a metaphor for human morality and desire, and how easy it is to be manipulated by factors like profound alienation. A person with no empathetic roots in the world will treat humans like fodder, as Grenouille has never been loved as a child and never grew to discover the sentiment. This is a chilling portrait of a monster whose story ends in a bizarre, haunting way that will leave you stunned.

Those details matter because they place the blockchain component within a recognisable debt-market transaction. Investors still care about the issuer, maturity, yield, settlement rules and custody. A digital token does not erase credit risk or replace the financial obligations underlying a security. It is a different technical representation and transfer mechanism for an instrument that remains subject to conventional legal and market questions.

National Bank Governor Timur Suleimenov outlined an ambition to work with the country's Finance Ministry on tokenised sovereign bonds, with the first quarter of 2027 described as the likely timing. Central-bank notes and Finance Ministry bonds are not interchangeable: they have different issuers and may serve different investor or monetary-policy purposes. The future deal should therefore be assessed on its own terms.

The Kazakhstan Stock Exchange has said its digital-asset platform used Solana, with BitGo providing custody infrastructure, for the note initiative. It would be premature to conclude that those same providers are definitively selected for the government-bond transaction. Technical participation, permissions and investor eligibility need to be confirmed when official issuance documentation is available.

Supporters of tokenisation point to shorter settlement cycles, clearer ownership records and the possibility of integrating securities with programmable workflows. Those benefits are plausible, but whether they materialise depends on legal recognition, interoperability with payment systems and the behaviour of market participants. A blockchain that records a bond is only one component of a working securities market.

For Kazakhstan, the experiment also sits alongside wider work on digital assets. The next useful documents will set out who can buy the bonds, how transfers are restricted, which custodians are responsible for assets and how payments are settled. They should also explain whether investors can hold instruments outside a single controlled platform and how disputes would be resolved. Those are the details that distinguish a market-ready security from a demonstration.

The same questions apply to other tokenised securities projects integrating shareholder rights. Kazakhstan has moved beyond slides and into a completed note placement. The proposed government-bond issuance will be more convincing if it brings the same clarity about the legal and operational substance behind the token.

What causes a sudden drop in phone battery percentage?

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An aging battery or a recent software update could be the reasons behind your phone's sudden battery drops.

Few things are more frustrating than noticing that your phone's battery is draining faster than usual. It gets to a point where it can no longer last a full day away from the charger. Under normal conditions, battery drain depends on what you're doing on your phone. Lighter uses like browsing the web and listening to music use less power, while taking lots of photos and playing games tend to drain your battery faster. But when your phone's battery suddenly drops without heavy use, something might be wrong.

At the same time, that doesn't mean your phone's battery is dying. Software can also cause your battery to drain faster, like when you install an iOS or Android update, or when an app is constantly running in the background. If you've been noticing that your phone's battery is draining faster, here's what you can try to fix it.

Your phone doesn't know exactly how much charge is left in the battery. In fact, it estimates that number based on the battery's voltage and current, and sometimes that estimate is far off from reality. Eventually, the software recalculates it, and that's when you see the battery percentage suddenly drop by a significant amount instead of gradually. This happens more often with batteries in poor condition. As lithium-ion batteries age chemically, they no longer retain their full charge capacity and may also fail when the phone needs a power boost to perform a demanding task, like opening the camera or using GPS. As a result, your phone may think the battery is much lower than it actually is.

Smartphones have mechanisms to prevent this from happening. On iPhones, Apple has implemented a system that detects when the battery fails to reduce performance in order to avoid these spikes in power consumption. Temperature also has a major impact, as most phones are rated to operate at temperatures between 0° and 35° C (32° to 95° F). Using your phone at temperatures above that range can significantly reduce battery life. When your phone gets too hot, the battery will most likely drain much faster. It's best to stop using it until it returns to a normal temperature. Don't put it in a refrigerator, though, as the introduction of cold air can create condensation within the warmer phone.

Also, it's perfectly normal to see your phone's battery drain a little faster for a few hours or even days after an update. Both iOS and Android run a series of optimizations to put all the files in their proper places, and this increases battery consumption. The good news is that this is temporary, and everything goes back to normal once the indexing process is complete. A buggy app or a process constantly running in the background, especially if it uses your location, will also drain your phone's battery quickly.

Start by closing apps running in the background to see if your battery usage returns to normal. It's also worth taking a look at your privacy settings and limiting access to your location to only when you have an app open. This prevents your phone from constantly triggering the GPS. That alone should have a significant impact on reducing battery consumption. Also, check your phone's battery settings to see exactly which apps are draining your battery the most — both iOS and Android provide details on this.

Pixel 6 and later models have a Battery Diagnostics tool under Settings. On a Samsung Galaxy, open Settings, then Battery and tap View Details. For iPhone users, it's as simple as going to Settings and opening the Battery menu. These menus usually include details about your phone's battery health. Apple, for example, says that the latest iPhones are designed to retain 80 percent of their original battery capacity through up to 1,000 charge cycles. If your phone's battery health is below 80 percent, you should probably replace it with a new one — which, at least, is much cheaper than buying a new phone.

Priority Pass is the world’s largest independent network of airport lounges, with over 1,900 airport lounges and experiences around the world.

Think of Priority Pass as almost being like a lounge access broker. Priority Pass doesn’t operate lounges itself, but rather the business model is to sell lounge memberships to travelers (either directly or through credit cards), and then in turn, pay lounges for admitting members.

It goes without saying that not all airport lounges participate in Priority Pass. For example, in the United States, no American Admirals Club, Delta Sky Club®, or United Club, participates in Priority Pass. That’s because they’d view joining Priority Pass as cannibalizing their own business of selling lounge memberships, getting people to pursue elite status, etc.
