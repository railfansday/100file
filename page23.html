25 Years Later, Gandalf's Wisest Lord Of The Rings Quote Has No Equal In Fantasy Movie History

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

25 years later, Gandalf the Grey's words of wisdom in The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring still hold true. If Samwise Gamgee is the beating heart of the Fellowship, and Frodo Baggins its soul, then Gandalf is its mind, the leader who understands that knowledge is just as great a power as any magical ability he possesses, and perhaps even more so.

There are a lot of great Gandalf lines from throughout the three Lord of the Rings movies (and, of course, J.R.R. Tolkien's books long before them), which help to make him one of the most beloved characters. "A wizard is never late..." is iconic, and his lines in the scene with the Balrog - "You shall not pass!" and "Fly, you fools," - have been endlessly quoted ever since The Fellowship of the Ring hit theaters in 2001.

But as memorable as they are, there is one that stands out from the rest as being the greatest words of wisdom imparted by the wizard, which not only shapes the entire narrative, but applies directly to all of our own lives, too.

The Gandalf quote in question comes when Frodo laments the heavy burden the One Ring has placed upon him, wishing it had not come to him, and that none of it had happened. To this, the grey wizard says:

"So do all who live to see such times, but that is not for them to decide. All we have to decide is what to do with the time that is given to us."

The line works on multiple levels. To Frodo, it is exactly what he needs to hear at that moment, even if it's not necessarily what he might want to hear. Gandalf offers him a sense of comfort, in that the Hobbit is not alone in his thoughts, validating his wishes. But at the same time, he doesn't pander to him and tell him that it's going to be easy, but does state his own sense of agency in these events.

This speaks to the Fellowship at large, and Middle-earth as a whole. It's a perfect way of distilling down the ideas of free will vs. what's already divined that are at play in The Lord of the Rings, which we also get a sense of in the same scene, as Gandalf tells Frodo there are other forces at play, and that: "Bilbo was meant to find the ring. In which case, you also were meant to have it."

That juxtaposition of those quotes might suggest a conflict between free will and fate or luck in this story, but that's not actually the case. We do know that fate exists in Middle-earth, via the creator of Arda, Erū Ilúvatar, but they also granted people free will. And we see time and again throughout the story that, while fate and luck play their hands, it's the choices people make that ultimately define the story.

This is clear with all the Fellowship of the Ring members, each choosing to follow Frodo ("my sword... my bow... my ax..."), and all being tested at various turns, and the outcomes are entirely dependent on which path they choose. Fate may have played a part in Frodo getting the ring, but it was, as Gandalf says, deciding what to do, such as Sam being by his side, or Frodo not killing Gollum, that all led to the One Ring being destroyed.

All of this is enough to make Gandalf's quote one of the most powerful in fantasy history, but it's just how relevant and relatable it is — whether it's 1954, 2001, or 2026 — that makes it even better. Because what Gandalf is really getting at is a core tenet of humanity and how we live our lives. None of us could choose when we were born, nor the surrounding circumstances, but all we can do is decide what to do with the time given to us. It's a simple, beautiful message that continues to resonate far beyond The Lord of the Rings.

That Gandalf line alone is incredible, but what's just as impressive is that we get another bit of profound wisdom in the same scene. When Frodo talks about wishing Bilbo had killed Gollum when he had the chance, the wizard responds:

"Many that live deserve death, and some that die deserve life. Can you give it to them, Frodo? Do not be too eager to deal out death in judgment. Even the very wise cannot see all ends."

Again, it's a quite simple yet elegant and fitting way of boiling down something that is so fundamentally human. It's easy to condone, but Gandalf here teaches an important lesson about humility, morality, and judgment that also endures, and connects perfectly to his line about time. After all, it is Frodo remembering these words, and sparing Gollum's life, that becomes central to the whole story, and thus free will and fate entwine together, as highlighted by Gandalf's classic The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of The Ring quotes.

Believe It Or Not, Mr. Ripley Gets Meta in 'A Talent for Murder' Origin | Review

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

You're likely much more familiar with Tom Ripley — the sociopathic social climber who murdered his way across high society in a series of novels, films and shows including 1999's The Talented Mr. Ripley and Netflix's recent Ripley — than you are with his creator, cantankerous crime novelist Patricia Highsmith. But in the new meta-thriller A Talent for Murder, director Anton Corbijn and screenwriter Joanna Murray-Smith make a bet that the author herself is just as fascinating and shocking as her most famous character. And they might be right — at least, when Helen Mirren is playing the aggressive, provocative and famously bigoted Highsmith, in a swaggering performance that lets the typically elegant actress cut loose.

Mirren's Highsmith is the heart of a story that's one-half eccentric literary biopic, one-half pulpy mystery: what if Highsmith, in her final days, was approached by a mysterious young editor begging her for one final book? The tale that unspools from there never quite becomes the sleek cat-and-mouse thriller it would like to be. But it is a lot of fun, and a risky third act twist transforms the film from a typical mystery into a commentary about the nature of life as an artist — one that will leave viewers either elated or enraged.

In early 1995, 74-year-old Patricia Highsmith (Mirren) is living in solitude at her isolated Swiss country home, indulging in her favorite pastimes: guzzling booze, reminiscing on her professional triumphs, terrorizing the townsfolk with her bad temper, and enjoying the company of her large collection of knives, poisons, and guns. Enter Edward Ridgeway (Alden Ehrenreich), a young book editor who has been sent all the way from New York City with a single mission: get Highsmith to sign the contract for a sixth Tom Ripley novel.

Ridgeway is a Highsmith fanatic, and knows that getting her back to work would make his career. But he's also not quite comfortable with the situation he's been dropped into — or the mysterious fate of a previous editor, who had also been sent to extract a new book from Highsmith, but soon vanished off the face of the earth. Without an easy way back home, he tries to make his case. Of course, the irascible author tries to send him packing, using colorful language and some threats to his life. But even as the two clash, Highsmith is soon seduced by the idea that there's still demand for her work, that a final book could cement her literary immortality, and that Ridgeway might be the one to pull her out of the writer's block that has swallowed her final years.

From there, the story of Highsmith and Ridgeway's relationship weaves in and out of the fictional tale they spin, as they begin work together on her next Ripley novel. In scenes illustrating their story ideas, Ripley (also played by Ehrenreich) is determined to marry a young American named Clementine (Olivia Cooke) — an heiress who happens to share a name, face, and life story with an actual young woman Ridgeway met on his way to Switzerland. As real and imagined storylines get increasingly tangled, it becomes difficult to tell what's true and what's just a story. What is Ridgeway's real interest in Clementine? What is Ridgeway's real interest in Highsmith, for that matter? Who's actually manipulating who? And what's so damned important about Tom Ripley?

A Talent for Murder's greatest shock isn't any of the crimes that occur on screen — it's watching Mirren, an actor known for maintaining an air of poise even in her more villainous roles, play a character who is rude, sloppy and purposely indelicate. Sporting a head of barely-brushed hair and a confrontational sneer, Highsmith is delighted by her own crassness, whether she's holding forth on the mating habits of snails, speculating on Ridgeway's own sexual prowess, or just casually fondling some items from her firearm collection. Highsmith is a nightmare, but an engrossing one — and through monologues that reveal more than she intends about her past, we learn exactly how she developed such a rough shell, and how those difficult experiences also led her to create her charming criminal mastermind. Mirren is clearly having a ball playing a complete asshole, which makes her antics a joy to watch — and reveals that even at 81, there are still sides we have yet to see of the actress.

Ehrenreich doesn't undergo quite as big a transformation, but viewers who are only familiar with him from his time as Han Solo will be shocked by his turn as the nebbishy Ridgeway, who swings between playing the innocent and revealing hints of a more threatening core. While he isn't always compelling on his own, he and Mirren fight beautifully; his seemingly inexpert attempts to manipulate her and her over-the-top diatribes about him bring to mind a platonic version of Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf? Their blackly comedic attempts to take each other down allow Murray-Smith to slip in grand statements about art, creativity, and the nature of good and evil in a way that's genuinely entertaining — you'll be so tickled watching Mirren suggest that she poisoned Ehrenreich's eggs, you won't even notice when the conversation pivots to the value of art or the greater meaning of a painful childhood.

Ehrenreich's scenes as Ripley, which mostly feature narration from Highsmith and Ridgeway in place of dialogue, see the actor take the killer to a lighter, campier place than Matt Damon, Andrew Scott, or others who have tackled the role; he glories in the character's external sleaze but skips the inner torment, a decision that makes these sections pure fun. This is especially true when he plays off Cooke, who makes her character such a wide-eyed cartoon of naïveté that you don't really feel much sorrow over her constant misfortune; instead, you just get caught up in the enjoyable nastiness of Ripley's scheming.

However, as A Talent for Murder enters its final act, you may begin to wonder why exactly you're watching these two plot threads play out. It's lovely to be out there getting up to no good in the snow-covered Alps or sunny Roman piazzas (which are captured in luscious cinematography by Robbie Ryan), but what is all this plotting and stalking and mysterious phone call-making adding up to?? A few moments of confusion towards the film's end culminate in an audacious climax that viewers will either love or hate — a reaction that honestly feels much more in line with Highsmith's legacy as a person and writer than any traditional mystery movie denouement. The film's decision to make a big statement on the nature of art and artists feels slightly out of left field when it arrives, but it's also far more meaningful than any possible whodunnit reveal. You'll likely be thinking about it days later — though whether that's because you loved it or because you want your money back is impossible to predict.

A Talent for Murder began as a stage play (also written by Murray-Smith), a pedigree that's fairly obvious in the way it plays out — there are rarely more than two characters on screen at any given time, and they are prone to occasionally making grand philosophical declarations in the way only theater characters seem to.

Its beginnings as a play are also clear in the way the film values character over plot. Though some of the twists and turns don't quite work out, the emotional battle between the protagonists always feels right. Highsmith and Ridgeway's fights are funny, frightening, and strange, but more than anything, they reveal that the things we scorn and the things we need are often more similar than we'd like to admit. Of course, noting that there's a thin line between love and hate isn't exactly a revolutionary idea. But I can almost guarantee, you've never seen anyone illustrate it the way A Talent for Murder does.

An unconventional biopic that isn't afraid to go to strange places.

Apple Just Stockpiled $10 Billion of Parts While Warning Memory Prices Are 'Unprecedented.' Should Investors Worry?

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Apple's annual report shows it loaded up on components as memory costs soar. Here's what that means for its margins, and for the stock.

Apple (AAPL -2.08%) just revealed to investors that it's buying parts as fast as it can, and it is paying up big to do it.

The company's annual report, filed Tuesday, shows components inventory of $10.4 billion as of Sept. 26, up from $2.1 billion a year earlier. Meanwhile, finished goods, meaning iPhones and Macs sitting in warehouses, barely moved, rising to $4.1 billion from $3.6 billion. In other words, nearly all of the buildup in inventories is parts.

The filing came a day after John Ternus, on his first earnings call as CEO, said "What we're seeing with memory pricing right now is unprecedented" and warned that costs will climb further. Put the two together, and you get a clear picture of how the world's most valuable consumer company is handling a memory shortage.

The parts pile isn't the only sign. Apple had $89.7 billion in manufacturing purchase obligations at fiscal year-end, with $82.7 billion due within 12 months. A year earlier, that total was $56.2 billion. Ternus said those obligations rose by $33 billion in the September quarter alone.

Building that stockpile cost real cash. Inventory soaked up $8.9 billion of operating cash flow in fiscal 2026, after adding $1.4 billion the year before. Of course, Apple can afford it: The tech giant still generated $145.1 billion in cash from operations, and it spent $85.8 billion buying back its own stock.

The 10-K is blunt about why. Apple says it is facing supply constraints and rising costs for advanced chips, memory, and storage, and it "expects these trends to intensify for memory and storage components." It also warns that price increases "may not effectively mitigate these negative impacts, and may also reduce demand for the Company's products."

The cost is already hitting margins. Apple's gross margin was 47.9% in the September quarter, but that included about one percentage point from tariff refunds. Without those refunds, CFO Kevin Parekh said, margin fell about a point from the June quarter, "driven entirely by memory impacts."

The December quarter looks tighter. Parekh guided gross margin to 45%-46%, mainly due to higher memory costs and some spending to roll out Siri AI. Ternus added that memory prices should keep rising beyond December, while the cushion from cheaper parts Apple bought earlier shrinks.

Apple has already passed some of that on to customers. Parekh said the company "reluctantly" raised prices on iPad and Mac around June and on iPhone in September, and it's too early to say how buyers will respond.

Memory is also limiting what Apple can sell. Chief Operating Officer Sabih Khan said Mac and iPad supply in the December quarter will be held back mainly by memory constraints, as demand has run ahead of expectations.

Despite the frenzy around memory, there's one big bright spot for Apple: Product demand is certainly not a problem. Revenue rose 12% to a record $114.5 billion for the September quarter, and iPhone sales jumped 17% to $57.6 billion. Meanwhile, services grew 10% to $31.6 billion. For the full year, revenue climbed 15% to $478.8 billion and net income reached $132.2 billion.

Apple also expects December-quarter revenue to grow 7% to 10%, which Parekh said would make it the biggest revenue quarter in company history.

And the stockpile cuts both ways. When memory is scarce, the company that can commit tens of billions of dollars to suppliers in advance gets served first. Smaller phone and PC makers don't have Apple's balance sheet, so the same shortage that trims Apple's margin could cost them sales outright.

The memory squeeze is also a signal about who's winning right now. When Apple, one of the largest buyers of memory chips in the world, says prices are "unprecedented," that's good news for the semiconductor companies selling them, including Micron Technology (MU +2.32%).

For Apple shareholders, an important number to track is gross margin. If it stays near the 45% to 46% range and revenue keeps growing, the memory hit is a speed bump. If price increases start to dent iPhone demand, as Apple's own filing warns they could, that's a bigger problem.

So, is Apple stock a buy? The company is choosing to protect supply and sales over a point or two of margin, and it has the cash to do it while still paying a $0.27 quarterly dividend and buying back stock. For long-term investors, that looks like the right trade. Just don't expect margins to look as clean as they did last year until memory prices cool down.

Apple Stock Fell After a Record iPhone Quarter. Here’s the Margin Problem Investors Shouldn’t Ignore.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Investors are looking past iPhone growth to how much profit Apple keeps on each device, and rising memory costs are pushing that down.

Apple (AAPL -2.08%) just delivered the biggest September quarter in its history, with revenue jumping 12% to $114.5 billion. Its iPhone business performed even better, and earnings per share topped Wall Street's expectations.

So why did Apple stock fall about 2% on Tuesday?

The answer has less to do with how many iPhones Apple is selling and more to do with how much money it's making on each one. Rising memory chip prices are putting pressure on Apple's profit margins, and management doesn't expect relief anytime soon.

I'm still bullish on Apple, but investors shouldn't dismiss this problem.

Apple's iPhone revenue jumped 17.5% year over year to $57.6 billion, beating Wall Street's estimate of $56.5 billion. That's an outstanding result for a product line that already generates more than $200 billion in annual sales.

Not everything went Apple's way. Its iPad revenue declined 10%, while wearables revenue fell 8%. Management attributed those declines partly to difficult comparisons with last year's product launches and to supply constraints affecting the iPad.

But the iPhone remains Apple's biggest business, and its performance suggests consumers are still willing to pay a premium for the company's flagship products. The problem is that Apple is paying more to make them.

Apple reported a gross margin of 47.9% for its fiscal fourth quarter of 2026 (ending Sept. 26), although that figure benefited from roughly 1 percentage point of tariff refunds. Without those refunds, its gross margin would have been approximately 46.9%.

For the December quarter, management expects gross margins between 45% and 46%.

That might not sound like much of a difference. But every percentage point of gross margin on $114.5 billion in revenue represents roughly $1.1 billion in gross profit. And Apple expects its December quarter to be even bigger.

The culprit compressing margins is memory chips, which are becoming increasingly expensive as demand for artificial intelligence infrastructure puts pressure on supply. CEO John Ternus described memory pricing as "unprecedented," while CFO Kevan Parekh said rising memory costs accounted for the entire underlying gross margin decline between the June and September quarters.

Apple has already raised prices on its iPhones, Macs, and iPads, but it's also benefiting from memory purchased earlier at lower prices. As those inventories are depleted, higher costs will increasingly affect profitability.

And there's another complication. Apple stock trades at roughly 35 times expected earnings, leaving less room for disappointing results.

I don't think rising memory prices fundamentally change Apple's long-term investment thesis.

For starters, Apple's services business carries a gross margin of approximately 76%, making it substantially more profitable than hardware sales. That business provides an important cushion against rising manufacturing costs, although management expects services revenue growth to slow to between 8% and 9% in the December quarter.

Apple also has the financial resources and purchasing power to secure memory supplies. Its manufacturing purchase obligations increased by $33 billion during the quarter, reflecting efforts to lock in future production.

That doesn't eliminate the margin problem, and Apple's Siri AI rollout will introduce additional costs. But memory prices are cyclical, and Apple has weathered component shortages and rising manufacturing expenses before. The bigger question is how much of those costs Apple can pass along to customers without hurting demand.

When Apple reports its Q1 2027 results, I'll be watching whether gross margins land within management's 45% to 46% forecast. I'll also be paying attention to services growth and whether supply constraints affecting Macs and iPads begin to ease.

Apple's iPhone business is performing exceptionally well, and I don't see any reason to abandon my bullish outlook over one disappointing margin forecast.

But at 35 times expected earnings, Apple needs more than strong sales. It needs to demonstrate that it can keep those sales profitable, even as the cost of producing its products increases.

Astronomers found the first star that would look green to the human eye

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Astronomers have identified the first known green star, a white dwarf tinted by sodium from planetary debris, alongside unusually colored stars.

A dead star called WD J1330+6435 is the first known star that would appear green to human eyes. Astronomers at the University of Warwick identified it while working out the true colors of nearby white dwarfs.

The star’s green tint comes from sodium in rocky material it has pulled in from its own planetary system. That sodium absorbs the orange part of the star’s light.

The same work flagged WD J1011+2845, just 48 light-years from Earth, as what may be the reddest star ever recorded.

White dwarfs are the dense remnants left behind when Sun-like stars reach the end of their lives. None is close enough for its color to be seen directly.

The researchers worked from each star’s spectrum, the pattern produced when starlight is split apart by wavelength, and ran those spectra through a model of human color vision.

The sample covered the 151 white dwarfs within 20 parsecs, or about 65 light-years, of Earth.

The results correct a common misconception in astronomy that white dwarfs are typically blue. Hotter, bluer white dwarfs are brighter, so surveys based on brightness alone over-represent them.

Dr. Mark Hollands, a Warwick Prize Fellow in the Astronomy and Astrophysics Group at the university, led the work.

“Most stars, including most white dwarfs, behave like ‘perfect radiators,’ so their colour is set almost entirely by temperature,” Hollands said.

“It turns out these incredibly dense stars can produce colours nobody expected, and some of them genuinely surprised me,” he added.

WD J1330+6435 has a temperature of roughly 7,200°F (4,000°C). It has accreted rocky material from its planetary system, enriching its atmosphere with calcium, lithium and sodium.

The atmospheres of these stars can reach roughly the density of water, millions of times denser than the Sun’s visible surface, the university explained.

Because this star’s atmosphere is so dense, the sodium absorbs light across a broad range of yellow, orange and red wavelengths. What remains gives the star a chartreuse green appearance in the team’s models.

As far as the researchers are aware, no other star, living or dead, has ever been credibly reported as green. That makes WD J1330+6435 the first known genuinely green star in the Galaxy.

“Imagine standing on a planet and looking up at a green star,” Hollands noted. “It feels like something out of science fiction.”

White dwarfs that pull in the remains of planets carry elements such as lithium, sodium and calcium in their atmospheres, where astronomers can read them in starlight.

In 2021, Hollands and his colleagues reported lithium in four cool, old white dwarfs with cooling ages of five to ten billion years. WD J1330+6435, catalogued then as SDSS J1330+6435, was one of the four.

That work placed the star about 87 parsecs, or roughly 280 light-years, from Earth.

The amounts of lithium and potassium compared with sodium and calcium “strongly suggest that all four white dwarfs have accreted fragments of planetary crusts,” the authors wrote in Nature Astronomy.

Another star in that set, WD J2317+1830, also showed extra infrared light, a sign that it was still drawing material from a surrounding disk of dust and rock.

In the new color study, white dwarfs enriched with fragments of shattered asteroids and comets tend toward yellow. Carbon-rich white dwarfs are typically pink or purple.

In WD J0902+2010, the convection zone has reached the core and lifted interior carbon to the surface. There, carbon forms C2 molecules that absorb blue, green and yellow light, so the star would look pink to the human eye.

WD J1011+2845 goes further. Carbon molecules distort its light so heavily that almost all of it is squeezed into wavelengths beyond 600 nanometers, producing a deep salmon-pink glow.

Cooler white dwarfs with mixed hydrogen and helium atmospheres often take on a cyan or pale blue tinge. That color comes from hydrogen molecules colliding with helium atoms in their ultra-dense atmospheres.

Sirius B, the nearest white dwarf to the Solar System, has a hot hydrogen atmosphere that gives it a bluish color in the models.

Of the 151 nearby white dwarfs, five showed unusual coloring: two yellow and three pink. That suggests roughly one in 30 white dwarfs in the Universe shines with an unusually colored light.

For the remaining 146 stars, the median color temperature was about 6,800 kelvin, roughly 11,800°F (6,530°C), which people perceive as white.

Professor Boris Gänsicke of the Department of Physics at Warwick co-authored the study.

“One in thirty might not sound like a huge fraction, but across the entire population of white dwarfs in the Universe, that is still a lot of oddly coloured stars out there,” Gänsicke said.

“Yet if we take the median across a representative population of white dwarfs, we found that the average white dwarf really is white,” he explained.

The study was published in the journal RAS Techniques and Instruments.

However, despite another solid earnings report, the stock hasn't generated much excitement. The reason has less to do with the company's real estate portfolio and more to do with how quickly it can grow earnings per share.

Realty Income just keeps getting it done. The REIT grew its adjusted funds from operations (AFFO) by 3.7% in the third quarter to $1.12 per share. The company remains on pace to invest $10 billion this year. That supports its guidance to deliver $4.45-$4.46 per share of AFFO this year, a $0.01-per-share increase from its prior range. That's a more than 4% increase from last year despite headwinds from higher interest rates.

Despite its solid quarterly results, Realty Income's share price isn't soaring. That's likely due to three factors.

Realty Income's growth remains modest. AFFO per share grew just 3.7% despite a 10.3% increase in total revenue. Meanwhile, it only increased its full-year guidance by $0.01 per share at both ends of the range. Due to its large scale and net-lease focus, the REIT isn't growing as quickly as others in the industry.

It also continues to face some headwinds from interest rates. Higher rates increase interest expenses when refinancing existing debt and also drive up borrowing costs for new investments. That's a headwind for AFFO per share growth. Higher rates also weigh on the value of commercial real estate. Realty Income's share price has fallen almost 15% from its peak earlier this year, coinciding with the recent spike in the 10-year Treasury rate.

Given its size, Realty Income requires significant external capital to fund its investment program. Even highly attractive acquisitions can have a modest effect on AFFO per share when accounting for financing costs.

Realty Income isn't going to grow fast, which is why we shouldn't expect its stock to surge, even with a solid earnings report.

The thing with Realty Income is that investors don't need rapid growth to earn an attractive return. At its recent share price of around $57.50, the REIT offers a 5.6% yield at its current monthly dividend rate. It would only need to grow its AFFO per share by around a 4% annual rate to produce an average annualized total return approaching double digits. Meanwhile, there's the potential for additional upside. Falling interest rates and incremental earnings from its expanding private capital platform could drive faster AFFO per share growth in the future. That could also drive some multiple expansion.

Realty Income doesn't need explosive earnings growth to deliver solid returns for investors. With a dividend yield around 5.6% and the potential to grow AFFO per share by more than 4% annually, the REIT could generate double-digit annualized total returns. Those returns likely won't come in one big post-earnings move but should arrive steadily as it continues to grow its earnings and pay its monthly dividend.

The route covers the entire Brahmaputra within India and should take 22 to 25 days in all. The river begins in Tibet and continues beyond India into Bangladesh, covering 2,900km in all.

This won’t be Bordoloi’s first time down the river. In 2020, he and two friends took a similar bamboo raft down a 400km section of the Brahmaputra. Earlier, in 2015, Bordoloi was one of 11 who tackled another part of the river over 11 days under the auspices of the Assam Mountaineering Association. This expedition, however, will test his endurance, pitting him against river conditions for longer, and with less support, than ever before.

Bordoloi on a previous raft expedition on the Brahmaputra. This raft took Bordoloi and friends from the world’s largest river island, Umananda, to its smallest, Majuli. Photo: Shekhar Bordoloi/Instagram

Bordoloi’s raft was hand-built from bamboo poles carefully lashed together over several days. Such craft are a traditional way of navigating rivers across the region, and are still used by local communities for fishing and transportation.

Though the river can be temperamental, Bordoloi’s first two days have been relatively calm, taking him down the Lohit, one of the Brahmaputra’s many tributaries and channels. Navigating the complex river system alone will be a challenge. The Brahmaputra is one of the largest river systems in India, the ninth-largest in the world by discharge, and is prone to flooding and powerful currents.

The logistical challenges of a long expedition will further complicate progress. On previous expeditions, Bordoloi traveled with minimal supplies or no food at all, relying entirely on foraging and knowledge of the natural landscape. One of the most prolific unresolved true-crime stories of all time revolved around the infamous Zodiac Killer. In David Fincher’s crime drama Zodiac, the manhunt is put into the spotlight in dramatic fashion. The film follows an obsessive, decades-long manhunt for the notorious Zodiac Killer, who terrorized the San Francisco Bay Area during the late 1960s and 1970s. Political cartoonist Robert Graysmith (Jake Gyllenhaal) becomes obsessed with the killer's cryptic puzzles, and the fixation strains his personal life. Alongside journalists and police investigators, he tirelessly chases elusive clues and dead ends, trying to unmask a killer who always stays one step ahead. A masterpiece thriller, Zodiac rejects traditional Hollywood crime formulas in favor of a chilling, hyper-realistic study of obsession.

Unlike standard serial killer movies that wrap up with a neat capture or shootout, Zodiac embraces the frustrating, unresolved reality of a cold case. The lack of a tidy resolution mirrors real life and leaves a lingering, unsettled feeling. And yet James Vanderbilt writes it as if history will be made and the serial killer will be caught. Fincher builds tension through procedural grind, quiet institutional exhaustion, and the suffocating realization that the truth may never be caught. With standout performances from Gyllenhaal, Mark Ruffalo, and Robert Downey Jr., Zodiac remains a stroke of cinematic genius. ​​​​​​​

The film that officially put Christopher Nolan on the map as a visionary to watch was his brilliantly risky Memento. The film follows Leonard Shelby (Guy Pearce), a man suffering from anterograde amnesia, which prevents him from forming new memories, as he attempts to find and kill his wife's murderer. To track his clues, he relies on a complex system of Polaroid photographs, handwritten notes, and permanent tattoos on his body. The film's unique, non-linear structure unfolds in reverse, forcing the audience to experience the exact same confusion and disorientation as the main character. Memento became a defining thriller because of its groundbreaking reverse-chronology structure, deep psychological immersion, and subversion of the traditional revenge formula.

The film's brilliance comes from Nolan’s narrative mechanic. Nolan splits the film into two distinct timelines: in color, every time a new scene starts, you are thrown into the middle of a moment without knowing what came before; in black and white, the story progresses chronologically until the two timelines meet at the film's conclusion. In doing this, the audience gets to play sleuth alongside Leonard. It’s brilliantly intended to be confusing and frustrating, rewarding viewers during subsequent watches. On the surface, it looks like a gritty neo-noir hunt for a wife's killer. Underneath, it exposes a darker truth: Leonard may be actively engineering his own delusions. Because his tools for truth can also preserve lies and manipulation, the film chillingly asks if the systems we use to find the truth actually lead us away from it. Memento reshaped the genre by proving nonlinear narratives would be the future. ​​​​​​​

Perhaps Joel and Ethan Coen’s magnum opus, No Country for Old Men miraculously turned Cormac McCarthy’s novel into a slow-burning neo-Western crime thriller that instilled instant dread. Instead of getting rid of your old AirPods, replace the battery and give them a new purpose.

In case your old AirPods are truly dead and you don't want to spend money on them, Apple will recycle them for free. Scientists uncover a troubling pattern beneath Earth’s frozen ground

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A global study of 156 monitoring sites reveals widespread permafrost thaw, raising concerns about infrastructure, carbon emissions, and climate change.

Every summer for the past three decades, Anna Klene has driven north across Alaska’s Brooks Range, and pushed a metal rod into the ground.

She records the depth at which it hits frozen earth. Then she moves on to the next site, and the next. It’s not glamorous science.

“It’s not super sophisticated,” she said. “You push down on the probe, put your finger on the rod and you can measure how many centimeters into the ground it went.”

These simple, long-term measurements have now produced something significant: the first global assessment of long-term permafrost monitoring data.

The findings confirm what climate scientists have feared. Permafrost is thawing broadly, persistently, and in ways that matter far beyond the frozen ground itself.

The measurement Klene takes is called active layer thickness. It refers to the depth of the ground above permafrost that freezes and thaws each year.

In a stable system, that layer stays roughly constant from one decade to the next.

When it deepens over time, it can signal that the underlying permafrost is warming and becoming less stable.

Permafrost is not simply frozen dirt. It’s soil, rock, and sediment bound together by ice that has remained frozen for at least two consecutive years. In many places, that happened for thousands of years.

Permafrost underlies 15 percent of exposed land surface in the Northern Hemisphere and almost all of Antarctica’s ice-free areas.

And it holds something that makes its stability a matter of global consequence. Roughly 70 percent of the organic carbon stored in permafrost soils sits in just the top three meters.

As the ground thaws, microbes can break down previously frozen organic matter, releasing carbon dioxide and methane into the atmosphere.

The study draws on data from 156 monitoring sites gathered between 2000 and 2024. The average record length per site is 20 years.

The pattern is consistent and troubling. Active layer thickness increased significantly at 55 percent of Arctic sites and 38 percent of Antarctic sites.

In mountain permafrost regions over 90 percent of sites showed significant increases. Several sites saw the active layer double in thickness over the study period.

At two Norwegian mountain sites, Juvvasshøe and Snøheim, the active layer increased by 8.45 centimeters per year. At Swiss Alpine sites, increases of 9 to 36 centimeters per year were recorded.

In the Arctic lowlands, sites with thick organic layers, including peatlands and densely vegetated areas, showed smaller increases.

That happens because the insulating properties of organic matter buffer the soil against air temperature swings.

It is the bare, sandy, and bedrock sites that are changing fastest. Alaska’s North Slope recorded active layer increases at most sites between 0.3 and 0.6 centimeters per year.

Sites previously impacted by wildfires showed larger increases, because fire removes the insulating vegetation layer, exposing frozen ground directly to summer warmth.

“One of Earth’s most important indicators of climate change is permafrost as it directly reacts to rising temperatures,” said lead author Dmitry Streletskiy, a geography professor at George Washington University.

“The significance of this study lies in the fact that we’re not just looking at isolated hotspots anymore.”

The regression analysis behind the study found that summer warmth is the dominant driver of active layer change in Arctic lowland permafrost.

Total liquid precipitation has a smaller but significant additional effect: wet summers deepen the thaw more than dry ones. Cold seasons, by contrast, have minimal influence once site-specific characteristics are controlled for.

Mountain permafrost behaves differently and is harder to model. The sites are highly heterogeneous and the available climate data at high elevations isn’t fine-grained enough to fully explain everything.

Some mountain sites, particularly in the Swiss Alps, are changing at rates that dwarf anything seen in the Arctic lowlands.

The Qinghai-Tibet Plateau in China deserves particular attention.

Active layer changes on the Third Pole – so called because it holds more ice than anywhere on Earth outside the polar regions – averaged 3.2 centimeters per year from 2000 to 2024.

That’s roughly four times the Arctic average for continuous permafrost.

The plateau is warming faster than almost anywhere else on the planet, and it sits beneath infrastructure that crosses some of the most challenging terrain in the world.

Permafrost degradation is not an abstract threat.

Roads, railways, airports, pipelines, and buildings across northern Alaska, Canada, Siberia, and the Tibetan Plateau are built on the assumption that the ground beneath them stays frozen.

When it doesn’t, foundations shift, roads buckle, and infrastructure built over decades requires expensive repair or abandonment.

Communities in northern Alaska have already been forced to consider relocation as coastlines destabilize and the ground under their homes subsides.

“It’s hard to see that the first frost and the first snow are shifting by a day or two a decade,” Klene said.

“It’s hard to see that kind of change and really appreciate that it will have a big impact over time. But with these long-term monitoring sites, we can see that this gradual change is happening, and it’s going to affect the whole system.”

That system includes the carbon cycle. Continued warming threatens to shrink the extent of permafrost worldwide, with consequences that could unfold for generations.

What that means in terms of carbon release remains one of the largest uncertainties in climate science.

The study is published in the journal Communications: Earth and Environment.

We sent half a picture with the radar map, and you have to fly through this line of thunderstorms, and the routes are closed because of outside. The people with a 1-hour delay, I think the numbers were negative 60 NPS on the normal way of sending it, plus 60 when we send a picture of the map. A 120-point difference.”

His case for United rests on technology: better tools for managing travel, clearer communication during disruptions, and connectivity that makes time onboard more useful. For example, he describes sending passengers radar maps to explain why distant thunderstorms were delaying flights even though the weather at their airport looked fine. His point is that passengers respond much better when they understand what is happening.

Thus, Kirby is acknowledging that competitors outperform it in some traditional measures of onboard quality, but arguing that United’s technological advantages outweigh those shortcomings.

I think he is right that these things matter. I am less convinced that they settle the question.

Start with the most obvious consideration: an airline is not particularly useful if it cannot conveniently take you where you need to go. I love trying new products and will sometimes take an absurd routing to do so. Most travelers understandably do not approach their trips that way.

A nonstop flight at a convenient time can be more valuable than a better seat reached through a connection. Frequency matters too. An airline with several flights a day gives you more options, particularly when something goes wrong.

This is a substantial part of United’s appeal. Its network gives me reasons to fly it that have nothing to do with whether another airline serves better Champagne.

But the best network for me may be largely irrelevant to someone living elsewhere. A traveler in Taipei and a traveler in Chicago are starting with very different choices. There is no universal route map that works best for everyone.

Emirates is a good example of why an airline’s reputation may need to be separated from the particular experience you are buying.

Mention Emirates and many people think of luxury (massive first class suites, showers, and bars onboard with beautiful flight attendants serving you extensive food and beverage offerings). That “halo effect” reputation is understandable.

But someone booking business class on an older Boeing 777 may encounter a product that falls well short of that image. The retrofitted 777s improve the situation with a 1-2-1 layout and direct aisle access, but I would not put that seat ahead of the leading business class products elsewhere.

Singapore Airlines illustrates this as well. Its reputation for service, built in part around the Singapore Girl branding, is extraordinary. Yet some travelers strongly dislike its longhaul business class seats, particularly their sleeping position.

A beautifully presented meal and gracious service cannot entirely compensate for a bed in which you struggle to get comfortable. Singapore’s new cabin reveal on Friday, November 6, will be particularly interesting for that reason.

My recent Emirates 777 first class flight demonstrated the reverse problem: a superb suite paired with service that did not live up to it. I loved the space, but the experience still fell short.

So what about Kirby’s argument that technology is most important?

We sometimes talk about technology as if it is separate from the passenger experience. But an app that lets you solve a problem without standing in line is providing service. A clear explanation of a delay is providing service. Working internet can determine whether a long daytime flight is productive or frustrating.

US airlines deserve credit here. Their digital tools can be excellent, and United’s app is absolutely the best in the business, no question about it. But even the Alaska, American, and Delta apps are–comparatively speaking–much better than their non-US peers.

To be clear, that does not mean every American airline leads every foreign airline or that an attractive app excuses indifferent employees. It does mean that evaluating service solely by how someone pours your wine misses a large part of what an airline does for its customers.

Connectivity is especially important to me. Extra legroom and a better meal are welcome, but painfully slow or non-functioning Wi-Fi can materially change how I feel about a flight. For someone hoping to disconnect and sleep, it may barely register. The features can be measured. The importance each passenger assigns to them cannot be universal.

I also recognize the limitations of my own perspective. Much of my coverage focuses on premium cabins, and those experiences influence my preferences. But what does the airline offer someone in economy?

Some Asian carriers offer a more appealing combination of legroom, meals, and attentive service than their US counterparts. That deserves weight, even if the business class cabin lacks the latest suite.

At the same time, the aircraft still matters. So do the fare restrictions, baggage allowance, and cost of choosing a seat. A generous meal does not tell us whether the ticket represents good value.

For a family paying for several tickets, the best airline may be the one offering a tolerable nonstop journey at an affordable total price. For a tall passenger, a little more legroom may matter far more than the entertainment selection.

An airline cannot claim superiority for everyone based on what it provides a small number of passengers at the front of the aircraft.

A great airline should operate reliably and treat passengers well when it fails.

Those are related, but separate, expectations. I want to arrive on time. When that is impossible, I want an honest explanation and practical assistance.

No airline can prevent every weather delay or mechanical problem. But how it prepares, communicates, and recovers tells me much more than a polished promotional video.

American Airlines is a useful example from my own flying. I would argue that some of my recent meals on American have been the best among the US network carriers. Its free Wi-Fi has also tends to work very well for me. Those are real strengths.

Nevertheless, my experiences with inconsistent service and operational problems place American well below Delta and United in my overall assessment. That is not a claim that every American flight is bad or every Delta flight is good. It is the cumulative effect of repeated experiences. A carrier earns my confidence over time, and it can lose it the same way.

The loyalty program belongs in this discussion too.

Award availability, redemption prices, and the usefulness of elite benefits all affect how much value I receive from an airline. A good program can persuade me to choose one carrier when the alternatives are otherwise similar.

But I try to separate the airline I enjoy most from the program in which I find the most value.

An excellent redemption does not make an uncomfortable seat comfortable. Status does not make a canceled flight depart. Conversely, a wonderful airline can have a loyalty program that gives me little reason to engage with it. Ideally, the program reinforces a relationship the airline has already earned through its service.

Qatar Airways and STARLUX are among my favorites because I have found their overall experiences so compelling. The appeal extends beyond a seat or a meal: I look forward to flying them because of the reliable Wi-Fi, excellent service, and quality in everything.

Air France has also come such a long way. Its newer business class seat is excellent, and the addition of Starlink on equipped aircraft addresses something that matters greatly to me. Combined with the food and service I enjoy, that makes it an increasingly attractive choice.

Yet I still have many reasons to fly United. Its network and technology have practical value in my life that an exceptional flight on another carrier cannot always replace.

That is why my favorite airline and the airline I choose for a particular trip need not be the same…maybe the point is that talking about which carrier is “best” is ultimately futile and our focus instead should be rewarding carriers who do things well and not patronizing those that continually disappoint.

Kirby’s claim is worth discussing because he recognizes that being the best involves more than having the fanciest cabin. Technology and network utility deserve more credit than they sometimes receive.

But I would not crown United the best airline in the world on that basis alone. The seat still matters. So does the person serving you, the reliability of the operation, and what happens when your plans unravel.

For me, the best airlines are the ones that bring those elements together consistently, but at the same time I realize how arbitrary my own evaluation can be. Thus, I tend to think asking “who is best” is the wrong question versus asking “who does X,Y, and Z well?”

Running a large American company means steering it through market upheaval while keeping its finances growing. The strain of that job commands lavish rewards, and those sums raise hard questions about fairness and about who captures the gains of corporate success. In 2025, the median chief executive among the 100 best-paid at U.S. public companies collected $39.4 million. No earlier year in the long-running analysis produced a higher figure, and the median climbed 35.8% from a year earlier. Anyone who owns shares, draws a paycheck from one of these firms, or wonders where corporate money flows has a stake in how boards arrive at those numbers.

The distance between the corner office and the rest of the workforce stretched at a similar clip. The median ratio of chief executive earnings to those of a typical employee reached 475 to 1 in 2025, up from 348 to 1 the year before, a 36.5% jump. The median worker at these employers took home $101,322, a solid income that looks modest beside packages worth tens of millions of dollars. Stock awards drive the surge in executive totals. Base salaries held flat at $1.3 million, while long-term equity accounts for more than 70% of total compensation in the usual package. The research credits movements in stock value with an outsized effect on what an executive receives. A share-price rally can reshape a leader's haul by a margin no salary adjustment could match.

Equilar has partnered with The New York Times for 19 years to track what chief executives earn, and its 2026 study lists the 100 richest pay deals that boards granted to CEOs at U.S.-based or listed companies. A 3,200-Pound NASA Observatory Is Crashing Down to Earth

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A 3,200-Pound NASA Observatory Is Crashing Down to Earth

NASA’s longtime Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory is expected to re-enter Earth’s atmosphere tomorrow, November 4. While most of the telescope will burn up as it careens through the atmosphere, NASA says it expects some components to survive and potentially even cause harm, although that outcome is exceedingly unlikely.

The Swift Observatory launched in 2004 to explore gamma-ray bursts throughout the Universe. These are the Universe’s most powerful explosions and second only to the Big Bang in terms of the most energetic and brightest phenomena known to science.

Swift’s initial prime mission was scheduled to last just a couple of years, but its scientific operations ultimately lasted much longer. The Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory has witnessed many incredible things over its lifetime, observing the Universe across a wide range of wavelengths.

“Swift has broadened our understanding of a wide variety of cosmic objects and events, from active galaxies powered by supermassive black holes and stars undergoing X-ray flares, to supernova explosions and comets and asteroids in our own solar system,” NASA says.

Just a few years ago, Swift captured the most powerful gamma-ray burst ever observed, which released a staggering 18 teraelectronvolts of energy. It was so powerful that even though the event was 2.4 billion light-years away, it shook Earth’s outer atmosphere.

Even earlier, in 2013, scientists built a 160-megapixel map of ultraviolet light in nearby galaxies using data from Swift. 13 years later, it remains beautiful.

Right now, NASA expects the 3,200-pound Swift Observatory to re-enter Earth’s atmosphere around 3:30 AM EST on November 4. However, there’s an 11-hour uncertainty, so that reentry could happen later today.

Re-entry locations of any components that survive the re-entry burn could land anywhere between 20.6 degrees south latitude and 20.6 degrees north latitude. NASA says the risk of harm coming to anyone on Earth from Swift’s re-entry is about 1 in 2,100.

While this risk is very low, NASA tried to avoid it altogether. NASA launched a $30 million rescue effort this past summer, but it ultimately failed.

Even after this failed attempt, Swift actually continued to do science. The team restarted observations and observed hundreds more targets, including remnants of stellar explosions and supermassive black holes. However, on October 20, Swift entered safe mode and prepared for the end.

Just a couple of weeks before that, NASA released a four-photo set of some of Swift’s last images. NASA’s Chandra X-ray Observatory and the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory teamed up to create these final photos, all seen below.

“NASA’s Chandra X-ray Observatory is one of the many telescopes that frequently teamed up with Swift to make important discoveries about the cosmos. Chandra and Swift have complementary abilities,” NASA’s Chandra X-ray Observatory explains. “While Swift can quickly capture rapidly-changing events in space in ultraviolet, X-ray, and gamma-ray light, Chandra is able to gather more detailed data with its sharp X-ray vision. When combined, these two telescopes helped provide a more complete picture of events and objects in space than either could alone.”

AI-generated 'slopaganda' in political ads puts internet platforms on alert

📅 2026-11-03  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The midterm election cycle has been dominated by "slopaganda," or campaign ads featuring AI-generated clips and images, creating another challenge for Instagram, YouTube and X as they attempt to monitor their platforms.

The term slopaganda dates back to early last year, when a group of AI experts used it in a paper to describe a new phenomenon combining generative AI slop with propaganda and misinformation.

There's no comprehensive federal law governing the use or disclosure of AI in political ads. And while there are some 30 state laws with different requirements about AI use and disclosure, candidates don't always abide by them. On the leading internet sites run by Meta, Google and Elon Musk's SpaceX, users run into AI-generated ads and AI-generated posts shared by politicians, alongside authentic videos and clips.

More than $187 million has been spent on nearly 300 ads generated or enhanced by AI, according to an analysis released last week by the Wesleyan Media Project. That's almost certainly an undercount, the organization says, because so many ads don't contain disclaimers. Over 90% of the spending came from Republican candidates, a Republican party committee or a Republican-leaning group, the report found, and most don't include disclosures.

CNBC found a number of AI-generated ads running online without disclosures, including a fake video of New York Democratic Governor Kathy Hochul that was posted by her opponent, Republican challenger Bruce Blakeman.

In Texas, Republican senatorial candidate Ken Paxton created an AI-generated video showing Democratic opponent James Talarico making controversial statements, with a three-second disclosure flashing in the middle. It's being shared as organic content on YouTube and X by Paxton's campaign.

The Blakeman and Paxton campaigns didn't immediately respond to requests for comment.

YouTube told CNBC that the company applies "prominent labels to realistic AI content."

"For organic content on YouTube, political candidates can request removal where their face appears to be altered or generated by AI," the company said. "For advertisers, we require disclosure when an election ad contains synthetic or digitally altered people or events to provide viewers with transparency."

YouTube parent Google also requires political ads to complete its election ads verification process and include "paid for by" disclosures.

X says advertisers must comply with applicable laws, putting the responsibility on the advertiser, not the platform. The company didn't respond to CNBC's request for comment.

Meta referred to its guidelines on political ads, including restrictions on their use in the week prior to the election. The company also mandates "paid for by" labels and maintains an ad library of political ads. In June, the company ramped up its labeling, adding a new "about this ad" tool for ads that Meta detects have been created with third-party AI tools.

Last month, Meta was ordered to pay $25 billion in penalties by a New Mexico court as part of a data privacy case that stemmed from the company's Cambridge Analytica scandal from nearly a decade ago. Cambridge Analytica, which was a political consulting firm, allegedly accessed the information of 87 million Facebook users in improper fashion.

One risk the tech platforms face with generative AI is being asked to create misleading images and videos. The Brennan Center for Justice tested ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, Claude, Perplexity and DeepSeek for their willingness to facilitate deceptive content. The study from August found that many models may avoid generating content around familiar conspiracies, but can generally be manipulated into creating misleading material.

A retest of those platforms in October found that ChatGPT had made progress. It had started redirecting prompts for harmful election images into illustrations so they look like comics, or refusing to take action on them altogether.

A number of national politicians are trying to address the issue.

The Protect Elections from Deceptive AI Act, backed by senators from both parties, would prohibit knowingly distributing materially deceptive AI-generated video or audio about federal candidates. Other bills are being led by Democrats in Congress, including the AI Ads Act, which is looking to expand current bans on campaign misrepresentation to include generative AI content.

None of the proposed bills have advanced out of committee.

The Dallas Cowboys collapsed in embarrassing fashion in Week 8. Despite leading 17-3 in the third quarter, Dallas blew Sunday's game against the Arizona Cardinals after the team benched its starting quarterback for Gardner Minshew.

It was a failure on all ends for Dallas. The team's defense suddenly couldn't get a stop despite a backup quarterback entering the game, and its offense couldn't put enough points on the board to put the Cardinals away for good.

When a team fails in such a spectacular way, the focus tends to shift to the head coach. Brian Schottenheimer wasn't a big-name hire when the Cowboys chose him to succeed Mike McCarthy. Instead, the team went with who they knew, as Schottenheimer previously served as the Cowboys' offensive coordinator.

That decision hasn't paid off just yet. While the Cowboys remain a strong offense, Schottenheimer is just 10-14-1 in a season-and-a-half at the helm. That record, combined with Sunday's result, have some wondering whether the Cowboys should have made a more splashy hire following the 2024 season.

Doing so would have cost money, something team owner Jerry Jones has not been willing to do at head coach. Jones was asked about that strategy during his weekly radio appearance Tuesday. He defended the practice, saying expensive coaches don't necessarily produce wins, per Jon Machota of The Athletic.

"There is zero correlation (in the NFL) between how much you pay a coach or how many coaches you have as to winning. There is no correlation. And that's a random sample of years and all 32 teams involved. So put that in your hopper and smoke it."

Jones is, seemingly, true to his word. Schottenheimer doesn't rank among the top-20 coaches in salary for the 2026 season, per Front Office Sports.

Whether that's a smart strategy remains to be seen. Schottenheimer has yet to prove himself as a strong hire, and his team's collapse in Week 8 doesn't speak highly of his ability keep a team in line in crunch time.

Jones is probably correct that having the right coach is far more important than having the most expensive coach. Unfortunately for Jones, it's his responsibility to make that decision.
