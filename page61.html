Marathon’s Big PvE Update Is Starting to Look a Lot Like Destiny

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Symbiosis looks to add some familiar features to Bungie’s extraction shooter

Since Marathon was released this March, developer Bungie has spent the majority of the year updating and tweaking the extraction shooter to make it more welcoming to a wider audience. This is largely part of a push to evolve the game after the studio revealed it hadn't met initial expectations. Following the Nightfall refresh in October, the game is set to change even more with the upcoming Symbiosis update on Dec. 8. Bungie has just shared a ton of new details and some of the additions seem very familiar.

While we already knew the broad strokes of the Symbiosis update, Bungie has given more insight into what players can expect in a post on the official PlayStation Blog. Perhaps the most interesting introduction is Expedition, a new PvE-only mode with roguelite elements. Players will be able to take on this new mode solo or with a crew and face environmental challenges without needing to worry about other players gunning them down mid-run. Bungie calls this phase one of its PvE efforts, so expect more in future updates. A Team Deathmatch mode will be available in beta as part of the latest update, pitting two crews of three against each other in bite-sized matches that won't risk your permanent loadouts.

Bungie also detailed Limbo, the new social space for Marathon. This will be an area where players can run around, test weapons and gear, and generally just hang out with each other without combat. Limbo sounds a lot like Destiny's social spaces (such as The Tower), which served as hubs where players could interact with one another. Adding this to Marathon brings the extraction shooter much more in line with Bungie's FPS MMO-like title. The introduction of more varied game modes beyond the initial extraction mode from the game's launch only adds to the comparison.

Players will also be able to test out the new Bishop Runner shell when Symbiosis launches. The latest shell is built around fast movement and blade-wielding melee combat. The introductory map Perimeter is getting a major overhaul in Symbiosis as well. The underground Burrows and submerged Reservoir will be new playable spaces on the map. Additionally, Runners will encounter the Tick Queen as a new fight.

To celebrate the release of Symbiosis, Bungie announced that it will be holding an Open Play Week from Dec. 8–15. During this time, you'll be able to download and play the game on PlayStation 5 or PC, though you will need a PS Plus account on PS5 to access multiplayer. It seems like Bungie wants to make Marathon a much more palatable game that caters to different types of players who may have been turned off by the PvPvE gameplay. With the chance to test out Marathon's new update for free, now might be the best time to dive into the extraction shooter.

Standing in the glass dock at Southwark Crown Court in London, awaiting his sentence for one rape and three sexual assaults to be handed down, Russell Brand was addressed directly by the judge. “No doubt your companions thought it was outrageous laddish behaviour that only you could get away with,” Justice Bennathan said, referencing an incident in which Brand had launched himself on an unknown woman in the middle of a studio corridor as his entourage looked on. “It was outrageous and possibly laddish, but also a criminal sexual offence. Brand may well have gotten away with it had it not been for a joint investigation by Channel 4 documentary strand “Dispatches” and The Sunday Times of London. Their reporting, published in Sept. 2023, contained numerous serious allegations, including that the former comedian had sexually assaulted four women between 2006 and 2013. Brand denied any wrongdoing (none of the women who made the allegations in the documentary were involved in the recent criminal trial although it is understood the Met Police are now investigating at least one of those complaints and Brand may face yet more charges).

The former comedian, who’d appeared in Hollywood movies and had a short-lived marriage to Katy Perry at the height of her success, had long cultivated an air of degeneracy as part of his persona, enabling him to operate in plain sight. Yet it wasn’t until the Channel 4/Sunday Times investigation was published that police opened an investigation. Whether it’s because the reporting had prompted more women to come forward – as Brand’s lawyer in the trial, Ian Winter, repeatedly argued — or because the media spotlight gave the authorities the resolution they needed to investigate a celebrity will probably never be known.

But Brand is not the only celebrity to have found themselves in the dock after being accused of wrongdoing in the media. In 2023 news anchor Huw Edwards, once the best-known and highest paid journalist at the BBC, was the subject of an exposé by The Sun, who revealed he had been in an inappropriate text-based relationship with a teenager during which he’d paid for nude photographs.

Although he had not acted unlawfully in that instance, Edwards was arrested less than a year later and eventually convicted of making indecent images after police found videos of children as young as seven being abused on Edwards’ phone. It emerged he had been sent them by a pedophile to whom he’d, apparently unrelatedly, gifted numerous instalments of cash.

Kevin Spacey has also faced an endless series of legal battles – both criminal and civil on both sides of the Atlantic – after he was accused of sexually assaulting a 14-year-old boy in a 2017 Buzzfeed interview. None have resulted in any finding of liability or criminality and Spacey has always denied any allegations of sexual misconduct but his career has never recovered. In the U.K. Spacey was, like Brand, tried at Southwark Crown Court, where he was on trial for nine charges of sexual assault against four separate men. He was acquitted on all counts. After the trial, some of the jurors asked to meet the Oscar-winner and shake his hand.

So it’s unsurprising that David Walliams, a comedian, bestselling children’s author and former “Britain’s Got Talent” judge, sought an injunction last month to prevent the same newspaper from publishing an investigation into his alleged sexual misconduct. Lawyers for Walliams told London’s High Court he would be likely to commit suicide if the story was published. The judge held that suicidal ideation is not a ground on which to prevent the publication of an article potentially in the public interest, although he noted his decision to dismiss the injunction does not mean the allegations are true.

Published today, the Guardian’s report accuses Walliams of grooming young fans and harassment. One woman told the newspaper she was 15 when she alleges Walliams drove her to his house and engaged in behavior that she now considers to be sexual assault. Walliams has denied all the allegations. There is no police investigation into Walliams nor any suggestion he may face charges, but, like Spacey, his career now lies in ruins. (Walliams has been dropped by his agent and his books will no longer be published).

Brand’s celebrity loomed large throughout his trial: some of his victims were fans who were initially thrilled to meet him, others were vulnerable because of his disproportionate power over them. In his sentencing remarks, Justice Bennathan accepted the former comedian’s fame was “an intrinsic part of each offence” as it allowed him to carry out his crimes. But, he noted, “I also need to be careful I am not punishing you more because you are famous.”

Coddled by their managers, obeyed by their assistants and adored by their fans, some celebrities have been able to abuse their exalted status. But when an intrepid journalist turns that spotlight to expose alleged misconduct, whether criminal or otherwise, fame can also be their undoing.

‘We Have to Take Over’: Far-Right Extremists Want to Seize Control of the GOP

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

On Election Day last week, white nationalist Nick Fuentes urged his supporters on X to “get out and vote Democrat today.” The post racked up over 12 million views. Hours later, in the wake of the Democratic sweep, Fuentes took credit for the victory. “We did it again” he wrote on Telegram, later calling the phenomenon “the Groyper Effect,” a reference to the nickname given to his followers.

In recent days, Fuentes has claimed the election results are an opportunity for him and his allies on the far-right.

“Let the Democrats rule, see how that goes,” Fuentes said during a livestream last week. “There will come a time when the far left will terrorize people, and they're going to push all of them into the arms of the right. Who's going to fight the far left? Only the far-right. This is why we have to take over the right wing now.”

In another livestream last week, Fuentes added a warning to the GOP leadership: “It’s our party now … You need to cooperate with us… Groypers are open for business. It has [to] be America First—and if it's not, we'll do it again for the enemy.”

A WIRED review of comments from white supremacist, Christian nationalist, and other far-right groups in the wake of the midterm results shows much of the same. While the groups are uniformly attacking Trump’s failure to prioritize white, Christian American values, they also believe that this sudden shift to the left will result in a backlash that will see more people embrace far-right groups and ideologies.

“Many prominent figures in racist extremist movements have taken to declaring the MAGA movement dead since midterm elections, claiming that the results validate their previous criticisms of the Trump administration and opens new opportunities for their movements to push disaffected voters toward their ideological beliefs,” Jared Holt, senior researcher at Open Measures, tells WIRED.

While some of the Republican party’s extremist candidates were rejected during the elections last week, many did win their elections.

The Institute for Research and Education on Human Rights tracked 535 candidates running in statewide races who were members of at least one far-right Facebook group. The researchers found that of the 476 races that had been called by last Friday, 338 of those candidates were victorious, representing a 71 percent win rate (the vast majority of these races saw GOP incumbents retain their seats). In Florida, for example, 34-year-old citrus farmer Marshall Rawson, who attended the Unite the Right rally in Charlottesville in 2017, easily defeated his Democrat opponent in the state’s 10th District State House race.

Many states also passed ballot measures that could further far-right agendas, including anti-transgender rules, onerous voter ID laws, and anti-immigrant measures.

“Arizona, Colorado, Nebraska, and Washington approved bans on trans athletes in female sports, so girls in Washington State, for instance, will now have to go through a genital exam or pass a DNA test before they can participate in youth sports,” Devin Burghart, executive director with IREHR, said on Friday during a briefing with reporters.

The success of these ballot measures were celebrated by extremists groups as evidence that Americans wanted that the current GOP could not deliver.

The Proud Boys, the street-fighting group that Immigration and Customs Enforcement agency operations made largely redundant in recent years, were optimistic following the losses. “The ultimate rebuke of the current Republican establishment was citizens voting them out of office then doing their job for them by passing ballot measures on issues the Republicans should have already handled,” the Cape Fear Proud Boys wrote in their Telegram channel.

“What this signals to me is even in those places where they lost, they're getting 41-47 percent of the vote [so] there's still a mass base to try to be mobilized,” Chuck Tanner, research director at IREHR, said during the briefing. “That's the lesson that some of these groups are taking out of it, is that there's still that mass base.”

And it is the existence of this base, extremist groups claim, leaves the Republican Party vulnerable to takeover from the inside.

“Now is the time, if you're a nationalist, to start looking at moving on the Republican Party where you live,” Warren Balogh, a white supremacist from Pittsburgh who attended the Unite the Right rally in Charlottesville in 2017, wrote on his Telegram channel. “And by that I mean, organizing your guys and going in to do a hostile takeover.”

“This may be the best thing that has happened to the Republican party in a half-century,” the Cape Fear Proud Boys declared in a post on Telegram days after the election. “Now you will learn to placate us, or you will continue to fail. Understand this: You’re going to run people we like and you’re going to do what we say.”

But other extremists appear less inclined to wait until the next general election in 2028, preferring a more direct takeover.

"I'm not saying that we will win through democracy, I'm just saying we'll win,” Joel Webbon, a Christian nationalist pastor said last week in a video posted online after the election. Webbon and fellow far-right pastors, as well as guest speaker and noted conspiracy theorist Alex Jones, are set to meet at a conference in Texas this week where attendees will discuss, in at least one talk, “battle plans” for “Christian nationalists on the right to be able to seize power.”

Experts WIRED spoke to said they have not yet seen any explicit calls for violence from the far-right groups they are tracking, but some figures are already looking ahead of January 3, when Congress is set to certify the midterm election results.

Far-right activist and pardoned January 6 rioter Jake Lang, however, is planning an event in DC on January 3. It is unclear how many people are planning to attend, but one of the Christian nationalist pastors listed by Lang as an official speaker has already said he won’t be speaking.

Lang, who spent last weekend disrupting a cat convention that he thought was a furry convention, did not respond to a request for comment.

A United States Marine Corps member says an Alaska Airlines employee was “anti-military” after declining his request for a free extra-legroom upgrade. He also alleges that the employee was rude and accused him of lying about previous upgrades. Those are separate complaints, and I think they deserve separate answers. Everyone deserves courteous service. Military service, however, does not entitle someone to a better seat.

The Instagram algorithim placed Taylor Yontz at the top of my feed this morning, where he describes an encounter at Portland International Airport. According to his account, he saw roughly 30 unassigned seats on the seat map and requested a complimentary move to an extra-legroom seat. He says he is 6 feet 4 inches tall, regularly makes this request, and receives an upgrade about nine times out of 10.

This time, he says, the employee told him free military upgrades were against policy. He alleges the agent spoke with disgust and accused him of lying when he mentioned previous complimentary upgrades. The employee offered a paid move, which he declined at $150.

Complimentary upgrades based solely on military service are not among the listed benefits. So at the outset, there is no policy for upgrading service members when space is available, which even Yontz concedes.

I don’t blame him for asking, especially because he is 6’4″. Sitting in economy class is difficult enough for an average-sized person…it’s brutal for someone that tall.

But “it can’t hurt to ask” needs to leave room for the employee to decline. Otherwise, it becomes a test the agent can pass only by giving something away. Yontz strikes me as highly-entiteld to suggest that he should be given this upgrade because he has in the past, as if it’s standard procedure.

We’ll talk about the Alaska Airlines employee below, but by arguing that you’ve been upgraded in the past and therefore you should today, entitlement is on full display.

If an agent actually accused him of lying about previous upgrades, I would find that inappropriate (I’m not convinced, but it is certainly possible). The employee should have politely explained the current policy without assumptions about past interaction the passenger has had with the airline.

There is no contradiction between “another employee upgraded me before” and “I cannot provide that upgrade today” and Alaska should examine his complaint about insulting language on its own merits.

But even if the employee handled the conversation poorly, that would establish poor service, not necessarily hostility toward military members. And even if it was one agent being hostile (again, certainly possible), it’s still a different matter than whether the upgrade should have been denied.

I appreciate the sacrifices military members and their families make. Airlines can recognize those sacrifices through practical benefits, and Alaska offers several. But I hate to see that appreciation reduced to whether an airport employee hands over a seat upgrade…Yontz’s conduct is unbecoming of a member of the U.S. military (and I was one too, thank you very much).

If one agent makes an exception, the next agent should not face an accusation of prejudice for following the published rules. That creates an incentive to stop offering discretionary favors altogether. Accepting a kind gesture with gratitude leaves room for someone else to receive one later. Treating it as the minimum acceptable response makes it harder for employees to exercise generosity without creating expectations their colleagues must then manage.

The practical result of this whole brouhaha should be a memo to gate agents that no one is to be upgraded for free.

If the Alaska Airlines employee was insulting, the passenger has every reason to complain about that conduct. He did not need to receive an upgrade to deserve a respectful conversation.

But declining a complimentary extra-legroom seat does not, on its own, make an employee anti-military. Alaska’s published benefits do not promise that upgrade, and previous exceptions do not change that.

To all travelers: ask politely, enjoy the upgrade if it happens, but if it does not, a declined upgrade is not evidence that someone disrespects you, no matter what uniform you wear.

While litigation over the legality of the 500-mile border project continues in the lower courts, construction crews can now move forward with work. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson dissented from the decision.

According to administration officials, Customs and Border Protection plans to install four- to six-foot-tall vehicle barriers, surveillance gear, and an access road in the area.

Opponents of the construction, which include a bipartisan mix of Texas ranchers, local landowners, and conservationists, warn that barriers will permanently alter the desert. Critics say the project threatens to cut off access to the river, disrupt livestock and crops, and interfere with the area’s status as the largest dark sky reserve on the planet.

Legal opposition to the project began in April when a coalition consisting of an environmental organization, a river guide, a church preservation group, and local landowners filed a lawsuit to stop the project. The coalition argued that federal officials had exceeded their authority by waiving environmental and conservation laws, such as the National Environmental Policy Act and the Endangered Species Act, without approval from Congress.

In early October, Judge Cardone temporarily blocked the construction after finding that the coalition was likely to succeed on at least one of its legal claims. After Judge Cardone denied the administration’s request to pause her order during an appeal, the government filed an emergency application with the Supreme Court, describing the lower court’s block as “baseless.”

The federal government argued that halting work would leave a gap of more than 300 miles in border defenses, which he said could draw cartels and other bad actors.

Still, the coalition urged the justices to keep the block in place. They argued the administration sought emergency relief to immediately restart bulldozing, blasting, and road building across an ecologically and culturally sensitive landscape, warning that continued work would permanently damage historic resources and private ranch lands.

In Monday’s order, the Supreme Court majority acknowledged the plaintiffs’ argument that construction in the Big Bend Sector would “diminish their ability to use and enjoy the area.” However, the majority concluded that federal law grants the Homeland Security secretary the power to waive conservation and environmental statutes, noting that the law shows “a clear preference for expeditious border construction over the very harms” cited by the challengers.

The decision marks the first major policy victory for President Trump at the Supreme Court following last week’s midterm elections, as the underlying lawsuit proceeds in the lower court.

Comparing car insurance quotes from different companies is one of the best ways to ensure you have the cheapest rate possible. Consider a higher deductible. Raising your car insurance deductible — the amount of a future claim that you agree to cover yourself — lowers your premium and signals to insurers that you’re less likely to file a claim. 4 Reasons to Hunt Geese Over Water

In late October, we got our annual Halloween push of ducks here in the Midwest, and a friend and I timed it just right. We set up on a big agricultural retention pond with winds gusting to 30 mph. I threw out three dozen goose floaters, and we set another dozen full bodies in the shallows. We added a few mallard blocks right at the water’s edge in the landing hole in front of the blind. It’s best to put ducks upwind of geese, as geese don’t like flying over ducks, while ducks will fly over geese.

The first ducks in the spread were greenwings, and we shot a pair. Not long after, a big group of mallards worked us, then dropped into the hole. And so it went, until an hour and a half later, I called the shot on a pair of greenheads at ten yards. We stood up, the blind blew down, and we shot our last two ducks of the day.

We added a couple of geese and passed on the shovelers and ruddies that came into the spread. A flock of bluebills landed at the edge of the geese, but they were too bunched up for a clean shot. The point is, everything liked the goose spread that morning. We had a nice bag of mallards, a couple of teal, a gadwall, and some geese. It proved that a spread of goose floaters can work on ducks just as well as a spread of goose silos and full bodies does in a dry field. And adding floaters to your collection of goose decoys lets you target geese on ponds and rivers, where there may be less competition than in dry fields. If you still aren’t convinced, here are four reasons big Canada floaters will improve your success on ducks and geese.

It doesn’t take a huge decoy spread to fool geese over water. Sometimes, as few as four decoys can be enough to pull geese on small ponds or marshes. Plus, geese feel comfortable enough coming to water that even mediocre (that’s putting it kindly) callers like me can honk and cluck a few times to get their attention, and then let the decoys do the work.

The best time and place to hunt geese over water is a loafing spot, which can be whatever pond, marsh, or stretch of river birds go to after breakfast. Find one of these loafing spots and hunt it at “goose time,” which starts about 8:30 and runs through the middle of the day. There’s no need to get up early to hunt a loafing spot, and shooting the roost in the evening is generally frowned upon, as it might move birds out of the area for good. Save a late afternoon roost shoot for the last day of the season. If you’re after a mixed bag of ducks and geese, however, set the alarm early.

If you’ve gotten this far, you’re probably a serious waterfowler, and I don’t have to explain the adrenaline rush of ducks and geese decoying from high altitudes. This leads me to my next point on why you should use goose floaters: geese will lock up as dots in the sky and drop down to the water as if on a giant, invisible escalator, in a way they rarely do in fields. I remember honking at a bunch of four passing over from behind me and seeing them stand on their wingtips, drop almost to the water, spin, and come into the decoys, barely skimming the surface as they crossed the pond. It was a sight mesmerizing enough that I turned what should have been an easy triple into a single bird on the water. Watching geese decoy over water is more fun than watching them decoy in dry fields.

You will shoot far more ducks over goose decoys than you will ever shoot geese over duck decoys. Up to a 90/10 goose-to-duck decoy ratio can work surprisingly well on ducks, and it pulls more geese than a few goose floaters with a large duck spread will. Goose decoys are big, highly visible, and serve as a confidence bird for other waterfowl. This makes your spread not only more appealing and visible to ducks, but also a more secure place for them to land.

Shanghai next? City’s art scene leans into an uncertain future

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Since 2014, West Bund Art & Design (shown in 2025) has expanded to include multiple venues Courtesy West Bund Art & Design

Shanghai is a restless city. Its impatient hustle may spare its art scene from the plateau of middle age as it navigates a crisis of transition after the Covid years derailed its late 2010s momentum. Going into Shanghai’s annual art week (10-15 November), with two established fairs anchoring an explosion of art events across the city, its galleries, artists and institutions are assessing how the city’s art market can weather China’s economic slowdown and whether the cultural momentum of the past three decades can be sustained.

“Shanghai has so many galleries, so many museums, so many eccentric little spaces that you could come in the middle of summer and still see art non-stop,” says Lorenz Helbling, who founded ShanghArt in 1996. One of the first contemporary art galleries in China, it now has locations in Shanghai, Beijing and Singapore. The Swiss transplant says the volume of artistic activity in the city of 25 million people can be compared to New York’s, though the scene can be harder to find. “You can make a lot of fun things here.”

The city’s two large-scale fairs, Art021 Shanghai (11-14 November) and West Bund Art & Design (10-15 November), established in 2013 and 2014 respectively, set the barometer and prime the mood. So far, Shanghai’s core galleries have largely survived China’s property crash and deflationary economy, but they need young artists and new collectors to continue buoying their businesses.

Lise Li, who founded Shanghai’s Vanguard Gallery in 2004, says the city is “still in an upward phase, just growing much more slowly than before”. During art week, Li’s gallery will participate in the second iteration of the boutique fair Hangover Shanghai (11-15 November) and in Art021, while showing the artist Tang Chao in the gallery and collaborating on a group show at the artist-run 33ml Offspace. Several artists represented by Vanguard are having big moments nearby: Liao Fei will simultaneously be showing at Hangzhou’s Imagokinetics and the artist group known as Jiū Society has an exhibition at Nanjing’s Beiqiu Museum of Contemporary Art.

Established in 2013, Art021 Shanghai (pictured in 2025) is one of the most successful art fairs in the city Photo: Lisa Movius

Though the buzz of the 2010s has quieted, Li says, “there are quite a few more art institutions, and more newcomers are getting involved. The number of young artists is increasing rapidly; we see a lot of new faces every year. But most exhibitions are relatively conservative, which is rather a pity.”

The market has “changed considerably” compared to what now “might be called the ‘golden 15 years’ of China’s contemporary art market: the shift in the broader economic environment over the past few years has been impossible to ignore”, Otto Neu says. In 2022 he co-founded Nan Ke Gallery, which has a group show about art and literature on view during the fair week. “For artists and galleries alike, it is certainly a challenging moment. We probably have to work twice as hard as we did before simply to survive and keep things moving.”

Change, Neu adds, does not have to be negative. He cites the Chinese expression “great waves wash away the sand and leave the gold behind”, adding that “difficult periods can force an industry to become more focused, more rigorous and perhaps more honest about what really matters. In that sense, this may also be an important stage of maturation for Shanghai’s art scene.”

In addition to this month’s two international art fairs, Shanghai’s many resident artists—ranging from established figures like Ding Yi, Zhang Enli and Yang Fudong to mid-career and emerging talents—can rely on year-round programming at a healthy variety of homegrown and imported galleries. Western outfits like Almine Rech, Perrotin and Lisson maintain spaces in Shanghai, as do Asian powerhouses including Japan’s Ota Fine Arts and Korea’s Arario Gallery. ShanghArt has been joined by several influential local galleries, among them Antenna Space, Gallery Vacancy, Capsule Gallery and Don Gallery.

Major state-backed museums like the Power Station of Art, Museum of Art Pudong and West Bund Museum (which is partnered with the Centre Pompidou in Paris) have a complement of private museums that have proliferated since the 2010s. The Rockbund Art Museum, Long Museum and Yuz Museum are the best known, but smaller, newer, specialised or further-flung institutions like Fotografiska and the Museum of Glass, and non-profit spaces like Pond Society, Longlati and Prada Rong Zhai, have also enriched the Shanghai art ecosystem.

Vanguard Gallery’s Li credits Shanghai’s “open-city vibe” and its large base of collectors and visitors, along with the museums, galleries and artists, plus a citizenry curious “about fashion and new things, a strong history of business, and a diverse cultural structure. The main downside is that the cost of living is too high.” The lack of art information platforms, even in Chinese, is the other main challenge, she says.

The sense of openness is what drew the collector Cherry Xu to open her art-meets-fashion space Cheruby in the city last year. With new initiatives, “the boundaries between artists, designers, musicians and people working in fashion are becoming increasingly blurred”, she says, opening the art world to new audiences. “This kind of natural crossover between different fields is something very particular to Shanghai,” Xu says. “Art here doesn’t operate as an isolated system; it is constantly in conversation with design, music, fashion, technology and a broader youth culture, with these different communities influencing one another.”

Compared to Beijing’s art scene, Neu says, Shanghai’s creative community maintains a “healthy sense of distance. There is a certain independence between galleries, institutions, artists and collectors, which has shaped a rather distinctive ecosystem here”, a happy, pragmatic chaos of sorts. Beijing’s art scene is built on personal connections, Neu says, while in Shanghai, “between institutions, between galleries and even between galleries and collectors, there is often an understanding that business is business, friendship is friendship, and collaboration does not necessarily require complicated personal obligations or relationships”.

However, Neu adds that those divisions could become vulnerabilities in the current economic upheaval. “Perhaps this is precisely the moment when Shanghai’s art community needs to become a little more connected and cohesive than it has been in the past—not by giving up its independence, but by finding better ways to support one another,” he says.

Helbling says that Beijing is “more split up into scenes which work on their own”, while Shanghai has various circles that interact and are more permeable. He likens the city’s art community to its fairly compact geography, with the art neighbourhoods all within comparably easy reach of each other.

Twenty years ago, Shanghai was the upstart artistic challenger to Beijing, the political capital home to China’s leading art academy. Hong Kong’s concurrent cultural rise proved complimentary to Shanghai’s, and many in Shanghai say the expanding art activity in Korea, Japan and Singapore—as well as in mainland Chinese cities like Chengdu, Shenzhen and nearby Nanjing, Hangzhou and Suzhou—have been net positives for the local scene.

“This situation didn’t happen overnight; it’s the result of years of collaboration between Shanghai’s art institutions, galleries and fairs, as well as the collectors and artists living here,” Li says. “It’s not simply due to fairs or art weeks alone. The rise of art in new Chinese cities has also become developed, likely influenced by Shanghai and Hong Kong.”

Shanghai “was one of the earliest cities in China to develop a contemporary art market, and its relatively open culture and international outlook have given it a particular advantage”, Neu says. “Whether we are talking about collecting, aesthetic awareness or public understanding of contemporary art, Shanghai has accumulated more than two decades of experience and audience development. That kind of cultural infrastructure takes time to build.”

He adds that growing art scenes in other mainland cities represent “something very positive for Shanghai rather than a threat. A stronger regional art ecosystem ultimately creates more audiences, more conversations and more opportunities for everyone. Healthy competition and exchange between cities can only make the broader Chinese art scene more dynamic.”

Regionally, Xu says, Shanghai “has a very distinct local culture, its own audiences and creative communities, [while] its relationship with other Asian cities is becoming much more direct. It feels increasingly natural for artists, curators and other creative practitioners to live and work across different cities in Asia, and for us to look to what is happening in Tokyo, Seoul, Hong Kong, Singapore, Bangkok and elsewhere in the region, rather than instinctively treating Europe and the US as the only points of reference.” For Shanghai’s art week, Cheruby is showing a solo exhibition by the multidisciplinary Thai artist Tanat Teeradakorn.

“Shanghai has an interesting place within this network,” Xu says. “It has a relatively mature art infrastructure, but also very strong fashion, design, commercial, technological and popular cultures, and these worlds exist very close to one another in everyday life. It also has a large and increasingly young audience, so when an artist comes to Shanghai, they are encountering more than an established ‘art world’; they are entering a complex and fast-moving urban environment that is constantly being reshaped by new forms of consumption, technology and youth culture. That complexity creates a lot of possibilities.”

After helping to build the careers of some of Asia’s biggest art stars, Helbling now focuses on cultivating rising talents through more group shows and dialogue. “You always look at what artists are doing,” he says. “Many of them now operate between here and the West—educated here and in the West”, with networks and roots in both cultures.

After suspended mobility during the Covid years, dual-culture artists in Shanghai and across Asia have resumed their frequent travels between regions. Often the children of artists or collectors, young Chinese artists are very well versed in both Western and Asian artistic traditions, giving them a sophisticated perspective. “They have an interesting job to do to figure out what kind of art makes sense today,” Helbling says.

He recalls the “loneliness” of Shanghai’s 1990s avant-garde, now rendered “impossible” by contemporary art’s citywide popularity. Still, he views the 2000 internationalisation of the Shanghai Biennale, the first contemporary art fair SH Contemporary (2007-14), and the flowering of galleries and museums as all part of one era.

“There was from the 1990s when Shanghai opened up until the Covid time, and there is after Covid time,” he says. “The first 30 years, and now the second 30 years.”

Ring's New $250 Indoor Camera Drone Sounds Terrifying

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ring made its name with its smart doorbells with built-in cameras, and now sells more indoor cameras than any other company, capturing more than 40% of the market. When you've already moved tens of millions of devices (enough to raise serious concerns about their use as part of the surveillance state), you always need to keep innovating, and today, the company announced a product it has been working on for a decade: an indoor camera drone powered by artificial intelligence. I got a chance to check it out during a media event yesterday, and I can report that, as harbingers of our dystopian future go, it is only mildly terrifying.

The drone camera is one of two products in the company's new Ring Robotics line, alongside a "rover cam" that looks kind of like the canister of a vacuum cleaner attached to an iPad. While the Ring Rover Cam is priced at $999.99 and targeted at businesses, the Ring Drone Cam is a much more affordable $249.99, reasonable enough that it will appeal to consumers looking for another way to keep (robot) eyes on their stuff.

The drone camera records 2K video and has a 148-degree field of view and the ability to rotate 360 degrees. It works with existing Ring cameras, doorbells, sensors, and alarms, as well as the Alexa Home Map. It has a flight time of eight minutes, and once it returns to its base station, it takes around an hour to fully recharge (eight minutes on the dock will give it one minute in the air).

As Ring founder Jamie Siminoff explained during the preview event, the company has been trying to build a workable camera drone for around a decade, but couldn't overcome certain design challenges until the advancement of AI models that allow the device to handle most of the computing—from route programming to threat detection—via the cloud, so the drones can be smaller and lighter.

According to Ring, when setting up the Ring Drone, you carry it around your house, allowing it to map the interior. It will "intelligently identify rooms and suggest patrol points: spots worth extra attention." When sent out on "patrol," the drone's AI pilot will travel between hotspots, sensing and avoiding furniture and other obstacles along the way.The Ring Drone works with other Ring cameras, so if an alert is triggered somewhere in your home—say, a window opening unexpectedly—the drone will take off to investigate. It has a built-in security light that will make its presence known, so it can also act as a deterrent, though the screaming sound of its spinning propellers will accomplish that just as well.

According to Ring, the drone camera can deliver peace of mind whether you're home or away, and admittedly I do like the idea of being able to check on my pets or make sure no pipes have burst while I'm at work or on vacation (though admittedly you can get the same sort of monitoring without involving an AI device that looks like a mini version of the flying robots from The Terminator).

I admit that the Ring Drone Camera seems pretty cool, provided I don't think about it too much. I don't know that I really feel the need to have every inch of my home covered in security cameras, but I do like the idea that the drone could, for example, go to check out what's going on if one of my smoke alarms' temperature sensors reports that one spot in the house is all of a sudden hotter than it should be.

But there's something slightly unsettling to me about the idea of a robot patrolling my home while I sleep. Yes, I know that there are privacy and security features in place—Ring says the videos its robots capture is protected by its "strongest default encryption yet"—but it's also yet one more example of how comfortable we've all gotten with cameras being absolutely everywhere, to the point that people think nothing of putting them all over their homes. (This is probably why I don't own a robot vacuum.) And it doesn't help that the drone sounds absolutely terrifying, which Ring claims is by design: "If someone’s there who shouldn’t be, a flying drone overhead...makes it clear they’ve been spotted. [The] devices are built to deter, not just monitor." True enough, but there's also no getting around the fact that drone propellers are unavoidably loud, and the sound of the Ring Drone Camera reminds me of nothing more than the screeching violins in a horror movie. (Note that the drone we were shown was white, but the final version sold will be dark grey.)

If you're ready to enter this brave new world of security, you'll have to wait a bit longer: The Ring Drone Camera isn't expected to ship until January 2027, but you can sign up to be alerted when it is available for preorder now.

Your ‘Healthy’ Soda Alternative May Not Be So Healthy for Your Liver

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

WE'RE LONG PAST having to make a decision between just Coke or Pepsi. You've got a lot of options if you're bored of water—from fizzy nonalcoholic drinks to flavored seltzers. But while a lot of these drinks market themselves as healthy alternatives to sodas, research suggests they could be silently doing a number on your liver health.

If you've done the work to switch to diet sodas or have learned to love prebiotic sodas like Olipop or Poppi, the news can be pretty hard to hear. So it's important to get this out of the way upfront: If you have a "healthy" soda every once in a while, the experts we spoke to say the risk of having liver problems is unlikely. The real danger to your liver is if you’re tossing these back on a daily or near-daily basis. Here's everything you need to know.

THE STUDY PRESENTED at the United European Gastroenterology (UEG) Week 2025 used data from 103,251 people who enrolled in the UK Biobank and didn’t have liver disease at the start of the study. Participants were asked to fill out questionnaires about what they ate and drank throughout the day. The researchers then paid close attention to how much sugar-sweetened and low- or non-sugar-sweetened beverages they drank.

The major takeaway from the study is that it pretty much linked any drink that wasn’t water to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). You might know it by its former name, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). But whatever you call it, it's no joke.

MASLD happens when too much fat builds up in your liver from inputs other than alcohol. It can lead to serious liver scarring or end-stage liver disease. It can also put you at high risk for heart disease and diabetes. According to the American College of Gastroenterology, heart disease is the number one killer of people living with MASLD.

DAILY DRINKING OF nearly 9 ounces of any type of drink that was not water was associated with a significantly higher risk of developing MASLD. That risk jumped 50 percent for sugar-sweetened beverages and was surprisingly higher—60 percent—for those who drank low- or non-sugar-sweetened beverages. (Though some of these prebiotic sodas have more sugar and less fiber than marketing claims might imply).

After a 10-year follow-up, 949 people developed MASLD and 103 died from liver-related causes. Both types of beverages were significantly connected to increases in liver fat.

Replacing nearly 12 ounces of sugary beverages with water lowered MASLD risk by 14.4 percent and by 13.5 percent with low- or non-sugar-sweetened drinks. But if you switched completely from a sugary beverage like soda to a low-sugar beverage like fruit juice, the authors found that this did not help lower a person's risk of developing MASLD.

Exactly which beverages raised risk is hard to say because the authors did not directly state what low- or no-sugar drinks people were drinking. That said, there are lots of sodas that are marketed as being healthier alternatives that are not as good as they claim, according to Danbee Kim, MD, nutrition expert and assistant professor at Rutgers New Jersey Medical School.

“Even beverages marketed as ‘natural’ or ‘health-oriented’ may still fall into this category if they rely on sweeteners instead of sugar,” Kim says. “The key commonality is that they provide sweetness without significant calories. However, they may still affect liver health.”

A FEW THINGS may be going on here. “Low/non-sugar sweetened beverages, although low in calories, may still affect liver health through several pathways,” says Lihe Liu, a graduate student researcher at The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University in China and lead study author. “They can alter the gut microbiome, interfere with feelings of fullness, increase preference for sweet foods, and in some cases stimulate insulin secretion.”

When you have something that’s sweet, even if it doesn’t contain much or any sugar, it sets off a chain reaction in your body the same way as if you were to actually have sugar, says Albert Matheny, RD, CSCS, registered dietitian and co-founder of SoHo Strength Lab. “You’re basically tricking your body,” he says. “Your body still reacts as if it has sugar.”

As a result, Matheny says your body produces more insulin (a hormone that helps regulate blood sugar), and encourages the liver to take in more sugar from your bloodstream. This can lead to metabolic disruption.

“Some artificial sweeteners may alter how the body handles glucose and insulin, contributing to insulin resistance, which is a key driver of metabolic dysfunction and fat accumulation in the liver,” says Kim. This process may even mess with your appetite, making you think that you're hungry when you're not.

Another reason has nothing to do with your gut, but with your mind. Kim says we can't discount the possibility that people who drink these low- and non-sugar-sweetened beverages may subconsciously compensate for the “saved” calories by eating more sugary or fatty foods later.

Keep in mind that all this is speculation. The explanation for why and how artificially sweetened beverages are linked to MASLD is still under investigation since Liu says her team didn't look too deep into this.

NOT NECESSARILY. “THEY’RE definitely an improvement over traditional diet sodas,” Matheny says. “That’s not to say that they’re a totally healthy option.”

When given a choice, water reigns supreme. And even then you still don't want to go too overboard.

If you still want to crack open a cranberry fizz prebiotic soda or even an unsweetened iced tea, Matheny says that’s probably fine, too. “In moderation, kind of everything is OK.”

Memory chip CEO warns 2027 shortage will be so bad that ‘even money can’t buy goods’

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The CEO of Taiwanese memory maker Team Group says the global memory chip shortage is about to get worse, to the point where “even money can’t buy goods.”

Speaking on the company’s third-quarter earnings call on Nov. 2, CEO Gerry Chen (Chen Ching-wen) said the most severe stretch will run from now through the end of the second quarter of 2027, according to a BigGo Finance report. He sees “no downward price trend whatsoever” next year and doesn’t expect supply and demand to balance until the second half of 2028 at the earliest.

The warning reaches well beyond memory makers. The DRAM and NAND flash chips that go into AI servers also go into PCs, smartphones, and cars, and AI buyers are winning the bidding war. That leaves device makers choosing among higher prices, thinner margins, and fewer units.

Chen said AI servers and enterprise applications are taking most DRAM output. The industry’s biggest suppliers, Samsung, SK Hynix, and Micron, have shifted capacity toward the high-bandwidth memory used in AI chips, leaving less conventional memory for consumer devices, as Fortune reported earlier this year.

The money behind that demand is enormous. A recent Stateline analysis called data centers “the largest financial bet in US history.”

The shift shows up in Team Group’s own business. Business-to-business sales made up 64.6% of revenue in the first three quarters of 2026, up from 24.6% a year earlier. Chen attributed the change to market demand rather than a strategic decision by the company.

Contract prices for a 16-gigabit DDR5 chip rose from $8.70 in October 2025 to $48 in September 2026, according to figures cited on the call. That is more than five times the price of a year earlier.

For Team Group, the squeeze has been a windfall. Third-quarter revenue hit a record NT$10.14 billion (about $312 million), up nearly 34% from the prior quarter. Net profit reached NT$3.62 billion (about $111 million), and gross margin climbed to 49.3% from 42.8% in the second quarter.

Device makers sit on the other side of that trade. Apple has reportedly cut component orders for its iPhone 18 Pro models after raising their starting prices by $100, and IDC expects global smartphone shipments to fall 16.7% this year. Consumers are likely to keep absorbing the cost, through pricier PCs and phones or through machines that ship with less memory at the same price.

The most striking part of Chen’s warning is that money and supplier relationships may not be enough. “We have contracts but can’t get enough volume, so we have to go to the spot market and pay a premium to grab supply,” he said, according to Tom’s Hardware. He put the gap between spot and contract prices at 30% to 50%.

New capacity will arrive slowly. Team Group’s new plant in New Taipei City is slated to begin production in the second quarter of 2027. SK Hynix said last month that the shortage will last until 2030, with long-term agreements already covering more than half of its capacity for 2027 and 2028. Harvard Business School professor Willy Shih expects new fabs to reach volume production only in late 2027 or 2028, and has warned that memory booms have always ended once that supply arrives.

The investigation into Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang adds a new variable. Nvidia’s board said on Nov. 8 that it would open an independent probe into Huang’s affair with Megaspeed executive Alice Huang, who is suspected of spying for China and is no relation. President Trump has since said the FBI is “looking into” the matter.

So far, the scandal has not directly touched memory supply. Nvidia, No. 16 on the 2026 Fortune 500, relies on Samsung and SK Hynix for the advanced memory in its AI accelerators, so the exposure runs through export policy. If Washington responds by tightening AI chip controls on China, Korean memory makers could face stricter end-user checks that raise compliance costs and delay shipments, the Korea Herald reported.

Even then, the effect on the broader shortage may be limited. South Korea’s government expects deliveries under existing contracts to continue without disruption, and Hanwha Investment & Securities analyst Park Se-yeon cautioned against tying Huang’s personal controversy directly to Korean chipmakers’ outlook, according to Korea JoongAng Daily.

The next signals arrive quickly: Nvidia reports third-quarter earnings on Nov. 17, and three House committee chairs have requested a closed-door briefing on the case for Nov. 18. For memory buyers, though, Chen’s outlook rests on a simpler problem. “How can prices fall when there’s nothing left?” he said.

The holiday hosting season is upon us, and while you’re planning your tablescape decor and seasonal trimmings, you might want to take a hard look at your guest bedroom.

“Most guest rooms get treated like overflow storage, and guests feel that the second they walk in,” says Marie Cloud, principal designer at Indigo Pruitt.

Even if you host only occasionally, your guest room should give visitors a comfortable place to unwind. No need for a guest book or fuzzy slippers: Designers say these four thoughtful touches can make the room feel luxe without costing a fortune.

Regardless of the design aesthetic, the ambiance of a room always hinges on lighting. “I always pay attention to the lighting,” says Louis Lin, founder of his eponymous New York City–based interior design studio. “A beautiful bedside lamp with a warm, flattering glow can transform the atmosphere of a room. Layering different light sources, rather than relying on overhead lighting, can do that. It's a relatively simple gesture that makes a room feel much more intimate and refined.” This also gives guests the freedom to tailor the air of their space to their liking. It’s one of the few details they can truly control in their temporary lodging.

While a pile of pillows might look inviting, designer Marie Cloud says the bedtime legwork promptly cancels out the aesthetics.

“Skip the stack of eight decorative pillows your guest has to relocate before bed,” says Cloud. Her perfect formula for a top-notch guest setup? Two sleeping pillows, plus one lumbar pillow, all set atop a good set of sheets.

“Sheets with real hand-feel, like a crisp percale or washed linen,” Cloud suggests. “Finish with a throw folded at the foot in a color pulled from the room. It looks considered, and it’s actually comfortable at 2 a.m.”

Most guest rooms get the all-white wall treatment in the name of neutrality, but all it really says is that your space lacks intention. Just because this room is (mostly) reserved for guests doesn't mean it has to lack character. In fact, Cloud says you should not hold back.

“Drench the room,” says the designer. “Walls, trim, and ceiling in the same color, ideally something deep and warm. Continuous color reads custom, it makes a small room feel wrapped rather than cramped, and it tells your guest the space was designed for them. A guest room is also the lowest-risk room in the house to be bold, so take the swing.”

The best guest setups make visitors feel anticipated, even when they show up unexpectedly. To achieve the boutique-hotel feel, designers recommend investing in small luxuries that leave a big impact. A few items Cloud and Lin always recommend having on hand are a small luggage rack, real hangers, fresh towels, and a select set of toiletries reserved especially for guests.

“The real luxury is making guests feel that the room has been prepared just for them,” says Lin.

High oil prices have led to a dip in global carbon emissions

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Bucking recent trends, fossil fuel emissions look to be down 0.4 percent.

Evaluating the annual global temperatures each year requires context. There is a fair amount of natural variability from one year to the next, so it’s important to focus on the trend over time. Famously, global warming did not end in 1998, as some claimed a couple of decades ago—we just had a run of La-Niña-dominated years after a very strong El Niño.

Annual greenhouse gas emissions are much less variable, having increased fairly steadily, but many complicating factors can still muddy the picture. Sometimes those factors conspire to throw a year off the trend. It looks like 2026 will be one of those years.

A new analysis led by Corinne Le Quéré at the University of East Anglia estimates that fossil fuel emissions have fallen about 0.4 percent from last year. This probably isn’t a peak that symbolizes heartening progress on climate change, though; the researchers think it’s a short-term dip driven by the war in Iran and its effect on oil prices.

This team works on the Global Carbon Project, which compiles stats on fossil fuel use and things like deforestation to track carbon emissions globally and by country. In this case, they looked at the stats and tried to pull out key underlying trends that show us what to expect over the next couple of years.

Last year, fossil fuel emissions increased 1.2 percent, a bit above the 2019–2025 average of 0.8 percent per year. This year’s decline featured a 1.9 percent drop in oil emissions, a smaller 0.3 percent drop in emissions from natural gas use, and a 0.6 percent increase in coal emissions.

Unlike the pandemic-driven drop in 2020, this wasn’t driven by a widespread decrease in energy demand. Demand did increase, but additions of electric vehicles and solar and wind generation covered almost 70 percent of the growth. Electric vehicles can account for only about 20 percent of that consequential decline in oil emissions, meaning the bulk is due to high prices.

One simple way to break down emissions is to connect it to economic output. Given a country’s gross domestic product (GDP), energy use per unit GDP, and carbon emissions per unit energy, you can calculate total carbon emissions. GDP might go up, but if that’s due to less energy-intensive industries or a cleaner energy system, carbon emissions might not rise with it.

Between 2019 and 2025, 59 countries cut their emissions despite economic growth, even as global emissions increased by 0.8 percent per year. The researchers estimated that, had the economic growth not been accompanied by the growth of electric vehicles and solar and wind generation, the increase would have been much higher, at 1.9 percent per year.

Given the International Monetary Fund’s projection for GDP through 2028, the researchers extrapolated these trends forward as well. From that, we would expect global fossil fuel emissions to continue increasing. That includes the present year—without the shock to oil prices, we would be seeing emissions increase in 2026 as well.

So while we could be close to peak emissions, we’re probably not there yet. The growth of electric vehicles and renewable energy is offsetting most of the growth of emissions, but not all of it. That would take just a bit more juice.

The researchers write, “If further acceleration of clean technology deployment can be supported through time, a sustained peak and decline in global CO2 emissions could be a few years away.”

Following is a transcript of his remarks:

The PREVAIL study was a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial with gefurulimab as the treating agent. Gefurulimab is a unique agent in that it's the first nanobody that'll be used in myasthenia gravis, which makes it a smaller molecule, which allows for greater tissue penetration and solubility -- so it can be given as a [subcutaneous] injection rather than the larger monoclonal antibodies, which have to be given in larger volumes underneath the skin or as IV infusion. So this would represent a greater ease of access for patients to have a [subcutaneous], once-a-week injection that doesn't require them to go to the infusion center.

As we had seen in the trial, similar to eculizumab [Bkemv] and ravulizumab [Ultomiris], which are C5 inhibitors, it met its primary outcome of MG-ADL [Myasthenia Gravis Activities of Daily Living] reduction, with a minus 4.2 reduction versus 2.1 on placebo, which was statistically significant. There were similar side effects between the treatment group and the placebo group, with injection-site reactions being slightly higher in the treatment group, but all of those reactions tended to be mild to moderate. The most common adverse events were headaches, upper respiratory infections, and muscle aches and pains. Everybody made sure they were fully vaccinated, and if they'd had to be started prior to 2 weeks, they received antibiotics. But we had a very typical safety profile, as I said, between the placebo arm and the treatment arm -- the side effects were similar.

This is going to make for ease of access for patients. You still have other molecules out on the market. You've got a once-daily complement inhibitor and you've got now a once-every-2-weeks IV infusion versus every 8 weeks. So kind of depending on what the patient prefers, this gives them greater access. We've got an easily portable complement inhibitor that patients no longer are tethered to an infusion center that they have to worry about. When's our next infusion? Do I have access? And I think that's going to be a huge advantage for the patients and their quality of life.

I would say that one thing that's unique about [gefurulimab] is that it is compatible with IVIG. Here's the inflation breakdown for October 2026 — in one chart

📅 2026-11-10  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Inflation throttled back in October from its blistering pace in September amid a more moderate rise in gasoline prices, but remained high as categories like food and housing put upward pressure on consumer prices.

The consumer price index, an inflation barometer, rose 0.4% on a monthly basis in October, after adjusting for seasonal factors, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported Tuesday. That's down from 0.6% in September but higher than the 0.3% that economists expected.

"It's uncomfortably high," said Mark Zandi, chief economist at Moody's. "The Fed's not going to be happy about it. It certainly makes it more likely we get another rate increase in December."

The Federal Reserve uses inflation data to guide its interest rate policy. The U.S. central bank raised rates in September amid a recent pickup in inflation, which economists largely attribute to the war in Iran. It held rates steady at its last meeting in October.

Economists generally assess a measure known as "core" CPI — which strips out volatile food and energy prices — to examine underlying inflation dynamics in the U.S. economy.

That core metric rose 0.3% in October, as it also did in September and August. A consistent monthly reading around 0.2% would be more in line with the Fed's 2% annual inflation target, Zandi said.

Inflation has been above the Fed's target for more than five years.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics didn't report an annual inflation figure in the latest CPI report because of the lengthy federal government shutdown that happened a year ago. That shutdown, which ran from Oct. 1 to Nov. 12, disrupted the agency's typical data collection, and it didn't release an inflation report for October 2025.

"Investors do not need year-over-year figures to see that this was a hot inflation report and that prices are not on a sustainable path back toward the Fed's 2% target," Jason Pride, chief of investment strategy and research at Glenmede, a wealth management firm, wrote in an e-mail Tuesday.

"It would have taken exceptional evidence of disinflation to dissuade the Fed from raising rates in December, and this report delivered the opposite," he wrote.

Economists pointed to four big reasons for the hotter-than-expected CPI reading.

Perhaps the largest contributor to the outsized inflation reading in Tuesday's CPI report was housing, Zandi said.

Housing is the largest component of CPI, and movements in the category strongly influence the index's month-to-month reading, he said.

The "shelter" CPI index rose 0.3% in October, accounting for over 30% of the CPI increase, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

Zandi said he doesn't expect that dynamic to persist, however, because it's not consistent with the trajectory of market rents in the U.S.

"It does feel like we have a high level of monthly volatility" in the CPI data, he said. "It's all over the map month to month."

But economists said broader factors are putting upward pressure on prices, too.

"Core inflation today reflects three outsized shocks: tariffs, energy prices, and AI-related demand," Josh Hirt, senior U.S. economist at Vanguard Group, an asset manager, wrote in an e-mail Tuesday.

The Iran war has raised oil prices, pushing up prices for fuels like gasoline, diesel and jet fuel, which are refined from crude oil, economists said.

Gasoline prices rose 1% in October, a significant pullback after a 9.1% monthly increase in September, CPI data showed.

While diesel prices have retreated from recent record highs, they remain elevated and could spill more broadly into the U.S. economy in the coming months, economists said.

"Diesel prices have a really slow pass-through to all the areas of CPI, given it affects transportation costs," said Thomas Ryan, a senior North America economist at Capital Economics.

"These things take time," Ryan said. "It'll be an inflationary impetus for at least the next three to six months, I suspect."

There was some evidence of that broader percolation of diesel prices in October, such as in food prices, Zandi said. Higher diesel prices add to the cost of transporting food to the grocery store shelves, he said.

Food prices rose 0.5% in October after an unchanged reading in September, according to CPI data.

The run-up in jet fuel costs has also led airlines to raise their ticket prices, economists said. Airfares climbed 0.4% in October, after monthly increases of 1.2%, 2.7% and 3.8% in the preceding months, respectively, according to the CPI report.

Meanwhile, the inflationary impact of tariffs imposed by the Trump administration should fade by the end of the year, assuming the White House doesn't renew its push for more import taxes, Zandi said.

Certain categories of physical goods, like toys, furniture, and appliances, saw elevated inflation rates in October, likely because businesses have passed on tariff costs to consumers, he said.

The buildout of data centers that power artificial intelligence has driven up prices for electricity and consumer electronics, economists said.

Those data centers have led to a shortage of technology equipment, such as memory chips used in household electronic products like iPhones, Ryan said.

Your AI Agents Need Job Descriptions, Not Better Prompts

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ask a company what a new accounts payable analyst is allowed to do and you’ll get a precise answer. She works in these systems, with these vendors, and anything over an approval limit of, say, $10,000 goes to her manager for a signature. She’s on probation for her first few months. All of that usually sits in a delegation-of-authority table that internal audit checks every year.

Ask the same company what its new invoice agent is allowed to do and you’ll often get a screenshot of a prompt.

That gap bothers me more than any model benchmark. We’re hiring a digital workforce without the management system we spent a century building for people: nobody writes down its authority, nobody tests its judgment before it starts, and nobody has practiced switching it off.

A prompt describes intent. It says what the agent is for. It rarely says what the agent may never do, where its limits sit, or when it has to stop and hand the decision to a person.

And when a prompt does say those things, nothing enforces them. A line telling an agent not to issue refunds over $500 is a request to a probabilistic system. No auditor would sign off on “we asked the analyst nicely” as a control for a human, so I don’t see why we accept it for software.

Security people already have a name for the result. The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications lists Excessive Agency as a core risk, rooted in excessive functionality, excessive permissions, or excessive autonomy. In plain terms, it’s an employee handed the keys to everything on day one. The OWASP guidance reads like a good manager’s checklist: give the agent only the tools it needs, scope its permissions, require human approval for high-impact actions, and enforce authorization in the downstream systems instead of trusting the model to police itself.

I’d underline that last one. The grant has to live where the action happens, in the agent’s own service identity, its tool permissions, and the spend cap at the API gateway. Running agents on a shared service account is like five people sharing one badge. When something goes wrong, the audit log says the service account did it, and you’re left guessing which agent.

Gartner predicts over 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027, citing escalating costs, unclear business value, and inadequate risk controls.

Hype and immature products are part of that story. Still, try reading the three causes the way a COO would. Costs escalate when nobody set a budget. Value stays unclear when nobody wrote down what the work was supposed to produce. Risk controls fall short when there are no limits, no reviews, and nobody clearly accountable. Gartner doesn’t frame it this way; that’s my reading. It’s the same thing that happens when people are set loose without a management system, and that problem is a lot older than AI.

The frameworks see the same hole. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework treats Govern as the function that runs through Map, Measure, and Manage, and its Generative AI Profile carries that into generative systems. What frameworks can’t give you is the working machinery, the grants and tests and reviews that a team actually runs on a Tuesday. Without those, governance stays a PDF on a shared drive.

Start with the job description, which for an agent becomes an authority grant. Before an agent touches a live system, someone should be able to write one page covering what it may do, what it may never do, its approval thresholds, which human owns it, and who can widen its limits. In the RACI, the accountable party is a person. If you can’t write that page, the role isn’t ready to fill, and you’d say the same about a human hire.

Two old rules from delegation of authority carry straight over. You can’t delegate authority you don’t hold, so a sub-agent should never get a wider grant than the agent that spawned it. Segregation of duties still applies too. An agent that can both create a vendor and pay that vendor is the same audit finding it would be for a person.

Then there’s the hiring test. We mostly test agents the way we’d test a calculator, by checking whether the answer is right. A test for a worker would check whether it stayed inside its grant, asked for approval when it was supposed to, escalated when the situation went beyond its brief, and stopped when it should have.

In my own research I’ve been building a benchmark of 50 scenarios around exactly these failures: crossing approval limits, acting on stale data, following injected instructions that widen scope, keeping access after a role change. Agents add a wrinkle people don’t. When a vendor upgrades the underlying model, you’ve effectively got a new hire wearing the old badge. I’d treat that as a change event and re-test before the grant carries over.

Termination has to be rehearsed before you need it. If you had to stop this agent and pull its access in the next hour, could you? Who has the authority to make that call at 2 a.m.? You can hold an employee responsible. You can’t hold an agent responsible for anything, and that’s where the comparison stops working. It’s also why the grant has to be enforced in systems, and why every agent needs a named person who owns it. Someone ends up accountable either way. Usually it’s whoever signed off, whether they knew they were signing or not.

If the honest answers are “a prompt,” “nobody remembers,” “hours,” and “unclear,” a better model wouldn’t have saved you.

Models are becoming rented inputs, swapped and upgraded on a vendor’s schedule. Two companies can run the same model and end up in very different places. One has agents with budgets, limits, owners, and an audit trail for every consequential action, while the other has an expensive pilot headed for someone’s cancellation statistics.

The difference is the management system around the model. That gets built slowly, mostly by people who have run organizations before.

If your newest agent went past its authority tomorrow, would anything in your company notice? And could you show an auditor, in writing, where the boundary was?

3 AI agents value a home similarly, could they be wrong?

Three AI valuation systems can produce similar home values while relying on the same market definition and comparable selection assumptions. Agreement does not validate accuracy if the competitive market area is misidentified.  AI Summary

The similarities in the estimations made by three independent systems might be perceived as confirmation of accuracy. However, when AI-powered systems operate under the same assumptions regarding a property’s competitive market, the agreement might bring no additional assurance.

Imagine three different AI agents estimating the value of your house. The first system offers a $1.59 million valuation. The second – $1.61 million. And the third – $1.60 million.

It looks like three independent systems agree on the price. This information can be quite reassuring for the homeowner who plans to sell. Moreover, the similarity in the numbers can be influential for the asking price.

However, this question comes to mind: Have three different systems produced the same number because they analyzed the data independently, or is the result just similar due to the same initial assumptions?

Let’s talk about this case in more details and figure out whether agreement can increase the probability of a mistaken valuation.

The issue can arise long before any of the numbers is calculated by the systems.

The real estate sector accumulates huge volumes of property data. Closed transactions, MLS records, active listings, public records and other sources provide rich information about the prices, property features and market activity.

Modern valuation models use highly advanced statistical techniques and machine learning algorithms to make sense of this information. AI agents can extract and analyze property records, use third-party valuations or generate an accurate price explanation based on the market data.

However, the three independent systems can be based on the same underlying sources, analytical methods or similar understanding of the competitive market. And, in case the initial assumptions are incorrect, the similarity of results cannot provide any additional assurance.

Let’s imagine a condominium in Miami on the 24th floor of a high-rise building.

AI generates valuation based on the recent transactions of condominiums with the same square footage, number of bedrooms and geographical proximity. It uses the information to make calculations and produce a detailed valuation.

Everything looks good. Properties exist. Transaction histories are true. Property characteristics match the search criteria. The calculations are accurate. But do these properties compete in the same market?

In dense residential markets like Miami, geographical proximity and similar property characteristics can hide significant differences in competitive positioning.

Two condominiums may have the same number of bedrooms, the same square footage, and even be located right across the street from each other. But one may be located in the entry-level residential community while another – in the waterfront development.

They may have identical property types, but different price ranges, ownership costs, amenities, views, and other characteristics that are relevant for the market analysis.

On paper, the properties look comparable. But in reality, they may attract different buyers and be in competition with different properties.

This concept is well understood by established organizations like Fannie Mae. According to the Appraiser Update of December 2024, two properties that are located side by side can be in the same neighborhood, but occupy different market areas since they appeal to different buyers.

According to the guidelines on the comparable sales, the comparable properties should compete for the same buyers as the subject property.

This is an important difference between searching similar property records and identifying relevant market evidence.

Competitive market area may begin with the condominium building where the subject property is located, include similar units within the same development, or some competing residential communities in the area. Price range classification and property characteristics can help with the organization of this analysis, but they cannot substitute the process itself.

The market area should be defined with the help of buyer alternatives, not the geographical proximity.

I have worked with residential property data and analyzed the range of automated valuation models and AI-generated valuations, and the distinction between preparing property records and organizing the market analysis keeps coming back.

Property records can contain accurate addresses, standardized property types, verified square footage, complete transaction history, and precise geographic coordinates. All of the duplicate records are deleted, inconsistencies are corrected, and missing information is added.

These are important steps in data preparation. But clean data does not tell anything about the competition between the residential communities, the price range segments they occupy, and the relevant properties for the market analysis.

This requires one more step: organizing residential communities, analyzing their characteristics, establishing the relations between the properties, and validating those relations on the market evidence.

These processes can be automated with the help of machine learning. But the underlying definitions and inferences require testing and maintenance as well. It does not mean that current valuation models are based on radius searches. Many of them use the much more complicated methods and can find the complex relationships between property characteristics, locations, and market activity.

The challenge here is more fundamental: can we make sure that the information used to support the valuation is the appropriate representation of the competitive market area of the subject property?

Larger dataset, more advanced model, or a better explanation of the results cannot guarantee this. More property data does not mean more market evidence automatically.

Defining market areas has its consequences not only for comparable sales, but for the statistics describing the supply, demand, and the current competitive conditions as well.

Inventory, speed of selling, days on market and months of supply depend on which properties are included in the calculation. Let’s take a simplified, hypothetical example.

Imagine a competitive market area that consists of a group of competing condominium communities with 12 active listings. This market area has been experiencing three sales per month. So, the inventory represents four months of supply.

Now, let’s imagine an AI system combining these properties with another residential area with 48 active listings and three sales per month.

Combined market area will include 60 active listings and six monthly sales, resulting in 10 months of supply.

Both calculations are mathematically correct. But the second number describes a different set of properties, not necessarily the competitive environment of the first market area.

For the homeowner, the difference is important. Four months of supply and 10 months of supply can indicate very different competitive conditions. Depending on the way of interpretation, this statistic may have impact on the pricing expectations and the decision whether to change the asking price.

Of course, the actual market analysis requires consistency of the measurement periods, sufficient transaction activity, and the consideration of changing conditions. Small samples can lead to uncertainties, even in case of correct market definition.

But the example shows an important point. Calculation can be mathematically correct, but the population of properties that is included in the calculation can be wrong for the particular decision.

The same concerns median prices, selling speed, and other market indicators. If the dataset includes communities from different competitive segments, its summary statistics may cover the completely irrelevant competitive conditions.

That is why market definition and maintenance of relevant residential communities is not just an improvement of comparable selection. This is an essential part of market analysis.

Research in other industries provides one more argument to be skeptical about the opinion agreement of several AI systems.

The 2025 study Correlated Errors in Large Language Models published at the International Conference on Machine Learning has examined more than 350 language models and has found out substantial correlations in their mistakes.

On one benchmark, when both models produced a mistake, they chose the same wrong answer in 60% of cases. Correlations were also found between models of different architecture and provided by different organizations.

However, the study was not concerned about the home valuations, and its results do not prove that AI pricing agents routinely make the same mistakes. But it reveals an important limitation of apparent model independence: different models can make correlated mistakes.

In case of property valuations, the risk of correlated mistakes is associated with shared assumptions.

If several AI systems analyze overlapping market information, use similar property-selection criteria, or rely on the same initial valuation source, their conclusions may be less independent than they seem.

And when the underlying market is improperly defined, these systems can reinforce misleading interpretation without generating a single false record.

It is important to remember that this situation differs from the usual problem of AI hallucinations. Hallucination provides some unsupported or invented information. Market definition mistake can produce misleading conclusion on the basis of the correct data.

Verifying the existence of transactions is necessary. But it does not guarantee that these transactions are really relevant for the analysis.

The most valuable AI pricing advice should do more than producing a number. It should help homeowners to understand the market evidence behind the number, assumptions and uncertainties associated with it.

First of all, it is the market itself. Which residential communities and competing properties have been considered as relevant, and why? Does the analysis distinguish between the properties that are geographically close and actually compete with the same buyers?

Then it is the evidence itself. Which closed transactions serve as the base for the recommended price? How the characteristics of the subject property compare to these sales? Which active listings show the current competition? Were the relevant transactions excluded, or less comparable properties were included just due to the geographical proximity?

Understanding of the limitations of the system is also important.

Model can have limited transaction activity in the subject’s competitive market. The particular unit may have some unique characteristics. Comparable properties may require adjustments, or the market conditions have already changed after the closing of the most relevant transactions.

In these circumstances, admitting the uncertainty is more valuable than producing an artificial precise conclusion.

Also, homeowners should be able to distinguish between explanation and validation. System can provide a good explanation of its estimation, but this fact does not confirm the predictive accuracy of the method.

Where possible, evidence of how a valuation method performs against actual sales prices for comparable properties and market segments can be more valuable than the agreement of several AI-generated estimates.

Not every automated valuation model operates with a conventional list of comparable sales, and not every proprietary system allows checking its inner workings. This is the reason why transparency of available evidence, limitations, and performance metrics is especially important.

For the homeowner receiving three similar estimates, the next step should be to check whether they are based on the different market evidence or they just reflect the same initial data.

Agreement may be reassuring. Understanding of the reasons for it is more valuable.

As AI becomes more and more accessible, the ability to retrieve property records, calculate statistics, and generate the polished price explanations will become commonplace.

The meaningful differentiation will consist in the quality of the market intelligence. It includes reliable property data, well-organized residential communities, relevant relations between the competing properties, consistent market definitions, and methods that can be validated.

Neither proprietary dataset nor publicly available records are superior. What matters is how this information is organized, validated, maintained, and applied to solve the particular problem.

Technological sophistication of models will continue to grow, but it should not be mistaken for independent verification.

For the homeowners preparing to sell, the consequences are practical. Pricing decision can impact the way of entering the market, the competition of the property with alternatives, its listing period, and the negotiating position of the seller.

The purpose is not replacing the professional judgment with the impressive number. It is providing homeowners and real estate professionals with the better evidence and the more precise market context.

Three AI agents valuing your home similarly sound convincing. However, before trusting their conclusion, one more question is important to ask.

Is the market they are analyzing the right one?

“A vulnerability (worse_query) affecting iOS 26.7.1, 27.0.1, and 27.2 allows malicious apps to read local files from other apps, putting seed phrases, private keys, and login credentials at risk across the entire ecosystem,” the exchange wrote on X. “This is an iOS-level issue, not unique to Binance.”

Binance told users to delete any apps installed from untrusted sources, such as sideloaded or manually signed IPA files, and to update iOS and every app to the latest official versions. Anyone who suspects a breach should move funds to a new wallet created on a clean device and reset all active login sessions, the exchange said.

Neither Binance nor the researcher behind the flaw has reported any thefts tied to it.

The bug is a sandbox escape. On iOS, each app is supposed to live in its own container that other apps cannot read. A researcher who posts as forcequitOS released worse_query on GitHub on Nov. 8, calling it “a zero-day sandbox escape for iOS 26 and 27, again.” It succeeds an earlier exploit, bad_query.

The project’s README says it works on iOS 26.0 through 26.7.2 and on iOS 27.2, a wider range than the versions Binance named. It lists paths it can reach, including other apps’ data containers and shared app group folders.

The researcher describes it as “a proof-of-concept for developers” that “isn’t useful by itself,” and stresses it is not a jailbreak. To use it, a developer has to build it into an app.

That has already happened in at least one case. A day after the release, developer Christoph Kolbicz published a file manager built on worse_query that lets users browse and edit app data on iOS 27 without jailbreaking, which he said was tested on iOS 27.0.1.

Blockchain security firm SlowMist called worse_query a container sandbox escape rather than a jailbreak. Its chief information security officer, who posts as 23pds, wrote that the flaw will not steal assets on its own. The risk comes when a user installs and runs an untrusted IPA that then reads other apps’ containers, and only if a wallet stores mnemonic phrases, keystores or WebView caches in plain text on the device.

Apple shipped iOS 27.2.1 and iOS 26.7.3 on Nov. 9, a day after the proof of concept went public. Their security notes list fixes for AirPlay, Bluetooth pairing, brotli and other components, but neither names worse_query or describes a sandbox escape of this kind.

That leaves it unclear whether the latest iOS builds block the technique.

The exposure depends heavily on how each wallet app stores secrets. By SlowMist’s reading, wallets that do not write recovery phrases or keys to plain files on the device are not exposed in the same way, and hardware wallets keep private keys off the phone entirely. The attack also requires a user to install software from outside the App Store first.

The warning comes from an exchange still recovering from its own breach. Binance lost $6.34 billion in an Oct. 29 attack that began with a fake job interview. Security worries have also pushed some large holders to swap self-custodied bitcoin for IBIT shares, while hardware wallet maker Ledger said an organized crime group spent millions targeting its users.

For iPhone users holding bitcoin in a mobile wallet, Binance’s advice amounts to basic hygiene: stay on official iOS releases, skip sideloaded apps and move funds if a device may have been compromised.

For our new report, “Generative AI and News Report 2026: From Standalone Tools to Information Infrastructure,”  we surveyed audiences across nine countries, including the United States, on their use of AI in their everyday lives, what they think its impact will be on different sectors of society, and what they think about its use in news and journalism specifically. I obviously think you should read the whole report, but here are some of the major takeaways.

The use of standalone conversational AI systems such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini continues to grow rapidly, with 51% of respondents across the six countries where we have past data saying they now use AI weekly (49% in the U.S.). That’s a faster spread, at least in the U.S., than smartphones or the internet saw.

Many people also encounter AI through “incidental” AI exposure in the AI-infused services and platforms they’re already using. Search engines such as Google and Bing are key drivers: 57% of respondents across countries said they saw or engaged with AI-generated answers or summaries in the last week. News media, too, drives AI exposure. An average of 24% of respondents across nine countries said they’d seen a label noting that content was made with AI in the last week, and 20% had seen a bullet-point AI news summary.

Despite all the talk about AI agents, we find that, so far, they haven’t broken into the mainstream. The vast majority of respondents across all nine countries (67%) said they had not used an AI agent in the last week. Fourteen percent used one for a real task and 13% tried one just to experiment.

Our data provides solid evidence that a broader transformation of the information environment is afoot. Conversational AI systems have arrived as information intermediaries. Trust is not the main reason people use them. Instead, people see conversational AI systems as friction-reducing machines that get them information more quickly.

“Getting information” continues to be the most common use case. Thirty-eight percent of respondents across countries (35% in the U.S.) said they had used generative AI for getting information in the last week, up from 24% in 2025 and 11% in 2024.

We looked more closely at what people do when they say they use AI for getting information. Across the six countries we’ve surveyed since 2024, we primarily see a mixture of search-equivalent behaviors like “researching a topic” (24%) or “answering factual questions” (18%). People are also increasingly turning to AI for advice (20%).

Using AI directly for news, meanwhile, remains a niche activity, as we demonstrated in the Digital News Report earlier this year. But that use is increasing: 9% of respondents across our six core countries say they use AI for news, up from 6% in 2025 and 3% in 2024.

Many people don’t hold clear or strong views about whether AI output is trustworthy. But people who do rely on it for information trust it. Across the nine countries, 75% of those who used AI to answer factual questions trusted the answers, as did 76% of those who used it for advice and 72% of those who used it for news. Only small minorities expressed distrust.

At the same time, trust and trustworthiness do not, at this stage, seem to be central to why people rely on AI systems. Instead, people tend to see these systems as friction-reducing machines. The convenience of getting a faster and easier answer was chosen by 64% as the main reason they use AI, followed by the interactivity afforded by natural language interfaces (56%). Only a minority (21%) said they use AI because they get more reliable information from it than from other sources.

As AI systems become widely used, their growing popularity is likely to affect the use of legacy content offerings and digital platforms and services. As my coauthor Rasmus Kleis Nielsen put it on LinkedIn, rapid growth in AI use isn’t just eating into what news sites have to offer. It’s eating into search, too — with people who use AI for information saying they use not just news, but search engines less, too, and by a wider margin. Challenging times may be ahead not just for news publishers, but also for the incumbent digital giants previously seen as unassailable.

As in previous years, we wanted to know what people think of AI’s use in journalism and news. We find that people’s attitudes here are remarkably entrenched and negative — though they also haven’t worsened significantly.

People across countries remain uncomfortable with the use of AI in the newsroom, especially with the idea of news being made entirely or primarily with AI. On average across nine countries, just 12% of respondents say they are very or somewhat comfortable with news made entirely by AI. That figure rises to 19% if there is some human oversight. This has hardly changed since 2024, and the gap between human- and AI-made news appears in every country we surveyed.

This also remains true regardless of whether it is breaking news or reporting. People are even more uncomfortable with AI being used for commentary or opinion — formats where the expectation that a human should share their view is strongest.

Our findings raise several important questions for journalists and publishers navigating the rise of generative AI.

First, generative AI is becoming an increasingly significant information intermediaries, with a number of possible knock-on effects on news. People are using AI to reduce friction by getting “good enough” outputs for some information needs — and skipping legacy platforms, established publishers, and the time and effort it takes to find the best available information. Discovery, brand attribution, data collection, and monetization all grow more challenging for publishers in this context.

Second, even though use continues to grow, the public remains skeptical of AI in news. This isn’t just a challenge for newsrooms’ internal adoption. It also shows that publishers using AI need to communicate to audiences what’s in it for them.

Third, beyond news, our findings speak to wider debates about AI’s societal impact. As in other studies on this topic, we find that uptake of AI is uneven. Weekly use is more common among people in professional occupations than those in entry-level or service occupations. Most people rely on free — usually less advanced — AI models.

The differences in how — not just whether — people use AI may become a new dividing line. As AI researcher and OpenAI cofounder Andrej Karpathy recently argued, there is a widening “gap in shared understanding of LLM capability,” with most people not seeing what has left those inside AI firms both excited and worried.

Expectations of what AI will do differ far more between daily users and people who have never used it than between any other groups in our survey. When two groups form very different views of the same technology because they have such different starting points, there will likely be consequences.

This doesn’t matter just for people’s individual choices around whether and how to make use of AI in their own lives. It also matters where AI is adopted and governed through collective decisions — in public services, for example — that apply to those who do not use it.

Open Real Estate is positioning itself as a centralized listing entry and distribution layer for agents, MLSs and portals. Industry voices say the effort could accelerate RESO adoption, but unclear economics and MLS specific data requirements may create added workflow overhead.  AI Summary

No one knows yet what to make of Open Real Estate.  Not even one of the companies asked to help build it.

Ocusell Founder and CEO Hayden Rieveschl said his company has been under an NDA with Open Real Estate for “many, many months.”

Ocusell already offers essentially what Open Real Estate’s founders have stated they want to build. A company FAQ states: “Ocusell gives brands & brokers a single, standardized listing workflow that protects data, ensures compliance and publishes listings across every system in their tech stack and MLS they belong to.”

The company announced an integration with BrightMLS on November 3, the press release for which even referenced Open Real Estate.

“The launch arrives as the Open Real Estate (ORE) brokerage coalition actively conducts a national technology vendor RFP to achieve ‘input once, distribute everywhere’ listing entry. With Ocusell already live across 30 MLSs and OneList, powered by Ocusell, now live at the nation’s largest MLS, Ocusell is uniquely positioned to turn that vision into reality,” the release stated.

Rieveschl’s agreement limits what he can share about the end product, though on that matter he admits he’s also in the dark.

“In regards to what Open RE has pitched, yeah, I mean, essentially what they pitched is a single point of entry with, you know, full status listings, proactive compliance accommodating entirely to, you know, the MLS or multiple MLSs’ rule sets,” he said. “And obviously, Ocusell is the only proven solution that’s been in production for many years with over 30 plus MLSs at this point that can accommodate that request.”

“What’s no secret is that the broker community has wanted this for decades,” he said.

Rieveschl said the founding team — AGNT Inc. (eXp Realty and NextHome), HomeServices of America, Keller Williams Realty and Real REMAX Group — didn’t present him with a fleshed-out vision for a final outcome. “But what I can say is that in all of the meetings I’ve been in with these guys, this has always been complimentary to the MLS.”

The group’s on-the-record comments appear to support Rieveschl, as they’ve been lacing appearances with terms like “seller choice,” “fair, open marketplace,” “transparency” and “fair housing.”

What is clear is that the four CEOs want to put listing data back under control of its producers and those licensed to wield it. And, when a revenue model emerges, the group said it will reward only agents and their brokerages.

A week in and knowing only those few things, Open Real Estate “secured 826 letters of support from real estate brokerage leaders across the country,” according to a HousingWire report. Prominent brands Brown Harris Stevens, Lamacchia Realty, Samson Properties and Windermere Real Estate were announced as part of the initial conglomerate.

However, goodwill and love letters don’t make you money. Nor do they provide actual operational clarity. And an effort this big is going to require a lot of both. So, what exactly is the plan?

On Andrew Flachner’s Playmakers podcast the day after the announcement, founding member and Real REMAX’s CEO Tamir Poleg called Open Real Estate a “data layer.”

“Essentially what Open Real Estate does is create a way for agents to send listings to one place and that place then distributes those listings to MLSs, to portals, to all of the stakeholders in the industry,” Poleg told Flachner. “It’s a data sharing platform on the residential side that serves obviously agents and should obviously benefit consumers.”

A 28-year-old man got a little bit more than he bargained for after a life-saving brain surgery. Karl (last name withheld for privacy) sees features of his grey tabby cat, Luna, on the human faces he encounters throughout the day. He’ll see Luna’s almond-shaped eye sockets, enlarged pupils, or stripes on humans, if her face is the first one he sees in the morning. Alternatively, he may see human features on the cat if he sees a human face at the beginning of his day.

Karl’s feline ordeal is detailed in a report published in the December 2026 issue of the journal Cortex. He has a form of palinopsia, a group of visual symptoms where an individual keeps seeing something even when it is not present. According to the Cleveland Clinic, palinopsia occurs when something disrupts vision-related processing in the brain. Many conditions and circumstances can cause it including migraine, seizures and epilepsy, strokes, some prescription drugs, and brain bleeds.

In Karl’s case, palinopsia leads to face distortions, a rare condition called prosopometamorphopsia (PMO), sometimes called  “demon face syndrome.” That scary nickname is because the distortions can sometimes look warped and scary like a demon.

Karl’s recurrent palinopsia began two years ago after part of Karl’s right temporal lobe was removed because the blood vessels between the arteries and veins there were tangled. This tangling can lead to a fatal brain bleed if left untreated. The right temporal lobe plays a critical role in face processing, so when Karl’s was partially removed it started messing with his ability to process faces.

“This case of Karl seeing his cat’s features in human faces and vice versa demonstrates that individual features can be pulled from memory and influence our ongoing perception,” Brad Duchaine, a study co-author at cognitive neurologist at Dartmouth College, said in a statement. Duchaine is also the principal investigator of the Social Perception Lab at Dartmouth, which studies these kinds of perception disorders.

After learning about Karl’s experience, Duchaine thought back to a 1986 study also published in Cortex. A woman on a bus saw a poodle on a passenger’s lap, and then saw that poodle’s face on every passenger riding the bus. The woman in that scenario had experienced a stroke nine months before.

However, in Karl’s situation, only components of faces are transferred rather than the entire face. To visually illustrate what Karl was experiencing, Duchaine prepared a sketch that he then fed into a large language model (LLM) to make the image look more realistic. He then shared the image with Karl, and the pair modified a set of images that are included in the report.

“AI provides researchers like us with the ability to generate more accurate visualizations of what people are experiencing when they’re having distorted visual perceptions,” Duchaine explained. “If you know what the person is experiencing, you can much better research what they’re dealing with and try to figure out ways to control it. And now the patient can also take part in that process of creating those visualizations.”

Duchaine’s lab has heard from over 250 people who experience face distortions like these, with some saying they have been told that they have a psychiatric problem. In a few of those cases, patients have been put on antipsychotics, when they are having visual problems, not a psychiatric issue.

The team plans to work with Karl in the future, to further probe when and if his condition changes over time and use similar AI tools to see what he is seeing. Understanding what he is seeing could help researchers better understand how to help people with this condition.

“The use of AI is also helpful for patients who want to convey not just to their healthcare professionals, but to their friends and family, what faces look like to them,” Duchaine concluded.
