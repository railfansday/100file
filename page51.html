It is claimed that the Hammers are among the clubs to have shown interest in the 22-year-old, but Spanish giants Atletico Madrid and Premier League side Aston Villa also have eyes on the goalkeeper. River Plate have been keen to extend Beltran’s contract, which expires in 2027, but they are willing to sell if $15m (£11.2m) is tabled.

The Argentine club would be willing to sell in January too, which will suit the Hammers as they look to resolve their goalkeeping issues. Alongside Hermansen and Areola, academy graduate Finlay Herrick is an option available to new boss Scott Parker.

Beltran is a product of the River Plate academy and has broken into the first team setup over the past 18 months. He made his international debut for Argentina in the summer but is also eligible to represent Italy.

Over the course of 2026, Beltran has locked down the starting berth with one of his country’s most prestigious sides. Given that his contract runs out at the end of 2027, it is little wonder that interest from West Ham, Atletico Madrid and Aston Villa is emerging.

In Liga Professional, Argentina’s top flight, Beltran has recorded 50 saves and an xG prevented of 3.93. It shows that while he isn’t necessarily highly active in terms of shot-stopping behind a tight defence, he is doing a lot of individual work to deny goal-bound shots when he is called upon.

He is solid in terms of his short-distance distribution with 259 successful passes, but he has a long ball accuracy of only 27.8%. This is an area of his game to improve, but at only 22, the WHUFC target has plenty of time to grow in this area (all stats via FotMob).

Beltran’s success in Argentina indicates he would be an upgrade on West Ham’s current options. Interest from Atletico Madrid and Aston Villa emphasises the high regard he is held in too.

Importantly, Beltran’s age means that he could become the Hammers’ long-term No. 1. He has both time and room to grow, mainly in his distribution, so he can become an important player off the bat while growing into a valuable financial asset in the years to come.

At around £11m, he wouldn’t be a cheap signing. But, at that price, WHUFC would be signing a goalkeeper upgrade who can only grow in value as he progresses. Beltran feels like a good target and a smart option in the effort to tighten up in goal.

This New Electric Paver Can Lay Down 1,200 Tonnes Of Asphalt A Day

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The construction world is getting quieter by the day, thanks to the gradual electrification of big rigs traditionally powered by diesel engines. After trucks, diggers, and cranes, asphalt pavers are now going electric.

Switzerland’s Ammann, which is known for its asphalt and concrete plant equipment, has transformed one of its mainstream diesel asphalt pavers to run on electric power. Thanks to a Volvo Penta powertrain, the eABG 4820 is now one of the largest electric pavers on the market, capable of laying up to 500 tonnes per hour and a maximum output of 1,200 tonnes per day with a charging stop.

Without intermediate charging, the machine can put down between 350 and 450 tonnes of asphalt.

Before getting into the details, the biggest advantage of an electric paver over a diesel rig is the significantly lower noise output. A 99-kilowatt electric motor is powered by a massive 270–kilowatt-hour battery pack arranged in three separate units. Volvo Penta supplies the batteries, motors, power electronics, and control technology, while Ammann used its expertise in hydraulic-powered machinery to put it all together.

“It comes down to energy management and architecture,” said Philipp Bramer, head of the e-paver development team at Ammann. “The electric driveline doesn’t just power the machine. It communicates seamlessly with the hydraulics. Simultaneously, the intelligent control system makes sure that the system supplies exactly the right amount of energy to the machine.”

The nickel-cobalt-aluminum oxide (NCA) traction batteries can be charged from a CCS2 connector at up to 150 kW in roughly one and a half hours, while AC charging at up to 22 kW can replenish the battery in 12 hours. Amman says that the battery provides enough juice for an entire work shift, but that an external power supply is responsible for its proprietary ABG e+ screed heat system.

The electric paver can handle widths of up to 21.3 feet (6.5 meters), making it a good choice for most paving scenarios, especially in emission- and noise-sensitive job sites. We’ve all seen and heard a diesel asphalt paver in operation—I vividly remember when the street I grew up on got its first layer of asphalt decades ago, and while I was fascinated by all the machinery, I still remember that the site was extremely loud.

This, coupled with the fact that an electric paver emits up to 70% less carbon dioxide into the atmosphere, should make diesel construction equipment a thing of the past—at least in crowded, already polluted urban areas.

What’s more, because an electric powertrain has significantly fewer moving parts compared to a diesel lump, a lot of maintenance tasks simply disappear, along with their associated costs. At the same time, though, construction companies need to install charging equipment at their depots and find ways of topping up their rigs on the jobsite.

A federal judge in New York denied a preliminary injunction sought by sports prediction market platform Novig, ruling that its sports-related event contracts do not qualify as swaps under the Commodity Exchange Act.

According to a Tuesday court filing, U.S. District Judge Colleen McMahon of the Southern District of New York concluded that the state may apply its gambling and sports-wagering laws to Novig’s activity. The decision allows New York to require Novig either to obtain a state gambling license or prohibit the listing and trading of sports event contracts on its platform.

Ludlow Exchange, LLC, the legal entity behind Novig, received the Commodity Futures Trading Commission's designation as a contract market in June 2026 and began offering sports event contracts to New York customers in August.

On Aug. 5, 2026, the company sued New York Attorney General Letitia James and officials at the New York State Gaming Commission, arguing that federal commodities law preempts state regulation of its products. It contended that the CFTC has "exclusive jurisdiction" over event contracts, which qualify as swaps under the act.

Judge McMahon rejected that argument at the preliminary injunction stage. She wrote that Novig’s sports event contracts “bear more than a passing resemblance to sports betting” and fall outside the CEA’s definition of a swap.

"The Court concludes that Novig’s contracts do not depend on events or contingencies that are ‘associated with a potential financial, economic, or commercial consequence’ within the meaning of § 1a(47)(A)(ii). Accordingly, they are not ‘swaps’ under the CEA," the filing said.

The ruling follows a similar decision earlier this year by another New York federal judge that denied Kalshi's request to block state authorities from enforcing gambling laws on its prediction market platform. Novig is also litigating the same issue in multiple other states, as Kalshi, Polymarket, and other prediction market platforms are doing.

"NY Attorney General Letitia James could literally cross the street and file a state-court civil enforcement action against Novig as soon as today," sports betting and gaming lawyer Daniel Wallach wrote on X.

Wallach added that he's seeing a larger shift where state regulators may move from defending their enforcement authority to exercising it proactively in state court. The lawyer said federal courts within the Second Circuit have now issued 10 rulings in sports prediction-market litigation, all of which have favored the states.

a pair of pontoons carries this 2.6-square-meter floating cabin, whose miniature comforts are put to the test during a three-day journey through washington.

American explorer and builder David Rule has taken the tiny living movement onto the water with TinyTanic, a fully functional houseboat measuring just 2.4 meters long and 1.1 meters wide. With a total footprint of 2.6 square meters, the miniature vessel accommodates a sleeping area, kitchen, running water and an electric propulsion system. Inspired by fellow creator Zach Fowler’s 3.25-square-meter floating home, Rule set out to build something even smaller without sacrificing the essential comforts needed for a journey on the water. He describes the result as the world’s smallest functional houseboat, although the claim has not been independently certified.

Rather than using a conventional boat hull, Rule constructed TinyTanic on a pair of modular pontoons, creating a stable platform for a lightweight timber cabin. Cedar, pine and plywood form the main structure, while an aluminum roof protects the interior from the elements. Round porthole windows punctuate the wooden walls, complemented by a large transparent panel at the front that frames views of the surrounding landscape. At the rear, a zippered panel adapted from a camping canopy can be opened to connect the cabin with a small outdoor deck. The entire construction is compact enough to fit into the bed of a pickup truck.

Inside the miniature houseboat, every available centimeter serves a purpose. The cabin has an interior height of approximately 1.06 meters, allowing its occupant to sit upright but ruling out the possibility of standing. A custom sleeping platform occupies one side, while a narrow counter integrates a small refrigerator, single-burner butane stove and sink. A 12-volt pump draws water from the surrounding river or lake through a purification system, while wastewater is collected in a container beneath the counter. LED lighting, storage compartments and a ventilation fan complete the interior, giving the tiny wooden enclosure the essentials of a considerably larger recreational vessel.

Power comes from two 100-watt solar panels mounted on the roof, connected to a 100-amp-hour marine lithium battery. The system supplies electricity to the refrigerator, lighting, ventilation and charging outlets, as well as the boat’s propulsion system. At the stern, Rule installed a Minn Kota electric trolling motor delivering approximately 133 newtons of thrust, enough to move the lightweight vessel through calm waters at a leisurely pace. The stove, meanwhile, operates on butane rather than electricity. The arrangement makes TinyTanic largely self-sufficient for short excursions, although its limited battery capacity places practical restrictions on how far and how quickly it can travel.

To determine whether his creation could function beyond the workshop, Rule transported TinyTanic to Washington State for a three-day journey along the Pend Oreille River. Starting near Metaline Falls, he navigated approximately 19 kilometers toward Peewee Falls, traveling through a rugged canyon surrounded by forested mountains. Along the way, the builder used the compact kitchen to prepare meals, fished from the rear deck and spent his nights inside the wooden cabin. Despite its unusual proportions, the pontoon structure remained stable throughout the expedition, while the zippered rear enclosure provided protection during periods of rain.

The journey also exposed the inevitable compromises of fitting an entire floating home into such a small footprint. Cloudy conditions reduced solar charging, and the electric motor’s continuous energy demands forced Rule to conserve battery power and rely more heavily on the river’s current. Nevertheless, TinyTanic completed its maiden voyage, demonstrating that its compact design could accommodate several days of basic living on the water. The project does not attempt to compete with conventional houseboats in comfort, speed or endurance. Instead, it explores how much of a home can be compressed into a functioning vessel before space becomes the one luxury that simply cannot be included.

Taiwan Creative Content Fest Takeaways: ‘Avatar’ VFX House Brings Its AI Ambitions to Taipei as Character IP Booms

Taiwan Creative Content Fest (TCCF) wrapped its 2026 edition in Taipei with a reshaped show floor, as animation and character IP companies moved to the front of the market.

They made up the largest exhibitor group, at 29 of 107 exhibitors, up from 19 in 2025, according to post-event figures from organizer the Taiwan Creative Content Agency (TAICCA). Of this year’s exhibitors, 69 were Taiwanese and 38 came from overseas, compared with 117 exhibitors in total last year. Elsewhere on the floor, television and media networks rose from 20 to 22 exhibitors, and new media, streaming and entertainment companies from six to eight. Film and TV production and distribution companies dropped from 22 to 16, digital tech, VFX and immersive media from 15 to eight, and publishing, comics and webtoons from 12 to five.

The Pitching program presented 93 projects and stories, including entries from TAICCA School, to industry participants from 28 countries. TAICCA is still compiling buyer attendance and the value of business done.

The headline deal came on opening day, Nov. 6, when Weta FX, the New Zealand visual effects company behind “The Lord of the Rings” and “Avatar,” signed a memorandum of understanding with TAICCA aimed at making Taiwan an AI hub for Asia’s content industries. Weta FX will partner with Taiwanese animation and VFX outfit Moonshine Studio and will set up a technology platform to back Taiwanese film and TV projects, along with exchanges and internships for young Taiwanese filmmakers.

Weta FX CEO Daniel Seah said studio legal departments had ruled out any use of generative AI on their projects early last year. By the turn of the year, clients were asking about Weta’s AI strategy, mostly to find out how much money the technology could save.

“It’s, ‘If you don’t have an AI strategy, don’t bother to work with us,’” Seah said of the period since the third quarter of this year.

Seah said AI had become a national strategy in Taiwan on the back of the island’s semiconductor industry, and Moonshine had been moving quickly as a result.

Japanese studios brought their own AI work to Taipei. Toei, the studio behind “Kamen Rider” and the Super Sentai series that became “Power Rangers,” has adopted rules for generative AI and is already using it for backgrounds, president Yoshimura Fumio said. “I believe AI is not something that takes away jobs, but a tool that expands the possibilities of expression and business,” he said.

At a TCCF panel on AI in animation, OLM Digital board director Yotsukura Tatsuo presented a coloring tool, developed under a Japanese government-backed research project, that learns a show’s look from three hand-painted frames. On the same panel, Charles Beirnaert, chief creative officer of French studio Blue Spirit, said the “Blue Eye Samurai” studio is barred from using AI on its Netflix work. Taiwanese technology company imCore Corp. launched Core Studio, an AI filmmaking platform with more than 24 specialized agents, at TCCF on Nov. 7.

“From a television broadcaster’s perspective, we’ve seen a significant increase in Vietnamese buyers this year,” said Grace Chan, a program planner in acquisitions and sales at Taiwan Television Enterprise (TTV). “Last year, Korean buyers and potential partners made up the largest group.”

Southeast Asia ran through the program. K7 Media’s Trang Nguyen presented a study of Indonesia, Southeast Asia’s biggest film market with box office revenue of $352.9 million in 2025, and pointed to horror as a natural meeting ground for Taiwanese and Indonesian producers. Thai director Thiti Srinuan explained how music video revenue bankrolled much of Taibaan Studio’s film universe before “The Undertaker” grossed more than THB700 million ($20.8 million) in Thailand. The top Pitching prize, the $35,000 TAICCA × CNC Feature Film Grand Award, went to “Somewhere in the South,” a Malaysian feature written and directed by Tan Ce Ding and produced by Anthony Chen, the Singaporean director of “Ilo Ilo.”

Japan was also looking at Taiwan. Cinematographer Wayne Lo, at TCCF for the first time, met several Japanese teams and found that up-and-coming Japanese filmmakers see the market and working directly with TAICCA as routes to getting projects made. “At the pitching sessions, I also sensed growing interest in Taiwanese content in Japan,” Lo said, though he noted that “Japan still offers a wider range of film genres than Taiwan.”

Toei’s Yoshimura said Japanese films were the most-released titles in Taiwan in 2025 and second only to U.S. films at the box office. He wants long-term tokusatsu partnerships and international co-productions with Taiwan.

The jump in animation and character IP exhibitors was echoed in the sessions.

Toei is launching a games label whose first three titles are originals rather than adaptations of its existing IP. “We aim to bring game creators’ talent on board over the long term as a new vein of IP creation, and make games the starting point for stories in place of novels and comics,” Yoshimura said.

Microdrama producers are thinking the same way. Sawamura Naomichi, founder of Japanese microdrama app Bump, said the company is considering adapting its microdramas into manga or animation.

Publishing, comics and webtoon exhibitors were down, but books and comics still drew interest through Pitching. Mirror Fiction’s “The Three Witches of Shetou” won both the TAICCA Award: Best Story and the friDay Video Original Story Award. Chang Chen-yu, producer at World Softest Production Film Co., said Taiwanese comic artist Gao Yan’s “The Song About Green” drew considerable attention after its TCCF presentation, and meeting requests were up on last year.

“Companies from Thailand, South Korea, Singapore and even Europe have expressed interest in collaborating,” Chang said. “This year, I’ve felt more strongly that creative talent and resources are concentrated in Asia, but the distinctiveness and audience appeal of the material remain key.”

TAICCA’s own audience research backs the push. Among viewers of Taiwanese film and TV, 40% had also watched Taiwanese screen adaptations, and the study flagged romance as a promising genre for cross-platform development, with links to comics, popular music and mobile games.

Telecoms Put Money Into Taiwanese Content

TAICCA presented the slates of its four joint-venture funds, which have backed 27 projects and drawn NT$4.9 billion in private capital alongside NT$740 million from the agency, about $180 million in all. Two of those ventures are partnerships with Taiwanese telecoms.

“Beyond OTT [streaming] platforms, the sharp decline in advertising has been the most visible challenge for television stations. Acquisition budgets, meanwhile, have not come down,” TTV’s Chan said. “Through the connections made at the market, we hope to find content that suits our needs and explore original productions and co-productions to diversify risk.”

The Chunghwa Digital Cultural and Creative Fund, majority owned by Chunghwa Telecom, totals NT$1 billion ($32 million) and backs both projects and companies. Enjoy Entertainment, TAICCA’s partnership with telecom Far EasTone, invested in 15 projects in its first 10 months. Its slate also includes a Taiwanese remake of Korean series “Our Blues,” which launches globally on Netflix on Dec. 3. The momomini Kids Content Fund focuses on children’s content, which COO Phoebe Tsai said had not previously been treated as a distinct investment category in Taiwan.

One of Enjoy’s investments ties the week together. “The Rope Curse 4: Kuntilanak,” a Taiwan-Indonesia horror collaboration that Toei also invested in, took NT$80 million ($2.6 million) at the Taiwanese box office, a franchise record.

It is true that Netanyahu told the Economist in January that he hoped to “taper off” Israeli dependence on U.S. military aid over the next decade. But he has given no indication of any agreement for military assistance to terminate as soon as this year. Feeling the pressure, Netanyahu’s office is now spinning the end of U.S. military assistance as a “transition out from aid towards partnership and mutual investment.”

The backlash in Israel was immediate. Trump’s announcement raises critical questions regarding what political price was negotiated—and specifically, whether any agreement to end U.S. aid served as the quid pro quo for Trump’s endorsement of Netanyahu in Israel’s election.

Former Prime Minister Ehud Barak accused Netanyahu of concocting “a stinking deal” that traded U.S. military assistance for a presidential endorsement, concluding that Netanyahu had “exceeded his authority” and was “utterly unfit to lead a country.” Opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot, who may yet replace Netanyahu as prime minister, was less conspiratorial but equally scathing. “Israel’s special relationship with the United States is a central component of its national security,” he wrote on X. “The fact that the security establishment is learning, together with Israeli citizens, about a change in the strategic relationship through the media shows just how much Benjamin Netanyahu’s conduct is a real danger.”

That Trump chose to make this announcement on Election Day speaks volumes about the collapse of Israel’s standing across the U.S. political spectrum. He evidently believed that it would resonate with voters amid rising public anger over inflation and the cost of living. The question of why a high-tech powerhouse requires billions in taxpayer-funded military aid carries growing political weight, even if much of the aid is reinvested in the U.S. defense industry.

The real elephant in the room, however, is the ongoing war with Iran and anger over U.S. support for Israel. Prominent Republicans, including Vice President J.D. Vance, have openly identified the conflict as the principal catalyst for the party’s electoral thrashing. Writing on X in the immediate aftermath of the vote, Vance broke ranks with traditional White House messaging, declaring bluntly: “The reason we lost is the war. … [E]veryone knows why we lost. And any honest conversation about where we go from here must start there.” He also claimed that voters wanted to see a change in the relationship with Israel. Vance highlighted internal polling showing that 2024 Trump voters who defected to the Democrats in the midterms specifically cited an end to the war, economic relief from soaring fuel prices, and a fundamental shift in Washington’s relationship with Israel as their primary demands. In an interview with CBS’s 60 Minutes on Sunday, Trump did not disagree with Vance’s observation that the Iran war caused the Republican defeat in the midterms, adding that “there is a great dislike, incredibly, for Israel.”

While it was Trump who launched a war against Iran in February, Netanyahu never concealed his desire for a joint campaign. Even following Operation Epic Fury, he has consistently tried to persuade Trump to renew the war, treating the Iranian threat not merely as an existential regional issue but as a political prop to distract the Israeli electorate from his leadership failures in the lead-up to and aftermath of Oct. 7, 2023.

Ahead of the midterms, the New York Times published a report claiming that Israel had shared intelligence with the United States and Gulf countries about possible Iranian plans to use chemical weapons against them. U.S. officials met the intelligence with skepticism, believing it was part of a coordinated effort by Netanyahu to push Trump toward renewed strikes.

Netanyahu’s rhetoric on Iran is riddled with glaring contradictions. Having publicly trumpeted historic success in dismantling Iran’s nuclear capabilities in June 2025, he has reversed course to manufacture an immediate, existential urge for military confrontation. On Oct. 25, Netanyahu posted an AI-generated image depicting a nuclear blast over Tel Aviv, warning voters that rejecting his leadership invited a “second Holocaust.” Political opponents accused him of manufacturing a crisis with Iran as an attempt to delay the election. This triggered an unprecedented rift with Israel Defense Forces Chief of Staff Eyal Zamir, who resisted political orders that risked sparking an immediate, unwarranted regional conflagration. Yair Golan of the Democrats declared that Netanyahu lacks “the necessary public trust” and “moral legitimacy or even basic capability” to lead such a campaign, while former Israeli Ambassador to Germany Jeremy Issacharoff noted that Netanyahu’s political machinations over Iran “severely damage Israel’s credibility abroad.”

During the opening session of the new Knesset on Tuesday, Netanyahu insisted that while his government had removed the immediate existential threat, Iran was renewing its nuclear program and had not given up on its intent to destroy Israel. He declared that the only way to remove this existential threat was by toppling the regime, trumpeting for good measure that his government had “changed the face of the Middle East.”

Netanyahu’s endgame is clearly at odds with the objectives of the Trump administration, which views military force primarily as leverage to reopen the Strait of Hormuz and force Tehran to climb down over its nuclear program. In his incendiary speech, Netanyahu also asserted that only a national unity government—presumably under his leadership—would enable Israel to meet the upcoming challenges, while accusing Israel’s Arab lawmakers of seeking the nation’s destruction. Eisenkot countered immediately, stating that Netanyahu’s “hate-filled” speech demonstrated that he was unfit for office. He has clearly failed to internalize the fact that, even among his erstwhile allies in the Republican party, his political standing is becoming increasingly toxic.

Netanyahu retains power for now as a caretaker prime minister while Eisenkot seeks to form a new government. Renewed hostilities with Iran in coordination with the United States would have been highly convenient for Netanyahu, as this could have delayed the opening of the newly elected parliament and formation of a new government. His overarching goal remains to block an opposition coalition that includes or is supported by Arab parties, who emerged as pivotal kingmakers in this election.

Deploying the Trump playbook to contest the election results, Netanyahu’s loyalist Shin Bet security chief, David Zini, has targeted Arab opposition lawmakers with unfounded investigations, raising grave constitutional alarm. Even though Eisenkot has received the mandate to form a government, his narrow victory and the opposition’s lingering hesitation toward joining forces with the Arab parties make coalition-building highly uncertain.

While right-wing factions in Israel view Netanyahu’s relationship with Trump and a renewed U.S.-Israel campaign against Iran as a strategic win, the long-term fallout could be severe. In putting all his eggs into the Republican basket, Netanyahu has wrecked U.S. bipartisan support for Israel. Following a decisive Democratic shift in Congress, he finds himself dangerously exposed: He faces fierce critics on the American left and an angry Republican base looking for a political scapegoat.

Trump is now signaling that a renewal of war is “highly unlikely” if talks with Iran show progress. Yet after last week’s midterm collapse, his close ally Chris Ruddy noted on the BBC, there was an 80 percent chance that the U.S. president could still authorize military action against Tehran. Netanyahu thus retains every incentive to gamble on regional escalation in order to entrench his political survival, even at the direct expense of Israel’s democratic resilience and its international standing. It appears that he has nowhere left to run.

An unpredictable lame-duck U.S. president may feel that he has little left to lose, but for Netanyahu and an increasingly isolated Israeli public, the stakes could not be higher.

As high food costs and cuts to food assistance squeeze families’ budgets, concerns are growing about whether low-income parents will be able to keep putting food on the table.

But these financial pressures threaten to do more than increase hunger. They also risk harming children’s diets and health in the long run — by making nutritious foods not only harder to afford, but also increasingly risky for parents to buy.

To understand why, consider the most universal of parenting frustrations: picky eating. Most parents experience it, but the stakes vary widely. For well-off parents, watching a child push away a helping of vegetables is annoying but manageable. A carrot left untouched one day can simply mean trying again the next.

But for parents living close to the bone, that same rejected carrot is more than frustrating. It represents money that could have been spent on something their child would have actually eaten.

Simply put: Children’s food refusals come at a cost — one that few cash-strapped families can afford.

Such financial risks profoundly shape how low-income parents feed their kids, as sociologists have documented for years. A decade ago, Caitlin Daniel first showed how the risk of wasting money on rejected nutritious foods led low-income parents to prioritize buying less healthy items they knew their kids would eat. Years later, one of us (Priya) found that catering to kids’ preferences was also driven in part by parents’ earlier bouts of food insecurity: Determined to spare their children those experiences, caregivers often relied on familiar, comforting foods they knew would fill their kids’ stomachs.

Our newest study adds another dimension to this picture. Low-income parents across three states described going to considerable lengths to prioritize children’s nutrition, often at the expense of their own diets.

At the same time, to conserve resources and keep dinnertime from turning into a battle, they paid close attention to their kids’ likes and prioritized buying what they knew their children would eat. As one mother put it, she’d rather her daughter be “happy and fed than sad and hungry.”

Within such severe financial constraints, catering to children’s preferences is strategic. But it can also undermine children’s diets and health over time. That’s because raising kids to be healthy eaters is a long game: Children’s tastes develop gradually as they encounter and become familiar with different foods, and young children often need to try unfamiliar, healthier ones — like bitter vegetables — multiple times before accepting them.

That’s why pediatricians and nutrition experts routinely encourage parents to keep offering fruits and vegetables, even when kids turn them down. It’s smart advice, but it assumes parents can shoulder the costs of following it.

For families with little money to spare, doing so has always been a gamble. And today, it’s becoming an even bigger one. Groceries now cost nearly a third more in dollar terms than they did in 2020. Meanwhile, participation in SNAP — the nation’s largest food assistance program — has fallen by over 5 million people, including an estimated 2 million children, since summer 2025.

These economic pressures are now showing up in parents’ food decisions. A national survey found that more than half of low-income parents reported cutting back on groceries, while over two-thirds worried about having to choose between paying bills and buying healthy food for their children.

Forcing parents into such trade-offs doesn’t just threaten their ability to keep children fed. It also undermines their efforts to help children eat — and learn to enjoy — nutritious foods.

Fortunately, there are ways to address both problems.

First and foremost, we need to ensure children have enough to eat. That means restoring access to food assistance. The next Congress should reverse the cuts to SNAP enacted through H.R. 1 and stop SNAP benefit costs from being shifted to states — a shift that could jeopardize continued access to a program millions of families depend on to put food on the table. To make a bill reversing the cuts veto-proof, it should be a bipartisan effort.

But we also need to invest in programs that support parents’ efforts to feed their children nutritious foods, including incentives that make those purchases more affordable and less financially risky. Protecting and strengthening WIC’s fruit and vegetable benefit and expanding produce incentives like Double Up Food Bucks for SNAP recipients can help families stretch their grocery dollars further, giving them more room to buy nutritious foods their children might not eat the first time around.

Schools and childcare programs must also be critical partners in this work. Beyond providing nutritious meals, they can give kids regular opportunities to encounter and try healthier foods without leaving parents to shoulder the full financial burden. Expanding initiatives like the Department of Agriculture’s Fresh Fruit and Vegetable Program and Farm to School programs, which help introduce children to new foods through fresh produce, taste tests, gardens, and other activities, can help.

Nutrition experts are right to encourage parents to keep introducing healthy foods, even when their children refuse them. But advice that families can’t afford to follow simply won’t cut it. We need food policies that not only ensure children have enough to eat, but also give parents the resources and support to help them develop healthy eating habits that last.

Bob covers the money in health care, focusing on health insurance and hospitals. His stories delve into Medicare Advantage, opaque prescription drug benefits, and how much executives actually make. This past August, Joe Hancock, a Kentucky plumbing and heating contractor, opened a just-delivered box packed with hundreds of sheets of paper. Leafing through them, he read seven words no small business owner wants to see: “A lawsuit has been filed against you.”

The complaint, from air ambulance firm PHI Health, claimed Hancock was on the hook for almost $27,000 in medical helicopter bills. They were tied to one of his now-retired employees, who needed an emergency flight after a heart attack.

Hancock, who owns Whitehead-Hancock Plumbing, Heating & Cooling, a family-run business in Frankfort, Ky., that has been around for more than a century, didn’t understand why he was being roped into a lawsuit that featured 31 other companies — for medical charges he was never aware of.

“I was terrified,” Hancock said. “I was like, ‘What in the hell is going on here?’”

He’s not alone. The PHI lawsuit highlights an evolving tactic by health care providers in a raging battle over out-of-network bills. Previously, providers targeted health insurance companies to pay outstanding amounts that were awarded through federal mediation. Now, they increasingly are dragging the insurers’ clients — employers — into court, claiming they are responsible for bills that insurers haven’t fully paid or have delayed paying. The practice is introducing a new shock that could further torch the affordability of workers’ health coverage.

“These big suits naming dozens of employers at a time is really an escalation,” said Garrett Hohimer, a vice president at the Business Group on Health. “And it will cause employers to incur expenses that [come directly] out of their health care plans.”

A 2022 law called the No Surprises Act shielded patients from the financial trauma of many types of unexpected medical bills, but it has also given rise to a vast industry that’s driving up costs across the health care system. The law uses an arbitration process to determine how much health care providers should get paid for out-of-network emergency care, like helicopter rides, for people who have some type of commercial health coverage. STAT has reported that providers are winning most of these arbitration awards, which often are far higher than the amounts in-network providers receive for the same services.

Already, high payments tied to this process have added approximately $20 billion in extra costs to the health care system, Georgetown University researchers have estimated. A large portion of those costs inevitably come out of the bank accounts of America’s employers and consequently lead to higher premiums, emphasizing how even well-intentioned health reforms can add to the financial pressure that’s crushing workers and businesses.

PHI Health, a national chain, is arguably the most aggressive company suing employers, which it claims are not “passive” bystanders in insurers’ refusal to fully pay awards. Other regional physician practices have sued some employers as well. But PHI and its army of attorneys have filed lawsuits against hundreds of companies, demanding they collectively pay tens of millions of dollars in unpaid out-of-network claims that it won in the federal arbitration process.

PHI isn’t just suing small, local firms like Hancock’s. It’s also taking legal action against some of the largest corporations — Amazon, Walmart, Eli Lilly, United Airlines, General Motors, PepsiCo, State Farm, and Toyota, to name a few — as well as unions, counties, cities, states, public school districts, and universities.

PHI has sued other health care organizations, including hospitals, nursing homes, and medical billing firms. It also has gone after nonprofits, such as the Catholic Diocese of Cleveland. In each instance, a worker or family member at these companies took an emergency medical helicopter, often at a cost of tens of thousands of dollars per trip.

Unpacking the business — and secretive inner workings — of the U.S. health care industry

In some cases, PHI wasn’t sure what company it should be suing, but sued anyway. Several lawsuits named specific health insurers and employers as defendants, and then added “ABC CORP” or “XYZ CORP” as a placeholder. If the insurer or employer “is not in fact the plan sponsor … that covers the patient transported by PHI, then the identity of the plan sponsor is unknown to PHI, and this action is instead brought against ABC CORP,” one lawsuit reads.

Katie Keith and Ellie DeGarmo are attorneys at Georgetown’s Center for Health Policy and the Law who have tracked No Surprises Act lawsuits, including those filed by PHI. They said the scores of cases reinforce the bloat in the health care system: above-market prices that providers are winning for not participating in an insurance company’s network, attempts from insurers to not pay at all or profit off lowball offers, and a booming revenue stream for arbitrators.

But trying to wring money out of employers — and, ultimately, from their workers — is a stark divergence from the public statements of physician groups and air ambulance companies, which have painted health insurers as the bad guys for not following the rules.

“It kind of undercuts the narrative that this is provider versus insurance company,” Keith said. “In this case, this is about provider versus everyone who pays for health care and is now stuck with the bill for [arbitration] awards that have gone totally off the rails.”

STAT reached out to more than two dozen employers that were listed in PHI’s lawsuits. Most did not respond to inquiries. Of those that did, most declined to comment.

“Because this matter involves pending litigation … we will not be providing any interviews,” said Clint Mitchell, the division superintendent of Spotsylvania County Public Schools in Fredericksburg, Va. PHI Health is suing Spotsylvania County’s school system for almost $28,000.

Jay Litten, the town manager of Bridgewater, Va., told STAT the town’s health insurer, Anthem Blue Cross Blue Shield, retained counsel and was handling the case. PHI sued Bridgewater in October for more than $37,000 in unpaid awards.

PHI has boasted about its success in the law’s arbitration process. In 2024, it told prospective investors that it was “able to maintain our profitability” by winning more than three out of every four arbitration claims, and it expected “this trend of favorable” outcomes from the process would continue.

The company has some private equity backing, according to financial filings from when the company considered going public in 2023. Its largest shareholder as of 2023 was Q Investments, a family office that has a private equity firm.

PHI declined to make anyone available for an interview. In a statement, CEO Scott McCarty defended the company’s lawsuits, saying the employers PHI has sued “have a duty to their employees and a responsibility to health care providers in their communities to abide by the law and pay for services delivered.”

McCarty said health insurers are asking their employer clients to choose between paying the unpaid arbitration amounts or authorizing the insurer to continue refusing payment.

“Once an employer is on notice of nonpayment and affirmatively endorses that practice, it is no longer a passive bystander,” McCarty said in the statement. “It has made a conscious decision to allow its insurance company not to pay binding federal awards in violation of federal law.”

In PHI’s lawsuits, the company alleges insurers, and now employers, are using an “unlawful scheme to ‘low pay, late pay, or no pay’ providers, including PHI.” All the cases are still pending.

The company also claims insurers float the money from unpaid awards, which are binding decisions, on the stock market as a way to earn extra interest or investment income.

James Gelfand, CEO of the ERISA Industry Committee, a lobbying group that represents large employers, said companies don’t condone insurers delaying payments as a way to make extra money. But he said under the federal law known as ERISA, employers are legally obligated to make sure they spend their health plan assets wisely on behalf of workers. If employers believe the awards are tied to ineligible claims — or are egregiously high compared with what they normally would pay — those payments could violate that obligation.

As a result, the employers’ health insurers “are operating with our blessing when we say, ‘Hey, if the [arbitration] award is inappropriate and wrong, do not pay it,’” he said. “How can we claim to be responsible stewards of people’s money if we’re just like, ‘Oh, here you go!’ That would be crazy.”

Jenny Goins, who is CEO of the National Alliance of Healthcare Purchaser Coalitions and used to oversee health benefits for Kentucky state employees, said “slow-walking the payments” to providers is one of the only ways employers can push back if they believe the awards are based on ineligible or incomplete claims. Medical claims are not eligible for arbitration if, for example, the employer or its health plan doesn’t cover a certain service or if there is certain information or data missing.

PHI has frequently named Anthem Blue Cross Blue Shield, owned by Elevance Health, as a defendant in the lawsuits against employers. Elevance did not make anyone available for an interview. The company said in a statement that PHI is charging prices for air ambulance services that are 700% of what Medicare pays and mileage rates that are 1,500% more than Medicare rates.

“We have already paid PHI substantial amounts for services provided to our members,” Elevance spokesperson Janey Kiryluik said in a statement. “We dispute whether additional amounts are legally owed for claims that were ineligible” for the arbitration process.

PHI said only arbitrators can determine the eligibility of claims. It added that the rates Medicare pays don’t cover the full cost of emergency air care, nor can those prices be considered during the arbitration process.

The bigger issue revealed by the lawsuits, Goins said, is how “broken” the law has become, even though she believed it was well-intended. The issue has now attracted interest in Congress, where lawmakers are proposing various plans to overhaul the arbitration process.

“Greedy people see an opportunity to make more money,” Goins said. “I also see there’s a true opportunity to shine a light on this.”

In September, PHI dropped Hancock’s company as a defendant, one month after its attorneys served him with court papers. Hancock said his heating, cooling, and plumbing business of 30 employees is not self-insured — meaning he buys his health insurance from Anthem, which takes on all the risk of his employees’ medical care.

PHI’s McCarty blamed Anthem in a statement, saying it “does not always receive clear or consistent information from insurers identifying whether a plan is self-funded or fully insured. In this case, after the lawsuit was filed, Anthem’s counsel confirmed that Whitehead-Hancock was fully insured, and PHI promptly dismissed it, without settlement.”

Other businesses are getting released from PHI’s lawsuits, too. It’s unclear if they were wrongfully lumped in, like Hancock’s company, or if they are paying PHI’s demands to clear a costly legal headache.

PHI’s legal team “threw a handful of darts, and they’re going to try to rattle as many cages as they can,” Hancock said. “It’s not right. It shouldn’t be legal.”

In the nearly 30 years since they opened their doors, Relic have been an enormously influential studio. Responsible for lauded RTS series Homeworld, Dawn of War, and Company of Heroes, they also dabbled in other genres with third-person shooter The Outfit and the original Warhammer 40,000: Space Marine. Yet, despite this history, in 2024, when Relic were negotiating their sale from Sega to UK investment firm Emona Capita, CEO Justin Dowdeswell knew the rights to Company of Heroes had to be part of the deal. "Without it we would have been a dead studio," he tells me.

Without naming names Dowdeswell points out that there are "numerous examples" of studios that weren't able to survive on their name alone. Not only did Relic need the ability to make more games in the series that they were known for, but they also needed the income from back catalogue sales a series like Company of Heroes provides.

Dowdeswell joined Relic shortly after Sega acquired the studio in 2013. This was in the immediate aftermath of publisher THQ's bankruptcy auction, where studios and franchises were sold off to the highest bidder. While Sega bought the studio, they didn't buy the licenses to everything Relic had created. "The other games that Relic made – Homeworld, Impossible Creatures, The Outfit... formative games in many people's memory – they were all split up, scattered," Dowdeswell says. "I think that was... I won't say a tragedy, but it's unfortunate because Relic is the home of those games. It would have been great for them to stay with the studio."

Just as a hint of the messy history of acquisitions and auctions: this year we see Dawn of War 4 coming from studio King Art Games and published by Deep Silver. Deep Silver are a subdivision of Plaion, formerly called Koch Media, which was acquired by Embracer Group back when they were called THQ Nordic. Not that they were actually the THQ that originally published Relic's Dawn of War, Nordic Games renamed themselves THQ Nordic after they bought the THQ trademark at auction.

As for Homeworld, Borderlands developer Gearbox picked up the license to the sci-fi RTS in the bankruptcy auction, then contracted Blackbird Interactive's support on the remasters, before handing the latter lead development on Homeworld 3. That chain of events does have a more clear throughline to Relic's past however, as Blackbird was created by two of Relic's co-founders Rob Cunningham and Jon Aaron Kambeitz. (Speaking of studios struggling without the series they made their name with, Blackbird was on the verge of bankruptcy before Gearbox got in touch with the Homeworld work. So we very nearly existed in a world where they never got to make the brilliant Homeworld: Deserts of Kharak and Hardspace Shipbreaker.)

None of this is intended to be a knock on Dawn of War 4. From what I've played it's a very good Warhammer 40K RTS, if it perhaps sticks a little closely to the model laid down by Relic's original. What do I know, though, talking about their own games, Dowdeswell tells me that while "one of the messages to the studio is we can't just make [the same] things that we made 20 years ago and hope it goes well", re-releasing the 20-year-old Dawn of War sold very well for Relic and netted the team some very positive reviews on Steam. "There's a bit of a paradox there," he adds"

Anyway, this is all getting side-tracked. Back to holding onto Company of Heroes when separating from Sega. "Fighting for Company of Heroes when leaving Sega was crucial because I already lamented the fact that the other games didn't come with Relic," Dowdeswell says. Dowdeswell says foundation building masks a lot of the work going on behind the scenes: "We now self-publish all of this," he explains. "That ambition to re-establish Relic as an innovator in this strategy space is a big part of what drives our creative direction on new games." Focusing on updating Company of Heroes 3, releasing DLC for the game, and making definitive editions of Dawn of War and the original Company of Heroes is what gets the studio to the place it can make a new game.

As to what Relic is making next, Dowdeswell wouldn't say. Though, he revealed trying genres besides real-time strategy was "almost a certainty", before adding "I don't think it's going to be a vast departure for where we are, so it will still be strategic gameplay. In an age of mistrust, communicating more isn’t enough. Influence comes from building networks

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Serbian President Aleksandar Vučić’s loss in recent elections was startling for a number of reasons. First, during his 14-year reign, Vučić had come to rule the country with an iron hand. Second was the scale: The opposition Student List candidates won 52.65% of the vote, while Vučić’s United Serbia party took only 39.2%.

But even more important, which you couldn’t see from all the big crowds and headlines, is what my friend the Serbian activist Srdja Popović calls the student’s “analog strategy.” Named Student in Every Village, it eschewed high-tech targeting databases and AI-generated graphics for old-fashioned door-to-door campaigning. 

This is part of a larger phenomenon that leaders need to recognize and act upon. As trust in institutions has diminished, traditional channels, such as carefully crafted press releases, slick promotions, and announcements from behind a podium are increasingly ineffective. Today, organizations need to build more visceral, on-the-ground networks to gain traction.  

The “Leaderless Revolutions”

In the early 2000s and 2010s, a series of popular revolutions brought down powerful authoritarian regimes. First came the color revolutions, which overthrew the remnants of communist dictatorships in Serbia, Georgia, and Ukraine. Then the uprisings of the Arab Spring sprang up across the Middle East, bringing similar pressure on hard-line despots.

Many referred to these as “leaderless revolutions” because they had no charismatic figurehead (like a Gandhi, Martin Luther King Jr., or Mandela). It seemed as if the streets just spontaneously filled with self-organizing networks of protesters, empowered by technology, to demand change. The brittle 20th-century-style regimes hardly knew what hit them. 

In 2013, political scientist Moisés Naím published The End of Power, in which he argued that because of increasing mobility and technological change, along with declining poverty, the power of institutions was diminishing. Power hadn’t ended exactly but, as he put it, power was becoming “easier to gain but harder to use or keep.”

While there was some truth to all of this, much of it was a mirage. I was there, in Kyiv, Ukraine, during the Orange Revolution in 2004, and it was clear even then that the protests were designed and planned by well-trained activists. Yes, technology played a part. Online message boards and text messaging were critical communication tools, but none of it just happened on its own. 

As Srdja recently put it to me, “There are two kinds of movements: spontaneous and successful.” Something else was going on.  

Saddleback’s Secret

When Rick Warren first arrived in Orange County, California, in 1979, he saw the opportunity to build a new kind of church. He had spent three months going door-to-door and found that while many residents identified themselves as Christians, they found church services boring and irrelevant. So he began to tailor his services and programs to meet their needs.

Today, his Saddleback Church is one of the largest congregations in the world, with 20,000 people attending services every week. Yet looks can be deceiving. What makes Warren such a powerful force isn’t those massive weekend events, but the thousands of small prayer groups that meet during the week.

The heart of the Saddleback movement is the groups of six to eight people who meet each week, build strong ties, and support each other in matters of faith, family, and career. It is the strong bonds, combined with the loose connections between those small groups, that give Saddleback its combination of massive reach and internal coherence.

My experiences in the Orange Revolution were similar. Yes, there were enormous crowds, but we largely operated in small groups, sometimes coordinating with other small groups, going online and texting to give and receive information. Later, as I got to know and work more closely with Srdja, I learned that these movements were designed that way—in no way leaderless, but very much decentralized. 

As I wrote in Cascades, transformational change is driven by small groups, loosely connected, but united by a common purpose. Decentralization is not the absence of leadership. It is a different form of leadership. The job of a leader is no longer to plan and organize tasks but to help those groups connect and inspire them with a sense of shared values and shared mission.  

The Disinformation Wars

When the COVID-19 pandemic hit in 2020, Dr. Anthony Fauci became a household name. Signaling institutional authority, he stood behind a podium, wore a white lab coat, and talked passionately about the “science” of the disease and its spread. He asked people to change their behaviors, wear masks, and distance themselves from their neighbors.

Yet many didn’t trust him. He felt distant, and they were skeptical that someone who felt so remote from their lives could have their best interests at heart. It was as if Fauci were a minister at the pulpit, but congregants never made it to the service. They were still in their prayer groups, reading from an entirely different hymn book, written by other people they saw in white lab coats just like Fauci’s on social media and YouTube but saying very different things. 

Recently, a team of researchers at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology looked into how we share information—and misinformation—in our little “prayer groups.” What they found was troubling. Their model suggests that when we’re surrounded by people who think like us, we share information more freely because we don’t expect to be challenged. We’re also less likely to check our facts, because we know that those we’re sharing with are less likely to inspect the facts themselves.

In Naím’s 2023 follow-up to his earlier book, The Revenge of Power, he showed how many of those same players that had been disrupted in earlier revolutions, like autocrats, corporations, and criminal networks, adapted the same tools that the movements used, such as the internet and social media, to discredit their opponents, reconstitute their power, and disrupt institutions. 

That’s what leaders are struggling with today. The old institutional channels are clogged. Messages must compete with myriad sources of information, ranging from good-faith institutions to nefarious players, rumor, and innuendo, before they can reach each of those small groups.

All change is local, now more than ever

In the 1950s, sociologist William Whyte made an observation about the spread of air conditioners. Back then, units were installed in windows, and he observed that these didn’t propagate evenly, but in clusters from building to building. People weren’t buying air conditioners after just hearing about them. They bought one after visiting a neighbor who had one running on a hot day.

Yet this was also the beginning of the era of mass communication. New electronic media, like television and later the internet, were measurable in ways that traditional channels were not. Increasingly sophisticated marketers divided audiences, tracked metrics, and built complicated mathematical models in an effort to make persuasion more predictable and accountable.

Yet the human brain never changed. We evolved to manage relatively small social networks that max out at about 150 people, a phenomenon known as Dunbar’s number. Beyond that, our capacity for trust diminishes greatly. Decades of research have shown that we rarely interact with information directly, but rather filter it through our peer networks. 

What recent events in Serbia, Hungary, and other places have shown is that this process can be countered, but as General Stanley McChrystal has put it, “It takes a network to defeat a network.” While it is true that institutions, nation-states, and oligarchs today have the capacity to broadcast messages that permeate every facet of our lives, it is becoming increasingly clear that people are no longer listening. 

It’s no longer enough to communicate a vision to shape opinions. Leaders today must learn to shape networks.

Lessons from the Founding Fathers and the Old American Tradition on Muslim Immigration

Western civilization is facing a crisis driven by mass immigration from the Muslim world. Across the Atlantic, the demographic consequences of Muslim immigration have begun to unravel the social fabric of Europe. America is following a similar course as its foreign-born population reaches historic levels. Coinciding with this trend, immigration from the Muslim world has increased dramatically in recent decades. After the Hart–Celler Act opened the United States to large-scale immigration from outside Europe in 1965, America’s Muslim diaspora has grown from roughly 100,000 in 1961 to more than 5.5 million today.[REF] Meanwhile, America’s Muslim population has more than doubled since the start of the Global War on Terrorism. If these patterns persist unchecked, they threaten to “change and corrupt the national spirit” of America.[REF]

The growing prevalence of Islamic culture presents a host of practical problems for American society. Although Islamification is not yet a national threat on the scale seen in Europe, select urban centers and rural communities have started to become transformed by the influx of unassimilated Muslim immigrants. The West’s Muslim diaspora has demonstrated a striking aversion to assimilation. The case of Lewiston, Maine, where a wave of Somali immigrants has overwhelmed the city since the early 2000s, illustrates the problem. According to Lauren Gilbert, many of the Somali diaspora in Lewiston do not wish to assimilate into America’s “majority culture.” “Many Somali elders,” she reports from interviews, “view assimilation into the American mosaic as a threat to the very survival of the Somali people.” Accordingly, the Somali elders chose Lewiston, in part, “because they believed that they could raise their children and practice their religion in relative peace, free from unwanted outside influences.”[REF]

Examples such as Lewiston’s Somali diaspora underscore how America’s embrace of multiculturalism has made it impossible to accommodate Islam without altering the character of the nation itself. As Gilbert acknowledges, “from a multiculturalist perspective, the desire not to assimilate and thus to lose one’s cultural and religious identity is a legitimate choice that the host society should respect and accommodate.”[REF] Many Muslim immigrants and their children reject the traditional “melting pot” settlement that integrated previous waves of immigrants into a unified American national identity.

A 2026 op-ed in The Washington Post by Shadi Hamid, a senior fellow at Georgetown University, exemplifies this view. Hamid argues against the premise that “a minority community’s right to be in the United States depends on its willingness to converge with the cultural mainstream.”[REF] In Hamid’s view, “America was not founded on the assumption that its citizens would eventually come to agree on foundational questions.”

This sentiment is widely shared by prominent Islamic organizations across America. The Council on American-Islamic Relations’ (CAIR’s) 2026 Civil Rights Report explicitly criticizes the old American tradition on immigration that sought to “preserve the ideal of U.S. homogeneity” and all attempts to restore the vision of America as united by a “single heritage.”[REF] To this end, the report asserts CAIR’s commitment to protecting Muslims’ “right to be different.” A 2009 article reposted by CAIR, which includes “assimilation” in a list of terms “not to use with Muslims,” is further indicative of this prevailing sentiment.[REF] In this same spirit, the Islamic Circle of North America declared in a statement issued ahead of the 25th anniversary of 9/11 that they “reject the premise that American belonging is contingent on loyalty tests or displays of patriotism.”[REF]

Contrary to Hamid’s supposition, the Western tradition has long maintained that a republic depends, as Cicero would say, upon a fundamental agreement on justice and the common good. Mass immigration from civilizations with radically different political theologies and conceptions of justice, whose adherents refuse to adopt American cultural mores and the American tradition of ordered liberty, threatens the very existence of that tradition.

The old tradition on immigration was rooted in the conviction that “America must be kept American.”[REF] That tradition recognized that too much immigration that is too disparate in culture, religion, and mores would change America’s regime of natural rights, self-government, and ordered liberty into something both foreign and undesirable. On this basis, the Founders would have viewed mass immigration from the Muslim world, and elsewhere, as a threat to both the country’s political and cultural heritage.

The Naturalization Acts of the Early Republic, which largely prohibited naturalization of immigrants from outside the Western world, epitomized this consensus. This precedent was subsequently affirmed by the Reconstruction Congress and by several decades of judicial precedents from the early 20th century. Together, these various writings, debates, speeches, statutes, and court opinions form a consistent political tradition that endured for 175 years, beginning with the Naturalization Act of 1790 until the passage of Hart–Celler in 1965. Restoring this tradition offers the surest guide for navigating the challenges posed by mass immigration. What follows is a reconstruction of this tradition’s prevailing ethos.

The Founders on Immigration and Republican Citizenship

The Founding Fathers did not directly address the question of Muslim immigration or the compatibility of the Islamic faith with American society. The dilemmas of late modernity, in many respects, are unique in the history of the nation. As a result, modern Americans cannot simply look back to the Founding generation for a manual on how to solve the crisis of this age. Nevertheless, the Founders thought deeply about the fundamental principles pertaining to those questions—the nature of citizenship in a republic, the moral conditions of freedom, and the need to preserve a common national sentiment. These general principles provide a star and compass for navigating the challenges posed by mass immigration from both the Islamic world and elsewhere.

Immigration raises vital questions for political life. A political community, Aristotle writes, “does not arise from any chance multitude” of individuals.[REF] The character of any given political regime depends on the character of its people. If that character changes, the very nature of the regime will change along with it. Aristotle thus warns that a “dissimilarity of stock” caused by an influx of foreigners facilitates regime change by introducing competing and often irreconcilable interests and notions of justice. The Founders likewise worried that indiscriminate immigration would threaten the stability of the republic by undermining national solidarity and transforming American society into “a heterogeneous, incoherent, distracted mass.”[REF]

A country’s dominion over its borders is a core aspect of national sovereignty. The abrogation of the 13 colonies’ authority to promote or restrict immigration as they deemed fit was among the principal grievances against the British crown listed in the Declaration of Independence. The Founding generation established the Constitution to “secure the Blessings of Liberty to [themselves] and [their] Posterity.” For the benefit and preservation of those people—the original parties to the social compact and their heirs—Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution grants Congress plenary authority to establish a “uniform Rule of Naturalization,” that is, to dictate the terms on which the American people are willing to consent to the admission of foreigners.[REF]

The Founders’ views on immigration flowed from their understanding of society as a social compact. “The body politic,” explains John Adams in the Massachusetts Constitution of 1780, “is formed by a voluntary association of individuals: It is a social compact, by which the whole people covenants with each citizen, and each citizen with the whole people, that all shall be governed by certain laws for the common good.”[REF] Through the ratification of the Constitution, the American people were bound together within a unified body politic by common consent. Accordingly, foreigners wishing to become citizens first had to receive the consent of the American people, who reserve the right to expand or restrict the criteria for new membership as they deem fit. As Gouverneur Morris observed during the Constitutional Convention, “every society from a great nation down to a club [has] the right of declaring the conditions on which new members should be admitted.”[REF]

From the outset of America’s history, the Founders permitted deserving foreigners to join the social compact and enjoy the privileges and immunities of citizenship on an equal basis with native-born Americans. In this spirit, George Washington expressed his hope “that this land might become a safe & agreeable Asylum to the virtuous & persecuted part of mankind, to whatever nation they might belong.”[REF] The Founders’ liberality was not unqualified, however. Washington’s attitude conveys a degree of both openness and exclusivity. America would remain open to immigrants, but only to those “who are determined to be sober, industrious & virtuous members of Society.”[REF]

For Washington, virtue encompasses both good moral character (“sober” and “industrious”) and the habits of good citizenship. As he explained to the Hebrew Congregation at Newport, America would extend the blessings of civil and religious liberty to whoever “should demean themselves as good citizens” (emphasis added) by giving to the government of the United States “on all occasions their effectual support.”[REF] Among the duties the Founders deemed essential for good citizenship is the rightly ordered love of country. Washington treats this civic obligation as equally binding on every member of the social compact, regardless of his national origin. “Citizens, by birth or choice, of a common country, that country has a right to concentrate your affections,” Washington wrote in his Farewell Address. “The name of American, which belongs to you in your national capacity, must always exalt the just pride of patriotism more than any appellation derived from local discriminations.”[REF]

The Founders expected more from prospective citizens than a mere verbal affirmation of faith in the American creed. Immigrants were called upon to meaningfully integrate into America’s national heritage.

In essence, the Founders believed that the preservation of the republic depends on the existence of a national identity shaped by what today is called culture, that is, a shared way of life defined by things loved in common. “The safety of a republic,” Hamilton observed, “depends essentially on the energy of a common National sentiment; on a uniformity of principles and habits; on the exemption of the citizens from foreign bias, and prejudice; and on that love of country which will almost invariably be found to be closely connected with birth, education and family.”[REF] (Emphasis added.)

Naturally, the influx of foreigners threatens this civil harmony, since immigrants are “apt to bring with them attachments to the persons they have left behind; to the country of their nativity, and to its particular customs and manners.” Hamilton thus warns that the “unqualified admission of foreigners” would transform American society into “a heterogeneous compound,” “change and corrupt the national spirit,” “introduce foreign propensities,” and be akin to admitting “the Grecian Horse into the Citadel of our Liberty and Sovereignty.”

Thomas Jefferson expressed similar concerns about the unqualified “importation of foreigners” in his Notes on the State of Virginia. “Suppose 20 millions of republican Americans,” he observes, “thrown all of a sudden into France, what would be the condition of that kingdom? If it would be more turbulent, less happy, less strong, we may believe that the addition of half a million of foreigners to our present numbers would produce a similar effect here.”[REF] Fearing this result, the Founders expected more from prospective citizens than a mere verbal affirmation of faith in the American creed. Immigrants were called upon to meaningfully integrate into America’s national heritage.

The Founders had a clear understanding of what defined that heritage. “Every species of government has its specific principles,” Jefferson observes. “Ours perhaps are more peculiar than those of any other in the universe. It is a composition of the freest principles of the English constitution, with others derived from natural right and natural reason.”[REF] These two sources, creed and culture together, represent the core elements of America’s national heritage.

Jefferson famously remarked that the Declaration did not aim at “originality of principle or sentiment,” but merely “was intended to be an expression of the American mind.”[REF] However, he could not boast that “all American whigs thought alike” if the American people did not first share a common way of life. Though the Declaration appeals to abstract, metaphysical truths, these were proclaimed by a people who shared a common political and cultural heritage.[REF] The We of the Declaration were those whose “forefathers, inhabitants of the island of Great-Britain, left their native land, to seek on these shores a residence for civil and religious freedom.”[REF] This cultural and political lineage serves as the foundation of America’s national heritage, the roux of the proverbial melting pot. As Thomas G. West observes, “it is unlikely that the American Revolution could have succeeded without something like the Anglo-American people with their distinctive ethnic character, religion, and legal heritage.”[REF] Reflecting this sentiment, John Jay boasts in The Federalist No. 2 how:

Providence has been pleased to give this one connected country to one united people—a people descended from the same ancestors, speaking the same language, professing the same religion, attached to the same principles of government, very similar in their manners and customs, and who, by their joint counsels, arms, and efforts, fighting side by side throughout a long and bloody war, have nobly established general liberty and independence.[REF] (Emphasis added.)

The Founders thus viewed naturalization as a process of both political and cultural assimilation. In a 1794 letter to Vice President John Adams, Washington expressed concern over the prospect of immigrants “retain[ing] the language, habits & principles (good or bad) which they bring with them.” He firmly maintained that if Americans were to remain “one people,” immigrants must become “assimilated to our customs, manners and laws.”[REF] As the younger Adams advised a German visitor, prospective citizens “must cast off the European skin, never to resume it. They must look forward to their posterity rather than backward to their ancestors; they must be sure that whatever their own feelings may be, those of their children will cling to the prejudices of this country.”[REF]

The Founders wanted to ensure that immigrants would successfully assimilate into American society. Among their principal concerns was prospective citizens’ capacity for self-government. In general, they rejected the naive idea that, as stated by Theodore Sedgwick, “the subjects of all Governments, Despotic, Monarchical, and Aristocratical, are, as soon as they set foot on American ground, qualified to participate in administering the sovereignty of our country.”[REF] Beginning with the Naturalization Act of 1790 and culminating in the Naturalization Act of 1802, the Founders established clear standards for determining which foreigners were worthy of citizenship in a free republic.

The Founders’ various comments on immigration and the nature of republican citizenship illustrate four main criteria by which foreigners’ assimilability would be judged: they must be virtuous, affirm America’s republican creed, be loyal to their adopted home, and be culturally compatible. Each of these criteria was reflected in the provisions of the Naturalization Act of 1802. Addressing Washington’s concern that only those “who are determined to be sober, industrious & virtuous members of Society” should emigrate to America, naturalization was limited to individuals of “good moral character.” To ensure that immigrants were devoted republicans, immigrants seeking naturalization were required to issue an oath or affirmation proving their commitment “to the principles of the [C]onstitution of the United States” and relinquish any titles of nobility they may hold prior to naturalization.[REF] Meanwhile, loyalty to the United States was ensured by requiring petitioners to renounce all allegiances to foreign powers, indicating that the Founders would have opposed America’s current practice of permitting dual citizenship.[REF]

In addition to these requirements, the Naturalization Act of 1802 dictated that prospective citizens undergo a probationary residency period of five years, declare their intent to seek naturalization three years prior to their application, and have been a resident of the state in which they filed their petition for at least one year. Naturalization petitions had to be approved by a court of “common law jurisdiction,” which in most cases were local county courts. In practice, courts often required two citizens to attest in a court of law to the applicant’s residency and that he was a devoted republican, possessed “good moral character,” and committed to the community’s “good order and happiness.”[REF]

These requirements provided a system for validating prospective citizens’ ability to integrate into both American society broadly and the local community in which they intended to live. Furthermore, by involving local communities in the process of naturalization, the Naturalization Act directly operationalized the principle of consent. All parties—the immigrant, the local community, and the nation itself—in a sense had to approve the immigrant’s petition to join the social compact.

The Founders described this process as an “apprenticeship” in self-government, whereby the “prejudices which the aliens had imbibed under the Government from whence they came might be effaced…before they were admitted to the rights of a citizen.”[REF] Simple affirmations of the American creed are not, Michael Leib of Pennsylvania observed, “magic,” which, “by hocus pocus, converted aliens suddenly into a fitness for citizenship.”[REF] The whole naturalization process thus facilitated the immigrant’s “transmutation” into an American. “It was this which gave the knowledge and feeling, and gave the opportunity, for the intercourse that amalgamated the aliens with us, and gave them a common interest. It was the surest standard by which to test the desire for citizenship; it was action, and not declaration; it was fact and not theory.”[REF] In other words, this process ensured, as Washington hoped, foreigners first had to intermix “with our people” and become “assimilated to our customs, manners and laws” to become citizens.[REF]

In practice, these provisions provided little barrier to immigration during the Early Republic, but only because they were appropriately tailored to the peculiar circumstances of the era. Foreign immigration to the United States was negligible for a half-century following independence. From 1790 to 1820, the United States’ freeborn population (free whites and free blacks) increased from around 3.2 million to around 8.1 million.[REF] In his 1823 Annual Message, President James Monroe proudly remarked on how the nation’s population boom was “almost altogether native,” while “immigration from other countries has been inconsiderable.”[REF] Roughly 250,000 immigrants arrived on American shores in the four decades after independence, equating to just under 7,000 arrivals a year according to estimates.[REF] Fewer than 150,000 immigrants arrived between 1821 and 1830—roughly double the average from previous years but still a low percentage of the population.[REF]

Meanwhile, almost all immigration during the Early Republic, and well into the late 1800s, came from the British Isles or Germany. As the U.S. Senate’s 1950 immigration report observes,

the immigrants who arrived after the original colonists were of a racial stock [simply meaning Northwest Europeans] closely related to that of the original settlers. Their language was either the same or similar enough to make the acquisition of English comparatively easy, and their national traditions were harmonious with those already established in America.[REF]

Consequently, the size and composition of the immigrant population eased the process of assimilation.

Nor were immigrants entering a multicultural society. John Jay’s description of America as a people united by a common heritage is borne out in the demographics of the Early Republic’s citizen population. According to one estimate, at the time of the Constitution’s ratification, more than 90 percent of America’s citizen population were of British extraction in the 1790 census (83.5 percent English, 6.7 percent Scottish, and 1.6 percent Irish).[REF] Meanwhile, virtually the entire population in 1776 was Christian, with approximately 98 percent Protestant and the remainder Catholic.[REF] As such, the homogeneity of the citizen population in the Early Republic created a strong imperative for immigrants to “cast off [their] European skin” and integrate into our cultural heritage.

These considerations, paired with the provisions of the Naturalization Act of 1802, ensured that immigration would not undermine the idea of America as “one people.” In this regard, the Founders’ views on immigration refute the notion that America was established as a nation of immigrants. The Founders’ immigration policy is best understood as a core element of American ethnogenesis, whereby “English, Scotch, Irish, French, Dutch, Germans, and Swedes” were “melted into a new race of men”—the American.[REF]

In applying these principles to the issue of immigration from the Muslim world, the relevant questions are whether those populations have the virtues and habits necessary for self-government, are committed to the principles of republican government, will display loyalty to their new homeland, and are compatible with America’s cultural heritage. The following sections infer how the Founding Fathers would have answered those questions.

The Founding Fathers on Islam

The Founding generation became acquainted with the Islamic world through trade, diplomacy, captivity, and war on the Barbary Coast. Generally speaking, the Founders came away from these interactions with the impression that Islam is incompatible with a free society.

A noteworthy misconception is that the Founders’ commitment to religious liberty necessarily means they would have supported the integration of Islam into America’s body politic. Exemplifying this view, Denise Spellberg argues that “Jefferson and others defended Muslim rights for the sake of ‘imagined Muslims,’ the promotion of whose theoretical citizenship would prove the true universality of American rights.”[REF] As evidence for this claim, Spellberg points to the Virginia Statute for Religious Freedom, which, according to Jefferson, was intended to include “within the mantle of its protection, the Jew and the Gentile, the Christian and Mahometan, the Hindoo and infidel of every denomination.”[REF]

Religious toleration and the freedom of conscience are undoubtedly American values but must be understood within the context of the country’s broader national heritage. The political culture of the Early Republic was not that of a secular society. Christianity served as the republic’s de facto national religion.[REF] As Joseph Story observes in his Commentaries, “the general, if not the universal, sentiment in America” at the time of the Constitution’s adoption was that any “attempt to level all religions, and to make it a matter of state policy to hold all in utter indifference, would have created universal disapprobation, if not universal indignation.”[REF] As such, “[t]he real object of the [First] amendment was, not to countenance, much less to advance Mahometanism [Islam] or Judaism, or infidelity, by prostrating Christianity; but to exclude all rivalry among Christian sects.”

On its face, the 1796 Treaty of Tripoli seemingly contradicts the claim that the Early Republic rested on a Christian foundation and suggests that the Founders had no inherent opposition to the Islamic faith. It declares that “the government of the United States of America is not in any sense founded on the Christian Religion” and “has in itself no character of enmity against the laws, religion or tranquility of Musselmen [Muslims].”[REF] The treaty, however, should not be treated as either a reflection on the character of American society or a guiding principle for immigration policy. Its actual meaning is simply a rearticulation of the principle that every sovereign state is entitled under the law of nations to a “separate and equal station” among the powers of the Earth.[REF] The 1815 treaty with Algiers conveys the same sentiment in clearer language: “the Government of the United States of America has in itself no character of enmity against the laws, religion, or tranquility of any nation” (emphasis added), whether Christian or Muslim, free or despotic.[REF] The Founders’ respect for the right of sovereign Muslim states to govern themselves in accordance with their own customs and traditions suggests nothing about whether the subjects of those states would make good American citizens.

That is not to suggest that the Founders categorically opposed granting citizenship to non-Christians. George Washington’s magnanimous “Letter to the Hebrew Congregation at Newport” proves otherwise. Indeed, the naturalization debates directly addressed the prospect of including a religious test and rejected the idea. Addressing the claim that the “good moral character” clause would serve such a means, Theodore Sedgwick answered for the majority that it “has no particular reference whatever to religion, or whether a man believes anything or nothing.”[REF] The Naturalization Act’s choice between offering a secular affirmation of one’s fidelity to the Constitution, as opposed to a religious oath, is further indicative of this prevailing sentiment.

Even so, it equally does not follow from the Founders’ decision not to implement a religious test that a prospective citizen’s confession of faith was of no relevance to their fitness for self-government. An immigrant’s religious convictions did not give him a free pass to oppose the “principles of the [C]onstitution” or to act immorally, as determined by the community’s (primarily Christian) standards. The freedom of religion is not unrestrained—the exercise of that right remains as subject to the constraints of ordered liberty as any other right. No man “under colour of religion” may “disturb the good order, peace or safety of the State, or…infringe the laws of morality, or injure others, in their natural, civil, or religious rights,” states the Maryland Declaration of Rights of 1776.[REF]

The standards which govern the application of this principle are even stricter in matters of immigration than when governing the conduct of citizens. To be sure, Jefferson’s sentiment that every American reserves the right to convert to Islam and freely practice his newfound faith holds true. It does not follow from this principle that the proliferation of every faith is healthy for the body politic. Nor does the right to religious liberty convey a right to join the social compact. The underlying principles of the First Amendment guarantee all American citizens the right to believe that monarchy is the best form of government, just as they guarantee the right to practice Islam. Despite this, the Founders wisely required immigrants to affirm their commitment to the principles of the Constitution as a necessary condition of naturalization. The freedom of association grants the American people the right to exclude whomever they may deem unfit for the mantle of citizenship. In exercising that right, the Founders recognized that the vast privileges and immunities afforded to American citizens imposed an even greater obligation to be discerning in who was permitted to join the social compact.

In applying the “good moral character” clause, courts in the 19th century established that “whatever is forbidden by the law of the land ought to be considered, for the time being, immoral.”[REF] Any person who violated state law, or demonstrated a disposition to do so, would not be considered “well disposed to the good order and happiness” of the country and thus barred from citizenship. Early Americans generally viewed Islamic culture as antithetical to America’s distinctively Christian mores, particularly when it came to the family. John Quincy Adams’s criticism of Islam for “degrading the condition of the female sex” and for the faith’s “allowance of polygamy” is illustrative of this consensus.[REF] On these grounds, it is likely that some orthodox Muslims would not have met the Founders’ standard of “good moral character.”

Supporting this conclusion, a federal circuit court reasoned in 1878 that the practice of polygamy disqualified an applicant from naturalization. The court further determined that any alien who insists that the civil law is wrong and maintains “that polygamy may be rightfully practiced…is not entitled to citizenship.”[REF] According to legal scholar Julian Lim, as America began to contend with its first wave of Muslim immigration from the Ottoman Empire in the early 20th century, Turkish nationals were subject to deportation for their doctrinal support of polygamy under the 1907 Immigration Act. This statute codified the courts’ earlier determination and largely carried forward the original intent of the Founders’ “good moral character” standard.[REF]

Even granting Jefferson’s more pluralistic view of society, his writings on Islam indicate that he questioned the faith’s compatibility with America’s civic creed. Underlying Jefferson’s support of religious liberty was his belief that “reason and persuasion are the only practicable instruments” for the advancement of “true religion,” therefore, “to make way for these, free inquiry must be indulged” and promoted by society.[REF] In his private notes, Jefferson criticized “Mahometanism” for “stifling free enquiry” and for how “Mahomet [Mohammed] forbade free argument,” thus preventing any “reformation” of the faith.[REF] Accordingly, even if Jefferson abstractly supported the freedom of a hypothetical Muslim citizen to privately practice his religion, it is doubtful that he would be indifferent to, let alone welcome, the mass immigration of people he recognized as hostile to the principles of enlightened liberty.

The Founders’ impression of Islam as a hostile force was solidified by their interactions with the Barbary States. The Algerians, writes an anonymous congressman from the First Congress,

are taught as a maxim of their religion from their infancy, that those who do not profess the Mahometan [Islamic] faith are the natural enemies to their great Prophet, and his religion, and that it is their duty to destroy them—they therefore indiscriminately cruise against all Christian powers, and make prize of the persons of all Christians whom they take alive, and sell them into perpetual slavery, unless they will become good Musselmen, and swear to support the Mahometan faith.[REF]

Jefferson and Adams reached a similar conclusion in 1786 following their meeting with the Tripolitan ambassador.[REF]

The Founders saw in the Barbary States’ tyrannical impulse among the community of nations a reflection of the internal character of Islamic society. In 1817, Captain James Riley published his memoirs detailing his grueling experience under Arab slavery after shipwrecking on the shores of North Africa. He described the Islamic governments of the Maghreb as “in the strictest sense of the word, paternal” and akin to the “feudal system.”[REF] William Eaton, U.S. consul to Tunis and commander of the American expedition into Tripoli during the First Barbary War, had a similar assessment, expressing his sympathy for how the Tunisians are “abject slaves to the despotism of their government” and subjugated under the “worst of all tyrannies, the despotism of priestcraft.”[REF] Echoing these assessments, James Monroe later commented that the Middle East has always been dominated by “despotism[s] with great wealth and splendor at the head” and “slavery with ignorance, poverty, and wretchedness among the people.”[REF]

Based on these considerations, Monroe argued that “Arabian[s]…Turk[s], [and] Persian[s],” among others, are not “capable of self-government.”[REF] His pessimism, however, was informed less by bigotry than by a reflection on history, which demonstrates “the effect which despotic Government has on the intelligence and manners of the people under it.”[REF] The disheartening and spectacular failure of the French Revolution confirmed for him this unfortunate observation. Monroe judged that if even “a great nation, distinguished for its improvement in civilization and in all the arts of civilized life” could not successfully balance liberty with public order, the prospects for self-government in the despotisms of Asia were even worse.[REF] Given that “those societies never had such government among them, or made an attempt to establish one, or showed any disposition to do so,” he concluded that “they are incapable of such [republican] government.”[REF]

Monroe’s verdict that such societies lack the capacity for self-government is the creed-and-culture test in another form. Creed asks whether a people can affirm the principles of the Declaration and the Constitution. Culture asks whether their formed habits make that affirmation viable.

The Founders worried that mass immigration from foreign despotisms could destabilize the republic.

America’s recent ill-fated attempts to establish democracy in Iraq and Afghanistan largely vindicate the Founders’ concerns regarding “the dangers incident to an effort, by an old and populous community, which had been long ruled by despotism, to subvert that government and establish a free one.”[REF] Following the U.S.-led coalition’s invasion of Iraq in 2003, President George W. Bush optimistically predicted that “Iraqi democracy will succeed, and that success will send forth the news, from Damascus to Tehran, that freedom can be the future of every nation.” President Obama expressed a similar hopeful sentiment in his September 2012 address to the United Nations General Assembly, after the so-called Arab Spring, commenting that “freedom and self-determination are not unique to one culture. These are not simply American values or Western values, they are universal values.”[REF] Neither the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan nor the Arab Spring delivered on their lofty promise of a democratic Middle East. The Founding Fathers would have arguably concluded from these failures that their assessment of Islamic societies’ compatibility with self-government was well-founded, thus casting doubt on any such people’s fitness for citizenship.

The Founders’ commitment to the principles of the Declaration made them equally critical of any society they deemed despotic and religiously intolerant. John Adams reproached Medieval Christendom as an “age of darkness” whereby the masses suffered under the despotism of “the canon and the feudal law”—the “two greatest systems of tyranny” to have sprung from Christianity.[REF] Following the French Revolution, Monroe leveled a similar criticism against the surviving Catholic monarchies of Europe, attacking them for depriving “man of his natural and civil rights” and raising “upon his religious faith…a long train of hierarchal establishments, which serve only to add to the power and the splendour of the court.”[REF]

As such, the Founders worried that mass immigration from foreign despotisms could destabilize the republic. In 1803, the Louisiana Purchase raised for the first time the question of how to successfully integrate into the social compact, en masse, a foreign population lacking experience in self-government. Under the terms of the treaty, the primarily French-Creole population was to be “protected in the free enjoyment of their liberty, property and the Religion,” and in time, “incorporated in the Union” as citizens of the United States. However, the Founders believed that prudence warranted not immediately granting them all the privileges and immunities of citizenship.[REF]

Following the acquisition of Louisiana, President Jefferson observed that “our new fellow citizens are as yet as incapable of self-government as children.”[REF] Congress accordingly organized the new Territory of Orleans in a manner reflecting the probationary period of the Naturalization Act. The French-Creole population would be ruled by a temporary colonial-style government administered by officials appointed by the President. This framework ensured that the inhabitants would remain secure in their civil and religious liberties, while also serving as “an apprenticeship to liberty,” whereby the inhabitants could be “trained up in a knowledge of our own laws and institutions” and gradually “raised to the enjoyment and practice of independence.”[REF] This “apprenticeship” continued throughout Louisiana’s roughly eight-year period as a territory, until its inhabitants were granted full citizenship in 1812 upon obtaining statehood.

If the Founders believed that prudence warranted requiring even the “civilized and Christian” Creoles to undergo an “apprenticeship” in self-government prior to obtaining citizenship, it is unlikely they would have deemed a people they considered utterly incapable of self-government as the ideal matter for a republic.[REF] The difference that ultimately permits one and not the other is that Americans still shared a common civilizational heritage with French Creoles, Californios, Germans, Irish, and others that made it possible to integrate those populations into the body politic without compromising the nation’s status as “one united people.” The American people shared no such relationship with the subjects of Islamic despotism.

James Madison’s comments in support of Catholic immigration during the debates over the 1795 Naturalization Act capture how the Founders weighed these considerations. Notably, Madison neither appeals to pluralism nor liberal toleration in justifying Catholics’ fitness for naturalization. He appeals instead to the principles of republican citizenship, observing that “there [is] nothing inconsistent with the purest Republicanism [in Catholicism].”[REF] In applying these standards to the prospect of Muslim immigration, the question then is whether the same could be said about Islam. The aforementioned assessments of Islamic societies indicate that early Americans did not believe that a people kept as “abject slaves” to priest and sovereign possessed the independent character necessary for citizenship in a free republic.

What the Founders criticized as the “despotism of priestcraft” can be attributed to Muslim societies’ adherence to Sharia law, which is predicated on the supremacy of Islamic law over secular authority. Indeed, it is for this very reason that Alexis de Tocqueville concludes that Islam is incompatible with free government. “Mohammed had not only religious doctrines descend from Heaven and placed in the Koran,” Tocqueville observes, “but political maxims, civil and criminal laws, and scientific theories…[t]hat alone, among a thousand other reasons, is enough to show that [Islam] cannot dominate for long in enlightened and democratic times.”[REF] There can be little civil or religious liberty in a regime where political authority is predicated on the word of the Koran and Hadiths, as interpreted by muftis and executed by the caliph.

Islam embodies the manners and principles of a rival civilization that is largely incompatible with America’s national heritage.

Sharia, as Tocqueville describes it, is not abandoned by a verbal profession of the American creed. It is a revealed civil and criminal code that rejects natural rights knowable by reason, popular sovereignty, equality before the law, and religious liberty. Moreover, it demands that its followers reject Washington’s commandment for citizens to “concentrate [their] affections” in the national community. Islam attaches its adherents to a transnational religious community, the Ummah, and divides their loyalty between two competing authorities, the Constitution and the Koran.

Among the Founders’ greatest concerns about immigration was that people are apt to “entertain opinions on government congenial with those under which they have lived,” making it “extremely unlikely” that immigrants from a nation with opposing political principles “will bring with them that temperate love of liberty, so essential to real republicanism.”[REF] Even today, support for Sharia law remains remarkably high throughout the Muslim world—as well as among the ever-growing Muslim populations in Western Europe and the Anglosphere. Pew Research Center surveys published in 2013 found that 99 percent of Muslims in Afghanistan, 84 percent in Pakistan, 91 percent in Iraq, 89 percent in the Palestinian territories, and 74 percent in Egypt “favor making Islamic law the official law in their country.” Anyone who adheres to a theological code that is antithetical to republican government is not prepared to pledge fealty “to the principles of the Constitution of the United States.”[REF]

By the Founders’ standards, adherence to Islam fails the test of republican citizenship on nearly every measure. It is not conducive to forming republican habits and virtues, it is at odds with the fundamental tenets of the American creed, and it demands allegiance to foreign theological codes and political authorities. In short, Islam embodies the manners and principles of a rival civilization that is largely incompatible with America’s national heritage.

Culture, Not Race: How the Founders Put the Civilizational Rule into Law

The Founders’ views on immigration from the Muslim world are not entirely hypothetical. In practice, they recognized that not all people could easily be integrated into American society. Those similar enough to America’s original settlers could gently be assimilated through residency requirements, oaths, and renunciations. Others were excluded. The Founders recognized that multiculturalism poses an existential threat to the stability of the regime. “In the composition of society,” Hamilton observes, “the harmony of the ingredients is all important, and whatever tends to a discordant intermixture must have an injurious tendency.”[REF] Consequently, the Naturalization Act categorically excluded from citizenship non-Westerners via the “free white person” clause. James Kent presumed that the clause excluded “the inhabitants of Africa” and doubted whether “the yellow or tawny races of Asiatics” were “white persons.”[REF] In effect, the Founding Fathers prohibited the naturalization of immigrants from the Muslim world.

The “free white person” clause is understandably the most controversial provision of the Founders’ immigration policy, since it sounds to modern ears like a purely racial category. Its meaning, often misapprehended, and its underlying rationale, rarely investigated, offers valuable insight into the Founders’ approach to immigration. Notably, there is no documented debate in the congressional record surrounding the clause, suggesting that it was widely approved. Absent these records, the clause’s rationale must be inferred from the Founders’ broader political thought. In part, the clause can be attributed to the unresolved issues over slavery and the legal status of American Indians. More fundamentally, the clause exemplifies the Founders’ understanding of the moral conditions of freedom.

Contemporary scholarship condemns this provision as part of “an anticanon of statutes [from American history] establishing and maintaining White supremacy.”[REF] Unable to look beyond the Naturalization Act’s anachronistic phrasing, critics fail to understand the Founders’ original intent or how their immigration policy squares with their broader political theory. These objections fail to appreciate the Founders’ principled statesmanship.

The Founders presumed that individuals from outside the Western world were unlikely to exhibit republican virtue, affirm the American creed, or be effectively assimilated into the national heritage.

The Founders’ immigration policy was not, in essence, a narrow-minded racial doctrine. The naturalization restrictions were deliberate instruments for preserving a constitutional regime rooted in natural rights and republican self-government, made possible by a shared national heritage. The Founders recognized that a republic depends upon the character of its citizens. Far from bigotry, their approach reflected a clear-eyed assessment of what sustains ordered liberty.

Under the provision’s original meaning, “white” did not have precisely the same biological connotation it eventually came to have in the antebellum South and progressive era. For example, Webster’s 1828 American Dictionary neither defined white as a racial category nor included the racial classification of Caucasian. Moreover, the common meaning of “race” in the Early Republic was more closely associated with what we would now call ethnicity.[REF] It is in precisely this register that Hector St. John de Crèvecœur referred to Americans as a “new race of men.”[REF]

Accordingly, as Thomas West observes, the Founders’ various writings on immigration “show that they were thinking of civilization, not whiteness, when they reflected on the positive qualities that at least some European immigrants would bring with them to America.”[REF] If written in modern terminology, the early naturalization laws would likely substitute “free white person” with something like “Westerner” without losing its essential meaning.

The Founders’ use of race was, therefore, an imperfect proxy for a political and civilizational determination, a presumption about a prospective citizen’s habits, manners, and customs derived from their place of origin that spoke to their capacity for assimilation and self-government. In essence, the clause can be understood as a broader, more restrictive test of the same criteria screened for by the Naturalization Act’s other provisions. It reflects the Founders’ presumption that individuals from outside the Western world were unlikely to exhibit republican virtue, affirm the American creed, or be effectively assimilated into the national heritage.

Though such views may offend modern sensibilities, the Founders’ prohibition of non-Westerners from naturalization is consistent with a proper understanding of the principles of the Declaration of Independence. Not all nations or cultures believe the principles of the Declaration to be true, and even fewer are equipped to put them into practice. As James Monroe illustrates, despite the fact all men are by nature equally free, because man is also “by nature a sociable being,” he is “in a great measure the creature of circumstances.”[REF] On this basis, Monroe argues that

no proposition according to my judgment, admits of a more satisfactory demonstration, than that in the formation of government, the condition of the society on which it is to operate is to be regarded; that the government which suits one state will not suit another, and that the most improved state of society is that which is best suited to the most free government, if it is not the only one that admits of it.[REF]

Hamilton echoes this observation in a 1799 letter to the Marquis de Lafayette, noting that “a government must be fitted to a nation as much as a coat to the individual, and consequently that what may be good at Philadelphia may be bad at Paris and ridiculous at Petersburgh.”[REF]

Monroe defined the ability of a given society to successfully implement the principles of the Declaration as “the capacity for self-government.”[REF] He viewed this standard as downstream from a nation’s “state of civilization,” which encompassed both a people’s understanding of the principles of liberty and their virtues relative to achieving that end. In his political treatise, Monroe observes that Europe is effectively the only quarter of the globe with a tradition of free government, the inference being that self-government must be the distinctive hallmark of Western civilization.[REF] He concluded from this premise that Arabs, Turks, Persians, and various other non-Europeans without an organic republican tradition lack the capacity for self-government.[REF] The “free white person” clause is an extension of this framework.

This logic is most clearly illustrated through the Founders’ views on the legal status of American Indians. Although they were excluded from naturalization under the law, Jefferson expressed the Founders’ vision that eventually American Indians would be incorporated “with us as citizens of the US.”[REF] The major impediment to this project, in the Founders’ view, was a matter of “civilization,” not race. As Monroe explains, the “rude and unlettered state” of American Indian society “made them utterly incompetent to the discharge of the duties of a well organized, free, representative government.”[REF] Therefore, before they could become American citizens, they first needed to become “civilized”—that is to say, develop the habits and virtues necessary for republican citizenship and become assimilated into American society. Once this process was completed, they could join with their fellow Americans as “one people.”[REF]

This broader, civilizational understanding of the clause was subsequently affirmed during the Reconstruction-era debates over the 1870 amendment to the Naturalization Act, which opened naturalization to “aliens of African nativity and to persons of African descent.”[REF] While expanding citizenship to black immigrants, Congress otherwise resoundingly voted to keep the free white person clause intact. The driving issue was lawmakers’ concern that repealing the clause would grant citizenship to Chinese migrant workers who maintained their cultural and political allegiance to Beijing.

Congress did not see this decision as inconsistent. Senator William Stewart (R–NV), one of the authors of the Fifteenth Amendment, expressed the majority’s reasoning as to how they could champion civil rights for blacks while simultaneously prohibiting naturalization from the rest of the non-Western world. Consistent with the Founders’ framework, Stewart explained that neither the spirit of the Fifteenth Amendment nor the Declaration of Independence required America to grant “political rights to all people throughout the globe.”[REF] Americans, he argued, “have a right to make our own laws; and it would be a crime in us to incorporate any people in the body-politic who are hostile to free institutions.”

In Stewart’s estimation, American and European countries shared a common civilizational heritage, ensuring European immigrants would “assimilate rapidly” into American society. He contended that the same was largely true of blacks, who had grown culturally indistinguishable from America’s white population. “This was [blacks’] native land. He [the black American] was born here…. [H]e was an American and a Christian, as much as any of the rest of the people of the country.” By contrast, the nations of the Eastern Hemisphere “have another civilization at wars with ours,” one which does not “believe in republican institutions” and is inhabited by a people who “do not understand our society” or “value the privileges of citizenship.”[REF] Stewart concluded that such people were unfit for citizenship “until they changed their devotion to pagan despotism” and became either “republicanized or Christianized.”

In this same spirit, the courts repeatedly determined throughout the naturalization cases of the 19th and 20th centuries that the Naturalization Act’s use of “white” reflected the common understanding of the Founding era—compatibility with Western civilization and not race. In the seminal 1878 case of In re Ah Yup, a California federal court excluded Chinese immigrants from citizenship, defining “white” according to its “well settled meaning in common popular speech” as encompassing Europeans and parts of Western Asia. To support its conclusion, the court drew on the congressional debates from 1870, citing Senator Oliver Morton’s concern that mass immigration from “another civilization” would have a corrosive effect on American society.[REF]

In 1921, a lower court in Terrace v. Thompson, the precursor to the Supreme Court’s decision on the constitutionality of alien land laws, captured the essence of the Founders’ original intent. In his opinion, District Judge Edward Cushman opined on the dangers of multiculturalism, remarking that the courts had a solemn duty “to hold impregnable the barrier erected by Congress [via the Naturalization Act] to preserve, in its purity, our own type of civilization.” It was precisely for this reason, he concluded, that immigrants from outside the West were precluded from citizenship. The basis of this objection was not

due to color, as color, but only to color as an evidence of a type of civilization which it characterizes. The yellow or brown racial color is the hallmark of Oriental despotisms, or was at the time the original naturalization law was enacted. It was deemed that the subjects of these despotisms, with their fixed and ingrained pride in the type of their civilization, which works for its welfare by subordinating the individual to the personal authority of the sovereign, as the embodiment of the state, were not fitted and suited to make for the success of a republican form of Government. Hence they were denied citizenship.[REF]

The Supreme Court subsequently reinforced this civilizational interpretation of the statute in two unanimous decisions. In Ozawa v. United States (1922), Justice George Sutherland held that Japanese immigrants were ineligible because they were “clearly of a race which is not Caucasian” in the popular understanding.[REF] In United States v. Bhagat Singh Thind (1923), Justice Sutherland determined that Indians were ineligible for citizenship, even if they were a “high-caste Hindu” who could be scientifically classified as “Caucasian.”[REF] Whatever the merits of this particular ruling, it hinged on culture and civilization, not race.

The federal government’s brief in Thind reiterates the common theme that the Founding Fathers only intended to grant citizenship “for those whom they knew and regarded as worthy to share it with them—men of their own type—men in a general way living under the same social conditions, sharing or wishing to share the same political institutions and capable of entering into the life of this country in a spirit of sympathy and cooperation.”[REF] As such, the federal government argued that immigrants from the Indian subcontinent were precluded from citizenship because “at the time the first naturalization law was passed the Hindus were regarded as a people wholly alien to Western civilization and utterly incapable of assimilation to Western habits and customs, mode of life, political and social institutions.” The federal government thus concluded that “a free white person” according to the standard of “which the Congress has now adhered for a hundred and thirty years, denominates what is historically known as the men of the Western civilization.” It is this civilization “which our fathers knew and from which they were willing to recruit the citizenship of the Republic.”[REF]

The Supreme Court agreed. Writing for a unanimous Court, Justice Sutherland declared that the statute used the term “white persons” in their “common speech” meaning as a stand-in for those who could “quickly merge into the mass of our population.” As he explained,

the immigration of that day was almost exclusively from the British Isles and Northwestern Europe, whence they and their forbears had come. When they extended the privilege of American citizenship to “any alien being a free white person,” it was these immigrants—bone of their bone and flesh of their flesh—and their kind whom they must have had affirmatively in mind.[REF]

The bar was “assimilation.” European children “los[t] the distinctive hallmarks of their European origin,” while others did not.

The old American political traditions’ keen sense of man’s social nature, and of what is now called culture, cannot and should not be dismissed as residual racism. Although a subset of lower federal court decisions during the early 20th century embraced the discredited theories of race science, they do not represent the overarching legal and intellectual consensus of the tradition. The prevailing theme throughout the major precedents on naturalization was the explicit rejection of any implication of racial superiority. Justice Sutherland stressed in Ozawa that “there is not implied—either in the [Naturalization Act] or in our interpretation of it—any suggestion of…racial inferiority.”[REF] In Thind, Justice Sutherland further clarified that the Court’s longstanding use of racial terminology, that is, “Caucasian,” was only because the term became colloquially synonymous with white, and was not an attempt to replace the Founders’ “common speech” terminology of the Naturalization Act with the “science of ethnology.”[REF] He also again emphasized that the free white person clause’s categorical restrictions were in no way premised on any idea of “racial superiority or inferiority.”[REF]

In applying the principles of this tradition to the modern immigration crisis, one can acknowledge that the Founders may very well have been overbroad in their determination of who is unassimilable. After all, the number of nations that now have experience with popular government has expanded considerably since 1776. Such qualifications, however, do not negate the validity of the underlying principle itself, which cautions against attempting to integrate people from civilizations whose social and political culture is utterly antithetical to the American way of life.

The Original American vs. the New Liberal Consensus on Muslim Immigration

The Founding generation was never tasked with applying the provisions of the Naturalization Act directly to the question of immigration from the Muslim world. When the issue finally arose during the 20th century, however, the enduring legacy of their principles resulted in widespread judicial skepticism over Muslim immigrants’ assimilability.[REF] Only when the old American political tradition was abandoned following World War II did courts determine otherwise.

Prior to the Supreme Court’s decisions in Ozawa and Thind, the earliest precedents concerning immigrants from the Middle East involved Christians, namely Maronites and Armenians. These cases initially produced a circuit split, but a consensus eventually arose granting eligibility. Courts often cited Christianity and historical ties to Europe as evidence of civilizational compatibility for Syrians and Armenians. By contrast, Arab Muslims were presumably excluded from naturalization under the law. Because these cases were decided prior to Thind, some of the precedents over-relied on the discredited science of ethnology, often using an applicant’s Christianity as evidence of their “whiteness.”[REF] Of course, such reasoning makes little sense if “white” is defined biologically, but it is coherent if “white” is understood as a stand-in for Western.

In the 1909 case In re Halladjian, the federal courts came close to fully capturing the Founders’ original meaning. The government argued that Armenians were ineligible on the grounds that “white” meant European, not in the sense that the European-born child of a Syrian migrant may be considered today, but in that an individual culture embodies “the prevailing ideals, standards, and aspirations of the people of Europe.”[REF] In turn, Circuit Judge Francis Cabot Lowell’s opinion rejected the government’s conclusion by taking its appeal to a civilizational standard to its logical conclusion. Judge Lowell noted that though Armenians are not European, “the outlook of their civilization has been toward Europe,” as evidenced by their Christian faith and long-standing historical alliance with the nations of Europe against “both Saracens and Turks.” Therefore, Armenians were eligible for naturalization under the law because their cultural heritage ensured that they easily “become westernized and readily adaptable to European [Western] standards.”

This framework was applied directly to the question of Muslim immigration in In re Ahmed Hassan (1942). Consistent with earlier precedents, Judge Arthur Tuttle recognized that the qualifications for naturalization should not be “how the group in question would be classified by ethnologists who have made a study of racial origins, but, rather, what groups of peoples then living in 1790 with characteristics then existing were intended by Congress to be classified as ‘white persons.’”[REF] He reasoned that “it is well known that [Arabs] are a part of the Mohammedan world and that a wide gulf separates their culture from that of the predominately Christian peoples of Europe. [Therefore,] it cannot be expected that as a class they would readily intermarry with our population and be assimilated into our civilization.” Arab Muslims were thus deemed ineligible for citizenship under the law for the sake of preserving America’s cultural and political heritage.

Precisely how and why the courts abandoned the Founders’ approach to immigration is revealing. Support for Muslim immigration began with the rise of a new liberal consensus under the Roosevelt Administration and the shift towards globalism during World War II. In 1943, Roosevelt’s Department of Justice released an official position statement rebutting the conclusion of Judge Tuttle in Ahmed Hassan.[REF] The following year, in Ex parte Mohriez (1944), Judge Charles E. Wyzanski broke with 154 years of legal tradition and declared Muslim Arabs eligible for naturalization as “white persons” in line with the Administration’s guidance.

The conclusions of both the opinion and guidance rested in part on the Muslim world’s supposed compatibility with Western civilization. More fundamentally, however, Judge Wyzanski appealed to liberal internationalism and America’s role as a world power over the Founders’ republican framework. He argued that rigid exclusion was both antithetical to the “American creed” and “repugnant to our vital interests as a world power.” The purpose of America’s immigration policy was no longer to maintain a common national sentiment, but to serve as an instrument of soft power, whereby the government could promote “friendlier relations” with the non-Western world by integrating foreign cultures into American society.[REF]

Echoing this view, President Harry Truman argued in his failed veto of the 1952 Immigration and Nationality Act that “our immigration policy is equally, if not more important to the conduct of our foreign relations and to our responsibilities of moral leadership in the struggle for world peace.”[REF] Truman’s message marked the beginning of the transition that would eventually culminate in the 1965 Hart–Celler Act. As Representative Jacob Gilbert (D–NY) observed, Hart–Celler was a “reaffirmation…of our enthusiasm for setting democratic standards for the rest of the world, [and] of our willingness to lead a global community of peoples toward universal recognition of human dignity.”[REF]

In many respects, Ahmed Hassan marked the culmination of a consistent legal framework originating in Ah Yup and refined over the decades by Halladjian, Terrace, Ozawa, and Thind. Together, these cases constitute a formidable body of precedent that faithfully applied the provisions of the Naturalization Act in accordance with the Founders’ original meaning. (See the appendix for a list of these cases.) In each of these cases, the Naturalization Act was interpreted not as a narrow biological test, but as a civilizational standard whose purpose was to preserve America’s republican character. America would remain open to immigration from across the West, while prohibiting naturalization from “hostile” cultures. In the words of Justice Sutherland, this principle deserved to be respected as “a rule in force from the beginning of the government, a part of our history as well as our law, welded into the structure of our national polity by a century of legislative and administrative acts and judicial decisions.”[REF]

Recovering the Original American Consensus for Today

After World War II, the newly established liberal consensus began to displace the long-standing American tradition on immigration. The year 1965 was a watershed moment in the establishment of new modes and orders. Today’s crisis is the result of that new system’s failure. What remains is a task of rediscovery, restoration, and adaptation. If America is to preserve its national heritage, it should recover the old American tradition on immigration rooted in the Founders’ theory of republican citizenship.

Critics of that tradition reflexively label any concern about civilizational compatibility as xenophobic, bigoted, or racist. According to their logic, to question mass immigration because of its threat to republican government is to forsake America’s commitment to the principles of the Declaration.[REF] In truth, such critics really challenge the Founders’ ends as well as their means. Their commitment to multiculturalism demands that America accommodate and even celebrate foreign ways of life, however many foreigners arrive and whatever their political theology, family structure, or attitudes toward self-government. The critics’ open-ended cosmopolitanism aligns less with the principles of the Founding than with a deracinated globalism detached from the American people’s traditional way of life.

The central question for statesmanship today is not whether immigration restrictions are “politically correct,” but what must be done to preserve the moral and political character of America. Though the old tradition may be controversial by modern standards, it was much more just and sensible than the current status quo of mass immigration. The Founders’ system of selective immigration committed no violence against those it excluded. Non-Americans have no inherent right to the privileges and immunities of American citizenship. Admission into the social compact rests wholly on the affirmative consent of the American people, who alone determine whether it serves their best interest.

The central question for statesmanship today is not whether immigration restrictions are ‘politically correct,’ but what must be done to preserve the moral and political character of America.

Statesmen today cannot simply reinstate the language or the policies of the Founding era, but they can recover and apply their underlying principles to modern realities. The Founders understood that America’s immigration policy must adapt as prudence demands. “In the infancy of the country,” Hamilton wrote in 1802, “with a boundless waste to people, it was politic to give a facility to naturalization; but our situation is now changed.”[REF] How much greater is the force of this observation after the closing of the frontier and decades of mass immigration that have severely tested the limits of assimilation?

Retrieving the long-standing vision of the American Founding will require an immigration system that once again recognizes that the will and faculty to be “assimilated into our civilization” is a non-negotiable prerequisite for good citizenship. “Hyphenated Americans,” who value the cultural heritage of their foreign ancestors over their status as American citizens, fail the test of assimilation. Immigrants may sometimes boost gross domestic product, but if they are unwilling to fully integrate into the American way of life, they do not belong in American society. Calvin Coolidge captured the essence of this ethos in his “First Annual Message”:

American institutions rest solely on good citizenship. They were created by people who had a background of self-government. New arrivals should be limited to our capacity to absorb them into the ranks of good citizenship. America must be kept American. For this purpose, it is necessary to continue a policy of restricted immigration.[REF]

Islamic culture poses distinctive challenges to the task of assimilation. However, Americans need not, and should not, resort to the crude and arbitrary racial categories of the past. Though the original meaning of the “free white person” clause is misunderstood, its structure and tone are still dissonant to contemporary ears. Even in the clause’s heyday, it was recognized as a blunt instrument with serious limitations, as illustrated by the Ozawa decision. 1 in 3 Infants Diagnosed With Peanut Allergy May Not Have the Condition

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Peanut allergies have been on the rise in recent decades.

According to statistics provided by Food Allergy Research & Education (FARE), an estimated 6.2 million children and adults in the United States are allergic to peanuts.

However, new research published in the November 2026 issue of The Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology suggests that over a third of infants thought to have this diagnosis might not actually have a peanut allergy.

In fact, having a history of reacting to peanuts and positive allergy testing on their own may not be sufficient to diagnose a peanut allergy for many infants.

The study’s authors suggest that a threshold oral food challenge — in which the child is given increasing amounts of the suspected food allergen under medical supervision to look for an allergic reaction — should be routinely considered to help confirm peanut allergy.

This additional testing could safely prevent children who are not allergic to peanuts from undergoing unnecessary treatment with oral immunotherapy.

It could also spare families from burdensome food restrictions and allergy precautions.

The researchers’ goal was to evaluate the accuracy and safety of the Allergy Development to an Accelerated Path to Tolerance (ADAPT) peanut oral immunotherapy program, which was established in 2024 at 10 public children’s hospitals across Australia.

This program provides a standardized approach to evaluating and treating infants with suspected peanut allergies.

The study included infants who had experienced a mild to moderate reaction to peanut before 12 months of age and had evidence of peanut sensitization through allergy testing.

Researchers reviewed medical records to examine how accurately the program’s approach identified peanut allergies and whether it could safely guide treatment.

A key part of the evaluation was a threshold oral food challenge. The challenge involved up to seven doses, ranging from 15 to 1,300 milligrams of peanut protein.

Medical teams monitored the infants for reactions and provided treatment when necessary.

Researchers reviewed records from April 2024 through October 2025. During that period, 1,275 infants were assessed through the program, and 778 underwent an oral food challenge.

The researchers also examined allergy test results and whether infants who reacted during the challenges could begin oral immunotherapy at an appropriate starting dose.

At the time of the oral food challenge, the infants had a median age of 13 months.

Of the 778 infants who underwent an oral food challenge, 507 (65.2%) had an allergic reaction.

However, 271 (34.8%) did not react to peanut during the supervised test, despite having a history of a previous reaction and positive allergy test results.

These findings suggest that relying on medical history and allergy tests alone may lead some infants to be labeled with a peanut allergy when they can actually tolerate the food.

Among the infants who reacted during the challenges, 61 experienced anaphylaxis, a severe allergic reaction that can affect breathing and blood pressure. This represented 12% of infants with a positive challenge and 7.8% of all infants who underwent testing. Medical teams safely managed these severe allergic reactions.

Researchers also found that infants who reacted to peanut generally had higher skin prick test results and Ara h 2-specific IgE levels than those who did not react.

However, these test results did not reliably indicate how much peanut would trigger a reaction or how severe that reaction would be.

Of the 394 infants who began peanut oral immunotherapy, 384 (97.5%) successfully started treatment at a dose below the amount that had triggered a reaction during their food challenge.

The researchers say these findings support considering supervised oral food challenges to confirm suspected peanut allergies before starting treatment or recommending long-term avoidance.

Erin Banta, MD, a board certified allergist and immunologist who treats both children and adults at Latitude Food Allergy Care in Great Neck, New York, said these findings are significant because they provide evidence for early use of oral food challenges in infants with peanut reactions.

“In those with mild-moderate prior reactions to peanut, early oral food challenge may allow for either delabeling of food allergy (in 1/3 of the patients in this study) or diagnosis of food allergy,” she told Healthline.

Banta, who wasn’t involved in the study, added that early delabeling takes a significant burden off the patient and their family.

“On the other hand,” she explained, “early diagnosis allows for early initiation of treatment with oral immunotherapy at a time in the patient’s life associated with the greatest success rates.”

Banta further noted that the study offers different pathways for oral immunotherapy based on the patient’s reaction threshold. This means that some patients may be able to complete treatment more quickly due to their individual threshold dose.

Lindsey Forstie, FNP, a family nurse practitioner with clinical training and experience in allergy and asthma care at Carefree Allergy in Phoenix, AZ, added that while further evaluation may be needed to confirm a peanut allergy diagnosis, it’s important for parents not to try introducing peanuts at home.

“Instead, they should speak with their pediatrician or allergist about appropriate testing and whether a supervised food challenge is safe and necessary,” she said.

According to Forstie, who was also not part of the study, the goal is to avoid unnecessary food restrictions while also keeping children safe.

“The key message is that not every positive allergy test means a child has a food allergy,” she told Healthline. “Accurate diagnosis helps protect children while allowing those who are not allergic to enjoy a more normal, less restrictive diet.”

The Allowlist Guarded the Destination, Not the Route: A Proxies Parameter Leaked Credentials From an Agent HTTP Tool

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Agent HTTP tools have a problem that ordinary HTTP clients do not: the caller is a language model that reads untrusted text. When an agent fetches a web page, a ticket or a README, whatever is in that content can try to steer the next tool call. That is indirect prompt injection, and it is the threat the advisory describes.

The Strands http_request tool let operators attach secrets to requests safely, in theory. You listed an environment variable and the hostnames it may be sent to, like this (the example comes from the tool's own docstring):

The model then passed auth_env_var="GITHUB_TOKEN" and a URL. The tool refused any URL whose hostname was not in the list. That control is sound for what it covers: the model cannot ask for the GitHub token to be sent to evil.example.

The gap was a second input the model could also set. The PR description puts it plainly: the proxies parameter "is included in the LLM-controllable input schema, allowing an LLM influenced by indirect prompt injection to route credentialed requests through actor-controlled proxy infrastructure, bypassing the HTTP_REQUEST_TOKEN_CONFIG hostname allowlist." The AWS bulletin adds the consequence: "The credential is attached, and the request is routed through the actor's proxy on the first hop, disclosing the credential in cleartext."

The GitHub advisory rates it Moderate with CVSS 6.9 and classifies it as CWE-863, incorrect authorization. At least one vulnerability aggregator lists 7.4. Scorers disagree, so read the vector and your own exposure, not the number. The advisory also notes a second weakness in the same neighborhood: GET requests bypassed the consent gate. I confirmed that in the v0.8.1 source, where the confirmation dialog applies only to POST, PUT, PATCH and DELETE. A credentialed GET to a hostile route therefore needed no human in the loop.

Because it guarded the destination, and a credential's journey has more than one position. Before the request reaches api.github.com, it passes through whatever the client was told to use as a proxy. That first hop sees the request, and the allowlist had no say over it.

This is the same shape as the lesson from the symlink and trailing-dot bugs I covered earlier: a check is only as strong as the set of inputs it actually constrains. Here the check constrained the URL's hostname, while the egress route, the scheme and the port were all outside it.

Here is the check in v0.8.1, trimmed to the part that matters:

.hostname drops the scheme and the port. So http://api.github.com/user and https://api.github.com:8443/user both pass a list containing api.github.com. Whether that matters depends on what the credential rides on, so I measured it rather than guessing.

I wrote a short script that starts throwaway listeners on 127.0.0.1 and uses the requests library (2.34.2) with a fake token. Nothing leaves the machine. The relevant part:

The listener for case 2 received only CONNECT api.github.com:443 HTTP/1.1 and Host: api.github.com:443. The secret was not in it, because with certificate verification on, the headers travel inside the TLS tunnel.

That second result refines the headline, it does not contradict it. The bulletin's "cleartext" statement holds when the approved hostname is reached over plain HTTP, and a hostname-only check does not stop that. The tool also gates disabling certificate verification behind a separate environment variable (STRANDS_HTTP_ALLOW_INSECURE_SSL), which is why the TLS case stayed closed in my run. I did not test every deployment shape, and I did not run the attack against a real Strands agent. Treat the experiment as a demonstration of the mechanism, and the advisory as the authority on the product.

I also checked one more egress knob that remained after the patch: redirects. The tool exposes allow_redirects, and requests follows them by default. Against a second local listener on a different port, the Authorization header was stripped on the cross-origin hop, but a custom X-API-Key header was forwarded. The tool supports auth_type="api_key", which sets exactly that header. This is general requests behavior, not a reported Strands flaw, and exploiting it would need an open redirect on an approved host. I include it because it shows how many places "where does this credential go" can be decided.

Small, which is the instructive part. PR #513 changed two files, with 14 additions and 26 deletions. It deleted the proxies property from the tool's JSON schema, deleted the doc example that showed a proxy, and stopped forwarding tool_input.get("proxies") to requests. The test that asserted "the proxy was passed through" now asserts the opposite: an LLM-supplied proxies field must not reach requests.

The patch's own comment answers it for proxies: "Proxies are an operator/deployment concern, not something the model should choose."

Three observations. Gate A, the hostname check, stops only the case everybody thinks of. Gate B closes three more by comparing the whole origin, and it costs nothing on legitimate traffic. Gate C closes the last one, the route, and it does so by removing the model's say over routing rather than by inspecting the route.

That last design principle is the one to carry away. You can try to validate a model-supplied proxy URL, but the validation has to know every host you consider safe, every scheme, every redirect, and every environment variable the HTTP library reads. Taking the parameter out of the schema is a smaller attack surface than any validator, and it is the option the maintainers chose.

Practical consequences for anyone running it:

What is CVE-2026-18394? An incorrect-authorization flaw (CWE-863) in the http_request tool of strands-agents-tools before 0.8.2. The model could set a proxies parameter, so an indirect prompt injection could route a credentialed request through an attacker-controlled proxy and bypass the hostname allowlist for HTTP_REQUEST_TOKEN_CONFIG.

Which versions are affected and what is the fix? Versions before 0.8.2 are affected. Version 0.8.2 removes proxies from the model-visible input. Proxies are now configured only through HTTP_PROXY and HTTPS_PROXY in the environment, or in code. The advisory also recommends rotating credentials configured on affected versions.

Would HTTPS have protected the token? In my local test, a proxy on an https:// request saw only the CONNECT line and not the Authorization header, because certificate verification was on. The bulletin's cleartext scenario applies when the approved hostname is requested over plain HTTP, which a hostname-only allowlist permits. Do not rely on TLS to rescue a gate that ignores the scheme.

Is an allowlist enough for credentialed tools? Only if it covers the origin and the route. A hostname list alone does not constrain scheme, port, proxy or redirects. Pin the exact origin, keep routing out of the model's hands, and add consent for credentialed requests regardless of HTTP method.

Should I upgrade to v0.9.0? Not as a security fix. The release deprecates the package and turns every tool into an import error. Stay on a patched version only as a bridge, and plan the migration to the replacement tools, reviewing their parameters before you give them credentials.

Un dataset con la etiqueta benchmark:official y un archivo eval.yaml define una o varias tareas. Los resultados no se envían a un formulario central: cada modelo guarda un YAML en su propio repositorio, dentro de .eval_results/, y Hugging Face los agrega en la tabla del benchmark. El sello verified se concede cuando hay un verifyToken válido, es decir, cuando la evaluación corrió en HF Jobs con inspect-ai. Un resultado enviado por pull request abierta se muestra como community. Todo lo demás es lo que el autor del modelo declara en su ficha.

Aquí está el cálculo que casi nunca se hace. Si un benchmark tiene n preguntas, un acierto más sube la nota 100 / n puntos. En GPQA Diamond son 0,505 puntos por pregunta. Un 94,44 equivale exactamente a 187 de 198, y un 93,43 a 185 de 198: dos preguntas de diferencia.

No es un reproche: simplemente no se puede saber, y esa es la cuestión.

Para estimar el ruido de muestreo uso el intervalo de Wilson al 95 %, que se comporta bien con proporciones cercanas al 100 % y con n pequeño (el intervalo normal clásico se sale del rango). Para el 94,44 de GPQA da de 90,3 a 96,9. Para el 100 de AIME 2026 con 30 problemas, de 88,6 a 100: un 100 sobre 30 problemas es compatible con un modelo que falla uno de cada nueve.

TensorRT-LLM 1.4 Makes trtllm-serve Run 8 HTTP Frontends by Default: RAM Cost, Connection Balance, and What Still Differs

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

TensorRT-LLM 1.4.0rc3 (GitHub release 2026-11-06) flips one default that changes the shape of your deployment: trtllm-serve now starts 8 HTTP frontend processes instead of 1. The release notes mark it BREAKING (PR #19379). I read that PR, the earlier prototype PR #16523, the metrics fix #19394, the current docs on main, and the release notes, then ran two small simulations of my own. I did not run a GPU workload.

The CLI default for --num_serve_frontends on the trtllm-serve serve subcommand went from 1 to 8. The launcher process owns the engine. The other 7 frontends re-exec the command line, attach to the same executor, and bind the same port with SO_REUSEPORT, so clients still see one URL.

Three details from the PR are easy to miss:

The same release candidate carries two other BREAKING entries (#21036 removes unused LlmRequest fields, #21041 removes obsolete TensorRT-backend compatibility surfaces). Those are API removals. The frontend default is the one that changes what a plain launch does on your host.

Every request on a single trtllm-serve process goes through one asyncio event loop: json.loads, pydantic validation, and then every streamed chunk written back. The prototype PR (#16523, merged 2026-07-22) reported this on a DeepSeek V4 disaggregated generation worker:

Doubling concurrency from 512 to 1,024 roughly doubled the delay. Doubling it again multiplied it by about 14, while decode step time stayed flat. The PR author called this an M/M/1-style blow-up, and I agree with the shape. In a single-server queue the mean wait grows like utilization divided by one minus utilization, so it looks harmless until the loop approaches saturation and then explodes. Adding GPU capacity does nothing for it, because the GPU is not the queue.

That is why eight processes help: the same parsing and streaming work is spread over eight event loops and eight GILs. The PR says the setting was already the production configuration in the authors' agentic disaggregated setups, with 8 on context workers and 4 on generation workers.

The kernel assigns each new connection to one of the listening sockets, effectively by hashing the connection tuple. The docs say it plainly: each request is handled entirely by the frontend that accepted its connection. Once a client holds a keep-alive connection, all of its requests land on the same frontend.

So the question that matters is how many long-lived connections your clients open. I modeled it with the standard library only. The assumptions are mine and deliberately simple: connections are assigned uniformly at random to 8 frontends, every connection carries equal load, and the total offered load equals 3 single-process event loops (so one frontend alone would be hopelessly saturated). Perfect balance would put each frontend at 0.375 utilization, a mean M/M/1 wait of 0.6 service times.

I ran 20,000 draws per connection count with a fixed seed:

With 1,024 connections you are within 13% of perfect balance. With 16 you are at a median utilization of 0.75, a mean wait of 3.0 service times against the balanced 0.6, and about one draw in thirteen saturates a frontend. This is an illustration, not a measurement of TensorRT-LLM. Real traffic is lumpier than equal-weight connections (a long streaming response weighs more than a short one), which I expect makes the tail worse, but I have not measured that. If your traffic comes through a router or gateway with a small connection pool, count its connections to this server before assuming 8 frontends means 8x headroom.

The PR's verification section is the only measurement I found, so I use it as is. One container with 1x GB300, Qwen3-0.6B, launched the way that cluster runs production:

Resident memory was about 1.99 GB per attached frontend and about 2.5 GB for the launcher. Every extra frontend is a full Python process that imports TensorRT-LLM and loads the tokenizer, which is what the docs warn about. The arithmetic for the default is 2.5 + 7 x 1.99 = 16.43 GB, about 13.9 GB more than a single frontend.

Two things to keep in mind. First, this is one run on a tiny model, so treat 1.99 GB as an order of magnitude, since a larger tokenizer or extra imports will move it. Second, a container memory limit sized around a single serving process will now get OOM-killed during startup. Attached frontends hold no GPU memory: in the PR, nvidia-smi listed only the worker (58.7 GiB including KV cache) and the launcher (748 MiB), identical with 1 or 8 frontends.

This is the part I almost got wrong. The body of #19379 lists a caveat: /metrics, /prometheus/metrics and /health are answered by whichever frontend accepted the connection, so scrapes see one frontend's share and counters appear to reset. It cites a measurement of trtllm_request_success_total showing 5 distinct values (6, 7, 8 and 10 among them) for 64 requests across 32 scrapes.

But #19394, "make multi-frontend metrics, profiling and liveness launcher-owned", was merged on 2026-10-28, two days before the default flip, and I confirmed with the compare API that both land first in rc3 (neither is in rc2). It reports the same 32 scrapes returning one identical value (64) afterwards. The mechanism: the launcher sets one shared PROMETHEUS_MULTIPROC_DIR before it spawns children, iteration-derived series come from the launcher only, and attached frontends forward GET /metrics to the launcher over a Unix socket. The current docs on main match that: /metrics is forwarded and /prometheus/metrics covers every frontend.

Why does this matter to you? Because Prometheus treats any decrease in a counter as a reset. To show what the sharded behavior would have done to a dashboard, I simulated 64 requests landing on 8 per-process counters, scraped at random every second request:

With shared counters the increase is the true value, 62 between the first and last scrape, every time. With per-frontend counters the 5th to 95th percentile spread is 47 to 86 (minimum 23, maximum 111), and 38% of runs were off by more than 10. Illustrative again, but it is why a build without #19394 and with 8 frontends is a bad combination for anything alerting on request rates.

How do I go back to a single frontend? Pass --num_serve_frontends 1 or set num_serve_frontends: 1 in the --config YAML. The YAML value overrides the new CLI default.

AI training helped some women more job interviews, but not as many as their rivals got

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In a hiring test, AI training lifted interview calls for underrepresented women from 11 to 14 percent, while others got 25 percent without it.

Learning a popular skill like AI is supposed to help you get a job. But a new study found that for women who already get fewer interviews, having an AI certificate only helped a little bit.

In a London hiring experiment, it lifted their rate of interview invitations from about 11 to 14 percent.

Prospective employers invited comparable women outside those groups, with no certificate at all, to interview 25 percent of the time. The certificate narrowed the gap, but most of the gap was still there.

Nick Drydakis, an economist at Anglia Ruskin University (ARU) in Cambridge, England, ran the experiment in 2025. He directs the university’s Centre for Inclusive Societies and Economies.

Governments, employers and training providers have been promoting AI training as a route into work. Most research on AI and hiring, though, has looked at bias inside screening software.

Drydakis asked what employers do when the applicant is the one with AI skills.

Between January and June, his team answered 1,016 real job ads in London, sending three matched applications to each one. That came to more than 3,000 applications in all.

Every applicant was a fictional woman applying for a full-time private-sector job that didn’t require a degree. Each held a Level 3 vocational qualification, a practical course usually started between ages 16 and 18, plus a one-year apprenticeship.

The study ran as four separate experiments. In each, one application came from a woman in the majority group, and two came from a woman in an underrepresented group.

The underrepresented groups were Black British women, women aged 50 instead of 28, lesbian women, and women who disclosed being on the autism spectrum.

Sexual orientation showed up as a partner’s name, Helen rather than George. Autism showed up as a short line such as, “I am on the autism spectrum and known for my strong focus and attention to detail.”

One of the two applications from the underrepresented group also listed a six-month AI training certificate.

Its description covered using AI tools to automate office paperwork and handle data, aimed at the routine digital work these jobs involve.

Before any applications went out, 71 people, including HR staff, employers, students and hiring researchers, checked that each signal read clearly and that the matched applications looked equally strong otherwise.

Every application went out within a day of the ad appearing, and the team saw no sign that any employer noticed the matched sets.

Across all four experiments, prospective employers invited women from the majority groups to interview 25 percent of the time. Women from the underrepresented groups, with matching qualifications and no certificate, were invited 11 percent of the time.

The gap appeared in every experiment. Because the applications were matched, Drydakis concluded that employers were responding differently at the screening stage.

With the AI certificate, the underrepresented women’s rate rose to 14 percent. That’s about 28 percent more invitations than the same women got without it, yet still far below the majority group’s 25 percent.

“AI training can help open doors, but it should not be viewed as a substitute for fair recruitment practices,” Drydakis said.

The boost wasn’t the same everywhere. Women who disclosed autism were invited about 7 percent of the time without the certificate and 11 percent with it, against 24 percent for women who didn’t disclose.

Lesbian women’s invitation rate rose from about 13 to 18 percent, against 26 percent for heterosexual women. Both of those gains held up in the statistical tests.

For women aged 50, the rise from about 10 to 12 percent was smaller, and its statistical support depended on the test used.

The invitation rate for Black British women showed a small change, from 15 to 17 percent. Because of this small change, the experiment cannot determine whether the certificate provided any benefit at all.

The interview invitations also differed in what the jobs paid. Underrepresented women without the certificate were more often invited by employers advertising lower wages, about 7 percent lower per hour on average.

With the certificate, that pay gap shrank to about 4 percent. It didn’t disappear.

So the certificate helped in two ways, more calls and better-paid ones. However, neither brought the women level with the majority group.

Drydakis has argued in earlier work that AI skills act as a form of AI capital, something workers can build that pays off in the job market. This experiment fits that idea, up to a point.

“If we want technological change to create opportunities for everyone, investment in digital skills must be matched by strong efforts to tackle discrimination and ensure qualifications are assessed consistently across all applicant groups,” he said.

No majority-group applicant ever carried the AI certificate. That means the study can’t say whether the training would help every applicant just as much. If it did, the gap between groups wouldn’t shrink at all.

The study also stopped at the interview invitation. It didn’t follow job offers, actual pay, or how anyone was treated at work. Nor can it tell whether employers acted on prejudice or on guesses about who would do the job well.

All the applicants were women seeking jobs without a degree requirement in one city, so the results may not hold for graduate jobs, men, or other places.

A certificate that stood out in 2025 may also become routine as AI training spreads, and repeating the experiment in a few years would show whether its value holds up.

The full study was published in the journal Industrial Relations: A Journal of Economy and Society.

Does the President's Removal Power Extend to the Voice of America?

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

The DC Circuit affirms an injunction barring the removal of VOA head Michael Abramowicz. Will the justices see the case the same way?

Trump v. Slaughter affirmed the President's presumptive power to remove principal officers (other than members of the Federal Reserve Board). But does this power also extend to inferior officers? And is it constitutional for Congress to effectively insulate some inferior officers from presidential removal?

Today, in Abramowitz v. Lake, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit affirmed a district court injunction barring the removal of Michael Abramowitz, the head of the Voice of America. In the process, it rejected the Department of Justice's arguments that the district court lacked jurisdiction to consider the case and held Congress may (as a practical matter) insulate inferior officers by presidential removal by vesting removal authority in the hands of an agency head that requires Senate confirmation.

Judge Pillard wrote for the panel, and was joined by Judges Srinivasan and Pan. Here is how she summarized the opinion.

Seeking to preserve the journalistic integrity of the news broadcasters within the U.S. Agency for Global Media, Congress empowered a separate agency, the International Broadcasting Advisory Board, with final authority over the hiring and firing of the broadcasters' heads-of-network. The leaders of the Agency for Global Media ignored that statute when they attempted unilaterally to fire Michael Abramowitz, the head of Voice of America. Abramowitz sued, and the district court permanently enjoined the Agency for Global Media from removing Abramowitz without the International Broadcasting Advisory Board's approval.

We affirm the injunction. The district court had jurisdiction to enforce the statutory requirements governing the Voice of America directorship. Those statutory requirements are consistent with Article II. And the district court had equitable power to enjoin defendants from effectuating the illegal termination.

While acknowledging that, as a general matter, Congress has channeled litigation concerning allegedly improper removal of government employees to the Merit Systems Protection Board under the Civil Service Reform Act, the panel concluded that this general rule does not apply to the VOA as "Congress gave no hint" that it sought to divest district courts of jurisdiction to hear cases such as this when it revised the statute governing VOA in 2021. The removal protection of the VOA head "is not a standard employment law," according to the D.C. Circuit, "but a durable barrier against any breach of the firewall protecting the journalistic integrity of Voice of America." Writes Judge Pillard: "Congress had not reason to channel Abramowitz's claim through the CSRA, and every reason not to."

On the merits, the panel concluded Congress lawfully insulated the head of the VOA--an inferior officer--from removal by the President by vesting authority to appoint and remove the head of VOA in the International Broadcasting Advisory Board (IBAB).

IBAB is a seven-member "separate independent agency," the members of which must be bipartisan and are subject to Senate confirmation. IBAB is the relevant department head, even though it does not directly supervise VOA (as that job is performed by the U.S. Agency for Global Media-- an arrangement the panel concluded is lawful under Morrison v. Olson.

The Department of Justice argued this unconstitutionally constrains the President's removal power, but the court was unconvinced.

So long as the President has adequate control over the department head, and the department head can control the inferior officer (as is the case here), the President can transitively control the inferior officer. The department head's supervision "preserv[es] the chain of command in Article II." Braidwood, 606 U.S. at 754.

Yet practical effect of the court's ruling is that there is nothing the President can do to replace the head of the VOA. Six of the seven IBAB seats are currently open (having been removed by President Trump in 2025). Thus the Board is inquorate, and lacks the ability to remove the head of the VOA (or do much else). And, should Congress refuse to confirm new IBAB members, the current VOA head will remain in place. The DC Circuit may conclude this arrangement formally leaves the President with "adequate control" over the Board, but what good is control over a department head that is incapable of acting?

I suspect the Administration will seek further review, ultimately in the Supreme Court.  The question is whether it files a petition for certiorari right away, or first seeks rehearing en banc. While there is no likelihood the full D.C. Circuit will disturb the panel ruling, a petition for rehearing en banc might generate a dissent that could help attract the justices' attention. This case would also give the justices the opportunity to revisit Morrison v. Olson and say more about the extent to which inferior officers may (or may not) be insulated from presidential removal.
