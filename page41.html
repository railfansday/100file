How did England cling on against Spain and what did Tuchel do differently?

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

James Trafford (right) kept Spain out in the latter stages

England held on to beat Spain 3-2 with a backs-to-the-wall finale in the Nations League to finish top of their group.

But many observers would have been feeling a sense of deja vu from the World Cup semi-final when England sat back and were beaten by Argentina.

So what was different this time? BBC Sport has a look.

First of all the context of the two games was entirely different.

England were 1-0 up against Argentina in the 55th minute on neutral territory in Atlanta - and still had all their players.

Anthony Gordon had put them ahead but Lionel Messi set up Enzo Fernandez and then Lautaro Martinez late on.

But against Spain the best performance of Thomas Tuchel's reign had them 3-0 up through goals from Harry Kane, Ezri Konsa and Ben White.

Elliot Anderson then fouled Lamine Yamal for a penalty, which Mikel Oyarzabal scored - and that began the siege at a partisan Riyadh Air Metropolitano.

Nico Williams made it 3-2 but Spain could not find an equaliser, thanks in part to England keeper James Trafford.

"Half an hour feels like two hours against a team like this [with 10 men]," Tuchel told ITV afterwards. "That's the way it is. Without the red card we were clear winners."

60 minutes of perfection, 30 minutes of chaos - an England win for the ages

England cling on to stun world champions Spain in thriller

Making defensive subs is fine when it works - but when it does not, boy will a manager hear about it.

In the World Cup last 16, England had been 2-1 up against Mexico in the Azteca when Jarell Quansah was sent off.

Tuchel brought on three defenders for a defender, a midfielder and a forward.

But England won 3-2 and Dan Burn's cameo was the stuff of legend.

However, nine days later with England 1-0 up against Argentina, Tuchel did the same again - three defenders on for a defender, midfielder and forward.

The first of those subs, with Konsa replacing Anthony Gordon, was in the 72nd minute.

And England just sat back until Argentina broke their resistance.

However, against Spain this time, despite England being down to 10 men and facing incessant pressure, Tuchel was not so quick with defensive subs.

Myles Lewis-Skelly, replacing Harry Kane, was the first on after 78 minutes - 15 minutes after Anderson's sending off. And then five minutes later he brought on two more players - but one of them was a striker in Dominic Calvert-Lewin.

Burn replaced fellow defender White in the 89th minute.

"Harry played 78 minutes, Jude [Bellingham] 83 minutes and then just defending and defending, it's tiring," explained Tuchel.

"So we needed fresh energy. I was asked yesterday what happens if there needs to be a critical or crucial substitution. And I said, I feel free enough to take any substitution if I think it is necessary to help the team and to influence the game.

"I feel free enough to do that and I think there was a lot of defending needed. So we just went for some fresh legs."

Dan Burn usually comes on when England are trying to hang on - but he was only introduced for the last few minutes

England actually faced more of a siege this time than against the Argentines, the stats confirm.

In the final 30 minutes they faced 19 shots from Spain, with seven on target and an expected goals (xG) total of 2.53.

Argentina, in that time, had 10 shots, with four on target and an xG of 1.41.

In terms of passes from England the numbers were similar with 65 against Argentina and 61 against Spain - both times with an accuracy rate of about 64%.

In Thursday's 2-1 win against Croatia, as a comparison, they tried 557 passes with accuracy of 89%.

"For half an hour it was Mexico reloaded and it felt like we were back in the Azteca. We had to overcome 30 minutes which, against Spain, felt like three hours," Tuchel told BBC Radio 5 Live.

"The difficult thing is it's not only a red card, it's a red card, and a goal so that makes a completely new game. Thirty minutes against the world champion feels like three hours. I said it before in the stadium and wave after wave after wave."

England were criticised for blowing a 2-1 half-time lead at Wembley in their first Nations League game with Spain.

But on that day their final 30-minute figurers were much better - with only five shots faced and 176 attempted passes with a 90% accuracy.

So what was different this time? Perhaps the team showed more resilience and unity?

After Spain had pulled one back England's players had a huddle.

"I love the fact the team went into a huddle and if anything the manager's stepped back because he wants to see the player reaction," said ex-England defender Matt Upson on BBC Radio 5 Live.

"I almost think that the trauma from that sending off of Elliot Anderson has almost been a positive thing for England because it's given the team a chance to get together and cope in what is an incredibly difficult situation and come through the other side.

"There was so much going on in this game. England had to suffer and came through the other side."

Defender and goalscorer White was asked about the huddle after the game.

"Just stay calm, when you're one down it becomes more difficult and things change so it was all about managing that," he said.

Another factor was the performance of England keeper James Trafford, who made six saves - with four of them coming in the final 12 minutes of the game with the score at 3-2.

He made an incredible double save to deny Fermin Lopez and Lamine Yamal in injury time.

Upson said: "I think that double save was the moment for Spain and Trafford had the answers."

In Prime Video's action thriller The Terminal List, a Navy SEAL sets out on a vengeance mission after his entire platoon is murdered. James Reece (Chris Pratt) abandons the rigid structures of the military to pursue the mysterious forces behind this act, drawing on nearly two decades of experience in warfare to hunt down those responsible. The series just returned for Season 2 in October and is one of the most-watched shows on Prime Video, ranking at number three on the streamer's top ten chart at the time of writing. This show wouldn't be possible without Jack Carr, the author of the books that inspired the narrative.

Carr is a former Navy SEAL turned author with over 20 years of Naval Special Warfare experience. His exploits have taken him to Iraq, Afghanistan, and the Philippines. His first book, The Terminal List, was published in 2018 and became a New York Times bestseller, leading to more entries in the James Reece series and an executive producer credit on the television adaptation. Now, Carr is bringing his television experience to NBC.

The author is officially an executive producer on NBC's new Bosch-style detective series currently in development. The series titled Worth is helmed by Mike Diliberti. It centers on Will Worth, a decorated Army Ranger who shares some characteristics with Carr: a war hero turned best-selling author and pop culture celebrity. However, the titular character dislikes the direction his life has taken and decides to stop coasting on his fame, returning home to Nashville to pick up his old job as a detective. Worth is also executive produced by Jake Coburn, who co-showrun NBC's The Hunting Party.

Carr will remain busy because Prime Video is adapting another one of his novels, Savage Son, in The Terminal List series. It is unclear yet whether the installment will be the third season of The Terminal List or a standalone story, but Pratt has been confirmed to return as Reece. The story is described as Reece's most personal and dangerous mission yet, "exploring the thin line between civilization and the savage instincts that emerge when survival becomes personal." Neither Savage Son nor Worth have a premiere date yet this early in the production cycle.

Why The Longest Single-Aisle Twinjet Ever Built Is An Aircraft Hard For Airlines To Get Rid Of

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

While not as rough as the more recent Airbus A330-800neo, the Boeing 757-300 variant was a disappointment for Boeing. Unfortunately, Boeing does not release figures for the incremental breakeven point needed to cover the variant's development costs, and it is unclear what sales Boeing envisioned when it commissioned the program in the 1990s. However, it is safe to say that it planned to sell a good many more than the 55 examples it did.

It is therefore somewhat paradoxical that one of Boeing's more disappointing variants has also become one of the most difficult to replace. Today, only two major passenger service commercial operators fly this type: United Airlines and Delta Air Lines. In recent years, the two giants have announced plans to replace their fleets of 757-200s and 767-300ERs, but not the 757-300s.

The 757 is a heavy narrowbody airliner with some characteristics typically associated with widebody airliners, such as a double-wheel bogie assembly. It was developed as a mid-sized airplane to bridge the gap between Boeing's narrowbody 737 series and the relatively small 767 widebody. It was produced in two basic variants (excluding freighter and special mission subvariants), the 757-200 and 757-300.

The original 757-200 version entered service with Eastern Air Lines in 1983 and had a full payload range of 3,860 nautical miles. The 757-200 was by far the more popular variant, accounting for 913 units sold, out of the family's total production run of 1,050 aircraft. The 757-300 was the stretched version of the 757 family and entered service with Condor in 1999, making it much newer. With a length of 178.7 feet (54.4 meters), it is notable for being the longest narrowbody airliner ever built.

The 757-300 replaced some older aircraft like the McDonnell Douglas DC-1, had a range of around 3,395 nautical miles, and was surprisingly unpopular. Only 55 units were ever ordered. Customers had the option of ordering the Rolls-Royce RB211-535E4B or the Pratt & Whitney PW2043 turbofans for the aircraft. The type is capable of seating around 234 to 243 passengers in a standard two-class layout and up to 295 in a high-density charter configuration. It also offers almost 2,400 cubic feet of cargo space.

As stated, orders for the stretched 757-300 were disappointing, with just 55 examples ultimately delivered to seven customers. Northwest Airlines had the largest order (16), followed by Condor (13), ATA Airlines (12), Continental Airlines (nine), Arkia (two), Thomas Cook Airlines (two), and Icelandair (one). All these operators (except for Arkia and Thomas Cook) also purchased the 757-200.

Boeing records show that Northwest was the only airline to opt for its 757-300s to be equipped with Pratt & Whitney engines; all the others came with Rolls-Royce engines. This means that of the 55 delivered 757-300s, Rolls-Royce accounted for 39, and Pratt powered the remaining 16. Northwest's aircraft were inherited by Delta Air Lines following the merger.

Data from Planespotters.net show all sixteen ex-Northwest aircraft remain in service with Delta. Meanwhile, Continental subsequently purchased around half of the world's supply of 757-300s on secondary markets, and United currently operates 21 of them. Ukraine's SkyLine Express operates five of them, and Fly Khiva (an Uzbek airline) operates two ex-Icelandair examples. Icelandair eventually took over the two Arkia 757-300s, but has since divested the type.

Boeing has been considering a 'New Midsize Airplane,' commonly known as NMA and the Boeing 797, but it has yet to come to fruition.

The 757 filled a specific niche: the gap between narrowbody and widebody aircraft. The issue was that while there was demand for such an aircraft, it was much smaller than demand for narrowbody 737s and A320neos or larger 767s (later 787s) and A330s. This was made worse after the September 11 attacks and the subsequent slump in air travel, with the resulting drop-off in new airline orders for the aircraft.

Boeing ended production of the 757 in 2004 without replacement. Airbus also didn't offer a mid-sized airplane to fill the gap. In the later 2010s, Boeing began to seriously consider a clean-sheet replacement to fill the gap. Such an aircraft would be able to both attract new orders and replace aging 757s. The peak of Boeing's customer consultations with over 50 airlines came in 2017 and 2018 with its New Midsized Airplane (NMA), or 797 concept.

But then came the 2018 and 2019 MAX crashes, which, together with COVID-19 and other issues, plunged Boeing into a prolonged crisis that it is still recovering from. Dennis Muilenburg (during the Q3 2019 Earnings Call) stated that the NMA remained "a serious project" under evaluation, though he acknowledged Boeing's immediate priority was safely returning the 737 MAX to service before committing capital to a new airplane program. But the NMA never happened; the MAX and 777X remained Boeing's priorities for its strained finances.

Now it appears the window of opportunity for Boeing's NMA has passed. Airbus developed the stretched A321neo and then developed the extended-range A321XLR. This aircraft is already filling much of the mid-sized airplane gap and is well on its way to replacing many of the world's aging 757-200 fleets while also opening up many new thin routes around the world. Widebodies, like the shorter 787-8, cost significantly more to operate per trip.

Making matters worse for any future NMA, JetZero, in partnership with Northrop Grumman, is powering on with its Z4 blended-wing-body aircraft. Should that aircraft actually enter service in the 2030s, it would be targeting the mid-sized market. It has secured investment from some of Boeing's most loyal customers, such as Alaska Airlines.

The A321XLR offers a dramatic improvement in fuel burn compared with the 757 family, but is better suited to replace the 757-200. It directly targets the thin transatlantic and medium-long-haul routes that the 757-200 served. But it is less suited to replacing the 757-300 as a high-capacity domestic/short-to-medium-haul stretcher. The other modern alternative, the delayed 737 MAX 10, also carried fewer passengers in comparable premium-heavy configurations.

There isn't a single airliner that can replicate all of the Boeing 757's capabilities, but perhaps the market doesn't require one.

Hypothetically, in a simplistic world where Airbus thought it could replace all 900+ 757-200s with a special A320 stretch, it could be worth it. This assumes no other customers or market growth. A modern high-end Boeing or Airbus clean-sheet airliner would likely need to sell 800 to 1,500+ aircraft just to break even over a 20- to 30-year production run.

A stretch is very different. As a rough guide, Airbus would likely need to only sell 150-400 incremental units to break even. This assumes no cannibalization of its other aircraft. In this overly-simple scenario, it makes sense for Airbus to develop a special variant (e.g., A321XLR) to replace the 757-200. But the 757-300 is very different. From the narrow point of view of replacing the 757-300, there is almost no market.

Only 55 examples were ever sold; that is not remotely enough for an airplane maker to recoup the costs of developing a stretch or special variant to replace it. In the real world, there would likely be considerable opportunity for growth beyond the remaining operators, but the basic point remains. This is a very small niche. Airbus has already been stung by disappointing programs like the A380 and A330-800neo, which almost certainly lost money. As stated, Boeing considered filling the much broader midsize segment, but ultimately, its core 737, 787, and 777X programs were much more pressing.

With Icelandair retiring its limited fleet of 757-300s, the conversation about replacing the aircraft is basically a conversation about United and Delta. That conversation is also related to the 757's twin, the widebody 767. The two aircraft were built to be as interchangeable as possible, and both United and Delta operate the world's largest 767 fleets. Replacing the 757, therefore, also concerns replacing the 767.

Besides filling specific niches that other aircraft can't and being heavily interchangeable with the 767, the 757-300 has another advantage. Being older, they are already paid off. This means the aircraft can partially compensate for higher maintenance expenses with 767 interchangeability and higher fuel consumption with lower capital/leasing costs.

United and Delta have repeatedly extended the 757-300's service life well into the late 2020s and the early 2030s. Both airlines are well into their efforts to replace their fleets of 757-200s (partially with A321XLRs). United's 757-300 fleet is now an average of 23 years old, while Delta's is closer to 24 years. Neither has announced a set one-for-one replacement for them, as no existing narrowbody matches their combination of high multi-class seating capacity and other profiles.

Star Trek Officially Replaces Everyone's Deep Space Nine Crush In Federation Fleet

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Star Trek fans have had a great year so far celebrating its 60th anniversary in 2026, looking back at some of the beloved and unforgettable characters from the franchise's six-decade run. From the OG Kirk and Spock to the beloved crew of The Next Generation, every era has its standouts. However, for fans who grew up watching in the 90s, Star Trek: Deep Space Nine will always hold a special place in the larger catalog.

Deep Space Nine had an incredible ensemble of characters, but there's no question that Jadzia Dax, played by Terry Farrell, was undoubtedly one of its top fan-favorites. The brilliant, witty Trill science officer was also a major 90s TV crush for plenty of viewers, making her eventual departure from the series especially heartbreaking.

Fortunately, Star Trek found a way to replace her without completely saying goodbye, bringing in Ezri and allowing Jadzia's legacy to live on through the unique biology of the Trill species. Now, nearly three decades later, Jadzia and her official replacement are making a surprising franchise return in Federation Fleet.

Star Trek: Deep Space Nine fans can now revisit one of the show's most heartbreaking character departures thanks to two new Magic: The Gathering cards, "Ezri Dax, Newly Joined" and "Jadzia Dax, Living Legacy." Ezri is featured in the new Federation Fleet Commander deck, while Jadzia appears in the main Star Trek set, including an impressive nine-card Deep Space Nine scene, "Deep Space Find."

Both cards offer a clever tribute to Jadzia's replacement during the original series, which is still one of the most emotional moments in DS9 history. Terry Farrell's Jadzia was killed off by Gul Dukat during the Season 6 finale, leaving fans devastated and her husband, Worf, heartbroken.

However, Jadzia was a joined Trill, meaning she carried a sentient symbiont known as Dax, which retained memories and experiences from several previous hosts. Following Jadzia's death, the symbiont was transferred to Ezri Tigan, played by Nicole de Boer, bringing along the character's memories and legacy to survive through an entirely new host.

The two MTG cards actually translate/adapt the unique symbiont process beautifully. Jadzia's card features an ability called "Transfer Symbiont," allowing players to discard her to strengthen another creature with two +1/+1 counters. Meanwhile, Ezri can enter the battlefield as a copy of another creature that died that turn, retaining her own name and Trill identity. It's a clever depiction of how Ezri effectively replaced Jadzia without completely erasing her in the show itself.

The larger Magic: The Gathering x Star Trek crossover officially launched worldwide on November 13, 2026, following pre-release events earlier in the month. The ambitious release celebrates 60 years of Star Trek, featuring hundreds of cards spanning virtually every era of the franchise, alongside four Commander decks, collectible scene cards, and even ultra-rare cards signed by legendary actors.

That said, reception among longtime MTG players has been somewhat mixed so far, with some criticizing the artwork, repeat characters, and leaning too hard into crossover sets, although others have praised them. For dedicated Star Trek collectors, however, there's plenty to get excited about, especially with so many beloved characters returning. 5 Brilliant Books To Read Before Kicking the Bucket

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Five stories that are bound to make a vivid impression

On principle, any list of “books you must read before you die” should be unending. Literary staples aside, the number of must-read books in existence is constantly expanding and evolving. Older books undergo revaluation, no-brainer entries are viewed through a fresh lens, and contemporary titles join an already massive library of must-reads.

Also, tastes are subjective. Not everyone might jive with Great Expectations or Les Misérables despite their objective artistic merit. The definition of “must-read” has drastically changed over time. Keeping this in mind, Polygon has chosen five brilliant, indisputable books to read before kicking the bucket. Some are obvious picks, while others, not so much.

Fyodor Dostoevsky’s body of work is often classified as essential reads for good reason, and The Brothers Karamazov is undoubtedly one such entry. The author’s final novel was written under dire personal circumstances, which included failing health and profound emotional grief. The novel is deeply philosophical in nature, raising pertinent questions about faith, morality, and skepticism. From a certain perspective, it's also about human evil, and how it manifests as mundane emotions like greed or hedonism.

The novel opens with wealthy landowner Fyodor Karamazov neglecting all three of his sons — Dmitri, Ivan, and Alyosha. Dostoevsky goes into intricate detail to flesh out the characters in this dysfunctional household, which is caught up in an inheritance dispute and subsequent scandal. After a murder takes place, The Brothers Karamazov turns into a tense courtroom drama. Each Karamazov brother represents a human archetype, which Dostoevsky uses to debate the nature of free will. While this book isn’t as relentless and claustrophobic as Crime and Punishment, it is sweeping and dramatic in its exploration of character motivations and human behavior.

A milestone in African literature, Chinua Achebe’s Things Fall Apart is about Okonkwo, an influential leader of the fictional Igbo clan. Okonkwo is an accomplished warrior and champion who vows to be nothing like his alcoholic, flute-playing father. After he takes a young boy named Ikemefuna as his ward, the oracle of his village declares the child must be killed. This incident haunts Okonkwo, who goes on to oppose rampant colonialism and reclaim the lost dignity of his people. Achebe presents an essential viewpoint, as most colonial literature portrayed Igbo culture in biased and problematic ways.

Things Fall Apart provides rich complexity to the tribes portrayed, granting an authentic look into local customs, laws, and traditions. Okonkwo is a deeply flawed but heroic protagonist who tries to hold onto tradition in the light of aggressive socio-political change. Few novels dissect colonialism like Achebe’s debut story, which explains how colonizers quietly infiltrate local cultures and turn close-knit communities against one another. This leads to a fractured sense of shared history and heritage that requires an enormous amount of time to acclimatize to.

Arundhati Roy’s most famous work, The God of Small Things, is an unflinching look at vulnerability in the face of rigid societal rules. The novel follows fraternal twins Estha and Rahel, who grow up amid the arrival of their British cousin in India and intense familial tensions. Roy uses a disjointed narrative to tell this moving tale about social discrimination, colonialism, and local politics that inevitably affect the personal lives of our protagonists. The prose is vivid and sensory, filtered through the perspective of children who view the world in a more tactile way than adults do.

The God of Small Things pits massive historical upheavals against unforgettable personal events, including the fleeting beauty of growing up and the wonder of young love. While focusing on these tender moments, Roy also critiques casteist social practices that prevent the romantic union between a divorced woman and a carpenter. The brutality of British rule goes hand in hand with these quiet tragedies, which Roy explores by weaving in and out of time to showcase how our memories work. It is a haunting book that radically reinvents language to convey the hyperspecific feeling of losing childhood innocence in a postcolonial world.

The book that established the dark academia subgenre, Donna Tartt’s The Secret History is a riveting campus novel that everybody should experience. Set in an elite liberal arts college in Vermont, the book follows Richard Papen, who joins an exclusive Classics and Greek class in a bid to fit in. The other members of the class are all wealthy and hand-picked by the professor, and revel in their social isolation, which they consider proof of their intellectual superiority. After one of them is murdered, The Secret History turns into an inverted detective story that dwells on Richard’s perspective of the other students, and the effect that this death has on everybody.

Tartt doesn’t spring this death on the reader. Instead, the book opens with the declaration of the murder. The tension lies in slowly unraveling why this happened, and how the others are responsible for such a ghastly event. Richard is also an unreliable narrator, being a self-confessed liar and someone who blatantly alters objective truth due to his biases. Tartt uses layered, evocative prose to explore morality via the lens of Greek ideals, penning a cautionary tale against the romanticization of intellectual elitism. The bubble that surrounds these students bursts, pushing them towards real-world culpability they’re not equipped to handle.

Humans share a complicated relationship with their sense of smell, detailed vividly in Patrick Süskind’s Perfume: The Story of a Murderer. The story follows Jean-Baptiste Grenouille, an orphan in 18th-century France who is born with an exceptional nose that can differentiate between subtle scents around him. While this gift initially pulls Grenouille towards becoming a perfumer, things take a horrific turn after he meets a young girl with a scent that’s impossible to forget. What starts off as intense fascination turns into a morbid obsession with human scent, which he hunts in order to make the most exquisite perfume.

Perfume paints Grenouille as a fascinating antagonist with no scent of his own. Süskind uses his journey as a metaphor for human morality and desire, and how easy it is to be manipulated by factors like profound alienation. A person with no empathetic roots in the world will treat humans like fodder, as Grenouille has never been loved as a child and never grew to discover the sentiment. This is a chilling portrait of a monster whose story ends in a bizarre, haunting way that will leave you stunned.

What causes a sudden drop in phone battery percentage?

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An aging battery or a recent software update could be the reasons behind your phone's sudden battery drops.

Few things are more frustrating than noticing that your phone's battery is draining faster than usual. It gets to a point where it can no longer last a full day away from the charger. Under normal conditions, battery drain depends on what you're doing on your phone. Lighter uses like browsing the web and listening to music use less power, while taking lots of photos and playing games tend to drain your battery faster. But when your phone's battery suddenly drops without heavy use, something might be wrong.

At the same time, that doesn't mean your phone's battery is dying. Software can also cause your battery to drain faster, like when you install an iOS or Android update, or when an app is constantly running in the background. If you've been noticing that your phone's battery is draining faster, here's what you can try to fix it.

Your phone doesn't know exactly how much charge is left in the battery. In fact, it estimates that number based on the battery's voltage and current, and sometimes that estimate is far off from reality. Eventually, the software recalculates it, and that's when you see the battery percentage suddenly drop by a significant amount instead of gradually. This happens more often with batteries in poor condition. As lithium-ion batteries age chemically, they no longer retain their full charge capacity and may also fail when the phone needs a power boost to perform a demanding task, like opening the camera or using GPS. As a result, your phone may think the battery is much lower than it actually is.

Smartphones have mechanisms to prevent this from happening. On iPhones, Apple has implemented a system that detects when the battery fails to reduce performance in order to avoid these spikes in power consumption. Temperature also has a major impact, as most phones are rated to operate at temperatures between 0° and 35° C (32° to 95° F). Using your phone at temperatures above that range can significantly reduce battery life. When your phone gets too hot, the battery will most likely drain much faster. It's best to stop using it until it returns to a normal temperature. Don't put it in a refrigerator, though, as the introduction of cold air can create condensation within the warmer phone.

Also, it's perfectly normal to see your phone's battery drain a little faster for a few hours or even days after an update. Both iOS and Android run a series of optimizations to put all the files in their proper places, and this increases battery consumption. The good news is that this is temporary, and everything goes back to normal once the indexing process is complete. A buggy app or a process constantly running in the background, especially if it uses your location, will also drain your phone's battery quickly.

Start by closing apps running in the background to see if your battery usage returns to normal. It's also worth taking a look at your privacy settings and limiting access to your location to only when you have an app open. This prevents your phone from constantly triggering the GPS. That alone should have a significant impact on reducing battery consumption. Also, check your phone's battery settings to see exactly which apps are draining your battery the most — both iOS and Android provide details on this.

Pixel 6 and later models have a Battery Diagnostics tool under Settings. On a Samsung Galaxy, open Settings, then Battery and tap View Details. For iPhone users, it's as simple as going to Settings and opening the Battery menu. These menus usually include details about your phone's battery health. Apple, for example, says that the latest iPhones are designed to retain 80 percent of their original battery capacity through up to 1,000 charge cycles. If your phone's battery health is below 80 percent, you should probably replace it with a new one — which, at least, is much cheaper than buying a new phone.

A 120-Year-Old Gap in Einstein’s Physics May Hold the Key to the Universe’s Earliest Matter

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A missing piece in Einstein’s century-old theory could help scientists understand how matter behaved in the universe’s earliest moments, revealing more about a time we can barely recreate.

In particle accelerators, scientists smashed ions together at nearly the speed of light to recreate the fluid that existed just after the Big Bang. The resulting droplets were roughly the size of an atomic nucleus and survived for only tens of septillionths of a second. One way to investigate their interior was to track heavy quarks, particles that traveled through the droplets without being part of the surrounding fluid.

That fluid, called quark-gluon plasma, offered a glimpse of the universe’s earliest matter. In the first fraction of a second after the Big Bang, matter existed as a trillion-degree mixture of quarks and gluons, subatomic particles moving at close to the speed of light. This state lasted just a few microseconds before the particles came together to form more stable protons and neutrons.

Reading the clues carried by heavy quarks required an accurate description of how the fluid pushed and pulled them. Albert Einstein established a relationship between a particle’s motion and a fluid’s microscopic structure in 1905. At speeds approaching that of light, however, the familiar description missed some of the interactions that shaped a particle’s journey.

Jean Du Plessis, a graduate student in MIT’s Center for Theoretical Physics, a Leinweber Institute, and Bruno Scheihing-Hitschfeld of the Kavli Institute for Theoretical Physics at the University of California at Santa Barbara (UCSB) developed a theory to address that gap. It predicted how a particle moving at relativistic speeds, meaning close to the speed of light, was likely to be pushed and pulled as it passed through a fluid. They reported their findings in Physical Review Letters.

The researchers began with a relativistic heavy quark traveling through a gas of weakly interacting quarks and gluons. Those weak interactions made the calculations manageable. They calculated the forces acting on the heavy quark through the strong nuclear force, the fundamental force that held quarks and gluons together, to determine how its momentum was likely to change.

Their calculation produced a probability distribution, a curve showing how likely different changes in momentum were. It also described the likelihood of kicks of different strengths, since stronger kicks produced larger changes in momentum.

“Our result reveals a new source of information carried by heavy quarks in rare, unusually large changes in their motion,” Du Plessis said. “How often those large changes occur depends on the microscopic nature of the plasma, raising the possibility that they could provide a new way to learn what the quark-gluon plasma is actually like inside.”

The idea of learning about a fluid by watching particles move through it had roots in a much more familiar setting. In 1827, botanist Robert Brown observed grains of pollen jiggling and swirling in seemingly still water. That erratic movement became known as Brownian motion and later inspired Einstein’s work.

Einstein’s 1905 theory explained how invisible activity within a fluid could keep a larger particle moving. Atoms and molecules in the fluid exerted random forces that combined to kick and jostle the particle, while drag from the fluid slowed it down. The relationship between these random kicks and drag determined how the particle eventually reached the same temperature as its surroundings.

Scientists subsequently used Einstein’s explanation to study many fluids, including quark-gluon plasma. They assumed that a particle passing through the plasma would experience a balance of microscopic kicks similar to that experienced by pollen in water. In this familiar picture, the kicks followed a bell-shaped probability distribution. Typical kicks clustered around the peak, while extreme kicks occupied the tails and were equally unlikely in opposite directions.

“Einstein showed that the drag and the random kicks must balance in just the right way for a particle eventually to come to the same temperature as its surroundings,” Du Plessis explained. “For the types of fluids he was thinking about, he was correct. But for particles that are moving at relativistic velocities, this relationship breaks down. And we’ve known for 20 years that something is missing. So we have to go beyond the work that Einstein did.”

The new calculation resembled Einstein’s bell-shaped curve only for small kicks.

“The usual Brownian picture assumes that large kicks become exceedingly rare,” Du Plessis said. “For heavy quarks, however, these rare kicks are more common than the bell curve predicts, and are not equally likely in opposite directions: A large kick that slows the quark down more than expected from drag is more likely than an equally large kick that leaves it moving faster than expected.”

When the researchers repeated their calculations for plasmas made up of different particles, they found the same tilted pattern, with uneven tails at either end of the distribution. They also recognized the same structure in an earlier calculation for a strongly coupled plasma, where particles interacted strongly, despite the differences in the underlying microscopic physics.

“This is the first time that this seemingly universal structure was realized and really formalized,” Du Plessis said. “This gives a concrete path forward for how to better understand the quark-gluon plasma, using heavy quarks.”

The researchers envisioned applying the theory to predict how heavy quarks moved through quark-gluon plasma, then comparing those predictions with experiments. That comparison could help characterize the plasma’s microscopic structure and establish what kind of fluid existed in the universe’s first moments.

Du Plessis said the primordial plasma was likely “more strongly interacting than this Brownian motion way of modeling it would suggest. It’s more like a liquid and less like a gas.”

Based on true events and adapted from Ben Macintyre’s acclaimed book about British intelligence officer Kim Philby, the six-episode miniseries leans into the inner workings and psychological pressure of espionage rather than the slick, explosive elements the genre often relies on. Instead of battling enemies across borders, the series reveals that the most devastating betrayals come from within in one of the biggest intelligence scandals of the 20th century.

The series frames their friendship as both a source of deep loyalty and a blind spot, which allowed Philby to operate as a Soviet mole for years without arousing Elliott’s suspicion. When Philby finally defects to Moscow, the British government launches an inquiry to determine whether Elliott was simply deceived or whether he might have been helping his friend all along.

The series begins in the aftermath of Philby’s exposure, sending Elliott to Beirut with the mission of extracting a confession from a man he'd known intimately for over 30 years. Over the course of four tense days and 34 hours of taped conversations, the truth slips further out of reach, culminating in Philby’s defection to the USSR. Back in London, Elliott faces his own interrogation at the hands of MI5 officer Lily Thomas (Anna Maxwell Martin), one of the show’s few fictional additions. Through her relentless questioning, the series unravels Elliott’s complicated mix of loyalty, denial, and self-preservation.

What ultimately sets A Spy Among Friends apart is its refusal to treat Philby’s treachery as a standard espionage mystery filled with high-octane twists and turns. Instead, it focuses on the emotional wreckage left behind when a deeply personal relationship becomes the site of catastrophic betrayal. Pearce and Lewis play off each other with remarkable precision, their performances grounding the series in the uncomfortable truth that the deepest betrayals rarely come from enemies abroad, but from the people we think we know the best.

As compelling as the story is on its own, A Spy Among Friends reaches another level thanks to the exceptional performances at its center. His portrayal of Nicholas Elliott taps into a similar tension. While Elliott is clearly devastated by Philby’s betrayal, there’s always an undercurrent suggesting he may have let his personal affection cloud his professional judgment. Lewis plays him with a quiet, tightly coiled restraint that makes Elliot’s internal crisis feel both painfully raw and deeply contained.

Guy Pearce, meanwhile, is mesmerizing as Kim Philby. His villainous turns have always had a distinctive nuance, from the cold, calculating betrayal he brought to The Count of Monte Cristo to the wounded arrogance of Iron Man 3. But here, he delivers something far more unsettling. Pearce resists the temptation to portray Philby as a conventional villain, instead capturing the strange magnetism that allowed Philby to manipulate everyone around him. The series keeps Philby’s motivations intentionally ambiguous, and Pearce leans into that uncertainty, playing him as someone who may genuinely believe his own lies. It’s the kind of performance that makes it entirely plausible that this man deceived governments — and his closest friends — for decades.

And while true stories can sometimes slip into the dryness of a history lesson, A Spy Among Friends never does. A Spy Among Friends isn’t the kind of spy thriller that relies on spectacle, and that’s exactly why it works so well. It’s a reminder that real espionage is far more personal than what movies like James Bond often make it out to be. Anchored by magnetic performances from Lewis and Pearce, the miniseries offers a darker, more realistic portrait of spycraft, one where friendship becomes a liability and trust is never guaranteed. For fans of character-driven espionage rooted in real history, A Spy Among Friends is definitely worth watching.

The urgency is underscored by real‑world stakes: rogue AI could breach security, manipulate markets, or, in worst‑case scenarios, orchestrate physical harm. A mechanism‑design approach offers a systematic, mathematically grounded path to mitigate those risks.

Cross‑Model Prediction Rewards Stephen Morris et al. propose rewarding a model not only for accuracy on a benchmark but also for how well its answers predict those of a second model. Is Current Second Amendment Jurisprudence Premised Upon a "Misquote"?

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

A New York Times article attempting to discredit Justice Scalia's Heller opinion misfires.

This past week, the New York Times published an article by Mike McIntire and Eli Murray alleging that a "pivotal legal test" for determining whether gun control laws are constitutional is "based on a misquote." Yet it appears it is the NYT's reporters who misfired.

When the Supreme Court begins weighing the constitutionality of assault-weapons bans next month, the justices will be guided by the court's own precedent: a landmark ruling that such restrictions are allowed only if a firearm is both "dangerous and unusual."

For nearly two decades, judges across the country have invoked this language to strike down gun-control laws. It is a pivotal legal test.

Justice Antonin Scalia, writing for the majority in the 2008 case, District of Columbia v. Heller, attributed the "dangerous and unusual" language to Sir William Blackstone, an 18th-century English jurist whose writings became the basis for much of American law.

But in his seminal 1769 treatise, "Commentaries on the Laws of England," Blackstone referred to weapons that were "dangerous or unusual," suggesting that a gun did not need to be both in order to be restricted.

But is there actually an error in the opinion? Here is the paragraph from Heller in which the alleged misquotation occurs, with the allegedly offending sentence underlined:

We also recognize another important limitation on the right to keep and carry arms. Miller said, as we have explained, that the sorts of weapons protected were those "in common use at the time." 307 U. S., at 179. We think that limitation is fairly supported by the historical tradition of prohibiting the carrying of "dangerous and unusual weapons." See 4 Blackstone 148–149 (1769); 3 B. Wilson, Works of the Honourable James Wilson 79 (1804); J. Dunlap, The New-York Justice 8 (1815); C. Humphreys, A Compendium of the Common Law in Force in Kentucky 482 (1822); 1 W. Russell, A Treatise on Crimes and Indictable Misdemeanors 271–272 (1831); H. Stephen, Summary of the Criminal Law 48 (1840); E. Lewis, An Abridgment of the Criminal Law of the United States 64 (1847); F. Wharton, A Treatise on the Criminal Law of the United States 726 (1852). See also State v. Langford, 10 N. C. 381, 383–384 (1824); O'Neill v. State, 16 Ala. 65, 67 (1849); English v. State, 35 Tex. 473, 476 (1871); State v. Lanier, 71 N. C. 288, 289 (1874).

Note that the claim, with the quotation, is followed by a "See" string cite (followed, in turn, by a "See also" string cite of further authorities).  If they were at all familiar with how legal citation works, this should have given the NYT reporters pause. As others have noted, the Supreme Court's style guide indicates that the "see" signal is not used for direct quotations. Rather it indicates that "the cited authority is basic source material supporting, although not stating, the text proposition" (emphasis added). Blackstone is cited first not because it is the dominant source but because the authorities are listed in chronological order.

What makes matters worse is that it appears the authors of the story were informed of all this before the story went to press. One of the story authors contacted John Bash, who clerked for Justice Antonin Scalia the term Heller was decided and worked on the case, and Bash explained all of this to the reporter, as Bash details in this LinkedIn post.  Bash writes:

This is stunning. A recent article in The New York Times claims that District of Columbia v. Heller--the 2008 Supreme Court case holding that the Second Amendment protects an individual right to own a firearm--contains a "misquote" of Blackstone. The supposed misquote is the phrase "dangerous and unusual weapons" in this passage:

We also recognize another important limitation on the right to keep and carry arms. Miller said, as we have explained, that the sorts of weapons protected were those "in common use at the time." 307 U. S., at 179. We think that limitation is fairly supported by the historical tradition of prohibiting the carrying of "dangerous and unusual weapons."

That passage is followed by the signal "See" and numerous citations, the first of which is Blackstone. The Blackstone source says "dangerous or unusual weapons" ("or" instead of "and"), but a number of the sources that follow say "dangerous and unusual weapons," in addition to sources that use the Blackstone disjunctive framing.

Back in October the New York Times reporter reached out to me about this, presumably because I was the law clerk for Justice Scalia on Heller. I immediately wrote back with a full on-the-record explanation of why there was no misquote. Yet the article does not refer to my explanation at all and asserts with no qualification that it was a misquote.

So if anyone is interested, I have posted my email below verbatim. I will also put in the comments my follow-up when the reporter asked me a question.

Here is the e-mail Bash sent to the reporter working on the story:

Hi Mike, thanks for reaching out on this.

Even if I remembered any conversation with Justice Scalia about this particular point (which I don't), it would not be ethical for me to share private conversations I had during my clerkship wth Justice Scalia about the substance of opinions.

But just as an appellate lawyer, I don't see the issue.  If the historical sources are mixed on the conjunctive or disjunctive framing, then it is true that there is an historical tradition of prohibiting weapons that are both dangerous and unusual, the narrower category that is encompassed by either framing.  The fact that Blackstone is the first citation is just because the treatise citations are in chronological order (consistent with making a point about historical tradition), followed by case citations also in chronological order.  The introduction of the treatise list with a "See" signal (rather than no signal) means that the quoted phrase is not necessarily a direct quote from each source (or any source)—as with the "See also" signal for the cases.  And assuming that it's correct that the conjunctive sources all trace back to a single 18th Century treatise, that would not detract from the point that learned commentators had endorsed or applied that test through early American history, which is the point the text is making about historical tradition.

Here is the first follow-up e-mail Bash sent "in response to the reporter asking if he could quote me and whether the opinion purposefully omitted the Hawkins treatise quoted in the conjunctive-framing sources so that Blackstone would be first":

Sure.  Happy to take a look at the quote.

Can't speak to the thinking that went into the opinion, but I really doubt that.  As I mentioned earlier, I don't think the provenance of the limitation is particularly relevant; the point was that commentators had recognized it throughout history.  For example, in a Fourth Amendment case, a court might make the point that the curtilage rule—that the immediate vicinity of the home is considered part of the home—has been recognized through American history, citing late Eighteenth Century and Nineteenth Century treatises and cases.  Even if that rule could be traced back to a single uncited treatise, that's not really the point. It's that courts and commentators endorsed it and so that history and tradition informs the constitutional analysis.

And all that aside, I just think it is implausible that the paragraph would be understood differently if Blackstone had been the second citation rather than the first—especially since Hawkins was himself a very influential legal commentator (if I recall correctly).  I've never heard of a court or litigant attributing weight to the order of sources subject to the same signal (e.g., "See"), which would seem to me especially unwarranted when the sources are presented chronologically (and so there is an obvious explanation for the ordering other than importance).  As I noted before, I think all the sources together make clear a uniform historical tradition of prohibiting dangerous and unusual weapons, the category encompassed by all of their articulations of the rule.

Interestingly enough, the NYT article concludes citing a scholar affirming Bash's contention that, as the relevant passage claims, "there is an historical tradition of prohibiting weapons that are both dangerous and unusual," or as some would put it, those arms that are "unusually dangerous." Insofar as some lower courts have applied a different analysis, it is not clear to me how that is attributable to Scalia's language in Heller, or the contested passage.

The NYT story ends citing a scholar for the proposition that "relying on centuries-old legal precedents requires understanding how earlier generations used language." One might conclude that those writing on the Supreme Court should heed a similar admonition.

Prediction: This Will Be Micron's Stock Price by Late 2027 (Hint: Wall Street Expects 106% Upside)

Wall Street thinks Micron is deeply undervalued. The stock has a median target price of $1,575 per share among 55 analysts, implying 107% upside from the current share price of $760.

My view is more conservative. I think Micron will trade at $1,045 per share by late 2027, which implies 37% upside from its current share price. Here's my logic.

Micron is a semiconductor company that produces memory chips and storage solutions based on NAND flash and DRAM technology. Both types play an important role in artificial intelligence (AI). NAND functions as long-term storage and loads data into DRAM, where it is processed by central processing units (CPUs) and graphics processing units (GPUs).

The AI infrastructure build-out has created immense demand for memory chips, resulting in a profound supply shortage that has driven prices higher across the board. This year alone, DRAM and NAND prices are projected to quadruple. Naturally, Micron's ability to charge more has led to tremendous financial results.

Micron delivered an encouraging report for the fourth quarter of fiscal 2026, which ended in September. Revenue increased 379% to $54.2 billion, marking the fifth straight quarter of accelerating growth. In particular, that momentum was supported by the core data center segment, where sales soared more than 1,000% amid surging demand for AI infrastructure.

CEO Sanjay Mehrotra provided encouraging commentary about the demand environment on the earnings call. "Industry demand has strengthened since our last earnings call," he told analysts. Mehrotra also said Micron does "not have line of sight to when supply and demand will return to balance."

Bears argue Micron lacks pricing power because memory chips are commodities, meaning prices are largely determined by supply and demand rather than product differentiation. Additionally, memory chips have historically been among the most cyclical segments of the semiconductor industry. Periods of strong demand encourage manufacturers to expand output, eventually creating a supply glut that drives prices down and compresses margins.

Indeed, Micron benefited from explosive demand at the beginning of the pandemic, when businesses were expanding cloud infrastructure and consumers were buying smartphones, laptops, and gaming consoles. But the subsequent downturn exposed the cyclical nature of the industry. As demand normalized and inventories accumulated, Micron suffered five straight quarters of declining revenue and negative earnings.

Bulls argue this particular memory cycle is different because, unlike the transient demand for electronics created by the pandemic, AI is a more durable source of demand. Bulls also point to Micron's growing list of long-term supply contracts, many of which have minimum purchase commitments and price floors. Those contracts provide greater visibility into future revenue and may reduce the severity of future downturns.

Micron CEO Sanjay Mehrotra believes those contracts will make the company's financial performance more resilient. "These multi-year take-or-pay agreements sharpen our long-term supply planning and enhance the durability and predictability of our strong financial performance," he told analysts on the latest earnings call.

Wall Street estimates Micron's earnings will increase at 65% annually through fiscal 2028, according to LSEG. That makes the current valuation of 10 times adjusted earnings look cheap. But there is a major caveat: The last memory chip cycle peaked in late 2021, and Micron traded around 6 times adjusted earnings by late 2022.

In that context, the current valuation of 10 times adjusted earnings could drop sharply once the current memory cycle peaks. However, I think that possibility has already been priced into the stock. Micron trades around 4.4 times projected earnings for fiscal 2027.

So what? If Wall Street's earnings estimate is correct and Micron trades at 6 times adjusted earnings after reporting fourth-quarter financial results for fiscal 2027 (which usually happens in late September), the stock will trade at $1,045 per share in late 2027. That implies 37% upside from its current share price of $760.

How to find your IP address

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It's quick and easy, if you know where to look.

If you're looking for a quick answer to "What is my IP address?", a simple solution is to go to WhatIsMyIP.com and get it there. But the real answer is a little more complicated, because your devices don't have just one IP address. Every device on your network gets a private one from your router, and your whole home shares a single public one from your internet provider. The public address is the one websites see, while the private one only matters inside your own network.

Your router does the translating between the two, which is how a phone, a laptop and a TV can all browse at once while looking like a single address to the outside world. The router also hands out the private ones through a system called DHCP that usually lives inside the router itself. So the real answer depends on whether you're looking for your public address or one of your private ones.

Open a browser and go to DuckDuckGo. Type "what is my IP" into the search bar, and there it is. The answer shows up at the top of the results. Sites like WhatIsMyIP do the same thing and add your approximate location and your provider's name.

If you'd rather stay out of the browser, there's a faster way on both Mac and Windows. Open Terminal on a Mac, or Command Prompt on Windows, type the following and press Enter:

It produces your public IP and nothing else. If you're after your private IP instead, here's how.

On Windows 11, right-click the network icon in the taskbar and choose Network and Internet settings. If you are on Wi-Fi, click Wi-Fi, then click the network you're connected to. If you are connected through Ethernet, click Ethernet instead. Scroll down past the toggles until you reach a long list of technical details. Most of them have IPv6 in the name, and you can ignore those. Look for the line called IPv4 address. The numbers next to it, four groups separated by dots, are your private IP. Windows 10 is nearly the same, though on Wi-Fi you may need to click Hardware properties first.

You can do the exact same thing by opening Command Prompt and typing ipconfig. It works on every version of Windows, and the address is on the IPv4 Address line under your active connection.

On a Mac, the quickest route is to hold down Option and click the Wi-Fi icon in the menu bar. Your IP address is listed there, along with your router's. The longer way works for wired connections too: Apple menu > System Settings > Network. Then pick your connection, click Details, and look under the TCP/IP tab.

On an iPhone, go to Settings, tap Wi-Fi, then tap the i next to your network and scroll down to the IP address. Android works the same way on many phones: open Settings, tap Wi-Fi, then tap the i or the gear icon next to your network and look for IPv4. On a Pixel, the path is Settings > Network & internet > Internet > gear icon > Network details. Manufacturers move things around, so your phone may differ a little.

The router has its own address, and it's the one you type into a browser to reach its settings page. Your device lists it as the Default Gateway (or just Router), and it's often 192.168.1.1, 192.168.0.1 or 10.0.0.1. On Windows, it's right there in the ipconfig output.

Mostly for troubleshooting. If your connection is laggy or dead and a restart didn't help, your provider may ask for your device's IP to work out whether the problem is on your end or theirs. It also comes up when you're setting up a printer, a game server or remote access to a machine at home.

Your public IP probably won't stay put. Most home connections use dynamic addresses, which can change when you restart your modem or router. Static ones exist, but they're mostly for servers, such as the ones hosting big websites or running email.

A common question is whether you can look up someone else's IP. The answer is no, not from your end. Random people on the internet can't see yours, and a public IP usually points to a whole network, not one person or device. Websites and advertisers can see it, though, and sometimes use it to build a picture of what you like. A VPN hides it from them, and a proxy does too, some proxies do not encrypt your traffic, and a VPN always does. If you want to try one, you can check out this guide on how to use a VPN.
