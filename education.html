Over the past year, UnitedHealth Group has conducted a series of independent reviews of its practices as part of a broader turnaround effort at the company.

The healthcare giant released the last of these analyses on Thursday, digging into the processes it uses to establish medical policies. The reviews were conducted by FTI Consulting and Rubrum Advising, a provider-led firm with expertise in medical coverage policies.

While the auditors dove into the processes the insurer uses to develop its medical policies, the report doesn't examine the application of those polices and any impact on coverage denials, an area where UnitedHealthcare has drawn significant public scrutiny.

The report concluded the company's methods "were strong overall and that the medical policy development process reflected numerous best practices."

For instance, UnitedHealthcare's Medical Policy team uses a structured and standardized process to establish medical policies, rooted in evidence-based and stakeholder-informed approaches. Clinical evidence comes from nationally recognized authorities, and epidemiologists play a key role in the review process, per the report.

In addition, the analysts concluded that the team is well-staffed with qualified workers and operates under a strong governance model. Medical policies are built "using systematic evidence review, evidence-based tools and industry best practices," and UnitedHealthcare is transparent about posting these policies and making updates.

All policies are reviewed at least once per year, the report found.

CEO Stephen Hemsley said in a letter to investors that the company will continue to conduct such analyses.

"We will continue to subject key practices across UnitedHealth Group to independent review, share results publicly and make changes where needed," Hemsley wrote. "This work reflects our broader commitment to transparency, accountability and continuous improvement."

While the report concluded that overall processes in place at UnitedHealth were up to snuff, the consultants did offer multiple areas of improvement for the company, too. For instance, the analysts said that the team could improve its documentation practices, including how it details criteria for policy development.

Plus, the report suggests that UnitedHealthcare review how it documents vetting criteria for internal and external subject matter experts. The company could also do more to formally document training and education for members of its governance committee.

Medical policy documents can be lengthy and complex, so the consultants recommended the insurer consider developing a template that standardizes the written format for each policy, which would make it easier to understand. For example, the template could make it easier to connect the evidence summary to the coverage rationale.

"While these gaps do not diminish the overall strength of the program, addressing them would further enhance transparency and consistency and provide opportunities for continued refinement," the analysts wrote.

4 Reasons to Hunt Geese Over Water

In late October, we got our annual Halloween push of ducks here in the Midwest, and a friend and I timed it just right. We set up on a big agricultural retention pond with winds gusting to 30 mph. I threw out three dozen goose floaters, and we set another dozen full bodies in the shallows. We added a few mallard blocks right at the water’s edge in the landing hole in front of the blind. It’s best to put ducks upwind of geese, as geese don’t like flying over ducks, while ducks will fly over geese.

The first ducks in the spread were greenwings, and we shot a pair. Not long after, a big group of mallards worked us, then dropped into the hole. And so it went, until an hour and a half later, I called the shot on a pair of greenheads at ten yards. We stood up, the blind blew down, and we shot our last two ducks of the day.

We added a couple of geese and passed on the shovelers and ruddies that came into the spread. A flock of bluebills landed at the edge of the geese, but they were too bunched up for a clean shot. The point is, everything liked the goose spread that morning. We had a nice bag of mallards, a couple of teal, a gadwall, and some geese. It proved that a spread of goose floaters can work on ducks just as well as a spread of goose silos and full bodies does in a dry field. And adding floaters to your collection of goose decoys lets you target geese on ponds and rivers, where there may be less competition than in dry fields. If you still aren’t convinced, here are four reasons big Canada floaters will improve your success on ducks and geese.

It doesn’t take a huge decoy spread to fool geese over water. Sometimes, as few as four decoys can be enough to pull geese on small ponds or marshes. Plus, geese feel comfortable enough coming to water that even mediocre (that’s putting it kindly) callers like me can honk and cluck a few times to get their attention, and then let the decoys do the work.

The best time and place to hunt geese over water is a loafing spot, which can be whatever pond, marsh, or stretch of river birds go to after breakfast. Find one of these loafing spots and hunt it at “goose time,” which starts about 8:30 and runs through the middle of the day. There’s no need to get up early to hunt a loafing spot, and shooting the roost in the evening is generally frowned upon, as it might move birds out of the area for good. Save a late afternoon roost shoot for the last day of the season. If you’re after a mixed bag of ducks and geese, however, set the alarm early.

If you’ve gotten this far, you’re probably a serious waterfowler, and I don’t have to explain the adrenaline rush of ducks and geese decoying from high altitudes. This leads me to my next point on why you should use goose floaters: geese will lock up as dots in the sky and drop down to the water as if on a giant, invisible escalator, in a way they rarely do in fields. I remember honking at a bunch of four passing over from behind me and seeing them stand on their wingtips, drop almost to the water, spin, and come into the decoys, barely skimming the surface as they crossed the pond. It was a sight mesmerizing enough that I turned what should have been an easy triple into a single bird on the water. Watching geese decoy over water is more fun than watching them decoy in dry fields.

You will shoot far more ducks over goose decoys than you will ever shoot geese over duck decoys. Up to a 90/10 goose-to-duck decoy ratio can work surprisingly well on ducks, and it pulls more geese than a few goose floaters with a large duck spread will. Goose decoys are big, highly visible, and serve as a confidence bird for other waterfowl. This makes your spread not only more appealing and visible to ducks, but also a more secure place for them to land.

Bangladesh: Academics Allegedly Targeted for Supporting Hasina

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image The University of Dhaka in Bangladesh, July 1, 2021. © 2021 Sony Ramany/NurPhoto via AP Photo

(London) – Scores of academics and students at public universities in Bangladesh have been arbitrarily punished because of their alleged affiliation with former ruler Sheikh Hasina and her Awami League party, Human Rights Watch said today.

Any action to punish persons for their peaceful beliefs or without ensuring due process would violate the rights to freedom of expression and association protected under international human rights law and reproduce abusive attempts under previous administrations to control political opinion and expression at Bangladesh’s public universities.

“Bangladesh has a long history of political debate in its universities, and only autocrats should fear such discourse, not a government formed following student protests,” said Meenakshi Ganguly, deputy Asia director at Human Rights Watch. “Bangladesh needs to turn the page on the repression of the past, not repeat it whenever the ruling and opposition parties trade places.”

The sanctions—dismissals, suspension, and teaching restrictions—focus on allegations that they were complicit in violence by Hasina’s Awami League government, which was toppled by student-led protests in 2024, allegations of other “subversive” activities in support of her party, or their alleged discrimination against students wearing Islamic clothing. Hundreds of students, allegedly Awami League members and supporters, are also being expelled, denying them their education rights.

Human Rights Watch interviewed over a dozen affected teachers, examined university correspondence, and reviewed and analyzed a televised discussion and a video posted on social media. Interviews with teachers and students also confirmed expulsions, cancellation of exam certificates, or barring students from campus.

According to media reports in August 2026, at least 404 more academics at 22 universities, who were part of a pro-Awami League Telegram messaging app group, have been put under investigation. “What is happening now is that the university authorities are targeting with false allegations the group of teachers who are aligned to the Awami League and did not support the 2024 student movement,” said a professor at a public university, who is not identified for security reasons. “We are not allowed to go take classes, we cannot do research work, we cannot do anything.”

Hundreds of people were killed in a brutal police crackdown on the protests in July and August 2024, which forced Hasina to flee and paved the way for the return of the Bangladesh Nationalist Party to power following an election in February 2026. The activities of the Awami League were criminalized by an interim government in May 2025.

For years, Bangladesh’s universities have been run in a partisan manner, employing and promoting academics close to the party in government and acting against supporters of other political parties.

Starting in September 2024, the newly appointed authorities at Dhaka University, the country’s most prestigious university, ordered 44 academics to stop their work and restricted teaching assignments of another 25, without a transparent or credible disciplinary investigation. Subsequently, six of the academics were formally “temporarily suspended” following the findings of an inquiry committee, and similar investigations have now begun against another 14 teachers.

At Bangladesh Agricultural University in Mymensingh, the authorities dismissed, suspended, or removed 12 academics and demoted or restricted the activities of another 34 after setting up an “investigation commission,” though this did not appear to comply with the university’s legally established disciplinary process. And in Sher-e-Bangla Agricultural University in Dhaka, 12 academics have been temporarily suspended and another 10 removed from academic activities following an inquiry process that teachers said they were not allowed to attend, an apparent breach of the law covering academic discipline. Another 85 teachers were denied promotions.

In all, over 20 public universities have been affected, Human Rights Watch found.

In March 2025, Dhaka University temporarily suspended 126 students, and withheld their exam certificates, after a fact-finding committee said it had found photographic, video, or social media evidence that they had attacked peaceful protesters. Cases filed with the police allege that another 277 Awami League students were involved. The university authorities have taken no formal action against them, but students and teachers confirmed that they stay away from campus for fear of arrest. Many of those named deny any role in the violence, yet the university has not provided a forum for them to answer the allegations.

Among the academics affected is Professor Mashiur Rahman, from the Dhaka University sociology department, who since 2017 was deputed as a vice-chancellor at another university. He was told to stop all academic and administrative work on September 25, 2024. The allegations against him include claims that during the protests he “directly opposed the movement, calling it illegal, baseless and irrational,” during a July 2024 television discussion, although a video of the broadcast, reviewed by Human Rights Watch, shows no such comment.

Another allegation is that at the prime minister’s house on August 3, 2024, he “incited genocide.” However, this was a meeting set up by the University Grants Commission that all vice-chancellors had to attend, and he has told Human Rights Watch that no evidence has been provided to him showing that he made such a statement, which he denies. On August 31, 2026, the university authorities recommended permanently suspending him, which he is appealing.

The allegations against the sanctioned teachers at the Bangladesh Agriculture University center on their participation in a pro-Awami League march on August 4, 2024, the day before Hasina fled the country. A report by a Commission of Inquiry set up by the university refers to the march as a “terrorist” procession.

A video posted on Facebook by the Bangladesh Agricultural University Journalists’ Association on August 4, 2024, and analyzed by Human Rights Watch, shows a scene from the march. In the video, a group can be seen walking on the campus chanting slogans. No direct confrontations are visible. Professor Purba Islam from the Department of Pharmacology was suspended on August 4, 2025, for her “sloganizing” at the procession, alleging “support for the oppression, persecution and genocide committed by the fascist and autocrat Sheikh Hasina.” No evidence of her shouting slogans is visible in the video.

Professor Yahia Khandoker, from the university’s Department of Animal Breeding and Genetics, was also suspended on August 4, 2025, accused of maintaining “on the university campus, a clear position in favor of Sheikh Hasina’s mass killing.” Throughout the whole protest period, he said, he was with his family in Dhaka, not at the university. The High Court has stayed his suspension, though the Attorney General’s Office is appealing.

Three academics, two of them in prison, were convicted by the country’s International Crimes Tribunal on crimes against humanity charges, with another awaiting trial.

“Any acts of revenge against people who peacefully supported the previous government would show that freedoms of speech and assembly are not protected,” Ganguly said. “Universities should be places where people are free to express their views, not places where support is enforced for the government of the day.”

Posted November 3, 2026 | Reviewed by Tyler Woods

It’s been 27 years—over a quarter century--since the Columbine High School massacre ignited the worldwide war against bullying. Yet many nations that enthusiastically embrace this war discover that bullying becomes a more serious problem.

South Korea is one such country. It has made bullying a major cultural issue, producing a large number of popular “K-dramas” (Korean TV series) depicting the brutality of bullying, including the recent Netflix hit, Teach You a Lesson. Despite this massive anti-bullying activism (or perhaps because of it?), a 2025 survey found that South Korea's violence victimization rate in elementary school hit a record high.

A basic tenet of antibullyism is that bullying must not be tolerated and needs to be met with tough and consistent punishments, or “consequences.” Make the price of bullying too high, it is assumed, and any bully’s appetite to cause people pain will evaporate. Although it has become widely recognized that punishment is not an effective response to bullying,1 the belief in punishment continues unimpeded, probably because it seems intuitively correct to the average person, and perhaps because nothing else bullying researchers have presented seems to work reliably.

Thus, South Korea has adopted what sounds like a tough but logical consequence for bullying. Students with bullying on their records are disqualified from acceptance into public universities. This punishment causes a huge financial loss for students and can seriously impact their future. Given the importance Koreans place on higher education, certainly such a policy must be an effective deterrence to bullying, right?

Not if we consider human nature. Let’s say you get a traffic ticket. When will you fight it harder? When the fine is $50, with no points against your driver's license, or $50,000, and your license gets suspended? Well, the same thing is true if the huge punishments are for bullying violations, as recent news stories from South Korea confirm.

Before we continue, it's essential to recognize that the bullying that typically occurs in Korean schools does not resemble the bloodcurdling cruelty that is depicted in the K-dramas. Such behavior should result in incarceration. As in many other advanced nations, bullying in reality consists of any behaviors that can cause someone to get upset.

Students don’t suddenly become nicer because anti-bullying policies threaten them with severe educational penalties. Instead, they—their parents, really—fight back. An increasingly popular way is to countersue the educational system. As the title of one article in the Korean JoongAng Daily informs us, As consequences of bullying records grow, perpetrators turn to countersuits for leverage. It reveals the nightmare unleashed by these Korean laws. These lawsuits typically last years and cost tens of thousands of dollars.

To help the cover the expense, a growing number of parents in Korea are purchasing bullying insurance policies, as described in another article titled, As school violence becomes legal issue, insurers cash in. :

The number of insurance payouts related to school violence among five major nonlife insurers… surged from 231 cases in 2021 to 1,297 in 2022, 3,388 in 2023 and 3,443 in 2025. This represents a nearly fifteenfold rise in just five years.... The increasing pace has accelerated further this year, with 2,056 cases already recorded in January and February alone.

Furthermore, these lawsuits are being filed even for the most minor of bullying complaints. The Korea JoongAng article presents one parent’s experience with a situation that hardly meets the criteria of bullying:

One parent…started looking into a school violence insurance program after their fifth-grader was mired in a 25 million won ($16,580) lawsuit for repeatedly asking to borrow items and was accused of “having a look” at another student in their school.

The local education office concluded that no action was necessary, but the other student’s parent did not accept the decision and filed a damage lawsuit. The court dismissed the case in full last month.

After going through both the committee process and a civil lawsuit, the parent faced significant psychological and financial burdens, leading them to seek insurance coverage.

The purpose of anti-bullying laws is to reduce the misery caused by bullying. In South Korea, however, the misery of bullying is being dwarfed by the financial expense and psychological pain inflicted by prolonged, expensive lawsuits.
