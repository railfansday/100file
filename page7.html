5 Ian Evatt replacements Blackpool must consider ASAP

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Blackpool have confirmed the sacking of Ian Evatt following a year in charge at Bloomfield Road, with the Tangerines now intent on reviving their fortunes under fresh management and avoiding a battle against relegation from League One.

Evatt had led the side to a respectable 13th-placed finish during the previous campaign after taking over from Steve Bruce, though the former Bolton Wanderers boss would struggle to get going once again at Bloomfield Road this term.

Last Saturday's 1-0 defeat away to fellow strugglers AFC Wimbledon marked the sixth in just ten games for Blackpool, who now find themselves only positioned above Doncaster Rovers in 21st by virtue of goal difference at this moment in time.

Blackpool's key decision-makers eventually listened to intensified sack calls from supporters by relieving Evatt of his duties on Thursday afternoon, and whilst the relegation-threatened third-tier outfit have confirmed that Steve Thompson will be in charge for this weekend's clash with Wigan Athletic, the search for a replacement has already begun.

With that in mind, FLW has taken a close look at FIVE managers who Blackpool should be seriously considering as potential successors to Evatt.

One manager who should surely feature within Blackpool's thinking is Andy Woodman who, remarkably enough, currently finds himself out of work following a controversial EFL sacking for the ages from League One rivals Bromley.

Woodman had, of course, inspired the plucky Ravens' unprecedented rise into the EFL pyramid for the first time in their history before winning the League Two title against all odds last season, although matters both on and off the field in that particular corner of South London have not been plain-sailing as of late.

Nonetheless, it was a huge surprise when the 52-year-old's historic 289-match reign at Bromley came to an abrupt end last month, and one suspects he won't be sat around unattached for too long.

Woodman's resourceful ability to get the very best out of what was at his disposal with the Ravens certainly reads well, and he frequently elevated and identified talent with great success.

His strong body of work, albeit at a lower level, means that he should be entering the thinking of the Tangerines hierarchy in the coming days.

A previous specialist of the firefighting genre, Gary Rowett's knack in that field has faded in recent times and he may need to re-attempt his hand in a lower division.

As such, Rowett could represent the most outstanding realistic candidate available to Blackpool right now, with the former Derby County, Millwall and Stoke City boss having been linked with the vacancy at promotion hopefuls Bradford City prior to Michael Duff's appointment.

The last few years haven't reflected too kindly on Rowett, who led both Birmingham City and Leicester to relegations while setting Oxford United up for the same fate in the same season that he was in charge of the Foxes' demotion last time out.

Therefore, the wily 52-year-old may not score favourably in the consideration of Championship decision-makers and could instead drop to League One for the first time in his managerial career.

Previously, of course, Rowett has excelled in firing teams towards safety and, though much of his fresher work has been conducted on a short-term basis, there's no reason why he couldn't provide a stable, steady and longer-term presence with the Seasiders.

Matt Bloomfield, meanwhile, having reportedly held talks with Blackpool following the aforementioned Bruce's departure in October of last year, is only likely to re-emerge as a contender for the Tangerines' top job in the near future.

Bloomfield was ultimately unable to prevent Oxford's relegation to League One after succeeding Rowett, yet many believed his subsequent sacking from the Kassam Stadium to represent a surprise and even harsh call - albeit Aaron Ramsey has since vindicated the Yellows' change in direction.

Still aged just 42, Bloomfield's stock has taken a few hits and he's perhaps yet to recover from an ill-fated spell in charge of Luton Town, in which he fell narrowly short of ensuring survival on the 2024/25 second-tier term's final-day before failing to inspire a tangible promotion push in League One.

He has, however, previously excelled at this level, with Wycombe Wanderers having been situated just two points behind eventual-runaway champions Birmingham at the time of his departure to Luton in January 2025.

Bloomfield may now require a sustained spell back in League One leading a side with more modest expectations such as the Chairboys, where he was able to work his magic - and Blackpool can provide just that.

In contrast to Rowett and Bloomfield, Stuart Maynard lacks pedigree above League Two level, but that shouldn't rule the ex-Notts County and York City boss out of contention.

Indeed, at the age of 46 and with an obvious appetite to prove his managerial mettle higher up the pyramid, Maynard could be an ideal candidate for Blackpool in their efforts to not only survive, but begin laying the foundational blueprint for longer-term stability and success.

Much like Woodman at Bromley, Maynard's dismissal from York in the wake of promotion back to League Two following a decade-long exile as National League champions with no fewer than 108 points was scarcely believable. The difference, of course, is that Maynard did not even receive the opportunity to lead the Ministermen into the current campaign, and their loss could turn out to be Blackpool's gain.

Maynard has a solid body of work to boast of from his time at Notts County, where he failed to deliver promotion but did take the Magpies into the fourth-tier play-offs during their second season back at the level and ultimately put the foundations in place for Martin Paterson to go one better with the Meadow Lane outfit.

Blackpool fans, having sat through an overly-patient and slow-paced possession-based style under Evatt, would likely warm to a more dynamic, attack-oriented approach through Maynard, who would tick many boxes for the League One strugglers.

A more left-field option with greater risk but no shortage of potential upside, Ryan Mason remains available following his departure from West Bromwich Albion at the start of the year and represents an interesting candidates for lower-ranked EFL outfits to consider.

Mason was highly-thought-of during his time coaching Tottenham Hotspur and even taking occasional interim charge of the Lilywhites, which meant that many were surprised when the one-cap ex-England international failed to get going at West Brom.

He would take just nine wins from 27 matches across all competitions with the Baggies before being dismissed ahead of a relegation battle in the deeper stages of the previous campaign, with James Morrison going on to prove just how much his two predecessors in Mason and Eric Ramsey had underperformed at the Hawthorns.

It was, however, Mason's first senior, full-time gig, and it would be harsh to judge him entirely from that alone.

Mason showed plenty of signs at West Brom that suggest he'd be a more-than-capable candidate somewhat lower down the ladder, with his progressive style, experience with young talent and connections within the game all representing healthy selling points for a club in Blackpool's position.

It's essentially a case of risk versus reward, and only Blackpool's key boardroom figures can decide whether it's a gamble worth taking amid the threat of relegation to League Two.

They do have a history of appointing older managers with their best years very much in the past, though, and there are worse options than going for a change of tact and rolling the dice on Mason.

Tokenized stocks track equity prices, not crypto, so a falling BTC doesn’t directly pull their prices down. What stands out, however, is that crypto users appear to keep buying them through the selloff. Demand for 24/7 trading and onchain access to US equities appears to have found strong product-market fit as they are holding up even as the rest of the crypto market weakens.

In September, rising share prices drove most of the growth in value of tokenized stocks. Since mid-October, new minting has driven the growth in value even as share prices dropped. Oman's sovereign wealth fund joins a round for robot labs that test what AI designs.

Chinese lab automation company MegaRobo Technologies has raised nearly $200M in a round joined by the Oman Investment Authority and CMB International. It builds systems that let AI design experiments, run them in robotic labs and learn from the results, and plans to expand across North America, Europe, the Middle East and Asia.

China’s MegaRobo Technologies has raised nearly $200M to build labs where AI can run its own experiments, the company announced on Thursday. The Oman Investment Authority, the country’s sovereign wealth fund, joined the round, along with new investor CMB International.

Existing backers Asia Investment Capital, Granite Asia and Sinovation Ventures also put in more money. MegaRobo did not disclose its valuation.

AI can read papers and predict molecules, but science still needs real tests. MegaRobo links AI models to robots and lab tools. A model can plan a test, run it and use the results to pick the next one.

“That shift changes AI from a system that interprets the world into one that can help create new knowledge, materials, processes and products,” said founder and CEO Daniel Huang.

MegaRobo says it has more than 500 customers, mostly drug makers, biotech firms and research labs. Its Megalaxy Lab lets scientists and AI agents send tests to robot labs, and has served more than 200 of them.

It has also launched Poreva Open, a protein design contest with Tencent Cloud and DNA maker Twist Bioscience. Megalaxy Lab will test more than 10,000 AI-designed proteins in a real lab, to set a shared benchmark built on actual results. Anthropic, for one, had outside labs check Claude’s protein designs in August.

Money is pouring into AI drug design. Alphabet’s Isomorphic Labs raised $5B on Tuesday, and Chai Discovery raised $400M in July. In Berlin, Dunia Innovations is building a €280M robotic lab to test what AI designs.

MegaRobo says it has raised about $600M in total, including a $300M Series C backed by Goldman Sachs in 2022. It plans to expand across North America, Europe, the Middle East and Asia, and to use the same technology beyond science, much like Jeff Bezos’ physical AI lab.

‘Birthright,’ About Differing Attitudes on Israel, Set For Broadway

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The play follows seven friends through 18 years, starting just after their Birthright trip to Israel.

Birthright, a play by Tony Award winner Jonathan Spector examining differing views about Israel among Jewish-American friends, will move to Broadway this spring.

The play is set for a 20-week run at Broadway’s Hudson Theatre starting Feb. 23, 2027, ahead of a March 15 opening night. It moves to Broadway after a sold-out and critically acclaimed run at the Off-Broadway MCC Theater this past summer. Teddy Bergman directs, making his Broadway debut after helming the Off-Broadway run.

Casting for the Broadway run has not yet been announced.

The play follows seven friends through 18 years, starting just after their Birthright trip to Israel in 2006, tracking their differing and changing attitudes on Israel as well as changes within their own lives. Spector returns to Broadway after last season’s Eureka Day, which won the Tony Award for best revival of a play, and centered on a school board dealing with select parents who did not want to vaccinate their kids.

“Just over two years ago, I was racing to finish a first draft of Birthright before starting rehearsals for Eureka Day at MTC. I could never have fathomed that such a short time later we’d be bringing it to Broadway, when the questions the play is wrestling with still feel so close to our present moment. Writing this was the scariest thing I’ve ever attempted to do, and (perhaps not coincidentally), has become the most meaningful and rewarding. I’m beyond thrilled that a wider audience will have a chance to encounter the play and see the work of our brilliant artistic collaborators,” Spector said.

iOS 27 gave AirPods Pro a convenient new setting, here’s how to use it

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

iOS 27 gave AirPods Pro a variety of new features, but there’s another addition that’s easy to miss: a convenient new setting in Control Center for Adaptive Audio.

Several AirPods models offer excellent Active Noise Cancellation and Transparency modes—especially AirPods Pro 3 (now only $179).

But Adaptive Audio is another option you may or may not have tried. The feature aims to blend the strengths of both ANC and Transparency in a single mode.

And in iOS 27, Apple added a new setting to let you conveniently fine-tune Adaptive Audio to your preferences.

While wearing AirPods, open Control Center on your iPhone and tap the volume slider, then hit ‘Listening Mode’ in the bottom-left corner. Here you’ll see the standard listening mode options.

New in iOS 27 though, when ‘Adaptive’ is selected, you can now use a Liquid Glass slider to adjust whether the mode lets in more or less external noise.

Slide it to the left and Adaptive Audio will more closely resemble Transparency, while sliding it to the right makes it cancel out more noise.

You can also move the new slider incrementally to achieve exactly your ideal balance of Transparency and Noise Cancellation. It’s not limited to just a few preset options.

This Control Center slider is available on all AirPods models that support Adaptive Audio.

AI features could be turned off with a simple toggle, Canva proves

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AI has taken over everything in the past few years, with AI features bombarding virtually every app or service available. If you’ve ever wanted to just turn off AI features, Canva proves that it could be a simple toggle, if companies actually wanted to give you a choice.

Canva today announced “AI Controls,” a new toggle that completely turns off generative AI features within the editing app.

Generative AI can be quite helpful in working on posters, editing images, and more, but it’s not everyone’s preference. Canva puts this well:

For some people, AI is the most exciting creative tool in a generation, while others prefer bringing their ideas to life without it. We believe the future of creativity should give everyone the freedom to choose their own path, whether that’s creating with AI, without it, or somewhere in between.

To give its users the choice, Canva’s Permissions menu now has a simple toggle for “Enable Generative AI features.” Toggling it off disables features such as Magic Write and Magic Edit. There’s also an option to limit how much AI-generated imagery appears in the Canva library.

A vanished bird from Haiti may help solve an ancient Pacific mystery

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

DNA reveals how snipes and woodcocks reached distant islands, stopped migrating, changed physically, and became vulnerable to extinction.

The sandpiper family includes some of the most extreme long-distance migrants among birds.

Two branches of that family, the snipes and the woodcocks, have behaved quite differently. These birds reached islands again and again, and migration was then abandoned.

Nearly every living species was examined in a new DNA study.

The results suggest that island settlement was accompanied by changes in body size and flying ability. Island species later proved vulnerable when people arrived with rats and cats.

The work was led by Min Zhao, then a postdoctoral researcher at the Florida Museum of Natural History. Colleagues at Virginia Tech and the Field Museum in Chicago also took part.

According to Zhao, the whole project grew out of one extinct bird from Hispaniola. That Caribbean island is shared by Haiti and the Dominican Republic.

The dates reported in the study were estimated from family trees built with DNA. The uncertainty attached to them can run to millions of years.

Florida Museum researchers dug the bird’s bones out of a cave in Haiti in 1984.

In 2015, those bones were described by two scientists as a new species of woodcock. Woodcocks are long-billed birds that probe soft soil for food.

One of the two scientists was David Steadman, who is now an emeritus curator at the museum. He is also listed as a co-author of the new study.

Zhao’s team extracted DNA from two of the fossil specimens. The researchers then read the mitochondrial genome, a small set of genes passed from mother to offspring.

Both specimens were placed with the snipes, which form the sister group of the woodcocks. Their closest living relative turned out to be the giant snipe of lowland South America.

The analysis suggested a South American origin for the Haitian bird’s ancestors. The two lineages are thought to have split between 5 and 12 million years ago.

Why, then, did the bones resemble those of a woodcock? The authors think the bird’s build may have been altered by island living, which could explain the mix-up.

The outer wing bone of the Haitian bird was unusually short when compared with its upper wing bone. The authors wrote that it could fly but probably not well.

The team sequenced 45 samples in total. Two were the Haitian fossils and 19 were toe pads taken from old museum specimens. The remaining 24 were fresh tissue samples.

All but one of the 29 living species of snipes and woodcocks were covered by these samples. Specimens were supplied by ten museums and research collections.

A family tree was built from the data, and both groups were traced back to Eurasia.

The ancestors of today’s American birds crossed from the Eastern Hemisphere to the Western Hemisphere four separate times.

One of those crossings took place roughly 20 million years ago. It was then that the ancestor of the American woodcock reached North America.

The researchers could not tell whether the bird arrived across the Atlantic or by way of Alaska.

The Austral snipes are found on the outlying islands of New Zealand. Scientists have long placed these small brown birds in a group of their own.

Some snipes from Eurasia spend each winter in Australia and on nearby islands. The DNA showed that those visitors are not close relatives of the Austral snipes.

Instead, the Austral snipes share a branch with several large South American snipes and the extinct Haitian bird.

Two DNA datasets placed the split between the Austral snipes and their relatives at about 14 million and nearly 18 million years ago, respectively.

The southern continents had broken apart long before either estimate. The snipes’ ancestor most likely crossed the Pacific.

How the crossing happened remains an open question. The authors suggested that birds straying far outside their normal range may have played a role. Such straying was perhaps caused by extreme weather.

The authors also raised a route through Antarctica. Patches of treeless tundra are thought to have lasted there until at least 12.8 million years ago.

Several living snipe species still breed on tundra in the Arctic.

The analysis indicated that the ancestors of both snipes and woodcocks were migrants. Their island descendants are not.

Ten of the 29 living species are found only on islands, and none of those ten migrates.

The habit was lost several times. Woodcocks gave it up when they settled the Malay Archipelago in Southeast Asia. Snipes did the same when they settled South America and Madagascar.

Body size changed as well, although not in a single direction.

The Amami woodcock of southern Japan is about 45% heavier than the Eurasian woodcock, its closest mainland relative. Madagascar’s snipe is about 91% heavier than the common snipe of Eurasia.

The opposite change is seen in the Austral snipes. Their average weight is 3.4 ounces (96 grams), which is about the weight of a deck of cards.

South American snipes on the same branch average 8.6 ounces (243.5 grams). That is more than twice as much.

The smaller size may be explained by scarce food, the authors suggested. Competition from kiwis and other birds that probe the soil for worms was also considered possible.

Ten species of snipes and woodcocks are known to have died out in the recent geologic past. Nine of them lived on islands.

The Austral snipes suffered the heaviest losses. Three of nine known species survive. All three are ranked as Vulnerable or Near Threatened by the IUCN Red List.

The South Island snipe died out in 1964, and introduced black rats are regarded as the likely cause.

No resident snipe or woodcock is left in the Caribbean. At least three species have been lost there.

The youngest dated bones of the Haitian bird are about 600 years old. Rats and feral cats brought by people are suspected of causing its extinction.

Snipes nest on the ground, where they can be reached by those predators. Island species had given up migration, and in some cases strong flight as well.

The authors wrote that these changes made such species especially vulnerable to human impacts.

Overhunting was named as one of those impacts.

Jessica Oswald, a co-author, also worked on the study at the Florida Museum.

“People will eat anything,” said Oswald. She said a crew of hungry explorers or whalers could wipe out an island’s birds in a single weekend.

Island woodcocks in Japan and Southeast Asia appear to have fared better. They live in mountain forests, which may be harder for people to reach.

New Zealand may have been reached first by the Austral snipes.

From there they could have spread to Norfolk Island, New Caledonia and Fiji. The opposite path cannot be ruled out, because no genetic data exist for those islands’ extinct snipes.

More snipe species may once have lived in the Pacific and are now extinct. In that case, today’s birds would be the last survivors of a larger group. Direct fossil evidence of that link across the Pacific is still missing.

The Haitian bird’s place on the tree rests on mitochondrial genes from two specimens. A possible close relative from Puerto Rico is also extinct and has not been tested genetically.

The authors suggest that DNA from fossils yet to be found on Pacific islands could help trace how snipes spread across the region.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences.

Not only did they figure out how to make delta-9 THC, the cannabinoid that is known to get us high, they also started to make a wide variety of other kinds of cannabinoids, including delta-8 THC (a slightly less potent intoxicant that is present in trace amounts in the plant), as well as some that don’t actually occur naturally. It was all legal because the products were derived from hemp, not marijuana, which is a Schedule I controlled substance.

Many of these companies self-policed, testing the products themselves and going through established liquor frameworks in order to be sold at national chains. Others, however, skirted the rules, offering shady products made with questionable cannabinoids and no real testing or oversight. This so-called “gas station weed” started to make folks nervous — particularly McConnell. So when Congress was putting together a bill to reopen the government last fall, after a record-long shutdown, McConnell added an amendment to the 2018 definition of hemp to get rid of the market as we know it.

The new law will change the definition of legal hemp. Previously, hemp was allowed to have up to .3 percent THC as measured in its dry weight. Hemp farmers could grow crops that adhered to this, then processors could extract cannabinoids, and companies put them into products so that they had enough potency to see effects. The new law, however, looks at the final product, restricting anything with more than .4 milligrams of THC per container.

There are two parts to the law, explains Neil M. Willner, an attorney with Vicente LLP, a New York-based firm specializing in cannabis law. The first went into effect on Nov. 12, and banned the sale of all synthetic THC products, or THC compounds that are not found naturally in the cannabis plant. On Dec. 11, the low-dose sodas, liquors, and gummies that have become so popular — and usually contain five or 10 mg of THC per serving — will no longer be legal on a federal level. People in areas with state-legal cannabis programs can still go to a dispensary to buy THC products, but the companies built around hemp-derived cannabinoids — and the people in states without legal weed — will be out of luck.

It will also make it nearly impossible to grow hemp, too. “We’ve talked to a lot of hemp farmers, and it’s very difficult for them to manage the THC content of their crops,” says Dein. “It’ll push a lot of farmers out of business.” Tweet, owner of IND Hemp, the processing facility, adds that the new law doesn’t define what a “container” actually is. “I ship 55-gallon drums of hemp seed oil all over the country, and by definition, that’s a container, right?” she says. “Like, we do make a product that likely will go above that 0.4 milligram [limit] just because of that volume. Even though hemp seed oil doesn’t have a lot of cannabinoids in it, it does have some, and so there is some risk to our business.”

In one word, bad. Take, for example, Texas, where outgoing Lieutenant Governor Dan Patrick introduced a bill over the summer that would have banned all hemp products that contained THC. This was wildly unpopular with many of the 1.5 million veterans who live in the state, among others. Outgoing Governor Greg Abbott ended up vetoing the bill, instead establishing guidelines for age and marketing restrictions. In November, Patrick lost his bid for reelection, as did Abbott and other Republicans statewide. “I think that’s one of the reasons Texas went blue,” says Serrao. But from this day forward, I knew I needed to start to emotionally divorce myself from the material. So, I can wield the ax if need be. You know, it is not like I’m burning the script. World War II is not going anywhere. In, say, five years, there will be a new crop of actors, and maybe Landa will be hiding in plain sight amongst them. Maybe one of the guys we’ve seen during this period matures to a more capable Landa five years from now. We’ll see. But as sure as shit World War II ain’t going anywhere.”

“Now, I’m explaining to Lawrence how I cannot go ahead without the Landa, and to give him credit, he didn’t freak out. He didn’t try and talk me out of it. He had been in the room, and there was also kinda the impression that we had seen the best, so, dare I say it, he even agreed with me. This was the best version of Lawrence, the very, very best side of him.”

Like any great producer, Bender listened. Then he gave himself a moment to allow the horrific feeling in the pit of his stomach to momentarily rise. After giving himself that moment, he pushed aside any angst and started to look at the situation from a logical standpoint.

“Here’s the thing: Part of your job as the producer is to fix things, solve problems. A lot of it is creative, but a lot of it is nuts and bolts and making things better,” explained Bender. “After you have dealt with your own disappointment, you go to instinct mode, and my instinct said, ‘Let’s stay.’ So, I said to Quentin, ‘Let’s give ourselves a few days. Here’s what our burn rate is: We’re burning through $100,000 a week, so it is worth you spending another $100,000. Let’s just spend the week looking for Landa. It is worth the risk for you.’”

As down as he was, this made perfect sense to Quentin. Universal would start financing the movie on Friday, so he had just days. If any plug was going to be pulled, it had to happen by Friday.

“Lawrence was 100 percent right. He said, ‘We have got to call the Weinsteins, Universal, Mike, and Carlos, and tell them the situation—and, most importantly, Simone. We have to tell her that for the rest of the week, it is Landa, Landa, Landa, Landa. Nobody else. We ain’t got time to fuck around. We’ve got to find Landa by end of business Thursday night, or we’ve got to shut the film down. It is just that fucking simple. We’ve gotta tell Simone what time it is. It is just that fucking simple. It’s just what we are doing.’ I needed the best of Lawrence, and that was him there and then,” said Quentin.

Casting director Alexandra Montag offered her recollections of the “Finding Hans Landa” casting meeting.

“Simone explained that we had worked to the criteria that Quentin had expressed, and that was that every German-speaking actor speak not only German but also English, French, and Italian authentically. There were very few actors in Germany who had this range, and we agreed that although the actors he had seen were very successful actors, there was something missing. We had seen the tapes.

“But it is also true that not every actor was available on that first day, and he had yet to see an actor Simone believed just right for the role. His name was Christoph Waltz. ‘Please wait for Christoph—Christoph will come,’ she told Quentin. It was her belief that he had all the charm, charisma, and cynicism that fit with Hans Landa.

“I, however, picked up Quentin’s fear as well, and when they had left, I said, ‘Oh shit, shit, shit, what are we going to do?’ But Simone was completely calm and always said, ‘Just wait for Christoph Waltz.’ She was completely confident.”

Making his way to meet with Quentin Tarantino, Christoph Waltz had very little reason to feel optimistic. He had been in his chosen profession since the ’70s, and since leaving the theater world to concentrate on television and film he had become, to a degree, a respected working actor. “Everybody knew who I was; I had played some good parts, of course, I had to do some crap also. But in all those years, Simone Bär had never once cast me in anything. It was odd, but there it was.”

So, not convinced that Bär believed in his talent, Waltz pushed open the door and entered her casting office. He was confident in his own abilities and ready to play the game, like any other actor.

Montag recalled Bär never doubting Waltz’s talent. It was more “because she never found the role for him in the way that this one fit him, the charm, the cynicism, which he could play so well. She knew how good he was. She knew in Landa she had the perfect role for him.”

Waltz, however, was unaware of any such confidence. It was, he felt, another audition he did not need to get excited about.

“We had had it happen many times before: an international filmmaker coming in to cast their film. When Spielberg came to cast Schindler’s List, everyone was in hysterical convulsion: ‘Spielberg, Spielberg, Spielberg.’ Yes, it is all exciting, but frankly speaking, what is in it for me, personally? When you are offered the role of a German soldier in the ghetto who shouts, ‘Get out, get out, get out’ for $500, why would I want to do that?

“So, that was my mindset. I’m an extremely skeptical character, and when they gave me the whole screenplay, it was very heavy, with its title page written in Quentin Tarantino’s own hand, and from there you can get a feeling of the person who wrote it. I said to my agent in Germany, ‘Okay, what role do they want me to do?’ ‘Well, you should read for that Landa character,’ I was told.

“‘Okay, I’ll read Landa.’ Now, I was convinced that I was only reading this big part of Landa because they wanted me for a part that didn’t have enough text to actually show anything. It was just text material.”

Ushered into the kitchen, Waltz took a seat and waited his turn to enter the audition room.

This was the landscape of the time. If a big American film production came to Germany or Austria, local actors would be passed over, so television was where one made a living. It was, Waltz said, how it was.

“We, the natives, would be left with the shitty small parts, and usually the plum parts went to English actors who put in blue contact lenses, dyed their hair blond, and put on a phony accent which no one has. I was used to that. It was out of the question to think it would be any other way. So, when Quentin came, I was under the impression that what always happened would happen this time. They’re in town for the tax credit. They’ll do their search for local actors, hold up their hands that nobody suitable can be found, and an English-speaking actor will play the part. I had it pretty much sussed out how it worked. Not that I’m correct in everything, but experience told me that, you know, I did not need to get excited.”

He had been assured that this time was different. Waltz decided that, if nothing else, he would enjoy reading with Quentin Tarantino, and that would be that.

“Nice to meet with you, Mr. Tarantino. Nice to meet you, Mr. Bender.”

Sitting opposite the American director and producer, the first thing to strike the actor was the director asking him if he would mind reading the opening scene. “Would I mind?” he thought. “Are you kidding?”

“But of course,” said Waltz, nodding in agreement.

Waltz’s Colonel Hans Landa introduced himself to the French farm owner, Perrier LaPadite, on this occasion played by Quentin Tarantino.

“First of all, from the get-go, Lawrence and I kept looking over at each other, and our smiles just became bigger and bigger and bigger,” enthused Quentin.

“We were literally kicking each other under the table in excitement,” agreed Bender.

Waltz’s complete absorption into the scene was nearly broken when he thought to himself, “Why is Quentin sucking his thumb?” Instantly, he realized the banality of such questioning. Perrier LaPadite smokes a pipe. The man in front of him, the writer of the weighty screenplay, was pretending to smoke one.

The room was devoid of any noticeable cameras. Instead, the great Quentin Tarantino was encouraging him to play, like a kid. He had never felt so safe, so relaxed, in an audition room. Nobody was looking at him probingly, questioning how he had the temerity to even be in the room. His natural pessimism waited for the curveball that was sure to come.

Quentin, on the other hand, never witnessed anything other than devotion to what he had written from the actor who sat in front of him. “It was amazing. It wasn’t as though none of the other actors had done the scene well. Some had done it well, but they didn’t own the role, especially that scene. The scene was an incredibly dangerous mountain that they managed to scale and survive, but they didn’t own the mountain—they just got to the top. Waltz owned the mountain.

“By the time we finished reading the scene, we knew that he had the role. He had saved the movie; he absolutely saved the film. So it is over with, and Lawrence and I are absolutely losing our fucking minds. We are just so effusive, and Christoph is just being very casual about it. He is looking at us as if we are just a couple of gregarious Americans. I pick up that he is thinking we have said this to everybody, and we are blowing a little sunshine up his ass. So, I make it plain: ‘No, no, you understand this character. You’re fantastic, you’re great.’

“To which Christoph replied, ‘Well, thank you very much. That is very nice to hear. However, I’m of the belief—and sometimes I run afoul of my colleagues in this regard—that an actor is only as capable as the role that he is playing. If you want an actor to be great, you need to give him great material. So, when you are effusively telling me how magnificent I am, in fact, you are just applauding your own material. Because, yes, I’m quite good playing this role, but that is because it is a damned good role, but ultimately, you are just complimenting yourself.’

“To which I reply, ‘Well, thank you very much. But be that as it may, you cannot deny that when it comes to this role, you have an inner compass.’

“He thinks about it for a second and responds by saying, ‘Okay, possibly I have this compass of which you speak. Nevertheless’—he points at the script—‘the North Pole is the North Pole.’

“Ha! ‘Well,’ I say, ‘you have just done the scene in English, and you obviously speak German?’

“‘Of course I speak German—I’m from Austria.’

“‘Can you do a little bit of it in German for us?’

“He picks up the script, and away he goes. It is perfect. ‘There are also parts written in French. Could you give us a little of it in French?’

“Picking up the screenplay written in English, he translates it into French, and he is doing it perfect. So, I ask him, ‘There is also a little in Italian—do you speak Italian?’

“Of course, he fucking nailed it! We found our guy!”

“I found him, I found him, I found him! I found Landa!”

Peacock's New Workplace Sitcom 'Dig' Unearths Amy Poehler's Best TV Role in Years | Review

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Amy Poehler hasn't led a TV comedy in over a decade, and the return vehicle she's chosen for herself is, of all things, an archaeological workplace comedy set in Greece. Dig, Peacock's new 10-episode half-hour series from Poehler, Mike Schur, and J.J. Philbin, based on Kate Myers' bestselling novel Excavations, reunites the Parks and Recreation brain trust, adding in a dash of Hugh Laurie for good measure.

It’s a strong starting lineup, and yet, Dig spends its early episodes trying to be a workplace comedy, leaning on dig-site hierarchies and academic turf wars, when what it actually turns out to be good at is something else entirely. Stick with it. The show finds itself once everyone stops bickering over chain-of-command nonsense and starts working together to solve a mystery (and maybe excavate their own long-buried issues).

Poehler plays Elise DeBellnick, a career archaeologist who has spent what feels like her entire adult life serving as the right hand to Dr. Neville Wycombe (Laurie), a legendary, pompous, impossibly charming British professor who keeps almost retiring every summer. She dreams of leading her own dig in Scotland, if the gods of academic funding allow it, but every season Wycombe announces his grand departure and then just . . . stays. The students clearly love him. What they don’t love is Elise's management style, which is blunt and humorless and entirely too by-the-book for a group of twenty-somethings spending their sun-drenched holiday on the Aegean coast.

The women surrounding Elise are where the show loads most of its more interesting character work, though it takes a while for any of them to land. Geraldine Viswanathan plays Dylan, a former dig favorite who ghosted Elise and the site years ago under circumstances the show treats as a big reveal but lands with a thud. Antonia Thomas is Clare, the hyper-competent lab manager who is roughly a decade younger than Elise and stuck in a Cold War with her that the show gestures at without ever making you feel the weight of. Dylan and Clare have history too, through Clare's fiancé Gary (Jon Pointing), who used to date Dylan. The love triangle, or whatever geometric shape this is, doesn't generate nearly as much tension as the show seems to think it should, mostly because we're never given enough about these people to understand why they'd care about each other this deeply, or at all. Their backstories are a bit too fill-in-the-blank, even for this modern age of television.

Then there's Patty Borkman. Fina Strazza, in a performance that should have casting directors rearranging their call sheets, plays Wycombe's twitchy, overeager assistant like a cartoon mouse who somehow earned a research fellowship. She's the character the show doesn't seem to realize it needs the most. Where the other women arrive preloaded with backstory the writers expect you to care about without earning it, Patty is a blank page, and Strazza fills her in with a goofy, wide-eyed sincerity that feels completely unmanufactured. She endears without trying. She's odd in a way the show badly needs more of, a little left of center in every scene she's in, and her chemistry with Poehler sneaks up on them both.

Kayvan Novak is a highlight as Stavros, the local Greek mayor who is ridiculous and openly besotted with Elise. The show also sprinkles in cameos from familiar Parks and Rec faces like Mo Collins, which land as fun surprises without being asked to carry too much weight. Instead, the focus is on the setting and job it houses.

The central mystery involves the discovery of a rare artifact the team dubs "Pretty Woman," and this is where Dig starts losing me. The discovery itself feels contrived, too perfectly timed to kick the plot into gear right when it needs kicking. It doesn't help that the artifact looks like it was unwrapped from museum gift shop packaging an hour before they shot the scene. For a show about people who spend their lives sifting through centuries of grime and sediment, nothing about this find has any texture. It's too clean, too white, too pristine. (Someone in the props department apparently decided that ancient Greek statues should look like they were 3D-printed yesterday.) It robs the discovery of the weight it needs to carry the back half of the season, and the resulting whodunit, while competently plotted, never feels like much is actually at stake.

But you push past it because what Poehler is trying to do with her characters is genuinely worthwhile. Schur, Philbin, and the team clearly want Dig to be an archaeology Hacks, but the early episodes lean almost too hard into the workplace comedy template with departmental rivalries played for laughs and the whole "dysfunctional team at a quirky job" structure that Schur has built a career on. It's fine. Some of it is sharp. But it's also where the show is weakest, because the dig site never quite feels like a real place with real stakes, and the workplace dynamics can't generate friction when we don't understand why half these people showed up in the first place. Poehler is doing something interesting with Elise even in these early stretches, though, playing a woman whose competence has fossilized into rigidity, whose commitment to one man's project has started to look less like loyalty and more like a failure of nerve. The show just isn't giving her enough to push against yet.

Tina and Amy are the peanut butter and jelly of Hollywood.

Where Dig transforms is around the midpoint, once the mystery forces the four women into actual collaboration. The workplace comedy scaffolding falls away, and what replaces it is a caper driven by friendship. The shift isn't subtle, and it's not entirely smooth, but it works because the actors have been building chemistry in the margins all along. Part of what helps it along is the generational math. Poehler has talked about how Elise sees herself at 19, 29, and 39 in the women around her, and when the show leans into that, it shines. Patty's wide-eyed intensity reminds Elise of who she was before she got cautious. Dylan's restlessness mirrors the version of herself that almost left. Clare's desire to be appreciated for her talents is what Elise feels now. They shouldn't get along, and for most of the early episodes they don't, but the mystery strips away the professional pecking order and forces them to actually see each other.

What saves Dig from collapsing under its own structural wobbliness is the thing you'd expect: the people in it are really, really funny. Laurie, who studied archaeology at Cambridge in real life, starts the season doing what amounts to a prestige comedy cameo, with his kerchiefs and copious Eton references. But somewhere around the midpoint, he unlocks something warmer and more vulnerable, and his scenes with Poehler in the back half of the season become some of the show's strongest. Strazza is the other revelation. She's a dark horse in a cast of heavy hitters, and she commits to Patty's weirdness with a fearlessness that most young actors would sand down into something safer. Instead, she lets Patty be strange and awkward and a little too intense, and it works because Strazza never signals to the audience that she's in on the joke.

Dig has the bones (sorry) of something great, and by its final stretch, it's closer to great than the first few episodes would have you guess. The setting is gorgeous when the show ventures off the lot and actually shoots in Greece and southern Italy. Poehler and Laurie are as good a comedy pairing as you'll find on television right now. The worldbuilding never fully recovers from the early stumbles, and Dylan's backstory stays paper-thin no matter how many scenes Viswanathan throws at it. Once the four women lock in and the show stops trying to be an office comedy with shovels, the gap between what Dig is and what it could be narrows considerably. But Amy Poehler on a television set, doing what she does best with people who know how to write for her, is something we’ve been missing for too long. We’re glad Peacock unearthed her talents again for this one.

Space and time seem like the bedrock of reality. But startling clues are leading physicists toward the possibility that something stranger lies beneath.

Every experience we have and every moment we live unfold naturally across space and time ‪—‬ so naturally that we rarely stop to question their existence.

Physics takes this just as much for granted as we do. For more than 100 years, the field has been built upon the foundational idea that space-time — the four-dimensional fabric of the universe — is fundamental.

But new insights drawn from the hearts of black holes, quantum computers and ultraprecise lasers, are now poised to challenge this assumption, leading a growing number of physicists to ask the same question: What if space and time aren't the bedrock of the universe, but emerge from something even deeper?

"You can build space from a theory without space," Jeff Forshaw, a professor of particle physics at the University of Manchester in the U.K., told Live Science.

For decades, we have known that two extraordinarily successful descriptions of nature are incompatible.

In Einstein's general theory of relativity, matter and energy bend space-time and we experience that curvature as gravity. Quantum mechanics, meanwhile, describes nature on the smallest scales of atoms and subatomic particles.

But quantum mechanics still has no adequate description of the force of gravity. The conflict between these theories of big and small becomes unavoidable in extreme environments, such as the centers of black holes or the smallest fractions of a second after the Big Bang, where gravity grows to be intense on the minute scales ruled by quantum mechanics.

An artist's illustration of the Big Bang with time running from the image's bottom to the top. During the first brief fraction of a second after the Big Bang the universe was so dense that quantum effects and gravity were equally strong.

So far, efforts to combine them mathematically and theoretically have led to nonsensical results.

"They seem not to fit together into a single, consistent framework that we know to be correct," Latham Boyle, a theoretical physicist at the University of Edinburgh in the U.K, told Live Science.

It's possible that one or both of these theories is incomplete, but an increasingly popular belief among physicists is that both theories are perched upon an untested assumption: space-time itself.

Both general relativity and quantum mechanics assume that space-time exists. Yet neither explains why our universe seems to have three dimensions of space and one of time, or where space-time comes from. If this assumption is wrong, the apparent foundation of reality could be a large-scale approximation of something even more mysterious.

In Einstein's model, space-time is a little like how people once viewed water: smooth and continuous, capable of bending and rippling. But water only appears continuous on large scales because we can't see its composite "bits," or individual molecules, said Boyle, whose research explores quantum gravity, the early universe and whether space-time is fundamental or emergent. One proposal is that space-time is similar.

"It seems smooth but only because we can't view it under a fine enough microscope," Boyle told Live Science. Yet no one has found direct evidence for space-time's microscopic "bits." That leaves physicists searching for indirect clues in places where existing theories are pushed to their breaking point: black holes.

At the hearts of black holes, general relativity breaks down by predicting space-time curving infinitely, while quantum physics gives their boundaries baffling properties.

Around black holes gravity (the curvature of space-time) is warped to an infinite degree.

Black holes also offer intriguing clues that space-time, like water, may have microscopic ingredients. One clue comes from thermodynamics ‪—‬ the laws governing heat, temperature and entropy.

Entropy is often described as measuring the disorder in a system, but physicists use it to describe how many hidden microscopic arrangements can produce the same large-scale behavior. A glass of water, for example, may have one temperature and pressure even though its molecules could be moving and arranged in an enormous number of different ways. The more possible microscopic arrangements there are, the greater its entropy.

This matters because thermodynamics usually describes the large-scale behavior of microscopic ingredients. If Einstein’s equations are thermodynamic in nature, their entropy may be due to hidden microscopic states of space-time that physicists cannot yet see.

"General relativity is thermodynamics," Forshaw said. "Einstein's equations are a statement of thermodynamics. They're a statistically emergent feature of an underlying theory."

In the 1970s, Jacob Bekenstein and Stephen Hawking showed that black holes, too, have entropy and a physical temperature. This raised striking questions: Are there microscopic ingredients that account for those thermodynamic properties, and if so, what are they?

If those constituents exist, then space-time's smooth geometry, like the continuous appearance of water, emerges only on larger scales.

Another clue is that Einstein's equations appear to contain traces of this hidden structure, somehow reproducing the relationships needed for black holes to have a temperature, even though the theory directly connects the shape of space-time only to the matter it contains.

"Einstein's equation, which we thought was just a classical equation, nothing to do with quantum mechanics, seems to secretly know — or at some level to have known — about this finite entropy associated with the black hole," Boyle said.

The entropy of black holes also gives rise to a second sign that space-time emerges from something deeper: a puzzle known as the black hole information paradox.

Stephen Hawking in front of a projection of the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy formula for black holes.

Quantum mechanics says that physical information can never be destroyed. Yet if black holes emit radiation — an idea famously proposed by Stephen Hawking in 1974 — they should slowly evaporate. This is a profound contradiction: A black hole's Hawking radiation is thermal, depending solely on its mass, electric charge and angular momentum. It contains no obvious record of what originally fell inside. That means that if the black hole eventually disappears, the information of what it once gobbled up seems to vanish with it.

But a black hole's entropy scales not with the three-dimensional volume enclosed by it but with the two-dimensional area of its event horizon, the point beyond which nothing, not even light, can escape. Therefore, it's possible that all the information within any region might somehow be stored on its boundary. This became known as the holographic principle, named after a hologram, in which a 3D image is encoded on a 2D surface.

In 1997, theoretical physicist Juan Maldacena made the idea mathematically precise. He showed that a higher-dimensional theory containing gravity could be mapped onto a lower-dimensional quantum theory without it.

The two descriptions appeared to be interchangeable, equally valid views of reality. But some researchers now go further: They think the lower-dimensional quantum theory may be the fundamental description, while the higher-dimensional space-time we seem to live in is what emerges when its information is decoded.

These ideas gave rise to holography. Its central principle is that a gravitational world in more dimensions may be completely encoded by a quantum theory without gravity in fewer dimensions. That lower-dimensional theory lives on the boundary of the higher-dimensional spacetime.

You should think of the lower-dimensional thing as being what's most real

Mark Van Raamsdonk, a professor of physics at the University of British Columbia, compares the concept to a computer's memory chip. At the fundamental level, a video game is encoded in physical bits switching between states. Yet on a screen, those bits produce a representation of a 3D world containing objects, movement and physical rules. The virtual world seems spatially real, even though its underlying information is stored very differently.

"You should think of the lower-dimensional thing as being what's most real," Van Raamsdonk told Live Science.

Quantum entanglement may play a central role in how this lower-dimensional information gives rise to higher-dimensional space-time. It describes how particles can share linked quantum properties, even when separated by vast distances.

"If I want to decode what space-time geometry is being stored by the quantum state of this lower-dimensional system, all I need to do is probe this entanglement structure," Van Raamsdonk said.

For decades, directly testing this theory appeared hopeless. Physicists thought they would need particle collisions approaching the Planck scale — the fantastically high energy at which quantum effects are expected to become as important as gravity, and far beyond anything existing colliders can produce.

But physicists realized they don't need to rely on brute force. They are now developing several ways around that energy barrier.

The CMS (Compact Muon Solenoid) detector at CERN's Large Hadron Collider. Reaching the Planck scale requires roughly 900 trillion times more energy than the Large Hadron Collider can produce.

How could scientists discover whether space-time has a deeper structure? One way is to search for a subtle jitter, or static hum, in space-time itself. Experiments such as GQuEST, a collaboration involving Fermilab and Caltech, and QUEST at Cardiff University in the U.K. split laser light along different paths and recombine it. Extraordinarily small changes in the distance light travels may reveal fluctuations, arising from discrete building blocks of space-time, that are consistent with some of holography's models.

In some holographic models, physicists represent the underlying units of information as quantum bits (qubits) — mathematically identical to those used in quantum computers. Physically, a computer qubit may be encoded in an ion or electrical circuit, whereas a qubit in a holographic model represents a possible microscopic unit of the quantum theory from which space-time emerges. This equivalence allows quantum processors to simulate space-time models.

In such models, the patterns of entanglement between the qubits dictate the physical distances between regions of space. The stronger those entanglements are, the closer together two corresponding regions are; if the entanglements are severed, the space-time between them tears apart.

QUEST compares measurements from two neighboring laser beam splitters, or interferometers. Some theoretical models predict quantum fluctuations of space-time that would affect both instruments in a related way. Researchers therefore search for matching fluctuations in their measurements while working to rule out shared sources of laboratory noise. Finding the predicted pattern could support those models; finding nothing would constrain how strong such fluctuations could be, killing off flawed models and refining how microscopic components (or chunks of information) translate into space-time's geometry.

Atom interferometers such as AION and MAGIS-100 extend the search for quantum jitters by using atoms as sensitive probes. When a laser pulse hits an atom, the atom enters superposition, in which it is simultaneously in two different locations at once. It then travels along two separate paths before being recombined by a final pulse. If space-time fluctuates slightly differently along one path than the other, a measurable shift is detected in the atom's interference pattern.

The cold-atom preparation and trapping apparatus for the strontium atom source in Fermilab's Matter-wave Atomic Gradiometer Interferometric Sensor (MAGIS-100).

Another approach called BMV experiments (after the surnames of their independent developers Sougato Bose and his collaborators, Chiara Marletto and Vlatko Vedral) take diamond nanocrystals weighing around a hundred-billonth of a kilogram and place them into a state of quantum superposition.

Painstakingly shielded from every possible electromagnetic and nuclear interaction, and placed at different distances from one another, the diamonds are put through a key test. If they emerge from the experiment with the electron states entangled, then gravity alone produced the entanglement.

That would be evidence that gravity can transmit quantum effects — something a classical gravitational field could not produce. And if gravity (the curvature of space-time) can exist in superposition, it could be a strong clue that space-time's geometry is woven out of quantum information. The challenge, so far, is in excluding all the other effects that may obscure the signal.

On cosmic scales, future gravitational wave observatories ‪—‬ including the Laser Interferometer Space Antenna, the Einstein Telescope and the Cosmic Explorer ‪—‬ could scrutinize gravity at the first clues' source: around black holes. After two black holes merge, the resulting object settles down by emitting gravitational waves with characteristic frequencies and decay times. Einstein's theory makes precise predictions for this "ringdown," allowing researchers to search for discrepancies.

An artist's illustration for how the Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA) will detect ripples in space-time created by merging black holes.

Such discrepancies could potentially point to the quantum nature of space-time near black holes. Interpreting them would require distinguishing between competing explanations ‪—‬ an unexpected signal could cryptically hint at space-time's underlying nature, but it wouldn't reveal a direct picture of its microscopic ingredients.

To identify the bits that make up the reality beneath space-time, scientists are also turning to quantum computers. By entangling many qubits in specific ways to mimic different models of emergent space-time, scientists could determine which mathematical models of the constituent bits are more likely.

"The study of many-body, highly entangled systems is where the future of this whole enterprise is," Forshaw said.

Precursor tests are already underway. In 2022, for example, Google's Sycamore quantum processor was used to reproduce the behavior of a simple theoretical wormhole — not by creating a real hole in space-time but by arranging nine qubits so that their behavior could also be described using gravity.

Earlier this year, researchers went further, using the trapped-ion quantum computer IonQ Forte to test how something resembling gravitational geometry could emerge from patterns of quantum information. The qubits in Forte were arranged into a model that could be interpreted in two connected ways: both as a network of quantum information and as a simple holographic geometry. The researchers then altered the entanglement between the qubits. In the model’s gravitational interpretation, this was akin to adding mass. Just as adding mass bends Einstein's space-time, the researchers expected changing the entanglement to alter the corresponding geometry — and it did.

The findings demonstrate that researchers can study a mathematical relationship between entanglement and geometry using quantum hardware. However, they do not establish that our space-time emerges from lower-dimensional quantum information.

"The experiment that we did is a baby step in that direction," Crystal Noel, an experimental physicist at Duke University who led the experimental side of the project, told Live Science. One limitation of this result is that it reproduces features of a simplified, static theoretical universe, rather than the expanding universe in which we live. Testing a more realistic model of the universe might require entangling 100 or 200 qubits, which may be possible in the near future.

Each of these experiments will probe different aspects of the relationship between gravity and quantum physics, although none would by itself prove that space-time emerges from information. Taken together, however, their results could begin to reveal what theories of quantum gravity match the real world — and whether emergent space-time is among them.

Such a discovery would raise profound questions not only about the universe’s foundations, but about what it means for anything — including ourselves — to exist within it.

"If I tell you that space doesn't exist, it really questions what it means to be," Forshaw said. "How can you actually talk about things when there's no space to put things in?"

The restaurant processes the young and mature paddles, fruit and their seeds, buds, and flowers, which are then grilled, poached, cold-smoked, juiced, dehydrated, nixtamalized, or preserved into jam, wine, and vinegar.

There are hundreds of edible plants in the Sonoran Desert. Ursa strives to showcase their potential, one 10-course meal at a time.

“We treat Ursa as an art showroom, demonstrating to people what these plants can do, what sustainable means, and what was here before it got easy,” says Lopez. The idea was inspired in part by his Sonoran grandfather, Hector Lopez, who died in the spring. “[He] was the first person I ever heard say they’re proud to be from the desert,” the chef said.

Ursa began as a pop-up in El Centro, California, in 2023. Lopez and Chee moved to a historic brick building in downtown Tucson, re-opening their doors in October 2025. It’s a homecoming of sorts for the couple, who are both from the Sonoran Desert borderlands. They met in Hawaii, where Chee was working in clean energy and transportation. Lopez, meanwhile, spent roughly 18 years cooking in fine dining and modern progressive restaurants like Redbird and Orsa & Winston in LA, and Pai Honolulu. But he was longing for the desert.

After helping a friend open a restaurant and hosting a few popups in Austin, Lopez and Chee bought a desert foraging guide, packed up their two dogs and began driving around the Southwest. It made sense to settle in Tucson, which was recognized by UNESCO in 2015 as a Creative City of Gastronomy for its continuous 4,000-year agricultural history and deeply rooted Sonoran Desert food traditions.

“That’s a unique thing about being here, the connection from seed to plant to restaurant that we hadn’t been able to grasp before,” Chee says.

Consider a recent course that Ursa built around tepary beans, in which the ancient, nutty bean is refried and topped with h:al squash that’s been lactofermented. The squash’s leaves are fermented to make Japanese-style kosho and its flowers are pickled, dried, crushed with dried saltbush, then tossed with barrel cactus seeds for a desert furikake. It’s served with grilled coins of chorizo made from Lopez’s great-grandmother’s recipe and housemade flour tortillas, to all be washed down with an amazake drink made from fermented wheat and goat fat. Don’t get too attached, however. Riffs owing to finicky ingredient availability occur here almost daily.

Running a restaurant using only ingredients foraged and farmed from within about eight to 10 miles means abiding by pretty intense guardrails. The only wheat used is pima club, an ancient soft white variety cultivated for centuries by the Akimel O’odham (Pima) people. Ursa does not use cucumbers, radishes or citrus.

“We’re not trying to substitute anything, like, ‘How do we achieve cucumber? Let’s grab a cactus,’” Lopez says. “We have to throw everything out, really strip it down. Sometimes we have to let the ingredients be bitter or spicy. We have to put our egos aside and let the ingredient be the star.”

For staff who didn’t grow up or learn to cook here, the learning curve at Ursa can feel a little like alighting on a new planet, even if certain flora happen to resemble prickly weeds in their own backyards.

“We have these really cool moments in the restaurant where people are like, Oh! I have lamb’s quarter just growing in my yard!” Chee says of the spinach-like desert green that appears all over Ursa’s menu.

Google Built a Giant ‘Mirror Dimension’ Internet to Test Its Chrome AI Agents

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As the Google Chrome team has worked to develop AI browsing features using the company's Gemini models, they realized they need to go the extra mile to keep things from going haywire. So they did the only thing they could think to do—they built an expansive alternate-universe internet that they completely control.

“Web-use” agents need to be familiar with the open internet, how to navigate real websites and platforms, and how to be hardened against web attacks. So training them in limited test environments is only so effective. Plus, models can develop what's known as “eval awareness” where they behave differently because they have determined that they are in a test or simulation. On the other hand, setting unrefined agents loose on the open internet is a bad idea. That’s where the alternate-universe internet comes in.

The “Mirror Dimension,” which Google is detailing in a blog post today for the first time, is a sweeping and diverse but isolated web ecosystem that the Chrome team entirely controls. It includes a vast array of websites and platforms—including social media sites, shopping, and bank dashboards—that are built with all the components and attributes of real websites. Browsing assistant agents must interact with the sites and complete tasks in Chrome the way they would on the real internet. And they face resilience tests as they go, like hidden prompt-injection attacks, to test their defenses.

“Agents are not programmed so you can’t predict every possible thing an agent might see, the exploration space that we have to address is huge and the web is hostile,” says Charles Qian, a software engineer on the Chrome AI security team. “Any user can ask any prompt on any website, so we really need to explore that space as thoroughly as we can. And we’ve seen the agent start to develop a mature sense of judgement when it comes to knowing what information is actually needed for a task and when to punt back to the user for a confirmation.”

Building out and populating the Mirror Dimension was a challenge in itself, because it needed to be vast and varied enough to represent the scale of the real web while being developed quickly. To construct it, the Chrome team gathered armies of website and web app development agents guided by a purpose-built AI harness to quickly create realistic web services and data flows—down to details like entering promo codes on a shopping site or populating social media pages.

Even though the entire environment is controlled by Google, Chrome is built with so many user protections and validity checks that in order to allow the agents to use real Chrome (which is crucial for accurate training), the researchers essentially had to hack the browser and take elaborate steps like making forged web certificates for validity checks so the agents could have a normal experience. The team emphasizes, though, that none of these attacks would be possible against regular Chrome users on the open internet. They were all facilitated by the god-mode type access and privilege the team inherently has because they control the entire environment and network.

The researchers say that while the browsing assistant agents they have been testing are benign and do not have cyber capabilities, their goal is to make the Mirror Dimension available to teams across Google so more researchers have access to the safe and controlled environment for testing.

“We’re sharing this approach so other teams both internally and externally think about doing it this way,” says Doug Turner, Google’s director of engineering. “This is the first step, there’s going to be continued investment in this approach to train models. And I would imagine as they become more sophisticated we’ll have to continue working on this environment to make it more and more realistic.”

‘Dig’ Review: Amy Poehler’s Talent-Rich Peacock Comedy Barely Scratches the Surface

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The great benefit of having clout in Hollywood — aside from fame, fortune, and a standing reservation at any restaurant in town — is being able to say “no.” You don’t have to take any job that pays. You don’t even have to work year-round. And you certainly don’t have to chase silly trends, like, say — I don’t know — making a comedy that’s also a mystery that’s also a drama? Beloved, funny folks like Amy Poehler and Mike Schur, who earned a huge chunk of their influence through the indelible sitcom, “Parks and Recreation,” should be able to make anything they want! Any way they want! They earned it!

Far be it from me to know what exactly went wrong with Schur and Poehler’s new series, but something did, unfortunately, go wrong. Each 32-35 minute episode strains to wrap up plots that limp to the finish line. Narrative momentum only exists from the minor twists revealed in the final seconds — then the credits roll, the “up next” button lights up, and your natural curiosity over what happens next wins out over your desire to scroll for something better. Worst of all, everything is “light”: The tone is light, the jokes are light, the mystery is light, the drama is light, even the lighting is light. (It’s so bright, all the time, even at night!) Watching “Dig” is like bearing witness to dwindling interest: Whatever originally compelled the “Dig” team to adapt Kate Myers’ 2023 novel, “Excavations” gets spread so thin across the disparate parts of this broad adaptation that there’s almost nothing left.

Nothing, that is, but the stars. Poehler leads an affable cast (astutely assembled by casting directors Allison Jones and Emily Buntyn) as lead archaeologist Elise, a devoted digger (no, not that kind) whose decades in the dirt have earned her respect and admiration but also turned her cynical and closed-off. She’s an accomplished expert in the field who’s been waiting to run her own site for years, working diligently under the tutelage of beloved British professor Neville Wycombe (Hugh Laurie), but events have conspired to keep her from the top job.

The most obvious issue is also the simplest: Neville has repeatedly promised to retire, only to rescind that promise when it comes time to make it official. This year he swears he’ll step down for real, but Elise knows better than to buy-in, especially when her informal audition (giving the welcome speech to a group of undergrads they call Ugs) exposes her greatest weakness: She’s not an inspiring leader. “I know your default setting is ‘tough love,'” Neville tells Elise, “but please learn to give people some grace.”

That advice is put to the test when Dylan (Geraldine Viswanathan), Elise’s flaky former protégé, shows up at the dig site after abandoning her post five years prior without so much as a goodbye. Returning with a questionable back tattoo that reads “a traveler on the adventure of life” and without any luggage, directions, or direction (as in, for her life), Dylan is a messy 29-year-old trying to sort things out as she turns 30. Luckily, she meets the too-put-together-for-her-own-good Clare (Antonia Thomas), a 39-year-old head preservationist who’s secure in her calling, engaged to Dylan’s ex-boyfriend Gary (Jon Pointing), and always dressed to the nines. (Surprise, surprise: Clare comes from money.)

The two will learn from each other, just as they’ll each learn from Elise, but they should all take more cues from Patty Borkman (Fina Strazza), the 19-year-old intern who’s assigned to be Neville’s assistant. That’s, in part, because Patty is the youngest and eagerist of a central foursome neatly divided by decades (did I mention Elise is 49?), but more so because Patty — as you may have guessed by her last name — is a fizzy vat of quirky energy that Strazza impressively dissipates into a charming, fresh, coming-of-age character.

Patty’s voice sounds like a shy version of Cristin Milioti’s adult baby from “30 Rock.” She wears silly hats, can’t drive, and struggles to stick up for herself. She kinda, sorta believes she’s a demigod (after reading one of Neville’s books on Greek mythology as a kid), and she’s always been an outcast (for these reasons and more). Watching Patty come into her own — in ways both delightfully weird and universally endearing — is the most rewarding and compelling aspect of “Dig”; a strange, distinctive spark that could grow into a roaring fire if the series continues (and learns from its strongest asset).

But that’s a big “if.” The first season (consisting of 10 episodes) pivots around the four women finding a rare statue and making the questionable decision to keep it secret from Neville. At the time, they say it’s because their boss takes credit for everything, and they want something that’s just their own, but that contention doesn’t hold up to scrutiny as the season progresses because Neville doesn’t hold up to scrutiny as the season progresses. Neville is whoever he needs to be in any given episode — a mentor, an oppressor, a goofball, a stick in the mud, a nice old man, and a villainous monster — and while Laurie can pivot with the best of them, his inconsistent character development betrays a show that’s hesitant to commit to much of anything.

The love triangle between Gary, Dylan, and Clare fizzles out without any substantial conflict. Elsewhere, an inappropriate crush is introduced and tossed away without consequence. Great guest stars like Jason Mantzoukas (a local looter) and Max Greenfield (as a sketchy art dealer) feel like they were hired for arcs that ended up getting cut, while regulars (like Gary) can feel adrift in their ample, listless screentime. Premises primed for comic hijinks never get there, mysteries teed up for suspense are left slack, and relationships that should create a little friction when brought together instead find a way to slide off each other without anything sticking.

Poehler, at the center of it all, is a problem. While it’s theoretically interesting to see the former Leslie Knope take the opposite tact — playing another hard-working public servant (every artifact they uncover remains in Greece) only without the unbridled optimism — Elise’s glum demeanor limits her ability to crack jokes, stir passion, and generally light up a scene. Patty is the only outsized character in a bunch of overly composed normies, and while everyone gets a moment or two to strut their silly stuff, it’s not enough in a series where the humor is already spread so thin.

“Dig” has all the talent it needs to be whatever show it wants to be. The most obvious option would be a comedy, but I have faith in the cast and creatives to excel in other genres, too. They just need to pick one and, when they do, dig a whole lot deeper than this.

Film Independent’s New President Wants to Build an L.A. Powerhouse — and Shake Up the Spirit Awards

Major change is afoot at Film Independent, the Los Angeles nonprofit that supports indie filmmakers. Not merely a restructuring of departments over the coming years or a revamping of its centerpiece offering, the four-decades-old Spirit Awards, but something more expansive, place-based, and integrated.Since the tragic passing of Josh Welsh, a beloved figure in the indie community who served as Film Independent’s president for 12 years, many felt a leadership vacuum at a time when the L.A. film industry was in severe crisis, from productions rapidly departing the city to studio mergers.

Juan Devis, appointed the new president in April, now envisions Film Independent to become something akin to a nonprofit studio, and equally, a pivotal cultural institution in Los Angeles, to be considered in the same breath as LACMA, the guilds, or the Academy Museum. A day after announcing John Early and Kate Berlant as the new hosts of the Spirit Awards, Devis, in his first public interview since his appointment, spoke to IndieWire about his all-encompassing vision and gave exclusives on the latest promotions following a significant restructuring.Devis’ office sits in the same corner of Film Independent’s headquarters in Miracle Mile as Welsh’s, and though it shares a similar calm, shaped by majestic 270-degree, ninth-story views of the Hollywood Hills to South L.A., Devis has made it bespoke with mementos from his childhood. Gleefully showing me miniature slides of scenes he recreated from the franchise’s toys he owned or borrowed, Devis reminisced about how, as an eight-year-old, he’d direct his family to reenact the movie from a script he recorded on a vintage audio cassette. He cast his father as Darth Vader, his sister as Princess Leia (before the revelations of “The Empire Strikes Back”), and himself, of course, as Lucas Skywalker.This obsession with film would lead Devis to major in it at Emerson College in Boston, return to Colombia to shoot his thesis, and find himself working in Los Angeles for New Regency on the Warner Bros. lot, driving up and down Barham Blvd and taking meetings at the Peninsula. He didn’t connect with the glamour of it all, and was eager to reconcile his “indie kid” notion of film with what he was seeing in L.A. In the early 9’0s, that meant the race uprising, the Northridge earthquake, O.J. Simpson, and studios eager to replicate the success of Robert Rodriguez’s “El Mariachi.”

Devis would spend 15 years at PBS, connecting, in his words, “storytelling with placemaking” and pioneering interactive documentaries, while serving on the boards of nonprofits like Arts for LA and as a California Arts Council member. When he got the phone call about his potential interest in becoming president of Film Independent, he saw a deeply “civic” role for storytelling that Devis felt was missing in the turbulent media landscape.Prior to accepting, Devis did his homework. He spoke to board members whom he is friends with, looked at their social media, and asked about the branding and positioning of the Spirit Awards (which he has never attended) vis-à-vis all the other “amazing programs supporting independent filmmaking both locally and globally.”Devis said he ran a collaborative workshop with staff leadership, asking them to break down everything they do. Film Independent does everything from development, funding, and production to distribution, education, and celebration, but these activities weren’t quite talking to each other.

For instance, the organization provides more than two dozen grants and donor-named fellowships across many scopes and areas. The #FiPresents folks offer an array of programming, including live reads such as the recent “Secretary” led by Jane Schoenbrun, suggesting the org’s expertise in exhibition. Numerous labs, from the Screenwriting, Fiction Producing, and Episodic Labs to robust international initiatives such as Global Media Makers, reach far to find underrepresented artists, taking them through development and mentorship. Fast Track is a four-day finance market that connects the film teams to funders. And the annual Forum and the triennial Sloan Summit convene folks from all aspects of the industry, making them key events on the nonprofit’s calendar.“I realized that Film Independent actually supports the entire life cycle of independent makers,” said Devis, “but they don’t see their programs and activities in that way. [Everything] is very siloed. So I started to really litigate that. For example, the [Sundance 2026 feature] ‘Union County’ found two-thirds of its funding through Fast Track. Project Involve’s shorts have turned into features and shows. Yet we don’t see ourselves as holding value in that regard. We simply create the conditions for these [films] to happen, and they go off on their own.”His solution? “If we are able to accordion out these [activities], to start to look at a vision of an interconnected organization that is supporting the entire life cycle, to look at how we can actually serve the filmmaker intentionally in each of these areas, then we are creating a different proposition. That is one of the main shifts we are doing right now. It’s looking at ourselves much more as a potential nonprofit studio.”Becoming something akin to a nonprofit studio is a huge cultural and institutional shift, so surely there must be resistance, since the staff has been functioning efficiently in their silos. Devis suggested that, generally, everyone is on board. “Staff needs to understand that we need to operate in a different way. As I talk to independent filmmakers, they [already] need the support in these areas, and they’re not getting it. It is a shift away from the [individual] lab mentality to the [full] life cycle. It is a shift in the culture of how we see the value we provide to the filmmakers, to our partners, and to the ecosystem in general.”The Humana Foundation grant is a pilot program to operationalize this shift. Five shorts will be selected from a submissions call, and each will receive $100,000 to develop stories about seniors’ contributions to society. Devis said, “We could have easily said, ‘Why don’t we create a lab to develop intergenerational stories about aging?’” But the goal is for Film Independent “to produce these shorts and other films down the line, identify director-producer teams, develop the IP, and figure out how to distribute it.” That’s how Devis positions the proposal to funders.In the competitive landscape of similar organizations like Sundance, the American Film Institute, or Gotham Institute, Devis argues Film Independent is singular in its goal of becoming like a nonprofit studio. Maybe ITVS, he suggests, but that is tied to documentary making and distribution via PBS. “Our landscape is completely open.”In this regard, IndieWire can exclusively reveal a series of promotions resulting from the three-to-five year restructuring strategy Devis has initiated.Kate Walker D’Angelo, previously senior director of Development, has been upped to executive vice president of Strategy and Institutional Advancement, which Devis said is a crucial top-level position, in part because of all the work Film Independent is doing with the government right now, and how it will continue to collaborate on the OTIS College Report on the Creative Economy with the goal of ultimately influencing policy. They already host labs for emerging filmmakers, which for many is around age 27 or 28. “We are helping these filmmakers enter the ecosystem. So it doesn’t make sense to me to separate our youth lab programs from what we used to call artist development.”One implication of this shift is a greater emphasis on curation. “Can we identify projects that are ready to be moved to other areas, those which need packaging, additional funding?” Devis asked rhetorically. To this end, another major move will combine what were previously siloed as “artist development” programs and labs, “international” programs, “education” initiatives, and “youth” programs into one department headed by Angela Lee, who has been promoted to vice president of Creative Development and Production.This is a remarkable integration, given how unique (and relatively less publicized) Film Independent’s international programs have been. Initiatives like the Global Media Makers (GMM) Residency are funded by a State Department grant (e.g., the 2026 Residency, which concluded in October, invited nine filmmakers from Malaysia, plus folks from other Asian countries). Devis acknowledged that “we are very lucky to have this grant. It is very unusual. But those things are very volatile, right? I have to think about the sustainability of these programs. I have been in the nonprofit sector for a very long time. The worry is when you rely on one source to keep you going.”

The big question: Where do the Spirit Awards fit into this grand reconfiguration?Devis is very excited and optimistic about this prospect, since for him, the annual event, which will enter its 41st year in 2027, is “the best place to tell our story. I don’t want to separate the Spirit Awards from Film Independent. They are the head of the sphere of so many things we do, and they should be a reflection of our values, programs, intentions, and aspirations.”“And they should not be just an awards show.”In their first move from the legendary beach tent in Santa Monica to the Hollywood Palladium about a mile away from where the Oscars are held, the 2026 Spirit Awards show garnered tepid to more harshly critical reviews, not just because of the venue. Devis is aware of the reception, even though he hadn’t yet been appointed president. The problem, he said, is that the Spirit Awards ended up being “just another stop” on the “Lazy Susan” that is the “exhausting” awards circuit, losing everything that made them unique.“I think we need to return to when [the Spirit Awards] were a party, a true celebration. This involves a healthy dose of disruption. I actually think we shouldn’t fight the fact that we’re at the Palladium. We’re in the heart of Hollywood. The film and TV business in L.A. and in California has taken a very big hit. We need to celebrate our resilience in the place it all began. Everybody was very sad we moved from the beach and that people could bike to get there. Luckily enough, I never went to any of those and don’t have that reference. But I can imagine what this party could be at the Palladium.”Here, Devis spoke with the pride of being an Angeleno of more than three decades. “One of the first things is to embrace the idea of LA. I truly believe independent films are small businesses. We [the Spirit Awards] are the small business of the ecosystem, right? We’re in the neighborhood where the cool restaurant just opened up, with the bookstore, the bodega, and the Armenian-owned [enterprise]. That’s job creation. That’s entrepreneurship. That’s community building.”Devis has already begun implementing this vision for the Film Independent Spirit Awards. “One of the first things I did when I got the job was to call the city and say we want to close Sunset Boulevard. What made the Spirit Awards shine is the opposite,” bringing up the example of the 2020 segment featuring Gay Men’s Choir of Los Angeles from Jordan Firstman that ended in an obsessed crescendo to queer icon Laura Dern.

Earlier this summer, fresh off the news of Film Independent’s biggest grant ever — $2.55 million from the Perenchio Foundation — the organization announced two new nomination categories: Best International First Feature, which Devis said reflects his commitment to capture the immense breadth of international cinema, and a new Digital Creator Award as a part of its Emerging Filmmaker Grants. “New formats and platforms extend the definition of “independent,” he said, claiming that the sensation of “Obsession” was just coincidental to the announcement of this award.Moreover, the Berlin Film Festival was added to the list of qualifying festivals, and the Robert Redford Environmental Vision Award was announced in June to recognize a narrative film or TV work engaging with environmental issues in an innovative way. Here, Devis thumped his desk with his knuckles, speaking with excitement and pride.“I want Film Independent to become a really important cultural institution for the city. Going back to this idea of small businesses, creatives, and the economy it generates, [we need to] celebrate who we are. MOCA celebrates contemporary artists. LACMA has a potpourri of things. Dig review: Amy Poehler and Parks and Recs Mike Schur have done it again

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The first time Amy Poehler and Mike Schur teamed up for a series about Poehler rolling up her sleeves and getting to work in a massive dirt pit, they gave us Parks and Recreation, a charming workplace sitcom about the power of friendship and doing what you love. The second time Amy Poehler and Mike Schur teamed up for a series about Poehler rolling up her sleeves and getting to work in a massive dirt pit, they gave us Peacock's new series Dig, which is — you guessed it! — a charming workplace sitcom about the power of friendship and doing what you love.

Story-wise, the two shows are miles apart. Literally. The former takes place in the decidedly unglamorous town of Pawnee, Indiana, while the latter, based on Kate Myers' novel Excavations, transports us to a sun-bleached archaeological dig in Greece. Yet despite the flashy location and a new mystery twist, Dig retains the core formula that made Parks and Recreation so irresistible: investing deeply in a lovable new crew of unlikely friends.

Kicking off that group is Elise (Poehler), the lead archaeologist on a long-running dig in southern Greece. She's qualified and passionate, yet has a hard time connecting with the undergraduates (or UGs) participating in the dig. Because of her more guarded exterior, she's consistently second fiddle to longtime dig leader Neville (Hugh Laurie). Perpetually on the brink of retirement, Neville seems unable to cede control of the dig to Elise, both because of his love for archaeology and the ego boost his role gives him. He's the Lucy to Elise's Charlie Brown, constantly yanking the football away at the last second.

So, when Elise discovers a perfectly preserved statue of Hera at an older dig site, the last thing she wants to do is tell Neville and give him an opportunity to claim any shred of credit. However, hiding any cultural artifacts from Neville and the Grecian government at large is illegal, so Elise must assemble a crack team to help her cover up her covert dig of the Hera statue, whom they nickname "Pretty Woman." First up is Dylan (Geraldine Viswanathan), an always flaky wild card who has unfinished business at the dig. Joining her are Clare (Antonia Thomas), a prim and proper conservator born with a silver spoon in her mouth, and Patty (Fina Strazza), a sheltered farm girl with a deep passion for Greek mythology.

Like with most sitcoms, the characters begin as the broadest versions of themselves before revealing their deeper nuances. At first, Elise is prickly to a fault, allowing Poehler to sidestep any preconceived comparisons you might want to draw to Leslie Knope. Elsewhere, Dylan is the hottest of hot messes, Clare is a haughty queen bee, and Patty is an out-of-her-depth klutz. They border on parodies instead of full-bodied characters, making Dig's earlier episodes a bit lukewarm.

But as Dig's title suggests, this is a show that won't be satisfied by sticking at surface level. As the show delves into what makes its characters tick, it becomes a heartwarming tale of found family, not unlike many other beloved Schur shows. Suddenly, character traits that felt one-note to start are revealed to be defense mechanisms or misconceptions, adding entirely new dimensions to Dig's core four.

Dig works best when that core works together in earnest, which is another reason why the show's early entries feel like slower burns: They're doing the groundwork to loop every woman into the Pretty Woman dig. Once they're all on the same page, Dig gets off and running — even more so once Pretty Woman mysteriously vanishes, leaving Elise and her motley crew to do some boots-on-the-ground detective work. Thus begins a madcap caper involving stakeouts, undercover identities, and the black market.

Along the way, Elise, Dylan, Clare, and Patty are allowed to be wonderfully messy and vulnerable, all while staying refreshingly supportive of one another. For example, a love triangle burbles up between Clare, Dylan, and Clare's fiancé/Dylan's ex-boyfriend Gary (Jon Pointing), yet the storyline never devolves into cat fights or possessiveness. Instead, Dig focuses more on what these relationships mean to Clare and Dylan beyond romance. For Clare, her engagement is a sign of stability and true adulthood, whereas Dylan is looking for closure, something she notoriously can never get.

Dig also dabbles in intergenerational conflict, highlighting the differences between each of its leads, all of whom are at very different stages in their lives. Again, Dig doesn't take a hackneyed "Gen Z versus millennials versus Gen X" route. It's more interested in showing the different wants that motivate us. Patty yearns for belonging and friendship, while Elise wants to cement her legacy and make sure she's done something big enough to call her own, no Neville required.

Once Dig's ensemble truly comes together, they mesh wonderfully. Poehler brings a weary exasperation to Elise that slowly gives way to warm leadership, a transformation that's a joy to witness. Thomas and Viswanathan are perfect foils, the former a picture of poise, the latter completely uninhibited. Watching them feed off each other offers up a perfect comedic contrast. Finally, there's Strazza, whose endearingly goofy Patty becomes the beating heart of the show. Laurie, too, is a joy, blending Neville's smarminess with a genial charm that makes him infuriatingly tough to hate.

In focusing on archaeology, Dig emphasizes the importance of excavating ancient objects and interrogating the stories of those who once used them. In many ways, Dig is doing the same thing with its characters: peeling back their layers to get at their core truths. Only instead of using trowels and brushes, Dig uses the power of the workplace sitcom. It's a power Schur and his collaborators know well, and the result is a show that will leave you feeling like you've just uncovered something great in a dig of your own — with far less dirt on your face and clothes, that is.

Everful is on track to reach $100 million in annualized revenue by the end of the year, about two and a half years after its launch, co-founder Sisi Song said. The company ships over 5 million items a month to customers in more than 60 countries and works with over 30,000 factory partners, she added. Its online wholesale catalog includes more than 20 million products.

Song, a former Alibaba executive, and serial entrepreneur Kevin Han, who sold a previous company to Amazon, co-founded Everful in 2024. The company began as a jewelry wholesale business before evolving into a multi-category, factory-direct sourcing platform.

Retailers and brands often rely on intermediaries, face high minimum order requirements, and experience long sourcing cycles, which tie up capital in inventory before sales trends are clear. Song noticed the growing gap between fast-changing consumer trends and traditional wholesale sourcing models while working as a venture investor at Bessemer Venture Partners, where she mostly focused on cross-border sourcing and meeting numerous startups in the sector. That same gap shaped Everful’s goal to simplify buying directly from factories through an AI-enabled platform.

The B2B sourcing industry is highly competitive. Big tech giant Alibaba connects international buyers with manufacturers and suppliers, while Faire, a San Francisco-based wholesale tech platform, enables retailers to purchase wholesale inventory directly from brands. According to Everful’s co-founders, its key differentiator is responsibility for the sourcing process, rather than simply facilitating transactions.

“We take care of everything,” Han said. “We handle sourcing, order consolidation, quality control, and logistics. That’s something the marketplaces don’t offer.”

Everful is built for smaller retailers and brands without dedicated sourcing teams, Song said, and it also verifies the factories it works with. While peers also offer services such as quality inspection, logistics, and AI-powered sourcing, Everful aims to integrate these capabilities into a more streamlined purchasing experience, creating an end-to-end process that reduces the work retailers must handle themselves.

Han added that Everful uses AI agents to automate labor-intensive sourcing tasks, including tracking consumer trends, identifying suppliers, coordinating orders across factories, and managing logistics.

Because Everful sources from Chinese factories, ongoing U.S.-China trade tensions and tariffs bear directly on its business. Song said the co-founders have been thinking about geopolitical issues since day one of building the company, and that this has shaped Everful’s strategy from the outset, leveraging her experience at Alibaba and Bessemer Venture Partners.

She mentioned the Trump administration’s suspension of the de minimis exemption, which had allowed eligible shipments valued at $800 or less to enter the U.S. duty-free, as one example of a shift in trade policy.

Song explained such changes could favor a model that combines overseas sourcing with local retail sales. “We’re empowering local retailers and brands,” she said. “Long-term cross-border commerce will be cross-border B2B sourcing plus local selling.”

Everful plans to use the Series A round to expand its presence in the U.S. and Europe, particularly the U.K., France, and Italy, while investing in AI to automate product customization and other sourcing workflows. It also aims to deepen integrations with e-commerce platforms, building on its Shopify integration as it works toward becoming an end-to-end infrastructure platform for global sourcing.

The startup employs about 70 full-time staff globally, including 45 in research and development. It has also established an AI operations team to connect engineering and business functions.

As Power Demand Surges, Energy Is Moving Closer To The Edge

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Darren H. S. Tan. Chief Executive Officer at UNIGRID.

​Over the summer, Europe suffered a heat wave that strained its power grids, simultaneously increasing electricity demand and forcing some power plants to shut down.​

This isn’t the first time extreme temperatures have impacted the grid’s ability to deliver power. Many places around the world have experienced record-high or record-low temperatures in recent years, and the demand for heating or cooling reached levels that existing energy infrastructure wasn’t designed to handle. ​

The problem is being made worse by the construction of hyperscale AI data centers, which require massive amounts of electricity and further strain the grid. Completely rebuilding the grid to meet modern demands has proven fiscally impractical for many governments, forcing many places to solve the problem on the local level using distributed generation and storage.

Energy infrastructure across the U.S. and Europe was built over a century ago, when energy needs were much different. ​

Even so, studies have shown that we use only about 40% to 55% of the grid on average, and that’s by design, so it can handle spikes when they occur. But recent extreme weather events and data center demand are causing power needs to exceed even that generous buffer.​

For example, the Electric Reliability Council of Texas (ERCOT), which manages electricity in Texas, has said its interconnection queue currently includes over 1,800 projects totaling 474 gigawatts of power, over five times the state’s record peak electricity demand. Ninety percent of those requests come from data centers. ​

The cost to completely rebuild the grid to meet today’s needs is astronomical, with some estimates putting the total replacement cost in the trillions. Because of the potential cost and the fact that the aging grid is a chronic rather than an urgent problem, it receives patchwork upgrades as needed and as government budgets allow. ​

With a full rebuild out of the picture, several strategies are in play to work within the system we already have. When demand approaches available supply, energy companies offer rebates for reducing usage at peak times, implement peak pricing or, in more extreme cases, resort to rolling blackouts, which are planned outages to alleviate the demand during peak periods. ​

Beyond the grid itself, some areas are reducing their dependence on the centralized infrastructure by moving toward local power generation and storage.

Battery energy storage systems (BESS) can store electricity and discharge it when demand exceeds the grid’s capacity. ​

They are already showing promise in several states, such as California, where battery storage capacity has reached over 21,000 MW, equal to about one-third of the state’s peak demand. Eighty percent of that capacity is represented by grid-connected batteries, but smaller batteries are also enabling residents to increase their own energy storage capacity at home.​

As these batteries become cheaper and more reliable, it’s not out of the realm of possibility that these energy storage systems become a standard household appliance in the future, much like a refrigerator or washing machine is today. They can also play a role in energy needs for small businesses.

Being able to store energy and use it in an emergency is only half the equation. The other half is being able to generate electricity independently of the grid. ​

This isn’t a concept that should be unfamiliar to many, as many organizations and homes already have backup generators or solar panels. Although these methods can keep small businesses and homes powered up, they have their disadvantages. Generators are noisy, pollutive and can be dangerous, while solar has significant upfront installation costs. ​

Plug-in solar is emerging as another option for generating electricity locally that has already seen success in Europe and is starting to gain support in the U.S. as well. Instead of being installed on a rooftop, these smaller panels can be mounted on a balcony or a small section of a yard or business property and plugged into a wall outlet to help power homes and small businesses. ​

These systems cost significantly less than traditional solar and are portable, so people can move them around wherever they’re needed. These benefits open up power generation to a whole new segment of the country: commercial and residential renters alike who might never invest in a hardwired battery system or solar installation.

As energy demand continues to increase, expanding traditional grid infrastructure will remain important, but those projects can be expensive and take years to complete. Distributed generation and storage offer another way to increase resilience in the meantime. ​

By generating and storing more electricity closer to where it will be consumed, communities and business leaders can help reduce their reliance on the centralized grid during peak demand.​

We’re unlikely to solve growing power constraints with any single technology, but as local generation and storage become cheaper and more accessible, they can play a larger role in managing peak demand and making better use of the grid infrastructure we already have.​

How to Do the Walking Lunge for Lower Body Gains

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Build stronger quads, hamstrings, and glutes one step at a time.

YOUR LEGS ARE built for movement, whether you’re running, walking, or jumping. So why do most go-to leg exercises have you planting your feet in one spot?

Enter the walking lunge, a truly dynamic lower-body exercise that’s a perfect complement to exercises like heavy squats and Romanian deadlifts. (A bonus: It’s a unilateral exercise that isn’t the dreaded Bulgarian split squat.) The walking lunge is one of the few strength moves that truly gets you moving. And in doing so, it exposes muscle imbalances, trains balance and coordination, and challenges your core and upper body more than you might expect—all while attacking every muscle in your legs.

Walking lunges might appear deceptively simple at first, especially if you have done standard reverse lunges before. And in truth, they can be simple. Here, Men’s Health fitness director Ebenezer Samuel, C.S.C.S. walks you through the steps to take so that you’re not dragging your feet but making gains toward powerful legs.

FORM IS EVERYTHING with this move. You’ll want to stand with your feet shoulder-width apart, squeeze your glutes and shoulder blades while keeping your gaze at a neutral spot straight ahead of you.

Once you’re comfortable with this slow and controlled movement, you can add weights to build extra muscle and strength. You can hold them at your sides, hold them by your shoulders in what’s called a front rack position, or grab one heavy dumbbell and hold it to your chest in a goblet position. “Regardless of how you hold the load, make sure to keep your upper-body tight,” says Samuel. “Grip the dumbbells like you mean it, squeeze your shoulder blades, and tighten your abs, too.”

Don’t obsess over keeping your torso too upright, though, says Samuel. “Especially as you work with heavier loads, don’t be afraid to lean forward slightly,” he says. “That can help you capitalize on your glutes, one of your strongest muscle groups, with each stride.”

When you’re starting out, says Samuel, aim to take a step that keeps your front shin perpendicular to the ground when you lower your torso into the lunge. But as you grow more experienced, you can play with the length of your stride to chase different goals. “If you take very small steps forward,” says Samuel, “you’ll attack your quads more. Larger steps can help you build hip mobility and knee health. If you take larger steps, go lighter with the load.”

“The greatest benefits of the walking lunge come from how it challenges us while in motion,” says Samuel. “In addition to building strength and muscle, every step forward challenges your front leg (primarily tibailis and quad muscles) to decelerate your body in space, and then quickly work to accelerate into the next step. That’s building coordination and balance, in addition to training almost every lower-body muscle and building serious strength.”

You’ll work a number of major leg muscles when you do walking lunges, including your calves, hamstrings, quads, and glutes.

SMALL MISTAKES IN your walking lunge technique can turn the lower body exercise into a potential knee-wrecking disaster.

You want to land on the heel of your foot first to provide a strong base for the rest of your lunge position. When you get started with walking lunges, aim for a vertical shin as you lower down to the ground to stay at a 90-degree angle in your front and back legs.

You also don’t want to think of taking a step forward, but stepping out a couple inches by slightly turning your glute. Another adjustment to protect your knee is to shift your knee slightly outward instead of toward the midline. (If you still feel any knee pain during lunges, try a reverse lunge instead).

Keep your torso straight and avoid dipping forward, which you may be more inclined to do if you’re carrying heavy weights for this exercise. Your chest should be aligned with the back of your thigh the entire time. As you get up from the lunge, you want to avoid using momentum. That means no shoulder or torso rocking.

THE WALKING LUNGE is a great way to sneak in some movement on leg day if most of your lower-body exercises have both feet anchored to the ground. Load up some weights, and try to aim for 3 sets of 8 to 10 reps per leg, 1 to 2 times a week. You can alternate each leg or finish up all your reps on one leg before switching to the other. Samuel says that will keep more tension and gives you an opportunity to really focus on the muscle.

An advantage of this move is its versatility, allowing you to do it anytime, anywhere. If you’re crunched for time or in a hotel room, try bodyweight walking lunges. Do it for 45 seconds with a 15 second rest for 5 minutes to break a sweat and build muscle.
