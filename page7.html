Ways you can help your video doorbell stay charged in the winter

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Winter can be tough on battery-powered video doorbells, but a few adjustments may help yours cope when temperatures drop.

While video doorbells aren't a necessity per se, it can be hard to imagine living without them once you own one. Knowing who's at the door saves you run-ins with unwanted salesmen. More seriously, they may help deter burglars — or help you catch an offender in the act if deterrence fails. Of course, your ability to capture that activity will depend in part on the distance at which your video doorbell stops detecting motion.

Another catch is that they're perpetually outdoors. That might not be such a big deal if you live somewhere warm like Miami or San Antonio. But in places with prolonged cold weather, battery life can suffer to the point that a video doorbell becomes more of a burden than an aid. It's inherent to the chemistry of lithium-ion technology, unfortunately.

The main way to alleviate this issue is by bringing your doorbell or its battery in from the cold. There may be other tactics you can employ, though, such as strategic positioning.

The critical point here is that an ideal charging range for lithium-ion batteries is between 50 and 86 degrees Fahrenheit (10 to 30 Celsius). When charging below the freezing point, there's a risk that metallic lithium will start plating onto electrodes, permanently reducing capacity. And even if the weather is slightly above freezing, charging will be less efficient than it could be. Cold-weather performance is one of the differences between sodium and lithium-ion batteries.

Before you charge a doorbell battery, make sure to let it warm up to room temperature first. This should not only preserve capacity but also help avoid cold-related charging delays. That's significant, considering that a Ring battery can take as long as 10 hours to top up. Let the battery charge fully before putting it back outside to maximize the time between recharges. If your doorbell has a removable battery, keeping a charged spare will minimize downtime. Some models in Ring's updated battery-powered doorbell lineup feature quick-release battery packs to make swaps easier.

Some doorbells don't offer removable batteries, however. One example is the battery-powered Google Nest Doorbell. In that case, you'll need to become familiar with removing your doorbell and bringing it indoors. Be sure to time this properly. Since a recharge might take several hours, it's probably wise to charge overnight unless you're worried about security and don't have any other cameras covering the front of your home.

In climates with borderline winters — that is, where the temperature only occasionally dips below freezing — you may be able to extend battery life by aiming or sheltering your doorbell in just the right way. If you can block the wind, for example, or maximize sunlight, your doorbell will naturally run warmer than it might otherwise.

A vital point here is that in extreme cold, it's possible for a doorbell to stop functioning for the duration. Anker, Ring, Google and others all agree on a minimum operating temperature of -4 degrees Fahrenheit (-20 Celsius). Between that and the freezing point, devices may still refuse to charge in order to prevent the plating we mentioned earlier.

Honestly, then, the best choice if you live in a frostbitten region may be to switch to a wired doorbell, as long as you can find one compatible with your home's design. While wired doorbells may still encounter problems around the -4 degrees Fahrenheit mark, they're otherwise immune to the cold, since they draw on the same electricity as the rest of your house.

Dollar Tree’s stock is skyrocketing. One number in its earnings report helps explain why

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The broader markets are seeing red today, primarily led lower by yet another steep selloff in technology and artificial intelligence stocks. But one company nearly as far from those sectors as you can get is seeing its share price surge by over 10% today: the discount retailer Dollar Tree. Here’s what you need to know.

What’s happened?

This morning, the discount retail chain Dollar Tree, Inc. (Nasdaq: DLTR) reported its third-quarter fiscal 2026 results, which cover the three-month period ending October 31. The most salient metrics from those results are as follows:

  • Net sales: $5.1 billion
  • Adjusted earnings per share (EPS): $1.16
  • Comparable store net sales: up 4.6%
  • Foot traffic increase: 3.8%
  • New stores: 84
  • Total stores: 9,511

To put those financials in perspective, LSEG analysts estimated $5.04 billion in net sales and earnings per share of 91 cents, notes Reuters. 

Announcing the company’s Q3 results, Dollar Tree CEO Mike Creedon said, “Our third quarter results show the strength of Dollar Tree’s value proposition and the progress we’ve made to improve the customer experience. Comp traffic accelerated to 3.8% and drove comparable sales above the high end of our outlook. Our broader assortment, better stores and stronger customer engagement give shoppers more reasons to choose Dollar Tree and come back more often.”

Why are Dollar Tree’s shares surging?

After announcing its Q3, DLTR stock surged—one of the few to do so today on Wall Street.

Dollar Tree’s price jump comes down to a few factors, both of which seem to have pleased investors, based on the company’s share price.

The first was the net sales and earnings per share beat. Net sales increased by 6.9% versus the same quarter a year earlier. This was helped by a 4.6% increase in comparable net store sales. Those comps were driven by a 3.8% increase in foot traffic and a 0.8% increase in the amount people spend on an average ticket.

In other words, most of the comp increase was not driven by higher tickets, but by increased foot traffic—which is exactly what you want as you enter the final quarter of the year: the all-important holiday period.

In addition to favorable quarterly results, another factor that buoyed investors’ view of the company was its full-year fiscal 2026 outlook. 

Dollar Tree increased its full-year earnings per share range to between $8.05 and $8.25. That was up from its prior range of $7.70 to $8.05.

DLTR’s up more than 60% in six months

As of the time of this writing, DLTR shares are currently trading up 11% to $154.41 on the back of its Q3 results. That’s an exceptional one-day jump for a discount retailer, but it goes to show the faith investors currently have in the company, which may be one of the few to benefit from an inflation-wary consumer.

As prices rise, people tend to cut back on discretionary spending, which can cause downward pressure on retailer shares. But as everyday costs get higher, consumers also tend to shop around for the best price for essentials and everyday items.

Dollar Tree has a low-cost pricing structure (many items cost around $1.25, with higher-priced items selling for between $3 and $7) that appeals to cash-strapped consumers operating in an economy where sticker prices at higher-end retailers seem increasingly out of reach.

While the company’s roughly 11% share price jump today (as of the time of this writing) is impressive, looking back a little further reveals even better stock price growth. In the past month alone, DLTR shares are up 22%, and over the past six months, DLTR shares are up 61%.

Vampire Therapist review - “The problems of unlife aren't so black and white.”

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

For over a century now, the vampire has fascinated humanity. From books to films, television and yes, even video games, the enigma of an undead bloodsucker that nonetheless has the wisdom of the ages is a contradiction we all seek to understand. Vampire Therapist isn’t concerned with tragic backstories or merciless sadism but with how to move past them.

In Vampire Therapist, you play undead cowboy Samuel Walls, who is invited to Germany by the three-thousand-year-old Andromachos. For some devious scheme? No, but to help other people. Vampires, that is. Because, as Samuel Walls, you are the one and only Vampire Therapist. And God help you, because these bloodsuckers have some problems.

How can I help you tonight? Vampire Therapist is a visual novel with puzzle elements where, as Walls, your job is to - surprise, surprise - provide psychological aid to your undead kin. To do so, you listen to their problems and aim to catch them in mental contradictions which hold them back from improving or seeing reality.

It can seem a little gamified, but there is definitely a fascinating deductive element to catching your client doing things like Labeling or Disqualifying the Positive, all of which seem to be real therapy terms, but which are explained in a digestible format. The focus is not on catching out your clients, but on taking advantage of these contradictions to show them where they can manage their problems.

You’ll also get to make occasional decisions on what to say or do, which can shift the story in different directions. The focus is mainly on the psychological element, and that in itself is enough to carry the whole game. In fact, I was a little miffed when it got interrupted by a blood-drinking minigame, not least because it was a little janky in the build which I played.

Whenever you have an issue, you can look back at your notes to study them for references to your client’s case files, as well as Sam’s notes that point to the issues they have. You can also look up definitions of the psychological contradictions, including some handy examples. Admittedly, I missed these on my playthrough, but they are there if you’re the journal-reading type, and Andromachos will be on hand to guide you through initial sessions anyway. The Looks and Sound of Death In visual terms, Vampire Therapist is your typical visual novel. You have character sprites with occasional shifts in pose and expression, while vivid backgrounds show which scene you’re in. The art style is a decent middle ground between realistic and stylised, meaning even the more stereotypical vampires, such as the half-century-old Renaissance aristocrat, aren’t boiled down to simple caricatures.

The music in Vampire Therapist is pretty simple, consisting of the main theme and occasional background noises. I did find the track that plays in the vampire club, which your patron Andromachos uses as his base of operations, a little repetitive, which can be an issue since you revisit it pretty frequently.

The voice acting, however, is undoubtedly a standout. Naturally, Matthew Mercer provides his vocal talent as incidental character Reinhard, and also appears later on in the full game. But Cyrus Nemati is a standout as Sam Walls, who provides cosy mix of stereotypical gravely cowboy and surprisingly self-aware, world-wise wanderer. What’s the story, morning glory? The actual narrative of Vampire Therapist is pretty engaging, and what we find out about Sam Walls, Andromachos and your clients such as Doctor Drayne (yes, really) and Isabella d’Este paints a fascinating picture of vampiric history. There are definitely shades of Underworld and Vampire: The Masquerade, but Vampire Therapist stays on the lighter side of things.

The motivations and backstory for your clients also manage to quite elegantly marry typical vampiric tropes with more realistic psychological conditions. Doctor Drayne struggles with addiction, only it’s to an experimental plasma he’s been making at his pharmaceutical company as part of a synthetic blood substitute.

The Contessa d’Este, meanwhile, is a literal Renaissance woman who struggles with depression and anger at the world, because after funding the great arts in her heyday, seeing Space Wars: Rise of Spacewalker (no prize for what that’s parodying) made her think all her efforts to enlighten humanity were for nought.

About the only issue I had is that Vampire Therapist, for what I played at least, seems to sidestep some more pressing issues and leaves a lot of questions unanswered about character motivations. Drayne, for example, is mostly concerned that his addiction to the experimental plasma is affecting his workaholic lifestyle, but the fact he has no memory of what happens when he consumes the substance isn’t given any attention at all.

Admittedly, it’d be a bit awkward to delve into Andromachos’ backstory as the Terror of the Mediterranean, and it might well be something brought up in latter parts of the story. Still, it can feel as though there are some elements that should be explored further, especially as we do get a lot of detail about Sam Walls’ path to redemption even in the first part of Vampire Therapist. What’s been biting at you this evening? Vampire Therapist doesn’t have much in the way of issues, as I see it. It’s a solid visual novel with an original premise, some intriguing puzzle gameplay and a genuinely fascinating, intriguing approach to explaining concepts of mental health and therapy that I found quite informative.

The only problem is that it does presume a lot about the player and how they want Sam Walls to act. Especially considering one early section has Walls nibbling (consensually) on some goth necks in a very sensually implied manner. It’s not as though you’re not warned that it delves into mature topics, but if you’re like me and couldn’t stand the romance in something like Baldur’s Gate 3 it can still feel a bit awkward.

But those are small potatoes, and my earlier mention of stuff I think the story could have done better is only evidence of how much I was paying attention to, and absorbing, the world and its characters, which I think is the highest praise you can give anything narrative-based. It’s lightweight, narrow in scale and perfect for relaxing while offering something of a challenge as you learn more about your clients and try to help them ground themselves and, if not resolve, then at least manage their problems. Let’s put a stake in this What I really liked about Vampire Therapist was how it connected the issues faced by immortals to real-world mental problems without seeming hokey. It’s not just that the Contessa is feeling depressed; it’s that seeing the world go from the Mona Lisa to Rise of Skywalker has seemingly given her concrete evidence that her years of patronage to the fine arts were worthless.

This makes Vampire Therapist feel worthwhile for fans of vampire fiction, as well as those just looking for a fascinating story. Add to that the intriguing explanations of therapy terms, psychological distortions and explorations of the (in)human condition, and ironically it feels as though a game about vampires helps to demystify the idea.

If you’re looking for a solid visual novel with a bit of meat on its bones, and some great art and audio that elevates what rather bashfully calls itself a low-budget indie, then Vampire Therapist is a must-play!

It’s been almost five years since Canton, Massachusetts resident Karen Read found the body of her police-officer boyfriend John O’Keefe unresponsive in the snow in the early hours of Jan. 29, 2022. Since that day, the circumstances around O’Keefe’s death and the subsequent investigation have turned Read into one of the most divisive murder defendants in the nation. Now, a new docuseries is tackling the internet-famous case and the behind-the-scenes details people don’t know.

Directed by Danielle Johnson Cutts and released Nov. 23, The Trials of Karen Read charts Read’s journey from unknown Canton resident to a true-crime mainstay. GTA 6 launch knocked Call of Duty's daily players down 42 percent, Alinea estimates

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Grand Theft Auto 6's launch took a big bite out of Call of Duty’s player base, according to new estimates from Alinea Analytics, and four days on, it still hasn't recovered.

Writing in Alinea's newsletter, Rhys Elliott said Call of Duty HQ's daily active users (DAUs) across PlayStation, Xbox, and Steam fell 42 percent when GTA 6 launched on 19th November, dropping from an average of 7.5 million to 4.4 million. On PlayStation, which Elliott describes as "CoD's main platform", the drop was 44 percent.

"Sadly, CoD has barely bounced back after four days," Elliott wrote. "It's still down 41% from its baseline."

Fortnite and FC 27 took a hit too, but both are already recovering. Fortnite's DAUs on PlayStation and Xbox fell 28 percent, from 17.8 million to 12.8 million, but by the end of the weekend it had clawed its way back to 19 percent below its baseline.

Epic is also holding a live event to close out the current season on 5th December, which Elliott says the studio "hopes will pull players back after they’ve polished off GTA 6’s story".

FC 27's DAUs dropped by around 27 percent, from 5.9 million to 4.3 million, and it's already back within 20 percent of its baseline.

According to Alinea, of those who'd played GTA 6 by the end of its launch weekend, 29 percent had also played Fortnite this month, 16 percent had played Call of Duty HQ, and 11 percent had played FC 27.

Elliott said the pattern is largely what Alinea had predicted: the big live service games took an instant hit from GTA, but bounced back "because they're ingrained habits". Call of Duty is "the outlier here", he added, "and Xbox should - candidly - be worried about one of its premier franchises".

Elliott also points out that all of this is the impact of GTA 6's single-player mode. He expects its online component and PC port to arrive "some time next year", and when they do, he says, "the bounce back will not be so obtainable".

Alinea also estimates that GTA 6 has sold 44.9 million copies and made more than $4 billion since launch.

BYD in talks to buy Ineos’ French factory as Grenadier moves to US

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The French factory Mercedes built for the tiny Smart city car, and that Jim Ratcliffe turned into the home of his Land Rover Defender tribute, could soon be building BYDs.

BYD confirmed today that it has entered exclusive negotiations to acquire Ineos Automotive’s plant in Hambach, France. If it closes, it would be the Chinese giant’s second passenger car factory in the EU after Hungary, and the first time a Chinese automaker buys an existing car plant in Europe.

The other half of the deal is Ineos packing up. Alongside the announcement, Ineos Automotive said it plans to move Grenadier and Quartermaster production to the US, which it says accounts for about 70% of its sales. It has shortlisted US sites, hasn’t named any, and is aiming to start production there in 2028.

The math is simple. Every Grenadier shipped from France to the US gets hit with a 15% tariff, and the Quartermaster pickup falls under the 25% tariff on imported pickups. When 7 out of 10 of your customers are American, building your trucks in Lorraine stops making sense.

Ineos says it will keep building both models at Hambach “for the medium term” if the sale goes through, so the two companies would share the site for a while.

Neither side disclosed a price or a closing date, and the sale still has to go through consultation with employee representatives. BYD will soon find out that France is nothing like China when it comes to employee representation. There’s also no word yet on which BYD models would come out of Hambach or when. Same for the plant’s roughly 1,000 employees: BYD made no commitment on how many it would keep.

BYD executive vice president Stella Li said the company wants to give European consumers “vehicles built in France” that use BYD’s technology.

Hambach opened in 1997 as “Smartville,” Mercedes’ factory for the Smart Fortwo, and it later built the all-electric Smart EQ Fortwo. Ineos bought it from Mercedes at the end of 2020 after dropping its plan to build a new factory in Wales, and Grenadier production started in 2022. Ineos says it has put nearly €540 million into the site since 2019.

Capacity is about 30,000 Grenadiers a year. That’s not much by BYD’s standards. BYD sold 3,561,615 passenger vehicles globally in the first 10 months of the year, which means 30,000 units is less than three days of its output.

There’s also a bit of irony in who’s buying. Ineos unveiled its first EV, the Fusilier, in early 2024 while romanticizing gas cars at the same event. A few months later, it shelved the electric model indefinitely, blaming “reluctant consumer uptake” of EVs. Now it’s selling its only factory to the world’s largest EV maker.

This isn’t a surprise. Back in May, Stella Li said BYD was in talks with Stellantis and “other companies too” to take over underused plants in Europe, and that BYD would rather own and run a plant than form a joint venture. Hambach fits that description exactly.

BYD’s first European car plant, in Szeged, Hungary, started trial production earlier this year and is expected to reach mass production in early 2027, with the Dolphin Surf and Atto 2 first in line. BYD eventually wants more than 150,000 vehicles a year out of it. Its separate factory project in Turkey has been put on pause.

The motivation is regulatory. China-built BYD EVs face a 17% countervailing duty in the EU on top of the standard 10% car tariff, imported Chinese hybrids are now subject to quotas, and the EU’s proposed “Made in Europe” rules would tie EV subsidies and public contracts to how much of a vehicle is built locally. A French plant solves a lot of those problems at once.

And BYD isn’t alone. Leapmotor started building the B10 at Stellantis’ Zaragoza plant in Spain in October, Dongfeng plans to build cars at a Stellantis factory in France, Geely is reportedly lining up a Ford plant in Spain, and Chery already assembles vehicles in Barcelona.

Chery is the one BYD should watch. Through October, BYD registered 320,445 vehicles in Europe, up 133%, according to Automotive News. Chery Group was just ahead with 320,631, up 235%. A gap of 186 cars.

Europe matters more than ever for BYD because home isn’t going well. BYD brand sales in China fell 40% to 1,544,699 vehicles over the first 10 months, and its global passenger vehicle sales are down 2.7%.

This is a smart, cheap move for BYD. Building Szeged took years, including land disputes with private owners. Hambach comes with a trained workforce, a paint shop, a supplier base on the German border, and a seller that’s motivated to leave. You don’t get a much easier entry into France, but the easiest in France is still probably more difficult than anything BYD faced in China when it comes to deploying new production capacity.

The 30,000-unit capacity is small, but BYD isn’t buying it for what it builds today. It’s buying a “Made in France” label ahead of local content rules.

We also can’t ignore the symmetry here. Ineos is leaving Europe to build in the US because of tariffs. BYD is buying into Europe because of tariffs. That’s protectionism moving factories. Europe keeps the jobs, and the workers who used to build Smart EVs might get to build EVs again.

And yes, it’s funny that BYD is moving into the home of the Grenadier, a vehicle that exists because Ratcliffe loved the old Defender, days after a court in The Hague barred BYD’s Ti7 from the EU for infringing Land Rover’s Defender design.

The real question is what BYD puts on the line in Hambach. If it’s battery-electric cars, great. If it’s plug-in hybrids built to get around quotas, Europe is trading one gas-burning 4×4 factory for another.

New York Just Banned Subscriptions For Features Your Car Already Has

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In-vehicle subscriptions are pretty universally maligned among car owners. Most folks don't like the feeling of having to pay every month for features their cars were supposed to come with, and now New York State is doing something about it. Governor Kathy Hochul just signed a bill that bars automakers, dealers and their agents from selling subscriptions for features that are already installed.

The new law, which takes effect on February 27, 2027, is part of a new section of the state's General Business Law, according to Spectrum News. You can read the law for yourself here. It lays out fairly simple ground rules when it comes to subscriptions dealers or manufacturers can offer you: The feature must use hardware already in the car when it was bought or leased, and it must keep working without continual costs or support from the automaker or a third party. Those two things pretty much bring an end to crap like subscription-based heated seats and alike. It also is supposed to cover hands-off driving systems and enhanced navigation. Basically, once the hardware is physically in the car and the manufacturer isn't paying anything for you to use it, they can't then charge you. OEMs will also not be able to charge for software updates that fix safety defects.

Hochul called the practice a "deceptive business model," according to CBS 6 Albany. She also told automakers to keep their hands out of New Yorkers' wallets. It passed through the New York State Senate 57-3.

As with all good things, there are some exceptions. First of all, your lease or payment plan doesn't count as a subscription, so don't even try it, slick.

The law also doesn't apply to navigation updates, infotainment features, satellite radio, in-vehicle WiFi, telematics, roadside assistance or "vehicle-connected services" that rely on cellular data or other networks, according to Motor1. It also exempts software-dependent driver assistance features, like Tesla's Full Self-Driving or GM's Super Cruise.

I know what you're thinking. Didn't I just mention that hands-free driving systems are covered? Well, yes, but subscriptions tied to software and data are likely to survive, while hardware-only features are the real targets. Automakers will almost certainly make the argument that things like FSD and Super Cruise are "connected" or "software-dependent."

One prime example of what this bill aims to get rid of is BMW keeping things like a 360-degree camera behind a paywall. That hardware is already built into the car, and, as we reported, it doesn't require a data connection to operate. It's the same thing with features like high-beam assist and remote start, so I imagine it'll be hard for BMW and other companies with similar schemes to argue that these are "connected." I mean, these are the same people who argue that buyers actually want subscriptions, which is certainly not true.

In any case, violators will face civil penalties of up to $250 per point-of-sale for each violation, though it's not clear exactly how that'll be counted. It's not clear if other states are going to follow suit, but here's hoping.

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Daniel is a lifelong gamer with a love for various genres and gameplay styles who enjoys exploring games and playing from various angles.

As part of the ever-growing ingress of AI slop into the gaming industry, a new threat has recently arisen in the form of AI game-cloning. In a nutshell, a user feeds footage and descriptions of a game into their toaster of choice, as well as more specific data like coding cribbed from released demos, and instructs it to make effectively the same game. The infamous recent example of this was Needle in a Haystack Simulator, a small indie game that, following its announcement, received a deluge of AI-made knock-offs that flooded the Steam store.

Developers are taking this threat very seriously. During the most recent Steam Next Fest, which was already plagued by AI slop, several indie devs decided against releasing demos out of concern that bad actors would churn out knock-offs before they could release their actual games. Some developers have admitted to receiving anonymous extortion threats, saying that if a ransom isn’t paid, the crooks will release an AI knock-off of their game before they can release the real one.

There’s even been rumors that the PC release of Grand Theft Auto 6 could be expedited just to get ahead of the proverbial slop factory that’s already churning out identical open-world crime sandbox games.

Obviously, this is a big problem, and it’s only going to get worse as AI gets smarter, but at least according to game publisher tinyBuild’s CEO Alex Nichiporchik, developers should not be afraid of these malicious duplicates. As he explained in an interview with Gamesindustry.biz, AI may be able to imitate a game’s basic aesthetics, but game feel is another matter.

If you're looking for interesting new games to keep an eye on, check out the Coolest Demos from Steam Next Fest October 2026.

As Nichiporchik points out, while AI-made knock-offs of existing or announced games may have some superficial similarities, such as setting, basic game concepts, or story beats, those elements are only part of what makes a particular game a unique experience. In fact, it’s the “experience” itself that serves as the primary differentiator, and despite its best efforts, AI can’t do much in that department.

“There's a reason that every kind of AI slop game feels the same," he explained. "It's because when you try to make something from zero, AI will multiply your skills, and zero times a thousand is still zero.”

Nichiporchik went on to say that, rather than being afraid of having their ideas stolen, developers should consider what they can do to make their games more difficult to duplicate, primarily in how the game handles and in its core gameplay loop.

As Nichiporchik himself puts it, you could have an entire army of designers and researchers trying to completely duplicate something popular, but it just won’t have that same distinctive zing as the real thing, authentically brought to life by its original creators. It’s not unlike the number of bodies games like Fortnite or Grand Theft Auto have left in their wake as other studios try unsuccessfully to ape their formulas.

“As long as you have great game feel and a great game loop, I think you're going to be OK,” Nichiporchik assured.

Despite his assurances, Nichiporchik admitted that these AI-made games, not unlike fast food, are ubiquitous and rather obnoxious. Part of the problem is that AI knock-offs are basically trying to capitalize on the hype generated by a game’s announcement, as well as promotion like trailers or demos, stealing attention away from the genuine article or even fooling players not in the know into buying the wrong product.

Nichiporchik’s suggestion for this is to narrow the gap between announcement and release. While the standard strategy is to give the hype thrusters time to warm up and let the game accumulate wishlists and word-of-mouth, playing things close to the vest with a faster turnaround seems to work just as well.

As Nichiporchik explained, the most recent game published by tinyBuild, Probably Stolen - Cyberpunk Shopkeeper Sim, was announcement-ready as early as May, but tinyBuild and Questing Goose Studio decided to wait until August to announce, then release in October instead. That game’s done gangbusters despite somewhat repetitive gameplay, so it seems like the strategy worked out, and without giving bad actors nearly as much time to try to copy their notes.

She and her mother stood in line before the Angel of Death, Dr. Josef Mengele. Mengele directed the mother to go toward the gas chamber, and pointed Edith in a different direction to live. As Edith watched the smoke rise from the gas chamber, she knew her separation from her mother was permanent. That night, Mengele forced Edith, a gymnast and ballerina, to dance for him. In the depths of her numbness and grief, she willed herself to dance, then kindly shared the bread that Mengele disdainfully tossed her with other prisoners.

In facing this experience and other childhood adversities, Edith showed remarkable courage and resilience. Her mother was critical, being stuck in grief and anger for losing her mother as a child. Edith felt like the invisible sister, only gaining a sense of worth when she was able to rescue her parents from their loneliness and pain. She was cruelly shamed for being Jewish and teased for her awkwardness and crossed eyes. She coped with her sense of shame by becoming a perfectionist in gymnastics and dance to gain approval.

In overcoming her pain, she learned wisdom that she shared well into her 90s as a practicing psychologist. For example, she taught of the peace of having a clear conscience, relating that Mengele would always have to live with what he’d done; he was more of a prisoner than she was.

Edith taught that one survives adversity by committing to a cause outside of self and focusing on what one still has. In the concentration camp, she participated in mock beauty pageants and fantasized about a delightful future and a loving marriage. Although the circumstances were depressing, she realized that she had a purpose and that only she could do certain things in the way that she could.

Rather than becoming embittered, she clung to her faith, reasoning that it wasn’t God who ran the death camps, but hateful people. Even among the Germans, she found people who were secretly kind. She experienced, and chose to believe, that imperfect people are redeemable. She even forgave her German captors, whom she believed were themselves prisoners.

Understandably, Edith suffered long after her liberation. Yet she came to believe that suffering can reveal inner strengths and grow the confidence that one can endure intense adversity. She chose to believe in hope, love, and her ability to find joy in nature and little children.

She also encouraged people to tell their story in safe and appropriate ways, to release pain’s grip. She courageously accepted an invitation to return to Germany, where Hitler had once stood, and returned to Auschwitz to come to peace with her haunting memories.

Edith showed us how to overcome crushing adversity by reclaiming one’s sense of worth and purpose. In her healing journey, she gained hard-earned wisdom that she generously shared with others, still high-kicking into her 90s.

Her life reminds us that suffering crosses everyone’s path. That suffering can be a stumbling block or a stepping stone to greater resilience and life satisfaction (Schiraldi, 2021; 2017). We thank her for her inspiring example. Rest in peace, Edith Eva Eger.

Condo vs. Townhouse: Which Investment Will Turn Around More Equity After a Decade?

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Between 2014 and 2024, the difference in price appreciation between condos and single-family homes was less than 5 percentage points nationally, according to an analysis from Realtor.com®. Townhouses came even closer to matching the performance of detached homes—trailing by just 0.8 percentage points.

It's a remarkable outcome for the buyers opted out of the which picket fence dream, and instead invested in properties that often offer a more affordable entry point into homeownership. But that outcome may be harder to replicate in today's rapidly changing housing market.

Condo values fell 0.8% year over year in August 2026, even as single-family home values rose 1.8%, according to data from Intercontinental Exchange. And as home price growth cools, expenses that were easier to stomach during years of rapid appreciation stand to take a bigger bite out of homeowners' returns.

Even so, those national numbers tell only part of the story. In some regions, condos substantially outperformed townhouses over the decade examined by Realtor.com, challenging the notion that owning a piece of land necessarily leads to stronger appreciation.

Understanding what drove those differences—and how the economics of owning each property type are changing—offers some clues as to where buyers might find the greatest opportunity to build equity over the next decade.

Single-family homes appreciated 87.3% from 2014 to 2024, according to Realtor.com senior economist Hannah Jones. Townhomes nearly matched that pace at 86.5%, while condos trailed only slightly at 82.7%.

That modest gap are striking given the disparity in purchase prices. In October 2026, the median existing condo or co-op sold for $365,100—nearly $57,000 less than the median single-family home, according to the National Association of Realtors (NAR).

“Single-family homes are still the most popular type of homes purchased in the U.S. However, condos do appeal to buyers who want a smaller square footage and less upkeep,” explains Jessica Lautz, NAR deputy chief economist and vice president of research. “Condos are also significantly more affordable than single-family homes, which allows buyers who are downsizing or those just starting into homeownership to embrace condos.”

Still, the numbers show townhomes have been edging closer to single-family performance.

“Over the last decade, condos and townhomes appreciated almost in lockstep, but townhomes have pulled slightly ahead recently as buyers chase ‘house-like’ living without the single-family price tag,” says Jones.

While both property types have delivered strikingly similar returns from a national lens, as in all things real estate, the real gains come down to location, location, location.

In the Midwest, condo prices rose 78.3% over the last decade, outpacing the 70.7% growth of townhomes. The South followed a similar pattern, with condos climbing 66.7% compared with just 53.1% for townhomes.

The dynamic flips in higher-cost regions. In the Northeast, townhomes surged 80.5%, far ahead of the 58.6% gain for condos. And in the West, townhomes appreciated 83.8%, edging past condos at 78.9%.

Affordability explains much of the split, says Jones.

“In the more affordable Midwest and South, condos have gained ground faster than townhomes. In higher-cost regions like the Northeast and West, townhomes have appreciated more quickly,” she explains.

In lower-cost markets, condos benefit from their clustering in downtown areas and their appeal to buyers chasing city living. But in expensive, supply-constrained metros, the premium shifts toward townhomes.

“Townhomes are commanding a growing premium as buyers seek a house-like property when single-family homes are out of reach. In more affordable markets, condos—often concentrated in dense city centers—have captured stronger growth as buyers compete for limited housing options,” she adds.

“Townhomes look set for steadier growth,” says Jones. Part of that is because of what you get when you buy one.

When you purchase a townhouse, you own both the structure and the parcel of land it sits on. That land ownership gives you more control, typically fewer restrictions, and it can be a stronger driver of long-term appreciation.

“Generally townhouses have strong appreciation potential because the land itself appreciates in a more reliable way,” explains Aman Sharma, a real estate agent at SERHANT.

Eric Hughes, a real estate investor and coach with Rental Income Advisors, agrees. “The price growth of townhomes will track extremely closely with the price growth of single-family homes in general,” he says.

That connection to single-family homes is key. The “single-family feel” broadens their appeal to a wider range of buyers—families, downsizers, and even first-timers looking to get their foot in the door.

They also tend to come with lower HOA fees than condos. That not only frees up more buying power for the mortgage itself, but it also makes resale easier since future buyers are less likely to be put off by steep monthly dues or looming assessments.

Builders have taken notice, too. Townhouses accounted for a record 18.7% of single-family housing starts on a one-year moving average basis in the third quarter of 2025, according to the National Association of Home Builders.

But Jones warns that this investment isn’t free of potential headwinds. In markets where single-family inventory is expanding and prices are softening, townhome demand may take a hit as more buyers opt for detached homes instead.

“In tight urban markets, condos can still shine as buyers get creative,” says Jones.

Even though the appreciation rate of condos has lagged behind townhomes and single-family homes nationally over the last decade, they can still deliver strong returns in the right conditions.

“In big urban markets, condos are often the most desirable type of urban residence, and can appreciate strongly,” says Hughes. That means that in places like Miami Beach, New York, and San Francisco—where land is scarce and demand for city living is strong—condos dominate.

“There are certain parts of our city where highly amenitized condo high-rises perform better than townhouses from an investment standpoint,” says Miltiadis Kastanis, executive director of sales at Compass, based in Miami.

Lifestyle amenities are another draw. Concierge service, pools, gyms, and other perks make condos especially attractive to luxury buyers or second-home seekers who prioritize convenience. For full-time residents, those amenities can also translate into higher perceived value when it’s time to sell.

But again, Jones stresses that there is greater potential for some bumps in the road for condos, ranging from rising ownership costs to competition from single-family homes and townhouses.

The experts we spoke to stress that your return on investment isn’t just about appreciation and loan paydown. The hidden costs of homeownership can chip away at returns. However, it’s worth noting that even single-family homes can fall prey to these same traps.

“While single-family homes may not have an HOA fee, some communities do even for single-family homes,” says Lautz. “Maintenance can be more expensive when a homebuyer must account for costly items such as roofs, which a condo owner is unlikely to be responsible for.”

While it’s true that owners of single-family homes must shoulder all their own maintenance costs, condo owners risk getting hit with special assessments that may cover repairs or upgrades to facilities they don’t use, or work that should have been done properly the first time around.

Rick Bannon, global real estate adviser at One Sotheby’s International Realty, points to a condo in Miami where residents were hit with a $1 million special assessment just to bring the building up to safety standards.

That’s why Joe Luciano, broker at Re/Max Bentley’s, takes special care when helping his clients through a condo purchase.

“When advising clients on a condo purchase, we always want to get the meeting minutes to see if there’s any chatter around potential projects coming up and then also the financials to see if they have the appropriate amount in reserve funds to cover any surprises,” he says.

Those reserves are set to become an even more important consideration for buyers. Beginning Jan. 4, 2027, Fannie Mae will raise its standard minimum replacement reserve requirement from 10% to 15% of annual budgeted assessment income for condo projects subject to its Full Review process.

While the change could help protect condo owners against the financial fallout of deferred maintenance, it could also put upward pressure on monthly dues in underfunded communities. And buildings that fail to meet mortgage eligibility requirements could face a smaller pool of buyers.

Outside of special assessments, Luciano says monthly HOA fees can take a big bite out of a homeowner's return on investment.

Nearly 3.4 million American households paid at least $500 a month in condo or HOA fees in 2025, according to data from the U.S. Census Bureau. Roughly 1 million paid at least $1,000 a month.

“When looking at the two as investments side by side over 10 years, the appreciation could be similar, but paying an extra $300 to $500 a month is a sunk cost of $36,000 to $60,000 that didn’t go toward your mortgage principal,” explains Luciano.

At the extreme end, he points to luxury Boston high-rises with $2,000-plus monthly HOA fees that have seen stagnant appreciation in recent years.

If the last 10 years prove anything, it’s that condos and townhouses will both continue to deliver strong returns—just not always for the same reasons, or in the same places.

Townhomes have shown they can reliably shadow the trajectory of single-family homes, powered by land value and a “house-like” appeal that buyers continue to prize. Condos, by contrast, thrive where affordability and density drive demand, making them especially potent in urban cores and lower-cost regions where buyers are squeezed out of detached homes.

The next decade may sharpen these divides. As affordability pressures deepen, first-time buyers could lean more heavily into condos, reviving demand in downtowns that have seen uneven recoveries since the pandemic. At the same time, zoning reforms and the ongoing shortage of single-family homes could keep pushing buyers toward townhouses as the closest substitute for a detached home. Policy choices—from property tax incentives to how cities handle condo reserve requirements after recent safety crises—will also influence which type of property captures the bigger share of appreciation.

For investors, the lesson isn’t to pick one winner and walk away. It’s to understand how local affordability, supply constraints, and regulation interact to tilt the playing field.

Uncommon Thinkers: Saf Rabah wants to make it easier to build housing, one permit at a time

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

by Grace Kaste on Nov 24, 2026 at 9:50 amNovember 24, 2026 at 9:53 am

Editor’s note: This series profiles five Seattle-area “Uncommon Thinkers,” who will be recognized Dec. 10 at the GeekWire Gala in partnership with Greater Seattle Partners.

When startup founder Safouen (Saf) Rabah decided to get in on the AI boom, he didn’t start by coding or by training any models. He got in touch with urban planners at Bellevue City Hall, and together they combed through nearly 300 land use permit applications to find common mistakes and bottlenecks.

Bellevue began using Rabah’s platform, Govstream.AI, in the summer of 2025, starting with an AI assistant for city staff. In a test using past applications, about three times as many were judged complete with Govstream’s tool. Rabah says that change alone has the potential to alter the housing stock of the city.

“Why can’t we build more housing? All the macro economic reasons, inflation, material costs, labor, land scarcity, are all exacerbated by the slow permitting process,” Rabah said. “That’s what we want to change.”

So, how did Rabah, a B2B software engineer by training, find his way to land use permitting?

Rabah hadn’t heard of the government technology sector, aka GovTech, until mid-career, but looking back, the signs were all there. He was born in Sfax, Tunisia, and grew up in Tunis. Throughout his childhood, he dreamed of running for office.

“I come from a small developing country, so there’s always this idea of, what could a small developing country become? How can it prosper and grow?” Rabah said. “Building has always been a big part of what I imagined myself doing.”

As a teenager in the 1980s, he attended a USAID-funded boarding school in Tunis. The school was part of Cold War efforts to expand American soft power around the Middle East; classes were conducted in English and many of the students went on to university in North America.

“The school had the first Macintosh lab in all of Africa,” Rabah said. “I can still picture the USAID logo on the back of each computer.”

Rabah got a scholarship to University of British Columbia, where he studied electrical engineering while running what he calls his “first entrepreneurial venture”: a service that built and hosted websites for small businesses.

That led to a job at a startup called Pivotal, a customer relationship management platform where Rabah led a team responsible for e-business applications. After Pivotal went public, Rabah left for a job at TELUS, one of Canada’s “big three” telecommunications corporations, but quickly felt frustrated by the dynamics at such a large company.

“Even though I worked on nascent products, it was very, very bureaucratic,” Rabah said. “There were committees, a hundred thousand employees. I missed building things.”

Rabah began to look for a new job that would not only allow him to build but also to make a difference.

“Being an immigrant, it’s always tempting to stay content with having a good job, good status,” Rabah said. “But I’ve always enjoyed the ‘what if?’ mindset, the clean slate. It’s this way today, but could we imagine it to be something different?”

That vision led him to Socrata, which in 2010 was just a nascent government technology startup.

“I really connected with the founder,” Rabah said. “It had a really cool mission, it combined the ‘do-well’ with ‘do-good.’”

Socrata was one of the first platforms to host data portals for local governments and federal agencies, allowing citizens to access and map public data sets. Many U.S. cities, including Seattle, still use Socrata’s software today to publish information including crime logs, permits, and budgets.

“We were a big part of pioneering the open data movement,” Rabah said. “At one point almost every major city was our customer, and half the states.”

Socrata was acquired by Tyler Technologies in 2018, and Rabah stayed on until 2024.

“The funny part is today we use that data quite a lot in our work because one of the most popular data sets that any city publishes is its permit data,” Rabah said. “We’ve even built a connector between Socrata and Govstream.”

But once Socrata was acquired, Rabah began to feel the same way he had at TELUS: boxed in by bureaucracy.

“Tyler was a really good company, but it is a big company,” Rabah said. “Tech was getting repetitive, and I was thinking about leaving it altogether.”

“The thing that pushed me over the edge is that AI happened,” Rabah said. “It was one of those moments where once you start working with it, you really can’t help but think, ‘I can revisit virtually every assumption I’ve made over the last 20 years, every constraint.’”

Immediately, he thought of his passion for building—building a startup, building a better country, and literally building a building.

“I’ve been fascinated by the built environment since my Lego days: What makes some cities livable and others not?” Rabah said. “Then, living in Seattle, you see the lack of housing, affordability, and the homelessness problem we have, and you’re like, why can’t we build more housing? I started believing that these problems are in fact solvable if we simply speed things up and simplify things with AI.”

In July of 2024, Rabah began working with his “design partner,” Bellevue’s Development Services Department, to learn more about common permitting problems.

“We started with something visionary and high-level, but you can only get there if you understand the granular problems of the day-to-day,” Rabah said. “So we talked with developers and builders and we talked with the people on the other side of the counter who are processing these permits and guiding these builders.”

He learned that most permitting mistakes are avoidable, like applying for the wrong zone or forgetting to include a north arrow on an architectural plan. Those oversights can send an application back to square one while clogging up the permitting process for all other projects.

“The wait time disproportionately affects lower margin projects,” Rabah said. “It really constrains the supply of housing, especially the housing we need more of, which is the more affordable kind.”

“I’ve always enjoyed the ‘what if?’ mindset, the clean slate. It’s this way today, but could we imagine it to be something different?”

Govstream’s AI assistants are designed to help first-time builders avoid those errors and make permitting faster for everyone. Rabah and his team began by building what they call an “intelligent intake” platform for a city permitting portal, which guided users through preparing to submit their applications.

Bellevue tested Govstream’s intake tool on 276 past residential building permit applications. The city reported a completeness rate of 67% in the test, compared with 22% when those applications were originally filed.

More recently, they’ve begun to build out an AI assistant for the backend, which assists city permit techs with the initial review and reduces what Rabah calls “toil,” like answering unnecessary pre-application emails and tracking down missing documents.

Bellevue staff estimated that Govstream’s tools saved them 152 hours over one month this summer by helping them research and respond to pre-application inquiries.

“For a developer, time is money, so the type of housing that can be built in Bellevue will become more affordable,” said Jake Hesselgesser, Bellevue’s development services director. “This technology has a direct and material impact on the cost of housing.”

Eventually, Govstream.AI will be an end-to-end service, assisting in later stages of the permitting process, too. But Rabah is emphatic that Govstream will not give AI the final say.

“We always put our AI systems in the role of assistants that suggest stuff, but never decide stuff,” Rabah said.

Rabah says Govstream uses preset rules, outside the AI model, to check whether a portion of the application is complete, whether a site needs a geotechnical report, or whether an architect is properly licensed. In Bellevue, city staff retain final authority.

Hesselgesser said that Rabah’s conservative approach to AI is what made the city of Bellevue want to work with his startup.

“Our core principle around Bellevue’s use of AI is to retain human decision making, and we wanted to ensure that we were aligned with a company with the same core value,” Hesselgesser said. “Saf took that holistic approach.”

Around the office, Rabah has become known for his eagerness to build. According to Mariia Derevianko, head of marketing, he spends his weekends building out a product or an idea in order to workshop it with the team.

“He’s very hands-on with the product, with design, marketing, all of the aspects,” Derevianko said. “He never waits for the engineers to build something.”

Most recently, he built a lead tracker to research which cities Govstream should partner with, and previously, he vibe-coded one of the research assistants in the Govstream platform as a template for his software engineers.

Derevianko, who joined the team last year after Rabah poached her from AWS, said he’s also known for his kindness.

“I originally didn’t want to join a start-up,” Derevianko said. “I only said yes because I saw he is a genuinely good person who really cares about this project.”

Govstream.AI has already won at least 12 contracts around the Puget Sound, including King County and the City of Everett, as well as cities across the country, such as Louisville, Kentucky.

Ultimately, Rabah has his eyes set on Seattle, where construction and development has been on the decline. Last December, the city council passed permanent “middle housing” rules to upzone residential neighborhoods and encourage the construction of ADUs, all of which will need a permit.

Seattle tested a competing AI tool from CivCheck in 2025. In June, city staff recommended a pilot that would let applicants check their submissions for completeness before filing.

“We need affordable housing in Seattle,” Rabah said. “We would really, really love to help our home city streamline permitting and remove it as a barrier.”

Does Working Harder Make Learning Fun?

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A new psychology study challenges the classic educational advice that simply putting in more effort makes learning enjoyable. Three experiments reveal that while cognitive effort improves comprehension, it does not spark curiosity or enjoyment, instead, preexisting interest motivates students to engage in the first place.

Parents and educators frequently repeat a familiar piece of advice when children groan over difficult homework: if you just try harder, you’ll start to enjoy it. Behind this common wisdom lies an intuitive assumption that sustained cognitive effort naturally blossoms into curiosity and intrinsic motivation.

Yet a new psychological investigation from Kyushu University suggests that this relationship works in the exact opposite direction.

Published in Acta Psychologica, the study demonstrates that forcing effort improves basic comprehension, but it does not make dry or unappealing material more interesting. Instead, genuine interest and liking come first—serving as the psychological spark that motivates learners to invest cognitive effort voluntarily.

“I’m curious if a student is made to work harder, does that bring the same benefits as effort they choose to give,” asked Yimeng Jin, a doctoral student at Kyushu University’s Graduate School of Systems Life Sciences and first author of the study. “It matters because many learning environments rely on required effort, such as assignments, on the assumption that more effort is always better.”

To disentangle voluntary motivation from mandatory mental exertion, Jin and senior author Professor Johan Lauwereyns at Kyushu University’s Faculty of Arts and Science conducted three behavioral experiments involving 110 university students.

Participants read pairs of seemingly unrelated sentences and decided whether they wanted to construct an explanation connecting them. By tracking participants’ initial choices, task completion, and time spent, the team isolated two distinct components of learning: willingness to engage and cognitive effort invested.

Across the three experimental phases, a clear cognitive pattern emerged:

“Interestingly, participants who were required to explain spent more time on the task than those who chose to do so,” Jin noted. “Perhaps they initially skipped it because the sentences were harder to understand, so they needed more time to make sense of them. It’s a bit like struggling through homework in a subject you don’t enjoy. Together, the experiments suggest that effort can help people make sense of information, while interest is more closely linked to their willingness to engage.”

While the researchers caution that short laboratory reading tasks among university students differ from long-term classroom learning in younger children, the findings carry broad implications for how educators, parents, and communicators design learning environments. Rather than relying solely on mandatory drill work to manufacture enthusiasm, sparking authentic curiosity first may unlock deeper, faster comprehension.

“The findings have implications far outside education. The connection between engagement and effort is key whenever our understanding depends on reading texts,” concluded Lauwereyns.

Does working harder on a subject automatically make it more enjoyable?

No. Researchers at Kyushu University found that while putting in cognitive effort helps students better understand difficult material, it does not increase their interest in or enjoyment of the subject.

What actually motivates students to put effort into learning?

The experiments showed that preexisting interest and liking come first. When students already find a topic intriguing, they are far more willing to engage voluntarily, and they comprehend the material more efficiently than when forced to work through topics they want to skip.

What happens when students are forced to work on material they don’t find interesting?

When participants were required to explain material they initially chose to skip, they spent significantly more time struggling with the task, gained less comprehension compared to voluntary effort, and showed no increase in enjoyment.

Effort enhances understanding, while appreciation drives engagement in Cross-sentence semantic processing

Comprehensive reading requires active semantic processing, going beyond explicitly stated information to construct connections and meaning. Building on discourse coherence theory, Reader-Response Theory and insights from Language Poetry, the present research investigates how active engagement and cognitive effort shape semantic processing.

We propose that evaluative dimensions central to aesthetic appreciation, namely understanding, interest, and liking, also play important roles in semantic processing.

To examine these processes, we developed a novel cross-sentence elaboration paradigm using unconnected, randomly paired sentences that create semantic gaps and encourage reader-driven meaning construction.

In Experiment 1, participants could voluntarily invest effort by thinking aloud during elaboration. Results showed greater volitional engagement was associated with higher levels of understanding, interest, and appreciation.

Experiment 2 dissociated willingness to engage from actual effort, revealing that appreciation predicted participants’ willingness to invest further effort, whereas additional completed effort did not consistently increase appreciation.

Experiment 3 examined forced effort, showing that externally imposed effort enhanced semantic understanding but did not consistently increase appreciation, highlighting the distinct roles of volitional and imposed effort.

An integrated 2 × 2 analysis of Experiments 2 and 3 showed that active engagement and cognitive effort made distinct, interactive contributions to evaluation and processing time, with completed cognitive effort more reliably predicting accurate recognition memory and active engagement more closely tied to memory for the processing context.

Together, these findings clarify the interplay between engagement, effort, understanding, and appreciation, offering new insights into the cognitive dynamics of active meaning construction in semantic processing.

NASA Spacecraft Snaps Moody Photo of Earth on Its Way to Mars

NASA’s ESCAPADE spacecraft has captured an incredible photo of the Earth as it slingshotted past the planet on its way to Mars.

The images were taken by the spacecraft’s Visible and Infrared Observation System camera, developed by Northern Arizona University, at around 9 A.M. EST on November 9. This was approximately three hours after the spacecraft made its closest approach to Earth, when it was about 38,500 miles away and had a clear view of the Western Hemisphere.

The images were captured by the second of the two ESCAPADE spacecraft to perform a powered flyby of Earth this month. Both spacecraft used a combination of engine burns and Earth’s gravity to gain momentum for their 10-month journey to Mars.

Once they reach orbit around the Red Planet, the spacecraft will study how the solar wind — a stream of charged particles traveling at around a million miles per hour from the Sun — interacts with Mars’ environment and contributes to the loss of its atmosphere.

A commercial satellite captured images of the pair from approximately 3,000 miles away, while a member of the mission team photographed the spacecraft passing overhead from his home near Los Angeles (below).

What Is Pons? The Robinhood Chain Meme Coin Launchpad Explained

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

What Is Pons? The Robinhood Chain Meme Coin Launchpad Explained

When Robinhood switched on its own Robinhood Chain blockchain on July 1, 2026, the trading app's stock tokens were supposed to be the main attraction. Instead, much of the early activity came from meme coins: tokens built around a joke or an internet trend.

Pons became the busiest place to make them. Like Pump.fun on Solana, it lets anyone pick a name, ticker and image and have a tradable token minutes later, without writing code. Its fees briefly made it one of the highest-earning protocols in crypto, and its own token, PONS, went on to list on Kraken and Coinbase.

That openness can also be exploited. In September 2026, an onchain analyst alleged that one group had used Pons launches to extract millions of dollars from buyers.

This guide explains what Pons is, how its two generations of contracts work, what the PONS token does, and the risks.

Pons is a token launch protocol running on Robinhood Chain, a layer-2 network built on Ethereum using Arbitrum's technology, and its website and documentation are published by Pons Labs, LLC.

Every token launched through Pons is a standard ERC-20 token with a fixed supply, 1 billion tokens on V1 launches, that can never be increased. The creator gets no allocation set aside before trading opens.

Pons is also non-custodial. Its documentation says every launch and trade is a transaction the user's own wallet signs, and the platform never holds user funds.

Pons went live within days of Robinhood Chain's mainnet. It picked up most of the chain's launchpad activity after an early rival, Noxa, stopped accepting new launches on July 11, 2026, according to Decrypt's reporting.

Pons has two generations of contracts, V1 and V2, and both remain live on Robinhood Chain.

In V1, a single transaction deploys the token and its trading pool, a Uniswap V3 pool paired with wrapped ETH (WETH), and locks the pool's liquidity so the creator can't withdraw it. Each launch costs 0.0005 ETH and carries a 1% trading fee, the documentation says. A launch "graduates" once 4.2 ETH has been paired in its pool by default, but trading carries on in the same pool.

V1 launches are protected for their first two blocks: in the launch block, only the creator's initial buy can execute, and for the rest of the window no wallet can hold more than 5% of supply or buy more than 5.5%. Fees are split 70% to the creator and 30% to the protocol for current launches.

V2 adds a bonding curve: a contract that holds a token's whole supply and sets its price by formula, rising as people buy and falling as they sell. According to the V2 documentation, the curve sells out, then "graduates" automatically into a Uniswap V4 pool, seeded with a fixed share of supply held back from the start. The pool's liquidity is then locked permanently; no unlock function exists.

V2 launches can be priced in ETH or in other assets Pons has approved, including tokenized stocks, in which case the creator is paid in that asset. Each trade pays a standard fee shared between Pons, the creator and an optional buyback, plus an optional creator tax that is capped by the protocol and fixed at launch.

When a creator turns buybacks on, part of their own fee is spent buying their token back. Those tokens are locked in a vault and released over five years, split between the creator and the protocol.

PONS itself was launched through Pons's own legacy V1 factory, and its documentation uses PONS as the reference token for developers testing their integrations.

"Sniping" means buying a large share of a new token in its first seconds, often with bots, then selling into later buyers. Every V2 launch opens with a "snipe tax" of 99% on buys that decays to zero over five seconds, the documentation says.

The launching wallet and the creator's fee wallet are exempt automatically, and creators can name up to 32 more exempt addresses at launch "for a team bundling its opening buys across several wallets."

That exemption became central to allegations published on September 27, 2026. Pseudonymous onchain analyst Wazz tweeted that a single operation had extracted at least $18.43 million from 53 Robinhood Chain launches between July 10 and September 21, 2026, mostly through Pons V2. Wazz said nearly every launch was sniped for 70% or more of its supply by bundles of 70 to 200 wallets.

Pons fees drove Robinhood Chain to a record of about $6 million in daily fees in early September 2026, The Block reported.

On September 5, 2026, blockchain analytics firm Arkham tweeted that crypto market maker Wintermute appeared to be buying PONS slowly in a series of small purchases, and held just over $3 million of the token. Wallets that Arkham attributes to Wintermute held about 3.4 million PONS at the time, and about 7.4 million as of November 2026.

As of November 20, 2026, DefiLlama put Pons's cumulative fees at about $205 million. Of that, $137 million came in September 2026 alone; fees fell to about $17 million in October 2026.

PONS is the ERC-20 token tied to the Pons launchpad, with a fixed supply of 1 billion. Its link to the platform is a buyback.

According to the V1 documentation, 80% of the protocol's fees are used to buy PONS on the market through an automated process and send it to a burn address, permanently removing it from circulation. The remaining 20% pays for infrastructure and the Pons team. Kraken's listing notice describes the same mechanism.

As of November 20, 2026, CoinGecko data shows about 669 million PONS in circulation, out of a fixed supply of 1 billion.

You can track PONS's latest price on Decrypt's price page.

PONS trades in Uniswap pools on Robinhood Chain. Binance Wallet added it to its Binance Alpha tier on September 2, 2026, and Kraken opened PONS trading on September 25, 2026, with access through the Kraken App and Instant Buy to follow once liquidity allows. Coinbase Markets tweeted on October 6, 2026, that it had added PONS to its listing roadmap, and said the next day that its PONS-USD pair would open later that day, if liquidity conditions were met.

Pons has flagged several changes still to come. Its V1 documentation says the 80% share of protocol fees used to buy back PONS is not yet fixed in its contracts, but will become "immutable, decentralized, and automated" in a future release.

On V2, three security firms (SB Security, Dingbats and Pashov Audit Group) are reviewing the contracts, and Pons says it will publish their reports in full, including any findings it did not accept, once the reviews close. Until then, its documentation tells users to treat V2 as unaudited, and only whitelisted addresses can create V2 tokens.

Pons also faces competition on its home chain. Uniswap, already Robinhood Chain's main exchange, launched its own launchpad, Pools.trade, on August 5, 2026. It charges no launchpad fee, locks each launch's liquidity permanently, and offers a "crowd launch" option designed to resist bundling. Within its first days, Pools.trade took about half of Robinhood Chain's launchpad volume, Decrypt's Morning Minute newsletter reported. The newsletter noted that Uniswap can afford to forgo launchpad fees because it earns from its other products, which most dedicated launchpads cannot.

Pons has since kept the larger share of fees. As of November 2026, DefiLlama showed Pons collecting about $5.6 million in fees over the previous 30 days, against about $115,000 for Pools.trade. The two charge different rates, though: Pools.trade's trading fee is 0.25%, while Pons's standard fee is 1%.

V2's tokenized-stock pairs link Pons launches to the stock tokens Robinhood built the chain to carry, which ties Pons's prospects to Robinhood Chain itself. On Robinhood's third-quarter earnings call on October 27, 2026, CEO Vlad Tenev said the growth of the chain and its ecosystem had exceeded the company's expectations, and that Robinhood was investing in how to support the chain's developer community.

The Best Books From the Last 10 Years That Hooked Me From the Opening Chapter

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sometimes you know from the very first chapter that you're going to enjoy a book, and it's a great feeling. Maybe it's a particularly strong opening line or a vivid POV character that makes the action all the more immersive. No matter the reason, the pages fly by, and you find yourself drawn deeper and deeper into the story.

With that in mind, this list looks at some novels from the last decade that reel the reader in practically from page one. They span a variety of genres and tones, from historical epics and revisionist fantasy to dark character studies and tense nonfiction. The fact remains, all are finely written and always engaging, wasting little time immersing us in their characters and world.

"When a man is sure he knows God's will... he is the most dangerous person in the world." Ken Follett (best known for Eye of the Needle and The Pillars of the Earth) is a master of historical fiction, and this is one of his best. We're quickly thrown into the deadly dynastic and religious conflicts of the time, buoyed along by colorful characters and gripping, page-turning prose.

The third installment in his Kingsbridge series, A Column of Fire begins in 1558, as the young Ned Willard returns to his hometown after spending time abroad. Although Ned hopes to marry Margery, the woman he loves, their relationship becomes entangled in the bitter religious divisions threatening England. Meanwhile, the death of Queen Mary I and the accession of Elizabeth I create enormous political uncertainty, forcing individuals and families to choose sides.

'When we exist, death is not; and when death exists, we are not." Karl Ove Knausgård caused a literary sensation in the 2000s with his autobiographical novel series My Struggle, and has since explored even stranger, darker territory with his recent work. That includes The School of Night, which tells the story of Kristian Hadeland, an ambitious young Norwegian who moves to London in 1985 to study photography. Convinced of his exceptional artistic talent, Kristian approaches his new surroundings with a mixture of arrogance, insecurity, and contempt.

Eventually, he crosses paths with a mysterious Dutch artist named Hans, whose influence introduces increasingly disturbing complications into his life. Indeed, beneath the apparently conventional account of a young artist's experiences, a sense of unease begins to grow. The result is a subtle but damning character study, one with restrained but unmistakable supernatural undercurrents.

"He's a bad dream with teeth." Yet another banger from Joe Hill, author of Horns and The Fireman (and son of Stephen King). The main character here is Arthur Oakes, a university student whose life becomes entangled in a dangerous criminal situation involving his mother. Desperate to protect her, Arthur turns to a group of friends who attempt to solve his problems through an extraordinary supernatural ritual. Their experiment involves summoning a powerful dragon-like entity known as King Sorrow, whose assistance comes at a terrifying cost.

From here, King Sorrow plays out as an epic, ambitious, character-driven dark fantasy, jam-packed with memorable scenes. We get phantasmagoric seances and summonings, shadowy government agencies attempting dastardly schemes, even a dragon going toe-to-toe with a pair of fighter jets. That said, even amidst all this sound and fury, Hill keeps the protagonists and their emotions front and center.

"The humanities are always in crisis." In recent years, author Ian McEwan (author of the modern classic Atonement) has shifted his attention to subtle sci-fi stories like the AI-focused Machines Like Me and this one, which uses the vantage point of the future to comment on our present. Set in 2119, What We Can Know follows Thomas Metcalfe, a literary scholar living in a Britain transformed by environmental catastrophe and rising sea levels. He becomes obsessed with locating a legendary poem written in 2014 by the celebrated poet Francis Blundy.

Supposedly composed for Blundy's wife, the poem was read aloud at a private dinner party before mysteriously disappearing, leaving behind a collection of historical accounts and tantalizing clues. Metcalfe thus undertakes a kind of literary investigation, in the process piecing together a portrait of the madness and contradictions of the 2010s and 2020s. A brilliant, perceptive book.

"The Beauty of the House is immeasurable; its Kindness infinite." Susanna Clarke wrote one of the finest fantasy books of the 21st century to date with Jonathan Strange and Mr. Norrell, and her sophomore novel Piranesi is a real gem, too. The title character is a gentle and inquisitive man who lives in an enormous labyrinthine structure known simply as the House. Its seemingly endless halls contain thousands of statues, while its lower levels are flooded by a vast ocean whose tides periodically sweep through the building.

Slowly, however, Piranesi begins to question his surroundings and dig deeper. In the process, an initially dreamlike fantasy tale morphs into an increasingly compelling psychological puzzle. There are twists and turns and revelations aplenty, all of it elevated by the author's typically stellar worldbuilding and character development. A must-read for fantasy fans.

"It is easy to charm a person who talks too much: all you have to do is listen." This is a work of nonfiction, but it's more gripping than most thriller novels. In November 2019, nineteen-year-old Zac Brettler, a seemingly ordinary middle-class teen, fell from the balcony of an expensive London apartment overlooking the River Thames. Although his death initially looks like suicide, every fact that emerges begins to complicate the picture.

Most striking of all, it turns out that Zac had been living a double life, presenting himself as the wealthy heir of a Russian oligarch. Author Patrick Radden Keefe (who also wrote the powerful book Say Nothing about the Troubles in Northern Ireland) doggedly chases the truth, eventually revealing far more than even the police. The result is not just a suspenseful true-crime story but a grim statement on modern London, a city besotted with wealth, no matter how dirty its origins.

"The king is dead." Author Lev Grossman subverted "wizard school" tropes with his Magicians series, and has played a similar trick with The Bright Sword, a wonderful reimagining of Arthurian legend. It's a grittier, more psychological take on the well-worn plot points involving quests and heroes. All in all, the book's mixture of melancholy, humor, and supernatural wonder feels faithful to the tradition and yet still refreshingly original.

The plot revolves around Collum, a young knight who arrives at Camelot hoping to join the fellowship of the Round Table. Unfortunately, his timing could hardly be worse. King Arthur has recently died, the kingdom is descending into political chaos, and most of its greatest defenders have either perished or disappeared. Indeed, instead of finding the glorious court he imagined, Collum encounters a diminished collection of surviving knights struggling to determine what remains of Arthur's extraordinary legacy.

"If I told you half of what he's done to keep us alive and safe, well, you wouldn't imagine it could be true." His Dark Materials is rightly regarded as a landmark of children's fantasy, so expectations were sky-high for Philip Pullman's La Belle Sauvage, and yet the book arguably surpasses them. It takes place approximately a decade before the events of Northern Lights/The Golden Compass, following eleven-year-old Malcolm Polstead, who must keep the baby Lyra Belacqua safe during a devastating flood.

On top of the dangers of nature, our intelligent, observant young protagonist must also dodge political conspiracies, scheming religious authority, various secret organizations, and ever-present spies. Along the way, he crosses paths with a river giant and a fairy woman, and comes into possession of an alethiometer, the truth-telling device that will become central to the entire series. In short, a smart, entertaining tale that deepens Pullman's mythology.

The Extraordinary Music Worthy of an Extraordinary Life in ‘W.E.B. Du Bois: Rebel with a Cause’

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Rita Coburn‘s “W.E.B. Du Bois: Rebel with a Cause” is an extraordinary documentary about an extraordinary man, a film that spans nearly 100 years in two hours, yet never feels rushed or superficial. In her storytelling, Coburn shares with her subject a talent for distilling complex ideas and expressing them with force and clarity; the result is a movie that fully honors the breadth and depth of Du Bois’ achievements as a groundbreaking Black writer, historian, and activist.

One of Coburn’s most potent tools in this regard is the music by composer Kathryn Bostic, who previously collaborated with the director on “Marian Anderson: The Whole World in Her Hands.” Bostic incorporates a wide array of styles and influences in her score, which references the various types of music Du Bois encountered and appreciated as a Black intellectual but isn’t beholden to them; as evocative of Bach and American folk music as it is of 19th-century spirituals and Asian motifs, it’s the sonic equivalent of Du Bois’ literary works. The scope of the project required Bostic to come at the material from multiple angles and perspectives. “Rita and I talked about what types of touchstones we needed to define the narrative musically,” Bostic told IndieWire. “There was the regional touchstone, the era-specific touchstone, the interior emotional aspects that we wanted to heighten with music. Then there’s always the discovery process as you begin to create.”

For Bostic, that discovery process is one of the most exciting aspects of film composing. While she had a number of reference points as she began work on the score, she soon found herself following intuition to take them in different directions than initially intended. “There’s always what I call the hands-on-the-clay moment,” Bostic said. “You have your list, and you have your frame of reference, but the process is fluid.”

According to Coburn, Bostic’s ability to bring a historical perspective to her scores made her an ideal collaborator for the film. “Kathryn pulled from the region of Great Barrington [where Du Bois was born] as well as New York in that time period, and then what was happening with the African-American community that Du Bois was entering,” Coburn said. “There wasn’t gospel then, so you were looking at a small group of Black people going to the churches there in New England. What were they listening to, and therefore what was he listening to?”

Bostic drew on the music of the Fisk Jubilee Singers, a Black a capella ensemble that inspired Du Bois, but then moved in a completely different direction to explore his time in Berlin, when he encountered and fell in love with classical music. She also incorporated Wolof music from Senegal and the Gambia in collaboration with Coburn’s daughter, who studied classical music and worked in the music department on “Rebel with a Cause.”

“She found a Senegalese professor online who worked with her so she could sing the Wolof,” Coburn said. “Kathryn and I agreed that we had to put the Wolof in the film because it connects Du Bois to his Ghanaian roots. But we had to find it, and we wanted it to be as authentic as possible.”

For Bostic, the challenge was taking an audience from 2026 and presenting the music in a way that would recreate the way in which Du Bois experienced it over a hundred years earlier. “There’s the historic arc of the times, as well as what he’s experiencing emotionally,” Bostic said. In the places where her score was informed by spirituals and the work of the Fisk Jubilee Singers, the composer tried to tie the emotional subtext to the context in which she knew Du Bois was consuming the music.

“When he went to Fisk University, his mother had recently died,” Bostic said. “So that music was a wellspring for his grieving, and I think that depth of sorrow and the catharsis that resulted from that sorrow inspired the sort of deep self-reflection that became such a deep part of who he was. That self-reflection led to a basic decency and humanity, and even now I think of him as being light years ahead of people in the way he embodied justice. I want to shine a light on that.”

Coburn felt a similar sense of purpose, especially since the more she dug into Du Bois’ life the more she realized how relevant his insights were to America in 2026. “He speaks to this current time,” Coburn said, adding that the importance of Du Bois’ work gave her own work a sense of urgency and responsibility. “I knew that it might be 20 or 30 years before we get another Du Bois documentary. I hope not, but I looked at his life and knew that I had to do the best job that I could. We did everything we could for Du Bois because we felt he had done everything he could for us.”

The greatness of “W.E.B. Du Bois: Rebel with a Cause” is that it brings the viewer into Du Bois’ perspective as his world view continues to expand over a 95-year life, and the music is a major part of that subjectivity.

“Kathryn understands that music is a character in any documentary, and her music embodies the character of W.E.B. Du Bois,” Coburn said. “ In the music, we had to grow with him. We had to expand with him. We had to see that his love of Rembrandt and his understanding of classical music, of music that came from an Asian culture, that all of that was important to him. We were catching up with him, and he was driving us.”

“W.E.B. DuBois: Rebel with a Cause” is currently streaming on the PBS app and website.

Running a Tesla with an expired warranty can be nerve-racking, especially because a battery replacement could cost anywhere from about $12,600 to more than $20,000.

How much you end up paying depends on your car's model and what other parts need repairing or swapping, but Tesla gives you no pricing chart to prepare for the expensive journey ahead. The thing is, battery degradation is just basic chemistry, so losing a few miles of range as that EV gets older should not surprise you, even if you avoid the leading causes of EV battery damage. It isn't exactly a sign for you to worry about a full battery replacement either.

A 2025 report from Recurrent looked at over 30,000 EVs overall, and fewer than 4 percent had ever needed battery replacement, excluding major recalls of course. Recurrent used data from across several manufacturers and different model years, so it isn't exactly a Tesla-specific failure rate.

While Tesla keeps its pricing under wraps, Earthling Automotive's 2025 pricing breakdown paints a pretty clear picture of how much you'd pay for a new EV battery. Keeping taxes aside, as they vary from state to state, you could end up shelling out anywhere between $12,600 and $13,350 with installation to replace the battery pack on an older rear-wheel-drive version of the Model 3.

That number shoots up to $17,250 for the all-wheel-drive (AWD) Model 3, and if you own a 2016-to-2021 Model S or Model X, you could be parting with about $19,500 to $20,500. But remember, Earthling's installation charges aren't Tesla Service rates, so your final bill could look a lot different based on things like battery configurations, labor charges and other replacement parts.

In 2021, an invoice for a Model 3 battery replacement made the rounds online. The car had hit a large rock, so Tesla's warranty didn't cover the damage. The invoice listed a remanufactured battery at $13,500, and labor and add-ons brought the total to $16,550.67. If you'd rather not pay Tesla's prices, an independent EV specialist may be able to source a refurbished pack for cheap or repair specific faults, since an EV battery's life isn't always over once it's removed from your car. Still, your final bill depends on what's actually wrong with the battery, and it becomes a hard pill to swallow once it starts to match your aging Tesla's resale value.

Now, before you pull your hair out over signs that it's time to replace your EV battery, take your car in for a full diagnosis. Your best bet is getting Tesla to draw up an itemized bill, which will clearly show the battery cost (be it remanufactured or brand new) along with labor and other add-ons.

Before you pay that bill, though, check whether your warranty already covers it, since Tesla's battery and drive unit warranty stays with you for eight years or 100,000, 120,000 or 150,000 miles, whichever comes first. The mileage limit depends directly on your EV's model and configuration, and Tesla promises at least 70 percent battery capacity retention while the warranty lasts. If your Tesla falls below that threshold, you could get a free repair or even a total battery replacement. If you somehow manage to wreck your battery or need a replacement after all, the new battery comes with a four-year or 50,000-mile parts warranty. And if for some reason Tesla swapped your battery for free under the original coverage, don't expect a fresh eight-year warranty period.

Even with all the support, if you're nervous about the most expensive part of your EV, send another $2,000 Tesla's way for the Battery Extended Service Agreement. You can get this for eligible Model 3 and Model Y cars before the original battery warranty runs out, and depending on what qualifies as a battery failure, you're covered with just a $500 service deductible. Mind you, this won't include regular wear and tear that every EV battery goes through over time. For the extra $2,000, you can add up to 24 months or 30,000 miles of added coverage to your old and faithful EV.

European court: States can turn back migrants in some cases

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Europe's top human rights court ruled that member states can turn back migrants even under "hostile" conditions, unless they've been identified as people who may be subject to ill-treatment.

The European Court of Human Rights on Tuesday ruled that states belonging to the Council of Europe group have a right to control foreign arrivals.

But the court ruling said all 46 member states of the group — that includes all EU and some non-EU countries and promotes democracy and human rights on the continent — must ensure and uphold human rights of people even in hostile environments.

The European court ruling is a complicated one since it deals with a case concerning the entry of nationals of Afghanistan, Iraq and Cuba into EU member countries Poland, Latvia and Lithuania between 2021 and 2022.

That was a time when a border standoff was playing out between Belarus and the EU's eastern member states, with Belarus having been accused of entirely manufacturing a migration crisis to put pressure on the EU.

Belarus arranged for people from various third countries to enter their state and then pointed them to the EU's frontiers.

Europe's most important human rights court on Tuesday ruled that states have a right to control the entry of foreign nationals into their territory even when migration has been weaponized.

The court calls Belarus' hostile actions of 2021 as having been an instance of "instrumentalization of migration."

But the court said that states must still ensure that migrants are protected against inhumane or degrading treatment.

In the context of a surge in migration, for example, national authorities were still to identify people who were particularly at risk of suffering or enduring ill treatment.

The migrants in the case brought before the European court said that they had been violently prevented from crossing into the EU without their asylum applications having been examined and that their human rights had been violated.

"In a context of instrumentalization of migration, the removal of a foreign national to the instrumentalizing state would not, as such, constitute a violation of Article 3 (prohibition of torture and inhuman and degrading treatment)," the court press release said.

The court ordered Poland, Latvia and Lithuania to pay compensation for their treatment of migrants at the EU's external borders with Belarus.

Five EU countries also last week announced a plan with Rwanda to establish a so-called return hub in the African nation.

The hub will host rejected asylum seekers waiting to return to their countries of origin.

But there are many concerns about the project since Rwanda ranks low on human rights measures and there are questions about whether people's rights will be sufficiently protected.

New York Times Prohibits Photographers From Using Photoshop 2027

The latest version of Adobe Photoshop, Photoshop 2027 (28.0.0), added many AI-powered photo-editing features. Not all photographers are thrilled about the increasingly heavy focus on generative AI inside Photoshop, and photojournalists are taking note, too.

As The Verge reports, The New York Times sent an email to freelance photographers and contributors warning them against using Adobe Photoshop 2027.

The email, which was shared on social media, specifically cites features inside Photoshop that use generative AI “in ways that may not be apparent to users.”

The Times‘ worries about Photoshop 28.0.0 are more about a perceived lack of clarity surrounding the application’s photo-editing features than the presence of AI itself. Photoshop has included generative AI in many tools for years, including Generative Remove and Generative Expand. The latest release uses generative AI for even more tasks, such as relighting and restyling photos.

New York Times photojournalists and freelance contributors cannot use generative AI on their photos. In fact, as the publication’s ethics standards outline, all published photos must be accurate depictions of real events. This means no people or objects can be added, rearranged, blurred, distorted, or removed. It also means that cropping must be done carefully so as not to omit any relevant details in a scene. Color can be adjusted, but only minimally and in service of accurately reproducing an event as it happened.

The Times adds that any use of AI, including generative AI, must be clearly disclosed to readers. The organization has long embraced Adobe’s C2PA initiative as it relates to image provenance and metadata, and that is not changing.

What has changed, apparently, is how easy it is for a photographer to inadvertently use a feature inside Adobe Photoshop that relies on generative AI, which will then show up in the metadata. This appears to be the primary concern for The New York Times, as it cannot work with photos that are labeled as AI-edited content.

“It has come to our attention that certain functions in Adobe Photoshop 2027 utilize generative AI in ways that may not be apparent to users of the software. At this time we are requiring that all contributing photographers avoid using this new version when working on assignment for The Times,” New York Times spokesperson Graham James tells PetaPixel.

To prevent these issues, all contributing photographers must entirely avoid using Adobe Photoshop 2027 (28.0.0 and subsequent updates) when working on assignments for The Times—an unprecedented blow to Photoshop’s already wavering reputation among professional photographers. Fortunately, it is possible for users to install previous versions of Photoshop through the Creative Cloud app, although it’s not necessarily straightforward. As of this writing, users can roll back to Photoshop 27.0, but no earlier.

Adobe’s insistence on publicizing its generative AI features, rather than the genuinely good and mostly inoffensive improvements made earlier this month to Adobe Camera Raw and Lightroom, has created quite a stir in the photography community. The Times‘s new policy will certainly not help Adobe’s PR problem with photographers.

PetaPixel reached out to Adobe for comment but did not hear back ahead of publication. This story will be updated with any response.

It’s a short workweek for most of us here in the United States. In just a day or two, many of us will load up our cars and trek around the country to our loved ones. If you’re reading this, there’s a decent chance you’ll be taking on of those trips in an electric vehicle. EVs have come a long way, so in theory, range anxiety shouldn’t be much of an issue for anyone these days. Still, with so many drivers on the roads, there are bound to be annoyances that come with long EV road trips.

Here are a few tips that can take the edge off a long trip.

DC fast charging times are getting better and better, but charging still takes longer than stopping for gas. Thus, bottlenecks can occur on well-traveled routes with limited DC fast charging stops. It’s just kind of the nature of the beast when most people are stopping for 20-30 minutes. Multiply that by the plethora of drivers also trying to charge up and get home, and well, it’s not hard to see why EV charging queues can balloon, adding time to your drive home.

But you can head some of that off if you prep the night before. For those with home charging, that means simply plugging the car in and ensuring it’s fully charged for the next day’s trip. For those who don’t have access to home charging, using a DC fast charger the night before, when everyone isn’t rushing to leave, could ease some of the frustration of charging up before getting on the roads.

Also, some DC fast charging companies give a discount for charging during off-peak hours. Prepping the night before won’t just save you time, it might save you money.

We may have a set route in mind for our trip, and that could be all fine and dandy, but is it really the best route for our cars? Electric vehicles tend to consume more energy at higher speeds and when traveling up hills. Thus, it could be advantageous to maximize range by picking a flatter route, or one with lower speeds.

In most cases this won’t be necessary, but if you’re pushing the limits of your car and the infrastructure around you, it’s good to know. Most route planning apps—like A Better Route Planner, Plugshare, or even Google Maps—take into account which route is friendlier to your car’s range calculator, and may suggest detours if you get a big range boost from a route that’s nearly as quick.

These route planners will tell you exactly when and where to stop, depending on your car’s efficiency, your destination, your desired state of charge at arrival, and road conditions along the way. They can be adjusted to fit your real-world efficiency data or your need for breaks. Rather than just winging, using a route planner ensures you’re never in an uncomfortable situation without the range or charging ability to get where you need to go.

This goes hand in hand with route planning, but I think it is relevant here. The Sunday after Thanksgiving is typically the single busiest travel day of the year. You’re not the only one on the road who will need charging. Prepping the night before can help with the initial charging wait times at the start of your trip. If you’re on a long journey, chances are you’ll find yourself in a big queue at a strange charger.

When you’re planning your route with charging stops, pay attention to what brands they are and what ports they use. An increasing number of highway stops often have several competing charging stations. A Tesla Supercharging station could have an Ionna Rechargery nearby. Or maybe it’s an EV Go station. Or maybe it’s a charging station by Circle K or Shell. In any case, they tend to cluster together near high-power infrastructure. Rather than fumble with apps and payments at the station, I think it’s best if you research the charging companies on your route and pre-emptively install their apps and set up payment.

Similarly, bring an adapter. NACS is quickly making its way across the country, but not every station has NACS ports, and not every one has CCS. Some of the stations with your desired plug will likely be busy. An adapter that allows your car to access both NACS and CCS ports will save a lot of headaches.

Once again, you’re not the only one trying to get home for the holidays. Let’s keep the queue moving, and try not to charge more than 80%, if possible. Charging speeds slow substantially when EV batteries surpass 80%. It may have only taken 15-20 minutes to get to 80%, but it’ll take another 20 or so to reach 100%, or much longer in some cars. Chances are, there’s someone waiting. Not only is it courteous to unplug and move on, but it’s also probably quicker to charge again (if you need it) closer to your destination.

I know this list has mostly been about minimizing time lost due to charging, but I think it’s important to remember that it’s okay to take a break. Charging stops are inherently longer than refuelling a gas car, but I think that 15-30 minute break is helpful for safe driving. Taking a rest, using the restroom, or getting a snack can take the edge off a trip. Use an EV’s characteristics to your advantage.

I’ve been doing EV road trips since 2021, and it’s amazing to see just how far both vehicle range and charging infrastructure have come. Long trips are possible for most drivers these days, whereas years ago, it was a huge hardship or even impossible. With better apps, better infrastructure, and better cars, it’s only gotten easier. But it still pays to know what you’re doing. Drive safely out there, folks.

United Airlines Remembers Your Bad Flight And May Upgrade You Next Time

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

United Airlines appears to be quietly remembering when passengers suffer through a bad trip and then surprising some of them with a complimentary Economy Plus seat on a future flight. It costs United very little when the seat would otherwise remain empty, but it communicates something valuable to the passenger: we remember what happened to you.

A passenger recently shared an interesting message from United Airlines with aviation insider JonNYC.

The traveler had missed a connection about a month earlier and then received the following message before another United flight:

“We know your previous travel experience may have faced some challenges, and we want to make your journey more comfortable.”

United moved the passenger to Economy Plus, offering additional legroom at no charge.

What makes the gesture especially interesting is that the passenger had no MileagePlus elite status. The upcoming flight was lightly booked, which presumably made it easier for United to offer the seat without giving up revenue.

JonNYC described it as United’s technology performing more “dark magic,” and I think that is a fair description. The system appears to have connected an earlier operational disruption with a future reservation, then identified an inexpensive opportunity to improve the next journey.

Reports of similar gestures stretch back at least to 2025, so this does not appear to be entirely new. One United Silver member reported receiving an Economy Plus assignment after an eight-hour delay, even after United had already provided a $75 travel credit.

But United has not publicly announced this as a formal benefit, and we do not know precisely how passengers are selected (not that I would expect otherwise with such a “Surprise and Delight” program, lest it become an entitlement).

With advancing technology, airlines already know when they have inconvenienced you.

They know when your flight was delayed, when you missed a connection, and when you were rebooked. They also know whether you have another reservation and which seats remain available on that flight.

Yet customers are often forced to explain the entire situation again in a complaint, including information the airline already has in its own systems. That creates more work for the passenger after the airline has already disrupted the trip, though I’m sadly not expecting carriers to offer proactive compensation, even when delays are within its control.

United appears to be using its data more intelligently, like with its ConnectionSaver technology, that can help some passengers without inconveniencing others.

I have written before about United introducing technology that saves callers from repeating information when they are transferred from an automated system to a human agent. I also welcomed the addition of an app feature that watches earlier flights for disrupted passengers while preserving their confirmed reservations.

This Economy Plus gesture reflects a similar approach: use the information already available to reduce friction and improve the passenger experience.

An Economy Plus seat is not a first class upgrade. The passenger receives more legroom, but the onboard service remains the same as regular economy class.

Even so, it has value. United charges for these seats, sometimes quite a bit depending upon the route and seat location. Receiving one unexpectedly can make a future flight noticeably more comfortable.

The economics also make sense for United. If the flight is lightly booked and the Economy Plus seat would otherwise remain empty, the marginal cost of assigning it to an inconvenienced passenger is essentially zero.

The value to the passenger can be much greater than the cost to the airline.

I also appreciate that the message explicitly connects the better seat to the previous experience. A random seat reassignment might go unnoticed, while a clearly explained gesture tells the passenger that United remembers what happened and wants another chance to deliver a better trip.

That is smart service recovery…I wonder if the system is smart enough to do this when passengers have to be moved anyway due to weight and balance reasons? (why let a good opportunity go to waste?)

This is not a published benefit, and passengers should not expect a complimentary Economy Plus assignment every time something goes wrong. We do not know what level of disruption qualifies, how much empty space must be available, or whether United considers the passenger’s fare or travel history…and that’s the point, lest it become an expectation.

And of course United must be careful when selecting a seat to avoid a familiar complaint on Delta Air Lines. Moving someone from a regular economy aisle seat to an Economy Plus middle seat will not strike every passenger as an improvement. Ideally, the system should preserve the passenger’s aisle or window preference whenever possible or offer the upgrade rather than automatically moving the traveler.

Furthermore, if a MileagePlus Premier is expecting an entire row and suddenly has a seatmate, I can understand there will be annoyance (though that’s a sort of entitlement that is not helpful either)…in the past I’ve passed on upgrades to domestic first class in order to stretch across multiple seats in economy class.

Still, those limitations do not diminish the appeal of the idea. This is a discretionary gesture, not something passengers can demand, and that element of surprise is part of what makes it effective.

When airlines discuss service recovery, the conversation often centers on travel credits or miles. Those can be appropriate, particularly after a serious operational failure. But compensation need not be limited to those forms.

A better seat, lounge invitation, meal voucher, or another small benefit can communicate recognition when it is offered thoughtfully. The key is identifying something the passenger will appreciate that the airline can provide at a modest cost.

United has become particularly skilled at using technology to address operational problems. ConnectionSaver is the best example, intelligently holding certain flights when connecting passengers can reach the gate without causing a significant delay.

United appears to be quietly offering complimentary Economy Plus seats to some passengers whose previous trips went poorly, including travelers without elite status.

The gesture will not erase a missed connection or make every passenger forget a miserable journey. But it shows that United remembers the disruption and recognizes an opportunity to do something helpful. An empty Economy Plus seat costs United almost nothing. Used this way, it can generate far more goodwill than another automated apology email…it’s a positive step.

Tidal announces ban on AI-generated music; 27 million tracks will be deleted

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AI songs account for 6.3 percent of Tidal’s catalog, but it’s about to be zero.

Most streaming music providers have opted to label AI-generated content on their platforms, and until now, Tidal was one of them. However, the company is about to take a harder stance. Tidal announced that in the coming weeks, it will fully ban AI music because of the harm it does to human artists.

Tidal debuted its initial AI policy in July 2026, which included labeling and demonetizing all songs created entirely with AI. That wasn’t enough to stem the tide of AI slop, though. Over the past 90 days, Tidal reports that 43 percent of new uploads were completely AI-generated. On November 6, new uploads to Tidal hit a whopping 60 percent AI-generated.

As things stand, Tidal hosts 27 million tracks labeled as AI, all of which were obviously added in the past couple of years. That accounts for 6.3 percent of Tidal’s total catalog. On December 9, Tidal will delete all those songs. The streamer notes this will have essentially no negative impact because 96 percent of AI-labeled tracks got zero plays in the past 90 days. Throughout its announcement, Tidal is careful to stipulate that it’s only talking about music that has been fully created by AI. Artists can still use AI tools as long as the final product contains human elements.

Credit: Tidal Credit: Tidal

Some people don’t particularly mind AI music, but the team at Tidal has come to believe the harm to artists is too great. AI models can pump out random noises faster than humans can create things of real value, which can mislead listeners and siphon royalty payments away from creators. There’s also an issue with AI uploaders actively attempting to steal royalty payments. Tidal says it has already removed about 66,000 tracks from its service for impersonating artists, which was against its AI policy even before this update.

As of December 9, individual uploaders and distributors will no longer be allowed to add AI music to Tidal. However, they could lie, couldn’t they?

Tidal hasn’t specified how it detects AI music, but it will probably rely on watermarking. Almost every AI song on Tidal (94 percent) comes from Suno, which added watermarking earlier this year. Tidal also cites Google Lyria as a source of AI tunes, which has SynthID labels embedded in the waveform. Deezer claimed it has a licensable AI music detector with a false-positive rate of less than 0.01 percent, but it’s unclear if Tidal is using that or something similar.

That will take care of most AI music, but this approach won’t detect everything. There are open models for music generation that you can run locally on a GPU, and some cloud-based providers aren’t bothering to watermark their outputs. Even if Tidal is using a separate detection system, new models may be able to evade it—it’s always going to be a cat-and-mouse game. This will be a problem with verifying provenance in all types of media in the future. For the time being, at least, Tidal’s policy should clear out most of the AI slop.

The people directing robots to make these songs will have plenty of other options after Tidal blocks AI. Spotify, YouTube Music, Deezer, and Apple Music all allow AI music on their platforms with labeling. Spotify has removed millions of AI tracks from its collection based on user feedback. Other streamers haven’t specified how much slop (if any) they’ve cleared out.
