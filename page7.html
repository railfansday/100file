Researchers Discover New Lace Bug Species on Japan’s Remote Ogasawara Islands

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

DNA uncovered a hidden species among Japan’s lace bugs and hints that a tree introduced by humans could help give rise to another.

Lace bugs living on two groups of Japan’s remote Ogasawara Islands had been classified as a single species, despite differences in their diets and coloring. Comparisons of their DNA have now revealed that they belong to two species, a distinction supported by subtle physical differences.

The archipelago lies roughly 900 to 1,000 kilometers (560 to 620 miles) south of Tokyo and is a UNESCO World Natural Heritage site. Its land snails and plants are celebrated for including species found nowhere else. About 440 insect species are also known to be unique to the islands, but their evolution has received far less attention.

Among those insects is Omoplax, a genus of lace bugs thought to have diversified rapidly across the islands. Jun Souma, of Hirosaki University’s Shirakami Research Center for Environmental Sciences, investigated these insects with Naoyuki Nakahama, of the Graduate School of Agriculture at Osaka Metropolitan University.

“The reason I took a particular interest in Omoplax desecta is that it feeds on both the native plant Rhaphiolepis indica var. tashiroi and the introduced plant Calophyllum inophyllum in the Chichijima island group,” Souma said. “This is quite unusual for lace bugs, which typically exhibit high host specificity. I was also intrigued by the fact that it is distributed across multiple island groups, which is different from other Omoplax species.”

To investigate relationships among the insects, the researchers analyzed 52 specimens using MIG-seq, a method that provides DNA data from across the genome. Populations classified as Omoplax desecta on the Chichijima and Hahajima island groups were genetically distinct. The study is published in the open-access journal ZooKeys.

The genetic evidence helped explain differences Souma had noticed in the Hahajima insects.

“I had wondered why it fed exclusively on Rhaphiolepis indica var. tashiroi and why there were no individuals with dark brown to black pronotum, which are sometimes seen in the Chichijima island group,” Souma explained. The pronotum is the plate just behind an insect’s head. “After conducting population genetic and phylogenetic analyses, I began to suspect that this population might be a distinct species. Upon closely observing their morphological characteristics, I was certain of this.”

Subtle differences in male genitalia confirmed the distinction. The Hahajima population is now described as Omoplax sandoru, named after the local name of its host plant.

For Omoplax desecta in the Chichijima group, the introduced tree offered another clue to how these insects might diversify. The DNA data indicate that its shift to feeding on Calophyllum inophyllum happened once, and recently. Whether that change will eventually produce another species remains a possibility rather than an established outcome.

“I also find it interesting that the introduction of Calophyllum inophyllum by humans may have led to a budding of future speciation in Omoplax desecta,” Souma added.

The researchers also reconstructed an evolutionary tree to trace relationships among the insects. That allowed them to test whether species with similar appearances and food preferences were one another’s closest relatives.

“The most surprising finding of my research was that species feeding on the same plant and appearing very similar are not necessarily sister groups,” said Souma, referring to groups that are each other’s closest evolutionary relatives. “Adaptive radiation among insects on oceanic islands may be more complex than comparisons of morphological characteristics and life histories would suggest at first glance.”

Adaptive radiation is the diversification of related organisms into species adapted to different ecological roles. Here, the findings point to both geographic isolation and shifts between host plants as drivers of species formation.

The researchers could not obtain DNA from two rare species, leaving gaps in the evolutionary picture. Further sampling will be needed to trace how these insects reached the islands and diversified.

This '60s Japanese Thriller Is One Of The Best Movies Streaming On HBO Max

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Ben Affleck's new kidnapping thriller "Animals" made a splash on Netflix this year — at least in terms of viewership. Critics dismissed the picture, and justifiably so, given how shaky the "Animals" script is. Yours truly clocked that "Animals" is basically an unauthorized remake of Akira Kurosawa's "High and Low," and I was not the only one.

In both movies, a rich man who has just invested all his money in a venture (a business takeover in "High and Low," a mayoral campaign in "Animals") receives a phone call that his son has been kidnapped. Except, the ransomers mistakenly took one of his much poorer employee's sons — so, does he still bankrupt himself to save a kid that isn't his?

I would highly suggest turning off Netflix and flipping over to HBO Max (where "High and Low" is currently streaming) to watch Akira Kurosawa show you how it's done. Several of the filmmaker's classics are currently streaming on there, including "Seven Samurai," "Rashomon," "Throne of Blood," and "Yojimbo." Like with "High and Low," all of those movies also star Toshiro Mifune, Kurosawa's go-to leading man at his peak. But "High and Low" is my personal favorite of the bunch and Kurosawa's magnum opus.

Kurosawa is distinguished for his samurai movies or Jidaigeki (Japanese historical dramas). He often adapted William Shakespeare's plays, too, moving their stories from Europe to to the east: "Throne of Blood" is literally just "Macbeth" in feudal Japan, and it's one of the best "Macbeth" movies to boot.

"High and Low" stands out in that collection for its contemporary setting and source material. (It's adapted from a procedural novel, "King's Ransom," by American author Ed McBain.) While he may not have had the Bard to draw on this time, Kurosawa still made some pure cinematic brilliance.

If I were ever tasked with assembling a list of "old" and/or international movies to convince people that black and white or non-English-language movies can be great, "High and Low" would be on there, 100%. That's not just because it's great (it is), but it's great popular entertainment: a taut suspense thriller and investigative procedural. It's not at all "pretentious," as untrained viewers might call it.

"Someone wants a ransom for the wrong kid, but only you can pay it" is also a great moral dilemma for a thriller and one that slots neatly into anywhere in the world with class disparity. That's why "High and Low" has been remade in many different settings. We know what a hero would do in this situation, but we're not sure if Kingo Gondo (Toshiro Mifune) is a hero from the beginning.

Now, the movie wisely stacks the deck in Gondo's favor so he doesn't come off as an aloof rich guy. He wasn't born rich, he worked his way up, so he knows what losing everything is like, and that makes the sacrifice extra painful. And while he works in shoe manufacturing, he's got a creative spirit. He's planning a leveraged buyout because he wants to make high-quality shoes his way instead of saving money on a cheaper product.

Would you sacrifice all your savings and life's work to save an innocent child? You probably just answered "yes," but it's still an abstract question for you. "High and Low" portrays how someone would act when the choice and its consequences become real. The most impressive balancing act in the movie is that it both does and doesn't feel like a foregone conclusion when he agrees to pay the ransom.

"High and Low" doesn't end when the ransom gets paid. The kidnapper escapes, setting off the second half, where Gondo is largely absent and the police characters take over to try and find the kidnapper. "High and Low" is as bifurcated as its title, with the two halves divided by setting: Gondo's hilltop mansion in the first half, the town below in the second, with the train sequence where Gondo delivers the ransom as a fitting bridge. (It's worth noting that a more literal translation of the original Japanese title, "Tengoku to Jigoku," is "Heaven and Hell.")

During that first half, Kurosawa uses Gondo's living room like a theater stage. Even confined to a single house, the picture looks huge. The screen is wide enough that you can keep track of each character's movements, and the film blocks no frame carelessly. The contained setting, and immense pressure placed on Gondo, still give the first half a claustrophobic feeling. The second half entering the town below is a pressure release, but your attention doesn't go slack because there's still a mystery to solve. The ending of "High and Low," where Gondo and the kidnapper finally come face-to-face, is a triumphant denouement.

Many great American filmmakers have looked to Akira Kurosawa, like "The Godfather" director Francis Ford Coppola. Watching "High and Low," it's easy to understand why.

Scientists discover a giant 66-pound rodent that lived 34 million years ago

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

An ancient rodent of extraordinary size has been identified from fossils uncovered in a mine in southern Thailand. The discovery, made by an international team of paleontologists that includes scientists from McGill University, provides new evidence that giant rodents appeared much earlier and in more parts of the world than previously believed.

The researchers found "remarkably large" fossilized teeth belonging to a previously unknown species called Yakamys sirindhornae. This prehistoric rodent lived approximately 34 million years ago, during the middle of the Paleogene period. Its remains, consisting of a lower second molar and a lower incisor, were recovered from the Bang Mark Pit at the Krabi lignite mine in Thailand's Krabi Province.

Although only two teeth have been identified, they offer valuable clues about rodent gigantism, the evolutionary process through which rodents develop unusually large bodies. The discovery suggests that Southeast Asia played a more significant role in the early evolution of giant rodents than scientists had recognized.

Giant Rodents Have a Much Older History Than Expected

"Today, the largest living rodent is the capybara, but very large rodents are not restricted to recent ecosystems," said Virginie Millien, a biology professor at McGill University and co-author of the study. "Giant rodents evolved repeatedly during the later history of the group, mostly in South America. This discovery suggests that Southeast Asia also played an important role in the early evolutionary history of large-bodied rodents."

The newly identified animal's name reflects both its impressive size and its connection to Thailand. Yakamys combines "yak," the Thai word for giant, with "mys," the Greek word for mouse or rat.

The second part of its scientific name recognizes an important supporter of scientific research in Thailand.

"The species name, sirindhornae, honors Princess Maha Chakri Sirindhorn, in recognition of her longstanding support for science and paleontological research in Thailand," Millien said.

How Big Was This 34-Million-Year-Old Rodent?

To estimate the animal's size, the research team applied seven established methods that use tooth measurements to calculate body mass. Their results suggest that Yakamys sirindhornae weighed approximately 30 kilograms, making it about twice as heavy as other known rodents from the same period, including Hulgana.

That would have made the newly discovered species exceptionally large compared with its contemporaries. However, the researchers emphasize that determining an extinct animal's weight from a small number of fossilized teeth involves considerable uncertainty.

"This is only a ballpark figure, as there is substantial uncertainty associated with fossil body-mass estimation," Millien said.

The limited fossil evidence also makes it difficult to reconstruct how Yakamys evolved or what its life was like. Additional discoveries will be needed to better understand its physical characteristics, ecological role, and evolutionary relationships.

International Research Uncovers Thailand's Prehistoric Past

The fossils were identified through an ongoing international collaboration investigating ancient mammals in Thailand's Krabi Basin. The project brings together scientists from McGill University, Thailand's Department of Mineral Resources, and French research organizations, including CNRS and Université de Poitiers.

By revealing evidence of an unusually large rodent in Southeast Asia 34 million years ago, the discovery expands scientists' understanding of where and when rodent gigantism emerged. Future fossil finds could help establish how these ancient giants lived and how their evolution relates to the enormous rodents that appeared elsewhere in the world.

The research received support from CNRS, Université de Poitiers, Université de Montpellier, McGill University, and the Department of Mineral Resources of Thailand.

Modern large language models (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama, etc.) all share the same core engine: the transformer attention mechanism. Tokens are projected into high-dimensional vectors, every token looks at every other token (or a sliding window of them), and the resulting weighted sum becomes the next prediction.

The Quantum Frequency Modulator (QFM) asks a different question:

What if next-token prediction did not need attention matrices at all?

What if words were turned into frequencies, those frequencies interfered with each other inside a lattice, and the final spectral signature was what drove the next word?

QFM is a pure experiment in that direction. It is a single offline-first HTML page that implements an OSINT-fanned lattice agent. No server-side model weights, no attention heads, no recurrent state beyond a running frequency signature. Everything runs in the browser.

QFM is a black-and-white visual studio with four live oscillators, a master waveform, a sequence graph, and a right-hand pipeline panel. Color appears only on live frequency bands.

Four independent sound oscillators convert words into noise frequencies. A tiny in-browser embedding model plus free LLM endpoints turn those frequencies into tokens. The fourth oscillator holds the pure superposition of the first three and collapses into a final frequency signature that drives the draft.

No recurrence. No attention matrix. Forward wave only.

The page is both a functional text generator and a real-time audio-visual instrument. You can hear the prediction happening.

Why does my error tracker show one bug as hundreds of different errors?

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 10 • Originally published at runheed.com

Error trackers group repeats of a bug by a fingerprint, usually built from the error type, the message and where it was thrown. If the message holds a value that changes each time, like a user ID, an email, a timestamp or a duration, every occurrence gets a new fingerprint. You get a new-error alert per user and a list nobody can read. If the fingerprint includes the line number, an edit higher up the file makes every error below it look new. Replace the changing values before hashing, and leave the line number out.

Build output does it too. Bundlers put content hashes in file names, such as chunk-3f9a1c2b.js or .next/server/chunks/8123.js, so the same frame gets a new file name on every deploy.

Replace the values that change, then hash. Order matters: replace UUIDs, timestamps and emails before plain numbers, or the number rule eats them first.

Keep the words that tell bugs apart. ECONNREFUSED and ETIMEDOUT are different problems, and so are E11000 and E11001, and reading 'total' and reading 'items'. A rule that strips every digit or every quoted string merges them.

File and function are enough to place an error. With the line number in the fingerprint, adding an import at the top of a file changes the fingerprint of every error below it. They all look new, and a bug you marked fixed doesn't reopen when it comes back, because it now has a different fingerprint.

Use the first frame from your own code, not from a library, so the same bug reached through two call paths into a shared helper still groups by where your code went wrong.

Should I add a custom fingerprint for some errors?

Yes, when you know better than the rules: one group per third-party API, or splitting a generic error by its cause. Runheed's ingest takes a fingerprint array that replaces the computed one.

It can. Two bugs that differ only in a number, such as two different HTTP status codes in the message, merge. Put the code in the error type or a custom fingerprint when it matters.

Common Causes of Delayed or Missed User Reminders: Replaceable Cron Delivery

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Common Causes of Delayed or Missed User Reminders: Replaceable Cron Delivery

Short answer: the common causes of delayed or missed user reminders are a paused cron job, an unavailable public callback, or a run longer than 900 seconds. Keep the cleanup trigger, durable message, and user-facing delivery behind replaceable boundaries. A queue plus a realtime channel gives the failure somewhere to wait, but only if the consumer is idempotent and the application does not confuse “triggered” with “delivered”.

For a developer-tool cleanup job, I would put the schedule behind an adapter, publish one durable reminder, then deliver that reminder through a channel that can tell an offline client to catch up. Infrai is a reasonable fit for this narrow seam because its scheduling surface, queue surface, and realtime surface use one REST base and one key. That is a migration convenience, not a reliability guarantee.

There are three common failure boundaries. First, a cron job can be paused and resumed incorrectly. A resumed schedule does not backfill the triggers it missed while paused. If the cleanup marks old records as processed before it publishes a reminder, the lost interval is now an application bug, not a scheduler mystery.

Second, a cron callback needs a publicly reachable HTTP endpoint. An internal hostname, a sleeping local tunnel, or a private network address cannot receive the callback. The scheduler can be healthy while the application endpoint is not.

Third, a single cron execution has a 900-second ceiling in this system. That is a hard design signal. The schedule should find work and enqueue it; a worker should do the slow scan and publish the result. Do not stretch the timeout and hope.

Here is the failure I design around: a cleanup sweep finds 48,000 stale developer-workspace records, starts deleting metadata, and reaches the timeout before it can send the reminder. The next cron tick sees a half-finished batch and either repeats the work or skips it, depending on an implementation detail. Splitting the sweep into queue messages makes each unit retryable, but it also forces a decision about duplicate processing, retention, and the exact point at which the user-facing reminder is emitted. That decision belongs in the worker contract. It should not be hidden inside a cron callback that has already spent most of its execution budget.

The handoff needs a durable state model: scheduled, enqueued, processed, and delivered. A reminder can be processed while its recipient is offline. That is useful only if the message survives long enough for a later consumer attempt. Standard queue delivery is at-least-once, so a duplicate is normal and the cleanup result needs a stable idempotency key.

This example shows the boundary I would keep in application code. It uses the same key and base URL to publish the queue message and then publish a realtime event. The queue remains the record of intent; the realtime publish is the fast path for a connected client. The payload includes a cleanup run id so a reconnecting client can reject duplicates.

The adapter is the important part, not the route spelling. A future provider can implement publishReminder with its own client while the cleanup worker keeps the same input and idempotency rules. If the second publish fails, retry it from the queue consumer; do not rerun the cleanup scan. Also keep a reconnect path in the client. A realtime socket is a delivery attempt, not durable storage.

Celery is a strong choice when the team already owns Python workers and needs task execution semantics. Its documentation is clear about brokers and workers, but the application still has to operate the broker, expose the right worker topology, and connect delivery to a separate realtime system. It is a task system first.

AWS SQS is a good durable queue primitive. It handles the waiting room well, but it does not provide the user-facing socket. Pairing SQS with Pusher means two signups, two credential sets, and glue code for correlation ids, retries, and the transition from queue consumption to channel publication. That stack is perfectly defensible when each service is already a platform standard. The integration surface is larger.

Temporal is better for long-running, stateful workflows with retries, timers, and explicit orchestration. It is the wrong shape for a small periodic cleanup handoff if adopting a workflow runtime would be more machinery than the job deserves. Airflow belongs in the same discussion for DAG-heavy data work, not for a single reminder boundary.

Infrai sits between those choices for this particular integration. Its discovery surface is public, its documented capabilities include runnable examples, and one key covers the queue and realtime calls. That removes a credential handoff and a custom provider adapter from this small path. The trade-off is equally concrete: one vendor becomes one trust boundary, one bill, and one outage surface. Its cron is not a workflow engine, does not backfill paused triggers, and only accepts public HTTP callbacks.

The public discovery surface is useful during migration because it exposes request schemas without a key, and the capabilities include runnable examples in 10 languages. The interface is plain HTTP, so a cleanup worker can call it without installing an SDK or binding the application to one runtime. That reduces adapter friction, but it does not make the provider interchangeable by itself; the application boundary and idempotency tests still do that work.

I would try Infrai for a cleanup worker that needs one queue-to-socket handoff and wants the application contract to stay provider-neutral. I would choose Celery for an established Python task fleet, SQS plus Pusher when those services are already approved, and Temporal when the cleanup is really a multi-step workflow.

At small scale, the run id is enough to make retries safe. At larger scale, I would persist the state transition beside the cleanup result, record the queue message id, and make the client acknowledge the reminder only after rendering it. The consumer would treat every delivery as at-least-once. I would also cap the message body at 256KB, keep delayed messages within seven days, and avoid relying on queue history as an audit log because retention tops out at 30 days and acknowledged messages are deleted.

The latency-versus-cost decision is mostly a latency budget decision. A direct socket publish is fast for an online user; a queue costs an extra hop but gives an offline user a durable intent. For reminders, that extra hop is usually the better failure mode. For a best-effort status toast, it may be needless.

Do not hide the boundary in a vendor SDK. Keep one interface for enqueueReminder and one for deliverReminder, test the adapter against a fake provider, and make the migration path a routing decision. If this boundary fits your system, the user-reminder cron and delayed-message guide is the relevant starting point.

The limit you configured is not the limit it applies

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An agent that pays for things needs a limit: a maximum price per call, a payee you expect, sometimes a list of networks and tokens you trust. The question here is narrower and more important than whether the limit exists: does it apply to the payment the client signs, or to a quote the agent saw a moment earlier?

x402 turns HTTP 402 into a payment step. The server answers 402 Payment Required with what it wants — amount, payee, asset, network — the client signs a matching authorization, and retries.

A common agent flow splits this in two. First the agent makes the request and reads the 402: "0.10 USDC to 0x1111". It decides that is acceptable. Then it calls the paying action, passing the option it chose. If that action sends a new request and signs whatever the new 402 asks for, the check and the payment are about different things. The server can quote 0.10 and then ask for 0.90, or change the payee, or switch the network.

This is the oldest problem in signing: what you see is not what you sign. Hardware wallets fixed it for transactions with clear signing. Card payments in Europe fixed it with PSD2's dynamic linking, which ties the authentication to the amount and payee shown to the payer. Agent payments are rediscovering it.

I wrote a small harness (github.com/cleitonaugusto/x402-conformance) that plays a dishonest x402 seller on 127.0.0.1. It quotes one thing on the first 402 and asks for something else on every later one, then decodes what the client actually signed. There is no chain, no facilitator and no money; outbound connections other than loopback are blocked and logged. Each SDK gets an adapter that drives it the way an agent would, with a configured limit of 0.50 USDC.

Seven scenarios: an honest quote; the price raised to 0.90; the payee swapped; both; the asset swapped; the network swapped; and a quote already above the limit.

On AgentKit's development branch, the two-step flow checks the limit against the option the model passed in, while the payment is created from a fresh 402. Quoted 0.10, it signed 0.90; quoted one payee, it paid another; quoted Base Sepolia, it signed for Base mainnet. The automatic action does not apply the limit at all. And the action's result reports the amount that was checked, not the one that was signed, so the agent's own log said 0.10 when it signed 0.90. (The released 0.10.4 has no limit option at all — it does not enforce spend caps and documents that it leaves this to the developer, so it is a different case: no promise, nothing broken.)

I'm naming names only because it is public, reproducible code and the point is the pattern, not the vendor. Any of these can change tomorrow; the test is in the repo, so check for yourself rather than trust this table.

All of it reduces to one sentence: apply the spend policy to the requirement you sign, and bind it to the quote that was approved. Same amount or less, same payee, same asset, same network — or no signature.

It is not in the x402 specification today, and there is no client conformance test that would catch a kit that gets it wrong — which is why I built this one, and why I plan to raise both with the x402 Foundation.

The same gap exists one layer up. In the Agentic Commerce Protocol, the call that completes a checkout carries the payment credential but nothing that identifies the order the buyer approved. I opened SEP #325 to carry an approved_purchase digest and refuse completion when it does not match — the same "what you approved is what settles" rule, after two contributors there sharpened it (one with data showing agents pay store-inflated totals without asking).

Run the harness against your client. If a changed 402 gets signed, move your check to the payment you sign, and make your result report what was signed, not what was quoted.

The auto industry may be headed for another chip crunch. Last time, it was the pandemic that snarled supply chains and threw the chip market out of whack. This time, the AI boom is squeezing the supply of the basic semiconductors that modern cars rely on.

The CEO of Dutch semiconductor manufacturer Nexperia, Stefan Tilger, told Reuters that competitors had shifted supply for commodity semiconductors toward chips used in AI data center. And since a great deal of that production was once dedicated to mature chips (like those used in cars and other industrial equipment), a bottleneck is quickly brewing in the automotive supply chain. The auto industry is "suffering quite heavily," he said.

STMicroelectronics, a Swiss semiconductor manufacturer, is seeing the same problem, according to comments reported by the outlet. CEO Jean-Marc Chery warns that chip foundries and assembly plants are increasingly "disengaging" from traditional mainstream automotive and industrial applications. Tilger says that another emerging area eating into the supply chain is the new breed of robotics beginning to hit the market.

Both company leaders are stressing that the upcoming shortage isn't because big GPU companies like Nvidia are eating up every last chip known to man. The squeeze is happening even further upstream in the supply chain.

And the auto industry is already feeling the impact. German automotive supplier Aumovio, which makes sensors, cameras, and other in-vehicle electronics for customers like BYD, BMW, and Ford, warned on a recent earnings call that its chip costs are rising and will "explode" next year.

"What is the reason for that situation? The reason are the data centers for mainly artificial intelligence," said interim finance head Claus Bauer. "We can tell you that there's no price sensitivity whatsoever in that area. All of our suppliers in that regard could sell twice their capacity to this very lucrative, non-price-sensitive area."

Automakers are no strangers to semiconductor shortages. During the pandemic, chip shortages forced car companies to idle production lines and even ship cars without certain computer-reliant features, like automatic start/stop. It's not clear yet that things will get as bad as last time. But if they do, the AI-driven race to make everything smarter could lead to less-smart cars, if only temporarily.

Romance gets a bad rep for being the guilty pleasure of TV. It's considered as the genre people binge in secret and never put on their "best shows" lists. That reputation is a little unfair, though. A truly great love story is one of the hardest things to pull off on screen. It takes chemistry that can't be faked, and a slow build that makes the audience desperate for two people to finally get it right. When a romance gets all of that right, it becomes something viewers return to again and again, long after they know exactly how it ends.

The shows on this list are the ones that understand romance is really about vulnerability rather than the boring, one-dimensional stereotypes people usually associate with the genre. They give viewers messy, complicated people who have to get out of their own way before they can fall in love. This is a list of romance shows that are 10/10 masterpieces because they do justice to the very act of falling in love.

One Day is a right person, wrong time story that is both heartwarming and unbearable to watch, and that is the mark of a true romance. The series, based on David Nicholls' bestselling novel, begins on July 15, 1988, when Emma Morley (Ambika Mod) and Dexter Mayhew (Leo Woodall) spend one night together after graduating from university in Edinburgh. Every episode then drops in on them on that same date, year after year. The two characters couldn't be any different. Emma is a sharp, working-class aspiring writer who never quite believes she’s good enough, while Dexter is a golden boy who survives on pure charm until his life starts to unravel.

The genius of the show is in its format. Since the audience only sees one day a year, entire relationships and careers happen off-screen, and every episode feels like catching up with an old friend who has changed as life has gone on. Both characters experience genuine growth over the years, and it's impossible not to root for their connection. Just be warned that the final episodes take a turn that hits like a gut punch, and the show ends with a twist that is guaranteed to stay with viewers for days. One Day is the perfect love story about missed chances, and is guaranteed to wreck anyone who makes it to the end.

Nobody expected a Canadian hockey romance to take over the internet, but Heated Rivalry did exactly that within a few weeks. The series, based on Rachel Reid's Game Changers novels and created by Letterkenny's Jacob Tierney, follows two of professional hockey’s biggest young stars. Shane Hollander (Hudson Williams) is a disciplined, focused Canadian, while Ilya Rozanov (Connor Storrie) is a cocky Russian who loves getting under his skin. The two have been rivals since their rookie days in 2008, and on the ice, they can't stand each other. Off the ice, though, they get secretly caught up in an affair that could end both of their careers if anyone found out.

Beneath all the steam, Heated Rivalry is really about two men who have been taught their entire lives that wanting each other could cost them everything. The six-episode season stretches across a decade, so the audience gets to watch a secret fling slowly turn into something neither of them is ready to name. The two are a perfect mismatch, and every stolen moment they get feels charged because things can fall apart at any time. On top of that, the show delivers a second great romance through hockey captain Scott Hunter (François Arnaud) and his boyfriend, Kip (Robbie G.K.), whose story nearly steals the spotlight. There are slow burns, and then there's a love story that literally spans two centuries. Outlander, based on Diana Gabaldon's novels, follows World War II nurse Claire Randall (Caitríona Balfe), on her second honeymoon in Scotland who is suddenly thrown back to 1743 after touching an ancient standing stone. Suddenly, Claire finds herself in the middle of a brewing Jacobite rebellion. Claire and Jamie face wars and impossible choices together, and their love grows deeper with every hardship instead of fading. It's hard not to melt every time Jamie calls her "Sassenach," the nickname that started as a jab and turned into a declaration of love. Claire is also a refreshingly modern heroine, a woman who refuses to stay quiet in a century that expects her to. After eight seasons, Outlander remains the gold standard for epic, sweeping romance on TV.

Bridgerton stands out from other period dramas because of how unapologetically modern it feels. The show reimagines Regency London with a diverse cast and swaps classical music for string-quartet covers of songs by artists including Ariana Grande and Taylor Swift to help the audience relate to the era. Not to mention the series tells its love stories from the women's point of view, which makes the fight for independence just as important as the search for a husband. Bridgerton took the stuffy period romance formula and turned it into a feel-good show that has made period romance cool for a whole new generation.

Instead, it grows alongside her confidence, which makes every step toward happiness feel truly earned. Anyone who has ever felt like the overlooked one in their family will see a little of themselves in her. The Other Bennet Sister is a sweet, clever twist on Austen, and it's the kind of underdog romance that's impossible not to love.

On Monday, the US Senate advanced the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 (S.5653), introduced by Senator Shelley Moore Capito, a West Virginia Republican, by a vote of 73–25. The bill is cosponsored by Senators Mike Lee, a Utah Republican; Sheldon Whitehouse, a Rhode Island Democrat; and Martin Heinrich, a New Mexico Democrat. Crucially, the act would narrow the scope of the National Historic Preservation Act (NHPA), in part by limiting which types of projects trigger review.

Speaking on the Senate floor Monday, Capito said that “for far too long, critical investments in our nation’s infrastructure, energy production, and economy have been forced to sit on the sidelines due to endless red tape, duplicative reviews, and regulatory uncertainty.” Data from the National Association of Manufacturers, she said, “shows that permitting delays are costing America’s manufacturers $12.1 billion every year, including $87 million in additional costs in West Virginia alone. According to new data from McKinsey, $1.8 trillion of investments are locked up in the federal permitting system right now, including $22.3 billion of sidelined investments in my home state of West Virginia.”

According to the Art Newspaper, about 800 protesters gathered on the West Lawn of the US Capitol, among them leaders from the Association of African American Museums, the DC Preservation League, the American Institute of Architects, and the Archaeological Institute of America. Representative Melanie Stansbury, a New Mexico Democrat, called the act a “dirty deal.” Critics cited by the paper noted that the senators introduced the 500-page bill just 48 hours before the Senate vote was scheduled.

S.5653 targets Section 106 of the NHPA, which requires federal agencies to consider the impact of federally funded building projects and requires those receiving federal funds or permits to consult with tribal nations. According to the Art Newspaper, the new bill would make consultation with tribal nations optional and remove the requirement that federal agencies notify and consult with the public and tribal nations about potential harm to historic sites. It would also narrow the definition of what constitutes a historic site.

On November 6, the 106 Strong Coalition, which includes more than 1,200 organizations, tribal nations, and advocates, sent a letter to Congress calling for the defeat of S.5653. Signatories include the American Alliance of Museums, numerous state museum associations, and many individual museums. The letter calls the bill “a sweeping assault on the protections Congress enacted sixty years ago to ensure that federal agencies consider the consequences of their decisions for America’s historic and sacred places.”

Inside OM Digital Solutions’ Okaya Manufacturing Site

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Inside OM Digital Solutions’ Okaya Manufacturing Site

I know how to take pictures, but ask me how the lens on my camera is made and I have a lot less to say. A visit to the Okaya Manufacturing Site in Japan, followed by some research afterward, gave me a lesson in a subject I’d never understood particularly well. I wanted to understand how the glass inside a lens is crafted, how the tools used in that process are prepared, and how people learn to do that work.

The facility was OM Digital Solutions’ Okaya Manufacturing Site in Nagano Prefecture, Japan. This was an overwhelming experience from a learning standpoint, so there was quite a bit that went over my head as we moved from station to station. Luckily, follow-up discussions with OM SYSTEM and research helped me fill in some of the gaps.

Okaya is the first optical lens manufacturing site in Japan for OM Digital Solutions, the company behind OM SYSTEM. It opened in 2025 and does two jobs. It produces lenses that are made in small quantities but come in many different types, and it works as a pilot factory, a place where the manufacturing processes for technically demanding new lenses are developed and proven before they’re used at scale.

The site works closely with the optical design team at the company’s headquarters in Hachioji, Tokyo. Designing a lens and figuring out how to build it are two different jobs that call for different expertise. Okaya’s engineers handle the second one, turning a design into a production process that actually works.

Mass production happens at the company’s factory in Vietnam. Before a production line for a new lens launches there, Okaya verifies the production technology, the manufacturing processes, and the work methods it will use. Working those out in Japan first is meant to make production in Vietnam more efficient and keep the quality consistent.

The place to begin is with an individual lens element, one of the many pieces of glass inside a camera lens. OM SYSTEM’s M.Zuiko Digital ED 40-150mm F2.8 PRO, for example, contains 16 of these elements. Before the lens can be assembled, each of these pieces of glass needs the correct curve and a smooth surface.

A piece of glass can be clear and look correct but still bend light the wrong way. A lens bends light to bring an image into focus on the camera’s sensor. Light from each point in the scene needs to come together at a matching point on the sensor. The curves of the glass help direct it there. If a curve is wrong, that light can spread around its intended point instead, making a small detail look blurry.

The sequence OM SYSTEM showed us at the Okaya Manufacturing Site starts with grinding, which gradually wears down the glass to form the basic curve. Finer grinding makes smaller adjustments to the glass. Polishing then wears away more material to give the surface the smoothness and accuracy it needs.

I got a small taste of this myself. During a workshop at Okaya, I tried lens polishing, including mounting the lens and taking it off again afterward. It looked simple. Then a slight mistake in my setup chipped the lens. That was enough to show me how delicate the process is and how much care and precision it takes.

Glass can look clear and smooth to the naked eye and still have flaws that affect the photograph. Tiny scratches, bumps, and dips, or a surface that strays slightly from its intended shape, can scatter light in directions it shouldn’t go. That can cause flare, the haze or bright spots stray light leaves in a picture, and lower the contrast, so the image looks flatter.

If the surface doesn’t match its designed shape, light won’t bend and come together the way the designers intended. So polishing has two jobs: leave the surface free of scratches, and reproduce the designed shape to within nanometers, or billionths of a meter. The engineers check the glass with a range of measuring instruments, but they also rely on experience and skill to make delicate adjustments to each lens.

Then they have to do it again on the next piece of glass, and the one after that. Even a slight difference between individual lenses can change how light travels through the finished lens and affect image quality.

Each glass element also needs to sit in the right position inside the lens. Imagine fitting one into a holder. It could fit neatly while the curved faces that bend light sit slightly off-center. That mismatch can send light to the wrong place. A manufacturing step called centering corrects that mismatch before assembly.

Getting the curve right and getting the glass into the right position both matter to the photograph. Even when we focus correctly, errors in the glass’s shape or alignment can leave fine details looking soft.

Even the tools OM Digital Solutions uses at the Okaya Manufacturing Site need preparation before they can work on the glass. These are the parts fitted to the machines that grind and polish the lens elements. The tools the engineers use to work on the glass need smooth, accurately made surfaces, too.

One of these tools has a small, round face that touches the glass. To prepare it, an engineer uses a second tool: a disc on the end of a handle. The engineer holds the disc against the first tool’s face, polishing the two surfaces together.

The accuracy of the tool affects the accuracy of the surface it produces on the glass.

I was surprised to see that they still prepare some of their tools by hand. The precision required helped me appreciate the care the engineers bring to preparing those tools and building the lenses. At Okaya, I saw veteran engineers working alongside younger colleagues to pass on their skills. Both generations were full of excitement and pride as they showed us how they make lenses in their facility.

That exchange between generations is a big part of how the skills developed at the Okaya Manufacturing Site get passed down. Bazaar’s Lynette Nylander and Camille Freestone join Laia Garcia-Furtado for a roundtable on her new book, Prada in the Nineties

Prada is as much an adjective as it is a noun. My colleagues and I often describe wardrobe twists, like a bejeweled brooch on a Peter Pan collar or an embroidered sock peeking out of a Mary Jane, as “very Prada.” But what is it about these little notes of character that signals “Prada” in our brain?

That’s one of the questions Bazaar’s deputy culture director, Laia Garcia-Furtado, answers in her new book, Prada in the Nineties. (Published by Batsford, it drops today.) Her deep dive into one of the brand’s most pivotal decades reaches far beyond the heroin-chic label we tend to slap on ’90s fashion. From runway reviews to cultural anecdotes, Garcia-Furtado takes us backstage, into the life and personality of Miuccia Prada, the industry’s north star of intellectual fashion.

Mrs. Prada’s arsenal of off-color prints and skirts have inspired generations of women to aspire to an ugly-chic kind of cool; looking at her today, it’s easy to find ourselves lost in her world. But in harkening back to the nascent stages of her career as a designer, Garcia-Furtado reminds us that the throughline of her genius lies in her sense of self.

It’s a story that began with nylon, an unexpected choice for a member of the Prada family, with its legacy of selling bespoke leather goods to royal clientele. Yet Mrs. Prada’s choice to bring nylon totes and duffels to the luxury market was not merely driven by rebellion—it was a manifestation of her own desire for a well-designed bag that embraced utility. And the groundbreaking success of the bags demonstrated that her dreams were shared by other women—a hint of where Prada would take the brand.

Garcia-Furtado understands this lore firsthand. After all, she’s spent a lot of time in Mrs. Prada’s shoes—well, the ones she designed, at least. She began building her own archive of Miuccia merch in college with a pair of woven leather Mary Janes by Prada’s little sister, Miu Miu. A lucky eBay find from the spring 2001 collection, they were marked by interlaced checks of leather with pops of plum purple and canvas buckle straps. She has continued to build her collection ever since.

Writing the book gave Garcia-Furtado carte blanche to dig into the nitty-gritty details that unite women of varying tastes and lifestyles as proud members of Prada’s hive. Below, she gathers with Bazaar’s fellow Prada obsessees, executive digital director Lynette Nylander and senior fashion writer Camille Freestone, to unpack all the things that have placed Mrs. Prada in a league of her own, from the ’90s to now.
