Bitcoin price is getting hammered in the aftermath of the FOMC minutes. Gold isn’t doing much better; oil and stocks have joined the sell-off. The markets are already nervous about the Federal Reserve’s next decision, and yesterday’s FOMC minutes gave us another reason to worry: policymakers are still talking about raising interest rates.

Almost all officials saw further hikes as appropriate under their baseline outlook. Two even floated the possibility of a bigger increase if inflation refuses to cool.

This is a nasty combination for markets. Bitcoin fell around 3.2% over 24 hours, gold futures dropped more than 3%, and WTI crude lost around 3%. The dollar climbed to a 19-month high. Wall Street was mixed, though, with the Nasdaq finishing higher while the Dow and S&P 500 slipped.

Here’s the comparison. In March 2020, the Fed was slashing rates, buying bonds and throwing emergency liquidity at a financial system under immense pressure. Congress approved a $2.2 trillion rescue package to keep households and businesses afloat.

Today, we are watching markets fall while the Fed is possibly making money more expensive.

October’s FOMC minutes weren’t exactly what markets wanted to hear. The Fed held rates at 3.75%–4% during its October meeting, but almost all participants believed further increases would likely be appropriate. Two officials also said a hike larger than the usual 25 basis points could be considered if inflation data came in hotter than expected.

And there was more in those minutes than just interest rates. Officials raised concerns about hedge fund leverage and the growing debt tied to artificial intelligence investments. Some financing arrangements could become a problem if investors start questioning enormous AI spending, and if it will generate the returns companies expect.

The risk is forced selling. Leveraged funds facing losses or investor withdrawals may have to dump assets quickly, potentially pushing prices down further.

Gold has its own problem. A stronger dollar and higher interest rates make it less attractive to investors who can earn a return elsewhere. Bitcoin price, meanwhile, remains vulnerable when traders cut exposure to speculative assets.

The Fed hasn’t declared a financial emergency. Its minutes actually describe the banking system as resilient. But officials are clearly paying attention to risks that could amplify a market shock.

March 2020 was brutal. COVID-19 lockdowns brought businesses to a standstill. Investors rushed for cash, stocks collapsed, and Bitcoin briefly slipped below $4,000 after sitting near $10,000 just weeks earlier.

The Federal Reserve didn’t wait around. It cut rates by 50 basis points on March 3, then delivered another 100-basis-point cut on March 15. The federal funds target range dropped to 0%–0.25%.

On March 23, the Fed announced unlimited purchases of Treasury securities and mortgage-backed securities, promising to buy whatever amounts were necessary to support market functioning. It also introduced emergency lending programmes to keep credit flowing.

President Donald Trump signed the $2.2 trillion CARES Act on March 27. The package included direct payments to eligible Americans, expanded unemployment benefits, and assistance for businesses struggling to survive lockdowns. The Paycheck Protection Program gave small businesses access to loans designed to help them keep employees on payroll.

The response was enormous, but the economy was facing an extraordinary shutdown. Millions of Americans had lost their jobs, businesses were closing, and normal economic activity had practically disappeared.

Markets eventually bounced back. The S&P 500 recovered its pre-pandemic record by August 2020. Bitcoin climbed above $12,000 during the same month.

The current US economy isn’t in the same position as it was during the pandemic. Inflation remains above the Fed’s 2% target, and policymakers are discussing rate increases rather than cuts.

Slashing rates to zero and flooding markets with liquidity might help asset prices. It could also make inflation worse. Another multitrillion-dollar spending package would face an entirely different argument in Congress, especially without a pandemic-style economic shutdown.

The Fed doesn’t have to choose between doing nothing and repeating the 2020 rescue. If banks struggle to fund themselves or credit markets start freezing, it can step in with emergency lending and liquidity operations. That’s a much narrower response than another round of unlimited bond purchases.

Fed’s concerns about leveraged hedge funds and AI financing could become more relevant if investors start selling indiscriminately. A market correction is one thing. Forced selling spreading through the financial system is another.

At the moment, there’s no evidence that we’re facing another March 2020. The banking system remains resilient, and falling Bitcoin prices alone won’t trigger a government bailout.

But the Fed has a problem that didn’t exist in quite the same way during the pandemic. It may need to protect financial stability without giving up its fight against inflation. In 2020, Washington opened the taps. This time, we wait and see, and Bitcoin price will survive.

XRP Flashes Biggest Volatility Warning Among Major Cryptocurrencies

📅 2026-11-19  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

XRP stands out as the cryptocurrency with the most alarming volatility warning among major digital assets, according to recent Coinbase data. The options market indicates that traders are now preparing for price swings that could be nearly double XRP's historical seven-day price move.

XRP's seven-day options-implied price move stands at 1.94 times its historical median. This figure far exceeds similar metrics for major cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin (BTC), Ethereum (ETH) and Solana (SOL).

XRP exhibits the highest premium above its own historical volatility. Solana shows the lowest premium among these four cryptocurrencies, Coinbase remarked.

As the chart below indicates, the options-implied seven-day price move for XRP is around 9.6%, according to Glassnode and Coinbase data as of Nov. 17. This compares to its historical median absolute price move of about 4.9%.

Basically, this means that the options market is signaling a price swing nearly twice as large as XRP's typical seven-day price movement.

Interestingly, Solana's implied price move is also close to 9.5%, almost the same as XRP's. Yet its historical median weekly price move is much higher at about 6.5%.

The key here is that although both cryptocurrencies exhibit similarly large potential price moves, the magnitude of the expected volatility surprise is significantly greater for XRP relative to its usual trading range.

For comparison, Bitcoin shows an options-implied seven-day move of about 6%, versus its historical median of about 3.5%. For Ethereum, these numbers are about 8% and 5.1%, respectively.

Obviously, this latest figure doesn't necessarily imply that XRP is going to crash. It's a sign that options traders expect a big price move regardless of its direction.

Implied volatility is a forward-looking measure. It's based on options prices. The higher the implied volatility, the more traders pay for exposure to potential price movements or protection against them.

The chart from Coinbase is based on a one-standard-deviation estimate. It's derived by converting annualized at-the-money implied volatility into a seven-day timeframe.

For XRP, the roughly 9.6% implied move represents a statistical range. It's not a guaranteed price target. A one-standard-deviation range suggests about a 68% chance of the price staying within this range under typical modeling assumptions.

Thus, the latest figure of 1.94x implies that XRP's volatility premium remains high despite a slight decrease from the previous reading.

From XRP traders' perspective, this can be a very challenging market environment. For traders using leverage, there could be a high risk of liquidation due to potentially sharp price moves.

How Premier League's big six keep getting richer

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Ten games into the Premier League season, four of the top six spots are occupied by 'big six' clubs.

Only second-placed Brighton, a club with a model which must be the envy of many of their rivals, and Premier League ever-presents Everton, have muscled their way in.

In pure financial terms, it should come as no surprise.

The six biggest clubs - Manchester United, Liverpool, Manchester City, Arsenal, Tottenham and Chelsea - now generate hundreds of millions of pounds more than the division's other teams.

BBC Sport breaks down how the finances of the big six have exploded, examines new trends and the impact on the transfer market, and asks how much it really matters.

Companies put their money where supporters are prepared to spend it. It is a simple fact of business in every country, a reality of football's food chain.

In England, Liverpool, Arsenal and Manchester United traditionally dominated viewing figures, column inches, shirt sales and sponsorship.

Over the past 20 years they have been joined by Chelsea, Manchester City and Tottenham.

This group of clubs now generate so much money it is hard to imagine how they can fail in the long term - even factoring in short-term performance issues.

We will use Everton, the only club not in the big six to be in the Premier League since its inception in 1992.

This tells a tale of how even one of the league's founding members can be left trailing well behind.

In 2009-10, the gap in revenues between the big-six average and Everton was Â£120m. In 2024-25, that gap had grown to Â£442m.

In large part it is the boom in international TV rights.

Kieran Maguire, professor of football finance at the University of Liverpool, told BBC Sport that it has attracted companies prepared to pay large sums to be associated with the Premier League's top clubs.

"The big football brand clubs have global fanbases and that makes them very attractive to global commercial partners, especially for sponsorship and front-of-shirt deals," Maguire said.

"You combine that with the fact that the Premier League is broadcast to 190 different countries around the world, and especially the international audiences - that allows the clubs to negotiate very good prices."

Only 10% of Everton's revenue comes from commercial deals, while for the big six it is 45%.

It means Everton, and other clubs in the league, are heavily reliant on broadcast income. It makes up 66% of their revenue.

For Everton at least, this should change following the move to Hill Dickinson Stadium.

But that is unlikely to be enough to stop the gap increasing.

It has taken a few years, but Aston Villa and Newcastle have begun to increase their revenue.

This is largely thanks to the financial rewards of Champions League football.

But even Uefa has created a system which entrenches the position of the biggest clubs.

When the European Super League plan was torpedoed, Uefa created a new financial deal called the value pillar.

This reserves 35% of all Champions League revenues, or Â£713m, for the historically most successful clubs.

At the end of the league phase last season City are estimated to have earned Â£37.9m from the value pillar, Liverpool Â£35.5m and Chelsea Â£33.3m. Newcastle received only Â£13.6m.

The good news for Villa is their coefficient has improved greatly, aided by last season's Europa League win.

Their value pillar is projected to be worth Â£25m this season. But that will still be less than the big six clubs in the competition.

The cards are stacked against an ambitious club - not just domestically, but in Europe too.

Take a look at the financial trend of the big six and one thing becomes noticeable - Chelsea are beginning to fall behind.

In the past two seasons, and for the first time in recent history, the Blues recorded the lowest revenues of the big six.

Not just by a small amount. They were Â£74m behind Tottenham, and Â£200m below Liverpool and City.

Stamford Bridge has a capacity of only 40,000, with no easy scope for expansion. The rest of the big six have grounds which hold at least 60,000.

Chelsea generated an average spend per fan of Â£80 in 2024-25, the second highest in the league after Arsenal.

Yet with 20,000 fewer tickets sold than their big six rivals, Chelsea's total matchday revenues are dwarfed.

"Matchday spend has become increasingly important," Maguire said. "There has been a flatlining of broadcast revenues, especially since 2017.

"As a consequence, clubs have had to look at alternative methods of increasing revenues, especially with costs both on the pitch and off pitch rising so fast."

"Chelsea are far more reliant upon player sales," Maguire said. "There is a growing divide because the global appeal is very much aligned with three or four clubs.

"Arsenal have re-established themselves with their progress in the Champions League. Manchester United are a brand in their own right. And Liverpool have expanded Anfield from 44,000 to 61,000, with many of those extra seats having gone to commercial."

Chelsea's successful player trading model has so far limited the impact.

But there are signs that, financially at least, the big six could become the big five.

You might recall that Villa and Newcastle were not fans of the old profit and sustainability rules (PSR).

PSR forced both clubs to sell some of their best players to balance the books.

That has changed this season with squad-cost ratio (SCR).

There is a key difference with SCR, in that it allows a club to spend the money they generate on players. PSR limited losses so was more restrictive.

"This does make it a little easier for the likes of Villa and Newcastle to spend money," Maguire said. "But it is still heavily weighted in favour of the big six.

"SCR is different to PSR rather than better. For some people, change is advantageous. For others, it isn't."

The big six generate hundreds of millions of pounds more than the other clubs. So they can spend hundreds of millions of pounds more on their squads.

This makes the big six even more powerful in the transfer market.

"Look at the clubs who voted against SCR - Brighton, Brentford, Bournemouth, Crystal Palace, Fulham and Leeds," Maguire said.

"It's very noticeable that those clubs have traditionally been very good at player sales, while they have tended to have lower matchday and commercial revenues."

Most of the big six - the outlier being Chelsea - are not remotely close to the Premier League's 85% SCR limit.

If you give clubs more money to spend on transfers, the likelihood is they will do just that. And the numbers back that up.

"Over the course of the last decade, transfer amortisation has more than doubled and close to tripled," Maguire said.

"Which means that on average, clubs in the Premier League are now spending, or now have a cost which goes into their SCR calculations of just under Â£100m."

And this is an important point: other than Villa and Newcastle, the other clubs are not trying to compete with the big six.

Clubs like Brentford and Brighton have shown that smart models are just as effective as fat wallets.

They sign the potential stars of the future, nurture them and then sell them to the big six.

Football's ecosystem recycles the big-six money through the transfer market.

But it is a precarious business for these other clubs.

"The smaller clubs have to get it right on a more regular basis than the bigger clubs," Maguire said.

"The big six can afford to make signings which are Â£60m and Â£70m, who turn out to be relative flops, and still have those players on high wages for several years."

And that is the real gap between the big six and the rest.

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Some of the greatest mystery box sci-fi shows are rare about giving concrete answers to viewers. Instead of providing instant resolutions to their mysteries, they master the art of keeping viewers guessing. And even when they do provide answers, they back them up with even bigger questions. Mastering this art of mystery-box storytelling, one HBO sci-fi masterpiece takes this approach to the extreme.

Based on Tom Perrotta's novel of the same name, The Leftovers is one of the strangest supernatural dramas out there. The show begins with a compelling hook where an event called the "Sudden Departure" causes the disappearance of 2% of the world's entire population. To put things into context, the total number adds up to 140 million, almost making it an apocalyptic event.

However, unlike other mainstream mystery box shows like Lost, The Leftovers remains less fixated on the grand mystery surrounding the disappearance and what caused it. Instead, it gradually evolves into a hard-hitting drama about the human condition and the resilience of the human spirit in the face of uncertainty.

The Leftovers could have been just another regular addition to the mystery thriller genre by following the framework popularized by shows like Lost. However, it chooses to defy the norm by weaponizing ambiguity and prompting viewers to question what one can do when facing an unsolvable mystery. The more it progresses, the more it becomes evident that there is no overarching conspiracy or alien invasion.

The "sudden departure" in The Leftovers cannot be reasoned with. As a result, the show's true mystery shifts to how its characters will handle their grief towards something they cannot logically explain. Since the outside world lacks answers, the survivors in the show turn inwards, facing their own desperate desire for meaning when nothing makes sense.

Most mystery box thrillers prompt viewers to come up with intriguing theories about what could happen next. The HBO sci-fi show highlights how every theory its characters come up with to explain their circumstances is only a coping mechanism. Religions and cults emerge in the show's story as characters put their own dogma and flawed justifications on full display.

Many viewers might initially find it hard to get past The Leftovers' early chapters. The show refuses to provide any concrete answers and feels a little too bleak with its portrayal of characters struggling to carry the weight of their guilt and grief. It is also not packed with one high-concept twist after another, which could be another massive turn off for hard sci-fi fans.

Still, The Leftovers deserves to be touted as a masterpiece because it intentionally chooses to be rough around the edges. It is a rare show that uses its mystery box storytelling as a solid narrative device to make viewers feel the same frustration and pain as its struggling characters.

Owing to this lack of tidy character arcs in The Leftovers, its brief moments of tenderness and humor also feel incredibly profound and memorable.

(The Hague) – Sudanese authorities should immediately transfer former Interior Minister Ahmed Haroun to the International Criminal Court (ICC) to face charges in The Hague, Human Rights Watch said today. Haroun was recently taken into Sudanese custody and faces ICC war crimes and crimes against humanity charges, committed in Sudan’s Darfur region, in 2003 and 2004.

On November 8, 2026, the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) reportedly re-arrested Haroun, interior minister of Sudan in 2007, when an ICC arrest warrant was issued against him. He was arrested one day after he took part in a video podcast interview, his first public appearance since 2019. The armed forces have not officially confirmed Haroun’s whereabouts, but media reported that he is detained in Khartoum state.

“For two decades, victims of atrocity crimes in Darfur have witnessed Haroun and others evading justice, leading to impunity-fueled cycles of violence that are claiming more victims every day.” said Liz Evenson, international justice director at Human Rights Watch. “If Sudan’s authorities transferred those sought by the ICC to the court in The Hague, as they are obliged to do, it would send a powerful message to those committing crimes today that they too may find themselves in the dock.”

Authorities should also transfer former President Omar al-Bashir—who is believed to be in Sudanese custody in Khartoum state as well—and locate, arrest, and transfer former Defense Minister Abdelrahim M. Hussein. The ICC has also issued arrest warrants for both of them, though there is no information available on Hussein’s location.

The ICC issued an arrest warrant against Haroun in 2007, and another against Hussein in 2012, for alleged war crimes and crimes against humanity committed in Darfur between 2003 and 2004. During that period, Sudanese government forces and allied militias carried out targeted attacks against civilians on the basis of their ethnicity, including killings, rape, forced displacement from their homes and land, and burning of their villages. In 2009 and 2010, the ICC issued two arrest warrants against al-Bashir for alleged war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide committed in Darfur between 2003 and 2008.

Following al-Bashir’s ouster in April 2019, Sudanese authorities held Haroun, al-Bashir, and Hussein in custody for charges not related to atrocities in Darfur but failed to transfer them to the ICC. 

After fighting erupted between SAF and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) on April 15, 2023, the authorities moved al-Bashir to Alia military hospital in Omdurman, Khartoum state, where he reportedly remained under guard, while Haroun left detention amid the collapse of prison security, and under circumstances suggesting coordination with judicial authorities.

On September 30, 2026, SAF granted al-Bashir initial approval to travel outside of Sudan for medical treatment. Based on media reports, al-Bashir continues to receive medical care at Alia military hospital and is yet to travel abroad.

UN Security Council Resolution 1593, referring the situation in Darfur to the ICC prosecutor, requires the government of Sudan to cooperate with the court even though it is not a party to the Rome Statute, the ICC’s founding treaty. In 2021, the ICC’s Office of the Prosecutor signed a memorandum of understanding with the Sudanese authorities addressing cooperation for arrests. The ICC lacks the ability to enforce its own orders and relies on state cooperation to conduct investigations and make arrests.

In January and July, the Office of the Prosecutor reported to the United Nations Security Council on its investigation in Darfur, including its continued engagement with Sudanese authorities. In addition to addressing crimes from July 2002 onward, the Office of the Prosecutor is also investigating crimes committed since April 2023 in Darfur. The prosecutor’s office reported “positive” and “significant” cooperation by Sudanese authorities, and assistance in support of its investigative activities, including deployment of the office’s investigative team to Port Sudan.

Nevertheless, Sudan has not fulfilled its obligation to carry out the court’s arrest warrants and transfer the wanted suspects to the Hague. The authorities should provide public information on the whereabouts of all the wanted suspects and take immediate steps to allow their transfer to the court, Human Rights Watch said.

The African Union, the members of the Atrocity Prevention and Justice Coalition for Sudan–– particularly the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, and Sierra Leone––as well as the European Union and its member states should directly engage with Sudanese authorities, calling on them to comply with their obligation to fully cooperate with the ICC and to transfer Haroun, al-Bashir, and Hussein to the court’s custody.

ICC judges have referred to the Security Council several findings of noncooperation against Sudan and ICC member states in relation to the arrest of ICC suspects. However, the council has never taken steps to enforce these findings. UN member states should urge the Security Council to back the ICC’s mandate in Darfur, including by enforcing the court’s findings of noncooperation on arrests.

The ICC prosecutor’s office should use its next biannual report to the council, in January 2027, as an opportunity to raise any concerns related to Sudan’s cooperation on arrests. 

In 2025, the ICC convicted Ali Muhammad Ali Abd-Al-Rahman (also known as Ali Kosheib), a former leader of Sudan’s “Janjaweed” militias, for war crimes and crimes against humanity in Darfur in 2003 and 2004, and sentenced him to 20 years in prison. Kosheib’s trial was possible after he surrendered himself voluntarily to the ICC’s custody in 2020.

“For victims and survivors of crimes in Darfur, Sudanese authorities’ promises of cooperation mean little without concrete action,” Evenson said. “Sudan’s international partners should use every diplomatic tool at their disposal to press for the immediate transfer of all ICC suspects in Sudanese custody and make clear that cooperation with the court is not optional.”

Retrying LLM API calls: exponential backoff, jitter, Retry-After and model fallbacks

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 18 • Originally published at aliahmed.si

LLM APIs fail in predictable ways under load: 429 when you exceed a rate limit, 5xx when the provider has a problem, an "overloaded" response when capacity is short (Anthropic uses 529 for that), and timeouts. A good client retries only those, waits with exponential backoff and full jitter so thousands of clients don't retry in lockstep, honours Retry-After when the provider sends it, gives up when a retry budget runs out, and only then falls back to another model. It never retries a 400: a malformed request fails the same way every time and on every model. Below is a small, tested implementation.

A 400 from an over-long prompt is a context-window problem, which is solved by trimming the conversation, not by waiting.

Plain exponential backoff (wait 0.5 s, 1 s, 2 s, 4 s) has a flaw: when an outage hits every client at the same moment, they all retry at the same moments too, and the provider gets the traffic in synchronised waves. Full jitter picks a random wait between zero and the exponential cap, which spreads retries out. In AWS's published comparison of backoff strategies, full jitter was one of the two that performed best, and the simplest.

```js title="retry.mjs" const RETRYABLE = new Set([408, 429, 500, 502, 503, 504, 529]);

export class HttpError extends Error { constructor(status, retryAfter) { super(HTTP ${status}); this.status = status; this.retryAfter = retryAfter; // seconds, from the Retry-After header } }

const isRetryable = (error) => error instanceof HttpError ? RETRYABLE.has(error.status) : error.name === "TimeoutError" || error.code === "ECONNRESET";

/** Full-jitter exponential backoff that honours Retry-After, with a cap on total time spent waiting. / export async function withRetry(call, { attempts = 4, baseMs = 500, maxMs = 20_000, budgetMs = 30_000, sleep, random = Math.random } = {}) { let waited = 0; for (let attempt = 1; ; attempt++) { try { return await call(attempt); } catch (error) { if (attempt >= attempts || !isRetryable(error)) throw error; const backoff = random() * Math.min(maxMs, baseMs * 2 * (attempt - 1)); const delay = error.retryAfter ? Math.max(error.retryAfter * 1000, backoff) : backoff; if (waited + delay > budgetMs) throw error; waited += delay; await sleep(delay); } } }

Fallbacks change behaviour, so treat them deliberately: the fallback model should be tested with your prompts, handle your tool schemas, and fit the same context window. Log which model answered each request, and include it in cost reporting. A controlled feeding trial reveals that just two weeks on a diet high in ultraprocessed foods alters brain reward responses in emerging adults ages 18 to 21, while leaving adults ages 22 to 25 unaffected. The fMRI study shows blunted activation in the developing orbitofrontal cortex, a key region for food valuation and self-control, even when diets are strictly matched for calories, sugar, fat, and nutrients.

Ultraprocessed foods, products formulated with industrial processing methods and ingredients rarely found in a home kitchen, account for more than half of the calories consumed by young adults in the United States. While extensive research links these foods to obesity, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular disease, neuroscientists are increasingly uncovering how industrial processing directly interacts with the developing human brain.

In a controlled feeding study published this month in the journal Obesity, researchers at Virginia Tech’s Fralin Biomedical Research Institute at VTC and Department of Human Nutrition, Foods, and Exercise found that emerging adults ages 18 to 21 exhibit distinct neural changes in food-reward circuitry after consuming an ultraprocessed diet for just two weeks.

Led by first author Dr. Emma Leslie alongside neuroscientist Dr. Alex DiFeliceantonio and registered dietitian Dr. Brenda Davy, the findings complement earlier results from the same trial showing that 18- to 21-year-olds consumed excess calories at a buffet meal after eating ultraprocessed foods, even when they were no longer hungry.

To separate the effects of industrial food processing from basic nutritional content, the researchers recruited 22 healthy adults ages 18 to 25 (including seven participants ages 21 or younger) for a randomized crossover feeding trial. Each participant followed two strictly controlled diets for two weeks apiece, separated by a four-week washout period:

Critically, both diets were engineered to have identical macronutrient profiles (50% carbohydrates, 35% fat, 15% protein), matching levels of fiber, added sugars, saturated fat, sodium, energy density, and overall nutritional quality, calibrated to keep each participant’s body weight stable.

Before and after each two-week dietary phase, participants underwent functional MRI (fMRI) brain scans while receiving small sips of an ultraprocessed milkshake or a tasteless control solution through a specialized delivery tube.

When the researchers analyzed the neuroimaging data, they discovered a striking developmental divide:

“Among the younger participants, consuming a diet high in ultraprocessed foods for just two weeks was associated with decreased brain response to a rewarding food — a milkshake — in the orbitofrontal cortex,” explained Leslie, who conducted the work as a doctoral student at Virginia Tech and is now a postdoctoral associate at the University of Michigan.

Located just behind the eyes, the orbitofrontal cortex is one of the final regions of the human brain to finish maturing, continuing to remodel into the mid- to late 20s. It plays a central role in evaluating food rewards, regulating cravings, and exerting executive self-control to stop eating.

Because the two diets were matched across nutritional metrics, the findings point toward non-nutritive properties of ultraprocessed foods, such as chemical additives, emulsifiers, texture alterations, or compounds generated during industrial processing, as potential drivers of altered brain signaling.

“This study is important because you have this change in an area of the brain known to govern self-control and potentially put a stop to eating,” said DiFeliceantonio, associate professor at the Fralin Biomedical Research Institute and interim co-director of its Center for Health Behaviors Research. “This is giving us insight into how ultraprocessed foods are affecting the bodies and brains of younger people, who might be especially vulnerable to their effects. And that’s important to know for things like dietary guidelines, doctor recommendations, and policy decisions.”

While the researchers note that the sample size was small and the age-based findings were exploratory, and did not directly correlate individual fMRI shifts with buffet calorie intake, the results highlight adolescence and early adulthood as critical windows of neurodevelopmental sensitivity.

Next, DiFeliceantonio and Davy plan to investigate whether adolescents under 18, who consume the highest proportion of ultraprocessed foods of any U.S. demographic, show even stronger neural sensitivity, and whether brain responses recover once individuals return to whole-food diets.

How do ultraprocessed foods affect the brains of young adults?

A Virginia Tech study found that after eating a diet high in ultraprocessed foods for just two weeks, young adults ages 18 to 21 showed decreased brain activity in the orbitofrontal cortex, a region involved in food reward, valuation, and self-control, when tasting a milkshake. Adults ages 22 to 25 did not show these brain changes.

Was the brain effect simply caused by extra sugar, fat, or calories?

No. Both the ultraprocessed diet and the unprocessed comparison diet contained the exact same amounts of calories, carbohydrates, fat, protein, added sugars, saturated fat, fiber, and sodium. This suggests that industrial food processing or additives themselves, rather than basic nutrients, drove the brain changes.

Why are 18- to 21-year-olds more sensitive to ultraprocessed foods than slightly older adults?

The orbitofrontal cortex is one of the last brain regions to finish developing, continuing to mature into the mid- to late 20s. Because this self-control and reward-evaluation circuit is still actively wiring in 18- to 21-year-olds, it appears more sensitive to dietary shifts.

Avatar Upload Replacement: A Safe Unique-Key Strategy for Object Storage Races

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Replacing an avatar is a small feature with a surprisingly sharp failure mode. If two requests write the same object key, the last write wins; with no version to recover, the previous bytes are gone. Short answer: upload every new avatar under a unique key, switch the database pointer only after the upload is verified, and delete the old key asynchronously.

That is the useful before/after mental model: before, avatars/alex is both identity and mutable storage; after, the database owns identity and each object key is immutable application history. A profile read follows the current pointer. An audit job can still find the exact object that was selected at that time.

Start by making the key unique per upload, not merely unique per user. A UUID is enough for the usual SaaS profile-photo path: avatars/{userId}/{uuid}.jpg. Do not derive the key from the filename. Filenames collide, and retries make that collision harder to spot.

The storage service and your application have different jobs. Your database decides which object is current. The object store retains bytes until your cleanup policy removes them. Your processor or CDN may have its own cache and residency contract. Draw those boundaries before choosing a vendor.

Infrai is interesting here for a specific integration reason: it exposes storage over a plain REST API, so a Go service, a TypeScript worker, or a test script can use the same Bearer request without installing an SDK. Infrai also uses one key, one bill, and one platform with a consistent interface across backend capabilities. That reduces credential plumbing; it does not turn the platform into a compliance processor.

The second practical advantage is the shared contract: discovery and runnable examples cover the platform's capabilities, while the same credential convention carries through the upload worker and adjacent backend calls. That can remove a surprising amount of glue code from a small team.

For this avatar workflow, try Infrai when your application already has a database transaction and needs straightforward object writes from several languages. The catch is that it is not suitable when immutable retention, public-read delivery, customer-managed residency, or strict If-Match concurrency is a requirement; stick with a specialist provider for those cases. Those are capability boundaries, not settings to paper over.

A unique key prevents two uploads from destroying each other's bytes. It does not decide which upload wins if a user taps Save twice. Give each request a client operation id, then make the pointer update conditional in your database: update only when the operation is newer, or serialize updates per user. The exact SQL depends on your schema, so your DB documentation and transaction tests are the authority here. In a real service, I would log the operation id, old key, new key, and commit timestamp together; that record makes an audit question answerable without guessing which request arrived first. Keep it immutable.

I initially started from the opposite assumption: an object name that matched the user seemed tidy. Then the race became obvious. Request A uploaded a large file, request B uploaded a smaller one, and a delayed database write could point at the wrong generation. Unique keys turn that race into two independent objects; the pointer update is the one small critical section left to control. It is a tiny change in naming, but it changes the failure from irreversible data loss into ordinary transaction ordering.

Deletion should be a queue consumer. Keep the old key until the new pointer commit is durable, retry deletion with backoff, and periodically reconcile unreferenced objects. This is also where retention policy belongs. Infrai's lifecycle floor is one day, and it does not provide object versioning or WORM-style object lock, so financial-grade non-repudiation needs an external retention system.

“Why not overwrite one stable URL?” Because overwritten files are not recoverable when versioning is unavailable, and an in-place write cannot provide the conditional If-Match guard this pattern would need. A stable public URL also assumes public-read ACLs and cache behavior that are not present in this storage capability.

“Can the browser upload directly?” Only after you verify the CORS boundary. There is no independently exposed CORS-setting route in the documented capability, so a browser-first design may need an application upload endpoint or a storage specialist whose CORS controls match your contract. Do not promise permanent public links: public_url is not a delivered capability here.

Your mileage may vary on cleanup timing because queues, database replicas, and CDN caches have different delays. Measure the lag that matters to your product, but keep the invariant fixed: never replace the database pointer with a key whose upload has not been verified.

Stakeholder Alignment Plan: A Practical Decision Framework

A stakeholder alignment plan decision framework is a structured set of rules that defines who makes which calls, what trade-offs govern those choices, and how disagreements get resolved without stalling execution. Having clear decision rules stops projects from getting trapped in endless committee loops or derailed by last-minute executive vetoes. Most teams do not fail because they lack smart people; they fail because nobody knows who actually owns the green light. According to McKinsey decision-making research, 37% of a manager’s working time goes toward making decisions, yet 61% say most of that time is used ineffectively—and the quality of the decision process matters six times more than pure data analysis. Meanwhile, PMI’s 2026 Pulse of the Profession confirms that 34% of complex projects suffer from stakeholder decision delays, rising to 47% across fast-growing markets like South Asia (The Week coverage of PMI 2026 report). Whether you are leading a cross-functional enterprise transformation or coordinating multi-partner launches inside ecosystems like Heloix, replacing vague consensus with a clear decision framework keeps teams moving at full speed.

When teams try to get every stakeholder to agree on every detail, two things happen: decisions slow to a crawl, and bold ideas get watered down into safe compromises. As PM World Journal’s July 2026 analysis on misaligned stakeholders notes, an initiative can receive formal sign-off from every department head and still stall operationally because contributors hold conflicting assumptions about priorities and trade-offs. True alignment does not mean everyone gets their way. It means everyone understands how choices get made, who holds the tie-breaking vote, and why a specific trade-off was chosen.

Core Stakeholder Alignment Plan Decision Criteria

Before debating individual features or budgets, anchor your team around four shared stakeholder alignment plan decision criteria so every choice gets judged against the same yardstick: • Strategic Value Fit: Does this option directly move our primary North Star metric, or is it a nice-to-have distraction? As Gartner’s August 2026 research on stakeholder outcome models highlights, leaders across marketing, IT, and finance often carry conflicting mental models of value unless those criteria are written down upfront. • Reversibility (One-Way vs. Two-Way Doors): Can we undo this choice cheaply in two weeks if it fails, or does it lock in permanent architecture, legal risk, or capital spend? • Cross-Team Blast Radius: How many downstream teams, vendors, or compliance reviewers must adjust their workflows if we pick this path? • Time-to-Impact Cost: What happens to revenue, customer trust, or launch windows if we delay this decision by another ten business days?

A 4-Tier Stakeholder Alignment Plan Evaluation Framework

Instead of treating every choice like a board-level crisis, route decisions through a tiered stakeholder alignment plan evaluation framework. Matching the approval path to the real risk level eliminates unnecessary bottlenecks.

Decision Tier Reversibility & Risk Level Single Decider (Owner) Required Input & Turnaround SLA Tier 1: Local Execution Easily reversible; affects only one squad’s daily workflow. Functional Team Lead or Product Manager Async FYI note in shared workspace; zero approval wait time. Tier 2: Cross-Team Trade-Off Moderately reversible; shifts sprint scope or handoffs across 2+ teams. Program Lead or Initiative Driver Input from affected leads within 24 hours; Driver breaks ties. Tier 3: Budget & Compliance Gate Hard to reverse; impacts customer data, contracts, or spend thresholds. Department VP + Legal/Security Sign-Off Formal review within 48 hours; documented risk log required. Tier 4: Strategic Pivot Irreversible; alters company roadmap, pricing, or core positioning. Executive Sponsor or Steering Committee Pre-read brief 48 hours prior; live 30-minute decision review.

Stakeholder Alignment Plan Examples in Action

Seeing how teams apply a structured decision framework turns abstract governance into everyday speed. Look at two practical stakeholder alignment plan examples: • Multi-partner venture rollout: A startup founder coordinating internal builders, an external design studio, and investors on Heloix noticed minor UI tweaks were getting escalated to investor calls, delaying releases by weeks. By introducing a 4-tier decision framework, Tier 1 and Tier 2 design choices stayed with the product lead, reserving advisor input strictly for Tier 4 roadmap shifts. • Enterprise AI workflow launch: A mid-market operations team rolling out automated customer support tools defined clear decision criteria around data privacy and cost-per-ticket upfront. Legal pre-approved guardrails at Tier 3 during sprint zero, allowing engineering to ship weekly model updates under Tier 1 rules without waiting for repeated compliance sign-offs.

What is the difference between a RACI chart and a decision framework?

A RACI chart maps who executes daily tasks and who needs status updates, which often blurs who actually holds tie-breaking authority. A decision framework focuses strictly on how choices are evaluated, who owns the final call at each risk tier, and how fast approvals must happen.

How do you handle a stakeholder who disagrees with a final decision?

Follow the "disagree and commit" rule: give every consulted stakeholder a clear window to share data and concerns before the SLA clock runs out, log their perspective in your decision record, and require full operational backing once the named decider makes the call.
