Jester Lands in Court: Florida Woman Accused of Stealing Properties With Forged Deeds

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A Florida woman has been accused of perpetrating a brazen deed fraud scam and illicitly transferred ownership of three properties in a scheme to fund her lavish lifestyle.

Melissa Jester, 41, of Bunnell, FL, is facing criminal charges after authorities say she forged a property owner's signature on deeds, transferred the land to a company she controlled, and pocketed the proceeds from one of the sales. She was arrested Nov. 6.

The victim, who owned three vacant lots in Florida for nearly two decades, recently discovered that the properties were transferred out of her name without her knowledge—and that one had been sold for nearly $30,000, according to the Flagler County Sheriff’s Office.

The victim contacted the authorities on Aug. 27 and said she had never authorized the sale or transfer of the properties. The three lots are located in the coastal city of Palm Coast, about 30 miles north of Daytona Beach.

An investigation was launched, and detectives uncovered what they described as a paper trail of forged documents, suspicious financial transactions, and a property sale that ultimately helped lead them to Jester.

Detectives traced a trail of forged documents and fraudulent financial transactions to Jester.

According to investigators, paperwork transferring ownership of all three lots to Florida Ohana Homes LLC, a company owned by Jester, was mailed to the Flagler County Clerk of Courts on June 29, 2026.

The paperwork appeared to have the property owner's signature on it. It was forged, investigators say.

Jester took further steps to hide her involvement by purchasing a money order on June 18 to pay the filing fees for the property under a different name, according to the affidavit.

The money order was purchased at the Amscot location on Palm Coast Parkway. The transaction did not require identification.

But the alleged scheme did not end there.

According to the Flagler County Sheriff’s Office, Jester, acting as managing member of Florida Ohana Homes LLC, then sold one of the stolen lots for $29,970 to an unsuspecting buyer on Sept. 10.

Surveillance video from the UPS Store on Belle Terre Parkway showed Jester signing the warranty deed and having it notarized. The money was transferred to the Bank of America account for Florida Ohana Homes LLC.

Authorities also allege that Jester transferred $8,000 from the business account into her personal account at VyStar Credit Union, where the money was spent on hotel stays and purchases throughout Flagler County.

By following the financial transactions, detectives were able to trace the money and recover a substantial portion of the proceeds from the alleged illicit sale.

More than $14,000 was seized from the company's Bank of America account and another $6,000 from Jester's personal credit union account. A total of $20,000 was recovered.

On Nov. 6, deputies located Jester driving in Bunnell, FL, and arrested her on charges of grand theft and obtaining property valued between $20,000 and $50,000 by swindle.

She is being held at the Sheriff Perry Hall Inmate Detention Facility on a $45,000 bond.

Flagler County Sheriff Rick Staly said the case stood out because, unlike many property fraud schemes involving suspects operating from distant locations, the alleged perpetrator lived nearby and conducted the transaction in person.

“Most of the deed fraud we see is run by scammers hiding in another state or another country. This one lived in Bunnell and signed the closing papers in front of a security camera in Palm Coast,” Sheriff Staly said. “Our detectives followed the trail of forgeries and money and recovered most of it for the victim.”

Big Walk, the co-op adventure from Untitled Goose Game developer House House, has been hit by a wave of negative Steam reviews after the studio disclosed that large language models were used during the final stretch of development.

House House added an AI section to Big Walk’s FAQ explaining that chatbot responses were occasionally used as starting points for modified game code. The studio estimates that “well under 0.1%” of the game’s code had anything to do with that usage and says generative AI was not used in any other part of development.

Big Walk launched on August 4 and remains rated Very Positive overall on Steam. However, its review history changed sharply after the disclosure spread. Steam recorded 135 positive reviews and three negative reviews on November 9.

The following day, that shifted to 426 positive and 366 negative reviews. The November 11 figures were 358 positive and 90 negative when checked.

House House said its primary use of LLMs involved researching problems with third-party code. The studio gave one example involving Unity’s Universal Render Pipeline, where shadow casters behind the player were reportedly rendered at an infinite distance.

The Dane Steadman Interview: Hard Going and High Stakes in the Karakoram

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There is much more to an expedition than summit or failure. In this exclusive interview, American climber Dane Steadman explains why his recent new route on Karakoram’s Sosbun Brakk (6,414m) “was much more difficult than the one on Yashkuk Sar, both on and off the mountain.” Strong words, considering that he and his partners were awarded a Piolet d’Or for Yashkuk Sar.

The one factor on the team’s side was the previous experience of Steadman and Cody Winckler. “It was our third trip, so we knew a lot more about climbing there,” Steadman said. Indeed, after Yashkuk Sar, Winckler and Steadman returned to Pakistan in 2025 and climbed two new routes with Blake Berghoff.

Grant Stewart of Canada, the third member of the Sosbun Brakk team, is a skilled climber but was less familiar with the Karakoram. His most remarkable climbs had taken place in Alaska and at home in the Canadian Rockies.

Comparing the two epic climbs, Steadman highlighted the differences.

“The trip to Yashkuk Sar went incredibly smoothly. Getting there and acclimatizing was great. We had an amazing weather window for the climb. Nothing on it was exceptionally hard, and we did it on the first try, and the descent went without a hitch,” he said.

He explained that the hardest parts of that 2024 first ascent were their lack of experience and the uncertainty that came with it. An unexpected mishap also occurred during the climb itself: a snow mushroom collapsed above them, forcing them to change their ascent route halfway up.

“On the other hand,” Steadman went on, “almost everything about the Sosbun Brakk trip was hard: lost bags, sickness acclimatizing, big storms beforehand and while on the mountain, hard climbing and route-finding, and a very difficult descent through terrible weather.”

The unclimbed north face of Sosbun Brakk. Photo: Dane Steadman

“[Overall,] on the Yashkuk trip, I think we felt for most of the time that we would be able to do it with only some moments of doubt here and there,” Steadman admitted. “On Sosbun, we thought much of the time that we wouldn’t be able to do it for all the reasons above. We were quite surprised [to succeed].”

One unusual aspect of the Sosbun Brakk expedition was its dates. They went to Pakistan and climbed in early fall, two months later than the Karakoram’s typical July-August climbing season. Yet, Steadman says this was planned to account for the changing climate in the high ranges.

Approaching the face. Photo: Dane Steadman

“First, while the Karakoram doesn’t receive as much of the Indian monsoon as the main Himalaya, it is still a monsoonal climate, and I think (and have heard from many locals now) that the weather tends to be much better in the fall than in the summer,” he explained.

“Second, we were concerned about rockfall and generally unsafe conditions on the face, being relatively low and lacking in buttresses. We thought the colder temperatures would make things safer and increase the odds of finding good ice.

“Third, Grant was able to get time off in October more easily than earlier in September. In a perfect world, we probably would have gone one to two weeks earlier, but we worked with what we had.”

Asked about other expeditions planning to visit the Pakistani mountains in the future, Steadman suggests that October could be a good time for certain climbs, but that September is also great. “This year, the weather was much less stable in September-October than I’ve experienced on my previous trips. Things got much snowier than normal for that time of year, [according to locals], but I think the weather was still better than through most of the summer.”

The team planned a single alpine-style push in the purest style. However, when they started on the route, they encountered an impassable hanging serac. They had to retreat and decide whether to try a totally different line or go home.

The team decided to try a route to the right of the originally planned one. But how could they be sure they wouldn’t reach another dead end?

Steadman admitted it was difficult. “The line we ended up climbing was entirely hidden from view at our base camp and where we acclimatized,” he explained. “[It was] an east-facing gash on a north face, which we only saw from the northwest until we tried it. From all the views we had before the first attempt, the central line we first attempted appeared to be the only route that wouldn’t involve climbing huge walls of monolithic gneiss, as it followed ice streaks and sheets for a good amount of it, and only looked to have relatively short cruxes.

At the base, they discovered that a tiny but menacing serac hung over the center of the wall, threatening the lower half. (The upper half appeared protected by an overhanging rock wall above it.)

“So we shifted to a different line to the left, which looked much harder but possible, and planned to rejoin our intended line at mid-height, under the protection of the overhangs.

That attempt didn’t so much dead-end as things compounded, forcing a retreat. “We were tired even on the approach from non-stop efforts to get there and acclimatize. We had some equipment issues, were moving slowly, and most of all, the climbing was damn hard,” he said.

In the end, they chose to rappel down after a two-hour-plus lead climb on a pitch that hadn’t looked hard from below. Dusk was approaching; there was no bivy spot in sight, and they were only 300m up the 1,800m wall.

“However, from our position on that attempt, we were able to see the right-trending, east-facing gash on the right side of the face, which was totally hidden by monolithic rock walls from all our previous vantage points,” he said. “So, after bailing, we felt like the central line would require fresh bodies, minds, and a huge weather window, but the right-hand line would be much more feasible.”

The climbers rested for three days in base camp and then set off again. “We certainly felt that we could get shut down at any number of points, especially at the steep mushroom-choked chimney on the headwall below the junction with the northwest spur,” Steadman admitted. “But it seemed like there was a good chance we could do it, and, most importantly, we felt like we could bail from anywhere [if necessary].”

The summit ridge of Sosbun Brakk after many tough decisions. Photo: Dane Steadman

However, bailing would not be easy. “The lower part would involve rappelling off V-threads, he explained, and if they had to turn back below the headwall, they would need to rappel out right to an icefield.

Route topo: line of ascent in red, line of descent in yellow. Photo: Steadman/Stewart

The trio summited on their fifth day on the wall, at 4 pm. Days were now short, and they still had a long, uncertain descent ahead. The descent began on the line of ascent, but eventually deviated to new terrain.

“We descended the upper half of our route, almost entirely rappelling down the northwest spur, with a little bit of downclimbing/traversing),” Steadman said.

They then reached a point west of their ascent line, where the face was slightly smaller and more covered in permanent ice. “Then we rappelled straight down, diagonaling back left partway down to avoid some serac hazard.”

Descending in worsening weather. Photo: Dane Steadman

Dane Steadman and Cody Winckler of the U.S., together with Grant Stewart of Canada, completed the first ascent of the north face of Sosbun Brakk (6,414m) in Pakistan’s Karakoram last month. The peak had been climbed only once before, by a Japanese team in 1981, from a different, milder side. The new 1,800m route includes difficulties of M7, WI5.

Legally restrict heading a football in training, says MP

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Guidance already exists to restrict heading in training in England and Scotland

An MP is calling for legal safeguards to limit repetitive heading in football training, amid growing concerns about the risk of brain injury.

Grahame Morris, the Labour MP for Easington, submitted a parliamentary motion on Tuesday urging the government to take action to reduce the number of times a player can head the ball in training.

Researchers have found footballers are three and a half times more likely to die from a neurodegenerative disease than the general population.

The Early Day Motion (EDM) describes the need to protect players' brain health as a "significant public health and player welfare issue".

"The aim of this EDM is to raise awareness of the growing body of evidence linking repeated head impacts, particularly from heading a football, to an increased risk of neurodegenerative disease," Morris told BBC Sport.

The motion encourages the government "to take a more active role in prevention, education and player protection" Morris added.

Morris's motion supports a national petition submitted in September by the charity Head Safe Football, and calls on the government to take a number of actions:

Impose legal limits on repetitive heading in football training

Establish a cross-departmental task-force involving medical experts, players representatives, campaign groups and football authorities to develop "enforceable heading safeguards"

Recognise chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) as a public health concern

Create a national action plan to protect players' brain health

Improve education and awareness of the risks associated with heading, including in schools and through the PE national curriculum

EDMs are seen as a means for MPs to express their opinion on a subject and to canvass support for their views. No matter how many MPs sign up to an EDM there is no guarantee that it will be debated in Parliament.

Football does already have restrictions around heading.

The Football Association has phased out deliberate heading in grassroots football matches for children aged 11 and under.

It has also issued guidance recommending limits on heading during training of no more than 10 headers per week at amateur level.

In the professional game in England, clubs have been advised to limit players to 10 "high force" headers a week in training.

Scotland has gone further, banning heading in training on the day before and after matches in the professional game.

However, campaigners such as Dr Judith Gates, founder of Head Safe Football, believe that voluntary guidance is not enough.

"What we are finding is that coaches and parents don't know of the guidance, or the ban. Or they do not know why it exists," she told BBC Sport.

"There needs to be greater clarity and greater specific restrictions in order to protect players of today and tomorrow."

Dr Gates, whose husband the former Middlesbrough player Bill died with CTE, a degenerative brain disease associated with repeated head impacts, accepts that enforcing a legal limit would be challenging.

The calls for greater protection came as Prime Minister Andy Burnham called, in an interview with the Telegraph,, external on English football to formally recognise the game's dementia crisis and establish an "industrial injuries" compensation scheme for former players.

In a statement to BBC Sport, he said: "I've spoken to families affected by dementia and brain injury and heard what they have been through.

"Their determination to get this issue taken seriously has been extraordinary, and they should never have had to fight so hard to be heard."

Burnham's intervention follows inquests into the deaths of former England World Cup winner Nobby Stiles and Scotland defender Gordon McQueen, which found repetitive heading had contributed to their deaths.

Big Walk used AI to fix a bug. Now the indie gaming world is having a reckoning.

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Any amount of AI coding is too much for many Indie Game Festival judges.

The last few years have seen significant debate over the widespread use of AI-powered tools in game development and the responsible disclosure of that usage to players. That debate has exploded into a full-blown controversy in the indie game development space this week, as Big Walk developer House House has publicly and belatedly disclosed minimal AI use in its popular co-operative puzzle exploration game.

This particular controversy has been brewing behind the scenes for months, as judges and jury members for the prestigious Independent Games Festival Awards discussed the issue in private discussion groups. But the debate started to break into public view on Monday when 19 members of the 50-plus-member jury released a signed statement outlining a stance “declining to vote for any game that uses generative AI in any capacity.”

Noting “a significant increase in the number of submitted games developer with generative AI tools,” the group of jurors called out AI’s ecological, economic, psychological, and labor harms in rejecting tools whose use, they said, is still “not a foregone conclusion in our industry.”

The jurors also urged the IGF as a whole to “adopt a strong anti-AI policy for future years that disallows games containing any AI-generated work.”

Although the jurors’ statement didn’t name any specific games, by Tuesday morning, one critical darling in particular was increasingly being noted as a central part of the debate. In a statement provided to Aftermath (and added to the game’s FAQ page shortly thereafter), developer House House admitted publicly that “LLMs were used sparingly” near the end of Big Walk‘s development “to help work through some difficult technical challenges and bugs.”

That limited AI use—which House House says represents “well under 0.1% of Big Walk‘s code—stemmed in part from a thorny Unity engine bug that was slowing the game down with “infinite length” shadows during some game situations. After consulting with a Unity engineer who determined a fix “wasn’t possible,” House House said it consulted an LLM chatbot, which suggested a possible fix that the developers “had to modify… extensively” into the 10 lines of code that ended up in the final game (At least one social media user says the bug was indeed fixable with significant human effort, though).

House House said it didn’t disclose this LLM usage for Big Walk‘s Steam release earlier this year because Valve “does not request disclosure of code or chatbot usage.” Indeed, Valve’s AI disclosure form for developers specifically notes that it is concerned with AI-generated content, not “efficiency gains” from “modern game development environments [that] have AI powered tools built into them.”

The IGF Awards make no such distinction for AI usage, however, which led House House to mention its AI-assisted bug fix in submitting the game for awards consideration. A version of that disclosure is being made public now because “it has become a matter of public interest,” House House said.

“This is not an endorsement of, or argument for, the use of AI tools, however minimal,” House House wrote. “We have some concern that voluntary disclosures like this might contribute to the normalization of this technology, or its treatment as ‘inevitable.’ We support the work of those who are trying to limit the harm generative AI is doing to videogames and our world, and apologize that Big Walk’s LLM usage may not meet their standards.”

Learning of House House’s AI disclosure was particularly painful for some IGF judges who had previously felt a special connection to Big Walk. In a blog post discussing his feelings, IGF jurist Austin Walker called the game “an achievement” that was “remarkable” and “beautiful,” featuring puzzles that “capture a range of play that is largely abandoned by the digital form.” Fellow jurist Damon Reece shared in a blog post that the game is “seriously special to me” and that it gave him “a profound and powerful emotional reaction to being in a space that felt like home.”

But when it comes to IGF awards consideration, Walker and Reece were among those who said that even minimal use of AI tools overrode that deep and abiding respect for the work itself.

“We have our hands on the lever here,” Reece wrote. “This is pain that has to happen [emphasis in original] to try and steer our culture away from this. We need to choose to speak loudly and clearly on this, to prioritize human creativity and labour, to tell the culture and the world that this is unacceptable to us. To do otherwise is rank cowardice.”

This isn’t the first time AI usage has noticeably affected game award judging. Last year, the Indie Game Awards (which are completely separate from the IGF Awards) rescinded awards for Clair Obscur: Expedition 33 after the developers admitted that some in-game textures were made using AI tools (these were later patched out).

“The Indie Game Awards have a hard stance on the use of gen AI throughout the nomination process and during the ceremony itself,” they said at the time.

Since House House’s public disclosure, the anti-AI contingent of IGF judges has found plenty of support from disappointed Big Walk fans and industry observers. In the last few days, the game’s Steam page has been bombed with over 400 negative reviews, though Steam’s anti-review-bomb tools seem to be limiting the impact on the game’s overall “Very Positive” reception there.

But others feel House House’s relatively minimal use of AI here is being blown out of proportion. They say it’s important to differentiate between games that use LLMs as a last-resort technical fix, for instance, and others that lean heavily on AI to generate the bulk of their art, music, or the core of the game engine itself.

IGF judge Liz Ryerson wrote on social media that she didn’t find House House’s AI use to be personally disqualifying for the awards and that “the fact that many acted like there aren’t any levels to this and it’s all the same is bothersome to me.”

“To feel like someone gets punished a lot more for transparency about a smaller level of fuckup vs. just not even attempting to be honest or transparent at all is what bothers me,” she wrote.

Steam Hype staff writer and prolific Steam curator Dominic Tarason also pushed back against the idea that “House House need to be memory-holed and their work never spoken of again” as a “penalty for touching the Forbidden Device.” He said he considers Big Walk “very mildly compromised art” that is still “absolutely, unquestionably art and very human.”

“They spent years on that work of art,” Tarason added. “They asked a chatbot for two minutes how to fix a bug. That’s a ‘That’s lame, don’t do that again’ failing. … Some of you are acting like they’ve been convicted of high crimes against humanity, to be sealed away in the darkest oubliette until not even bones remain.”

But some have argued that using AI to fix a bug rather than hiring an expert to figure it out did make the game’s development at least a bit less human than it could have been. “House House aren’t laying off developers to replace them with Claude, and I’m sure they would be firmly against the mere thought of doing this, but we can also clearly see how their actions are part of the same problem,” AI researcher Mike Cook wrote on his blog.

Still, game developer, author, and professor Brendan Keogh wrote that he thinks “an extreme zero tolerance of any [generative AI] is ultimately counterproductive now. [There’s] a lot of anger at devs that feel deeply conflicted enough about their glorified Google search to disclose it, and [that is] encouraging those using it to actually steal labor to hide that use.”

8 Forgotten '80s Comedy Movies That Aged Like Fine Wine

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The 1980s produced some of the most iconic films of all time. Whether it was groundbreaking sci-fi hits like Star Wars, the reinvigoration of action-adventure stories like Indiana Jones, or the rise of young stars in the Brat Pack films, the decade was filled to the brim with brilliance. In the comedy realm, it’s the time we were given classics like Spaceballs, Clue, and The Three Amigos. But while those films have remained timeless hits, there’s a bevy of titles that have fallen by the wayside.

Many films have aged quite poorly as unfortunate products of the time, but the films on this list may be forgotten, but they’ve aged like fine wine. From satirical road trip comedies to silly sci-fi wonders, the eight films on this list deserve another shot at being revered. How many of these movies do you remember, or saw the VHS case at Blockbuster back in the day?

It might sound like a joke, but back in the day, Martin Scorsese directed a comedy. In the neo-noir black comedy After Hours, Paul Hackett (Griffin Dunne), a mild-mannered Manhattan word processor, has a simple late-night date with Marcy Franklin (Rosanna Arquette) in SoHo that spirals into a surreal, Kafkaesque nightmare. Stranded downtown without money and hunted by a hostile neighborhood mob, he spends the night desperately trying to make it back uptown before his morning shift begins. An overlooked cult classic, After Hours plays out like a modern-day Odyssey, turning a simple late-night date into a frantic, anxiety-inducing laugh fest.

One of the tensest comedies ever made, After Hours plays out like a darkly comic, high-stress thriller where the entire city of New York feels like a vast, conspiring labyrinth. Stellar performances anchor the film, including Catherine O'Hara, John Heard, and Tommy Chong. Having an innate bond with New York City, Scorsese filmed the movie entirely at night with kinetic style. He captures a pre-gentrified SoHo, filled with artists, lofts, eccentric characters, and a grimy, independent energy. With sharp editing by Thelma Schoonmaker and a pulsing synth score by Howard Shore, the movie captures a breathless, ticking-clock momentum. The film resonates today as its core themes of urban alienation, the loss of control, and the absurdity of modern life remain timelessly relatable. Though forgotten, After Hours helped pioneer the “yuppie nightmare” template that directly influenced modern cringe-comedies and surreal urban thrillers. It may have a cynical ending, but it mastered the hamster-wheel concept of modern labor and urban survival.

While many science fiction films of the decade scared and thrilled its viewers, Innerspace’s mission was to make people chuckle. Directed by Joe Dante, the film follows adventurous test pilot Tuck Pendleton (Dennis Quaid), who is miniaturized in a microscopic pod for a secret experiment and accidentally injected into the body of a neurotic supermarket clerk named Jack Putter (Martin Short). The unlikely duo must team up to outwit criminals, recover the stolen technology, and restore Tuck to his normal size before his oxygen runs out. A truly goofy high-concept comedy, Innerspace blends manic energy, a wildly inventive premise, and an all-star comedic and action cast that delivers a highly entertaining sci-fi romp.

Innerspace mixes classic Saturday-morning adventure with Looney Tunes-style slapstick and a surprisingly grounded emotional core. The odd-ball humor comes from the odd-couple chemistry between Quaid and Short. Quaid’s hotshot pilot serves as the perfect foil for Short, who masters the frantic, high-strung physical comedy. Meg Ryan, who met her future spouse on the project, stars as Lydia Maxwell, Tuck's ex-girlfriend who gets swept up in the chaos, bringing a charming performance that soon established her as a star. The film won an Academy Award for Best Visual Effects thanks to the innovative practical effects, scale models, and interior anatomy sequences. Industrial Light & Magic's work gives the film an enduring timelessness. Innerspace is a rare, self-contained high-concept original pitch that doesn’t require homework or franchise setup to enjoy.

The absurdity of the crime drama Johnny Dangerously is what makes the film an utter delight. The film follows an honest, good-hearted man, Johnny Kelly (Michael Keaton), who becomes a 1930s-style mobster to pay for his mother's expensive medical bills and put his younger brother through law school. Filled with rapid-fire slapstick, fourth-wall breaks, and visual non-sequiturs, the film is a smart spoof of crime thrillers in the vein of Airplane! or Top Secret! Johnny Dangerously works wonders thanks to its high-energy parody of 1930s and 1940s gangster films, rapid-fire gags, and a uniquely talented cast.

Directed by Amy Heckerling, the film throws a massive volume of gags at the screen so that even if a joke misses, the sheer speed keeps you laughing. The humor is one of the film’s charming attributes. One of the most famous running jokes involves the mobsters, namely Roman Moronie (Richard Dimitri), mangling curse words into bizarre, kid-friendly gibberish, like "fargan iceholes, to bypass the brand-new PG-13 rating. Long before Keaton’s iconic roles of Beetlejuice or Batman, he took on the sweet, good-hearted guy who turns to a life of crime for purely good reasons. He plays the part like James Cagney dropped into a live-action cartoon. Alongside him is a stacked comedy ensemble, including Joe Piscopo, Marilu Henner, Peter Boyle, Dom DeLuise, and Maureen Stapleton.

When it comes to hilarious aspirational concepts, the initial premise of Lost in America is it. But like a good comedy should, nothing goes according to plan. Directed by Albert Brooks, the comedy centers on David and Linda Howard (Brooks and Julie Hagerty), a wealthy Los Angeles yuppie couple who quit their jobs, liquidate their savings, and buy a Winnebago to travel cross-country. Their dream of dropping out falls apart when Linda gambles away their entire nest egg in Las Vegas, forcing them to take menial jobs and confront the realities of the American Dream. Lost in America sharply and hilariously mocks the narcissistic illusion of dropping out of society and chasing the Easy Rider counterculture dream.

Lost in America targets 1980s materialism, entitlement, and corporate ego through a satirical approach. Through a comedy of errors, the Howards are forced to come to grips with the reality of how fragile their anti-establishment fantasy truly is. The concept still hits home today. The chemistry between Brooks' frantic, high-strung neurosis and Hagerty's comedic innocence drives the humor. A standout highlight is David's pathetic, frantic negotiation with the casino manager, played by Garry Marshall, trying to get their money back by invoking "society". The script centers on genuine, cringe-inducing human behavior, desperation, and the humiliation of having to crawl back to reality. The majesty of the film is its ability to target the timeless human anxieties of burnout, economic fragility, and the illusion of the dream future. Despite a 95% score on Rotten Tomatoes and a National Society of Film Critics Award win for Best Screenplay, the film has largely been lost to time.

In a perfect marriage of sci-fi and humor, Real Genius provided a smart spin on the campus comedy. Directed by Martha Coolidge, the film follows Mitch Taylor (Gabriel Jarret), a 15-year-old physics prodigy, and Chris Knight (Val Kilmer), a slacker college senior, who team up at Pacific Tech University to build a high-powered laser. When they discover their professor, Jerry Hathaway (William Atherton), is secretly weaponizing their research for the military, they orchestrate an elaborate, high-tech prank to sabotage the project and get revenge. Real Genius is celebrated for its sharp wit and positive message about intelligence.

The heart of the film centers on its unique character duo. Instead of playing a generic party animal, Kilmer makes intelligence look appealing and helps ground the younger, high-strung prodigy. Kilmer’s natural charisma brings dry humor, irreverence, and effortless cool to the role. The movie consciously moves away from the crude, sex-obsessed tropes seen in other 1980s college comedies, forming its own identity in the process. That said, it’s those sex-obsessed comedies like Revenge of the Nerds that have stood the test of time. The film relies on a grounded scientific core as Coolidge researched real laser tech, consulted experts, and modeled Pacific Tech after Caltech, giving the scientific background a clever, grounded foundation. What is fueling the brightest gamma-ray star pair ever found?

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A warm gas trail near LMC P3 offers new clues to the extraordinary gamma rays coming from this mysterious binary system.

One pair of stars in a small galaxy next to the Milky Way gives off more gamma rays than any other pair of its kind.

Gamma rays carry more energy than any other form of light, and astronomers have struggled to explain where this pair’s gamma rays come from.

In the cold gas near the stars, they have now found a long, straight streak of warmer gas that lines up with the pair.

The warm streak is strong evidence that the pair, called LMC P3, is shooting out a jet of fast-moving matter. A jet like that could account for the gamma rays.

The work was led by Yasuo Fukui, a professor emeritus at Nagoya University, with colleagues at Gifu University.

“We discovered a gigantic molecular jet,” said Fukui. The team didn’t see the jet itself, though. What they measured is gas they think the jet has heated.

LMC P3 lies in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy that orbits our own. When astronomers look toward the middle of the Milky Way, dust blocks much of the view.

The Large Magellanic Cloud has far less of that problem.

One member of the pair is a big, very hot star. The other is a compact object, the crushed core left behind when a giant star exploded.

That core is either a black hole or a neutron star, and nobody has yet confirmed which.

The two circle each other about every 10 days. They orbit inside the debris from the explosion that made the compact object, a leftover cloud called DEM L241.

LMC P3 is the most luminous known gamma-ray binary and the first discovered outside the Milky Way. Astronomers first reported it in 2016.

Starting in 2022, the team pointed the ALMA observatory in Chile at the region.

The array of radio dishes picked up the faint signal of carbon monoxide in the cold gas near the stars.

That signal told the researchers how the gas was spread out, how it moved, and how warm it was.

One streak of gas stood out. It runs about 26 light-years long and three light-years wide, and it lines up with LMC P3.

That length is roughly six times the distance from the Sun to the nearest star.

The streak is still extremely cold: minus 400°F to minus 352°F (33 to 60 K). The gas around it measured about minus 433°F (15 K), which is normal for quiet gas clouds in that galaxy.

Measured from absolute zero, the streak is two to four times warmer than its surroundings.

The team found no nearby hot stars that could have warmed the gas by shining on it.

Fukui said the warm temperatures, spread across a large part of the gas cloud, make “a very strong case” that the gas is being struck by a high-energy jet from the compact object.

That same kind of jet can power gamma rays.

For years, there were two main explanations for LMC P3. In the first, the compact object is a fast-spinning neutron star that blows out a wind of charged particles.

The wind slams into the wind coming off the big star, and the collision speeds particles up enough to make gamma rays.

In the second, the compact object pulls gas off its partner. The gas swirls into a flat, spinning disk around it, which astronomers call an accretion disk.

A disk like that can launch narrow jets of matter moving close to the speed of light.

The second setup is a small version of a quasar.

Quasars, among the brightest objects in the universe, are powered by giant black holes feeding on gas at galactic centers. Some also launch powerful jets.

A star-sized version is called a microquasar. The new streak supports the microquasar idea.

Because black holes generally drive stronger jets than neutron stars do, Fukui sees LMC P3’s huge gamma-ray glow as a clue to what kind of object powers it.

The high gamma-ray output “provides a very strong support for the possibility that its central object is a black hole” rather than a neutron star, Fukui said.

In the paper, the team argues for a jet but doesn’t name the compact object.

Roughly 20 microquasars are known in the Milky Way. Most were found with optical telescopes, radio dishes, or X-ray instruments, aimed at parts of the sky that are mostly clear of dust.

Few microquasars have turned up in the crowded, dusty middle of our galaxy.

The researchers suggest that searching for straight streaks of warm carbon monoxide could turn up microquasars there.

LMC P3 is the second object where this kind of carbon monoxide streak has been found. The first was a gamma-ray source in the Milky Way called HESS J1023-575.

A jet can only build a streak where there’s enough gas in its path. LMC P3 is close to a gas-rich region where new stars are forming.

Around the well-known microquasar SS 433, the gas is thinner, which the team thinks is why only scattered clumps formed there instead of one long streak.

No telescope has detected the jet directly, in radio waves, X-rays, or gamma rays. The researchers wrote that this fits with how sensitive current instruments are.

The streak also shows up only on the north side of the pair, possibly because that’s where the gas cloud is.

The ALMA map didn’t cover the south side, so a matching streak there hasn’t been searched for at this level of detail.

A second, fainter streak runs at an angle of about 16 degrees to the first.

If the jet slowly changes direction over time, it could have made both, but that idea hasn’t been tested.

The team plans sharper ALMA observations that can pick out details smaller than about three light-years.

Those would give better temperature readings and could show whether the jet has been shifting direction.

The full study was published in the journal The Astrophysical Journal Letters.

Amazon’s open-source AWS Emulator lets developers validate their code locally, without the cloud

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Amazon Web Services Inc. is turning its public cloud computing platform on its head with the launch of a new service called AWS Emulator.

It’s a free, locally hosted software suite built by autonomous artificial intelligence agents that can replicate hundreds of the company’s cloud services on developer’s workstations. In a blog post, AWS Senior Principal Engineer Matt Goldberg said that the entire service was designed and written exclusively by AI agents, with not even a single line of its code written or reviewed by a human engineer.

AWS Emulator is designed to help customers overcome a major bottleneck in the application development process that has arisen with the adoption of AI coding agents such as AWS Kiro and Claude Code. Those agents are generating line after line of code faster than human programmers can blink, and the sheer velocity of that production is causing real issues for the teams tasked with verifying it before it can ship in production.

All AI-generated code needs to be validated against real cloud infrastructure, but doing that in the real AWS cloud is becoming all but impossible given latency and cost considerations. Though there are some existing third-party AWS emulators that developers can use, those services cover only a small fraction of AWS’ sprawling catalog of cloud services and quickly drift out of sync as those services evolve. With AWS Emulator, developers now have access to a free-to-use, locally hosted emulator that developers can use to run multiple iterative test loops on their own machines, Goldberg said.

The new tool was the brainchild of Goldberg, but he was actually inspired by the creation of AWS’ foundational Paxos-based distributed locking service, called “Alf,” which still underpins core cloud services such as Amazon S3 and DynamoDB.

In a second blog post, Swami Sivasubramanian, AWS’ vice president of agentic AI and emerging technologies, explained that when he and his colleagues first built Alf more than 20 years ago, they could test it only by simulating its entire environment inside a Java virtual machine built manually over several months.

Sivasubramanian explained that his team would have to inject faults and isolate concurrency bugs manually before they could deploy new services in production. But as AWS’ cloud has scaled up, it’s no longer feasible to hand-craft a simulation of its entire platform.

This is the conundrum that inspired Goldberg to see if that task could instead be done by AI agents. After seeing research that showed how AI agents were able to build a C code compiler from scratch, he concluded that they should also be able to replicate any AWS service if given a formal specification and reference implementation.

So he set about doing so, using AWS’ public Smithy API models as the specification and the live AWS platform as the ground truth. He began by prompting AWS Kiro to rebuild the Amazon Simple Queue Service, and it generated a functional replica of that service within just 15 minutes.

Seeing the potential, Goldberg and his team then set about organizing dozens of autonomous agents running on Amazon Bedrock AgentCore into three separate teams. The first is tasked with generating thousands of end-to-end fidelity test suites from API models and verifying them against the live AWS cloud. The second runs the tests against the emulator to try and find any bugs or discrepancies, and if any are identified, those agents will automatically file a GitHub issue.

The third team was tasked with creating and implementing the fixes for those bugs. As a final step before the code enters the GitHub repository, it goes through an automated merge gate that’s supervised by adversarial security agents to check for any vulnerabilities.

The AWS Emulator is available for anyone to download starting today. It’s built in the Rust programming language and runs as a local HTTP server that can be accessed via a command-line interface on Linux, Windows, macOS or as a Docker container. To get started, developers simply have to redirect their AWS CLI or SDK calls to the localhost to enable rapid, offline validation that’s free.

AWS Emulator has been open-sourced under a Creative Commons CC0 public-domain dedication license, together with its raw commit history and a public backlog of known discrepancies filed under “things-that-do-not-work.”

Sivasubramanian said AWS Emulator illustrates the dramatic evolution of the role of the software engineer. With AI agents now responsible for the grunt work of writing, testing and debugging the actual code, humans are now becoming “systems orchestrators,” tasked with defining the boundaries, architecting the test specifications and designing the guardrails that guide the work of those agents.

Neoclouds face a shakeout as cloud-native software skills decide who survives

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AI infrastructure clouds have multiplied as GPU capital pours into new data centers, but standing up capacity is proving easier than running it at scale. With Kubernetes now hardened into the operating layer for AI factories, the cloud-native community holds expertise that many of these new providers lack.

KubeCon has been shifting toward an AI infrastructure event, with container orchestration for AI and distributed inference now central to the conversation. The newer AI clouds run on the same computing fundamentals as the hyperscalers before them, yet many specialize narrowly and lack a bridge to the enterprise systems that will ultimately consume their capacity, according to Sarbjeet Johal (pictured), chief executive officer of Stackpane.

“I’m afraid that the neoclouds, if they don’t partner with the ones who are appreciating the brownfield, they will suffer,” Johal said. “They can’t survive on their own because, who is going to take that compute from them? It’s the hyperscalers. The neoclouds are the overflow.”

Johal spoke with theCUBE Research’s John Furrier and Rebecca Knight at KubeCon + CloudNativeCon NA, during an exclusive broadcast on theCUBE, SiliconANGLE Media’s livestreaming studio. They discussed the event’s first-day takeaways, from AI-written code and token economics to the maturing neocloud market and the skills needed to run it. (* Disclosure below.)

Kubernetes spent more than a decade evolving in fits and starts, and the payoff is now showing up in AI. The Cloud Native Computing Foundation added a dedicated AI inference and agentic track to this year’s KubeCon North America schedule, a sign of where practitioner attention has moved. Kubernetes now runs across about two-thirds of the enterprise, sits inside Nvidia Corp.’s reference architectures and underpins the neoclouds, Furrier noted. Skills honed through years of operating Kubernetes are now being applied directly to the commercial buildout of AI factories, where most of the capital is going.

“Now it’s hardened. So when you have hardened Kubernetes, we’re seeing inference being talked about,” Furrier said. “Who would have thought at this show inference would be discussed? Well, guess what? Distributed inference is the hottest area right now.”

That maturity matters because so much AI capital spending is flowing to new providers. SoftBank Group Corp. unveiled plans to enter the U.S. neocloud business this summer, joining a crowded field. Many existing operators started out building data centers for bitcoin mining or arbitraging spot pricing rather than running large-scale software, according to Furrier, who sees a reckoning ahead. The hard part is less about acquiring GPUs than operating them as a true cloud.

“Half the neoclouds will fail,” Furrier said. “To run a big neocloud, you’ve got to look like CoreWeave. Well, guess what CoreWeave is? The founders know data centers. … People who know large-scale software stacks at scale win.”

The rescue, in Furrier’s view, will come from the cloud-native community. He pointed to CoreWeave Inc., whose engineering is run by former Google Cloud LLC executive Chen Goldberg, as the model for pairing data center know-how with large-scale software skills. He also suggested private equity investors could buy smaller Kubernetes companies to fill the gap. Those companies could command billion-dollar valuations as data center owners with land, power and shell seek help with connectivity, multigeneration Nvidia hardware and workloads that span data centers, he explained. Johal, who watched a similar story play out at Rackspace Technology Inc., agreed that software will decide which providers last.

“We have seen this movie earlier. So if you want to be a good hyperscaler, if you don’t have the software chops, you’re dead,” Johal said of the new AI infrastructure clouds. “We knew how to run data centers at Rackspace, but we didn’t have software chops. … Without software chops, you’re dead in the water.”

Attend, the First Marketplace Connecting Filmmakers Directly to Theater Owners, Finally Launches Today

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Way back in October 2024, The Fithian Group, led by former NATO chief John Fithian and founding partner Patrick Corcoran, announced plans for an ambitious new marketplace, one that would help connect filmmakers and their projects directly to theater owners looking to book their movies and effectively cut out the distribution middleman. Today, that vision is now a reality.

The Fithian Group on Wednesday at the American Film Market announced the formal launch of Attend Theatrical Marketplace, the group’s online platform and marketplace for filmmakers and exhibitors that is now live and ready for business.

Attend launches with today just a curated selection of four films, all of whose filmmakers will now be doing the legwork to connect with theaters and find venues at which to exhibit their work. But the platform is already loaded with exhibition partners who will be able to browse available films, with more to be added soon, and Attend will eventually be a fully open platform to any filmmaker who wishes to do outreach directly to theater owners. Related Stories DC Studios’ Latest Reboot: James Gunn and Peter Safran’s Exit Is About Cost-Cutting, Not Just Creative Issues ‘Paper Tiger’ Review: Adam Driver Gives a Career-Best Performance in James Gray’s Devastating Tragedy

IndieWire got an early demo of Attend and how it aims to simplify the work that distributors do every day in putting movies into theaters, as well as the work exhibitors have to do in booking movies, paying invoices, and more. And IndieWire will on Wednesday lead a panel discussion at AFM with Corcoran, Charles Beale of the site’s designer Titlepool, and filmmaker Lilian T. Mehrel, whose Tribeca award-winning film “Honeyjoon” will be one of the four inaugural films shopping for theaters.

Beyond “Honeyjoon,” the other three films available on Attend at launch are Kenny Riches’ crime drama “Mouse”; “Spoke,” which is a documentary about firefighters and the trauma they deal with after the fires; and “Censurada,” an LGBTQ+ drama from Marco Garza about forbidden love in 1969 Spain.

The vision of Attend was outlined to IndieWire over a year ago with an indie rom-com called “You, Me, and Her” serving as an early guinea pig for the idea. The thinking was, if you had a film that could demonstrate it already had an audience and you had a clear sense of where your audience was and how to target them, all you needed were the screens to showcase your movie and the means to get your movie into the hands of those theaters.

That was and remains the tricky part. Few people have the connections with theater owners to be able to reach them and get a film out in any meaningful capacity. This year has produced a number of examples in which enterprising filmmakers have done without distributors and found the audience they already knew was there to release their movies. YouTuber Markiplier went directly to theaters and made $50 million with his horror movie “Iron Lung.” The documentary “NAZA” skipped a distributor in order to get its story about the IDF out with urgency. Even an AI movie called “Gods Don’t Give Gifts” is next month releasing without the aid of a distributor.

Attend seeks to use technology to simplify that process, to get your film out to an even wider audience and a more targeted one. For filmmakers, the platform is designed to eliminate the guesswork of who you need to talk to, how you reach them, and what attributes a given exhibitor is looking for in a film. For exhibitors, it filters all your titles and screens into one place, and it makes the receiving of deliverables or the payment of invoices more automated than the often tedious work it is for most exhibitors today. It’s exciting to see the whole vision come into fruition.

On the filmmaker end, the way Attend works is you as an individual can upload details and assets about your film, from simple details like its runtime, logline, and genre, to much more granular details about the aspect ratio, the available formats, the awards it has won and festivals it has played, the social networking reach of talent, and many more data points. There may be assets you don’t realize will be significant to a given exhibitor but will jump out as they browse your profile. You can then identify themes that speak to the film’s target audience and narrow the demographics for which your film is intended.

From there, you can filter and browse theaters to identify which theaters would be the best fit for your movie. For instance, Attend has data that can show a theater is within walking distance of a firehouse or that the theater has ties to a local advocacy group that aligns with the themes of the film’s story, and Attend’s predictive AI engine can recommend those theaters that would be available and the best fit for your film. Then, instead of reaching out to each of those theaters individually, filmmakers can easily select all the relevant theaters they want to message and have the option to manually add or subtract more beyond the recommended targets. You can even modify proposed theatrical terms for individual theaters or personalize messages, and as theaters come in, Attend can dynamically estimate P&A spend for those locations.

When theater owners are contacted, they’ll receive push notifications about films that are available for them to review, and they can further browse any available films in the marketplace and do their own outreach. Exhibitors can create tabs and info on any number of their circuit’s screens or locations and can upload in spreadsheets specs like screen size, audio, and seat count that filmmakers can view themselves to better get a sense of which theaters are the best fit. Exhibitors can specify rental prices for individual screens, you can mark your theater as being willing to offer simple four-walling options or that it’s capable of hosting big premieres, whether you want to offer “split screens” or “clean screens,” or you can set terms for how long you’re able to hold a movie. And not only does it also itemize marketing costs, Attend clearly indicates what the share of a film’s box office would be if they were to accept it and program it.

This helps theater owners in a number of ways. It takes the busy work out of things like paying numerous individual vendors or distribution partners, and it lets you download and easily generate a W9 form based on that film’s booking, being able to do so across multiple locations. But for theater owners that generally don’t know what they’re playing until the Monday before, this gives theaters far more advanced leeway and awareness to then be able to do the necessary marketing, put tickets on sale, and actually get butts in seats. All that is especially important for the smaller indies that need that word of mouth.

For now, theaters haven’t linked their loyalty data or other point of sales or scheduling tools to Attend, but that could come with time, and as people actually begin to use the platform, the predictive functionality will improve in the long run.

Corcoran and Fithian see Attend being useful for smaller distributors themselves in the long run, not just individual filmmakers who want to cut out the middleman. Distributors can use it to plan the campaigns of multiple titles and use its automation features to save time and costs. And while today Attend just supports new releases, there’s no reason why older library titles couldn’t be marketed here to exhibitors as well.

Time will tell if filmmakers find Attend an effective form of reaching theaters or if exhibitors adopt the platform widely to make it a useful tool for all parties. But it’s an ambitious vision that if implemented properly could have enormous impacts for the independent film world. And after two years of effort and hype, that day could finally be here.

Amazon Web Services has made its UAEF agent evaluation tool open source.

AWS has released UAEF, a free open-source tool that tests AI agents on 36 measures, from tool use to safety. It works across several agent frameworks and flags when a new prompt or model makes an agent worse.

Amazon Web Services has released a free tool that tests whether changes to an AI agent made it better or worse. The Universal Agent Evaluation Framework (UAEF) is open source, AWS said in a blog post on Wednesday.

AI agents are built with different software kits, such as LangGraph, LangChain, Strands Agents and Amazon Bedrock AgentCore. Each one records an agent’s steps in its own format. That means teams often have to rebuild their testing whenever they switch tools.

UAEF converts those records into one shared format, then scores the agent on 36 measures in seven areas. These include how well it picks and uses tools, the quality of its answers, safety, speed, and how it handles long conversations or teamwork with other agents. Some scores are calculated directly, while others are judged by a second AI model on Amazon Bedrock.

Teams can save each test run and compare it with an earlier one. Any score that drops by more than 5% is flagged as a regression and rated minor, moderate or severe. AWS says 12 organisations in telecoms, healthcare and financial services have used UAEF to compare agent versions before release.

The tool is free to use, but scoring with AI judges and saving results both rely on AWS services. AWS also warns that UAEF should not be used to make security decisions.

Getting agents to work reliably remains a struggle. AWS has said almost 90% of prototypes Amazon built two years ago never shipped. Startups such as Raindrop have raised money to catch agent failures in production.

Researchers have also shown that one prompt could take over AgentCore agents, which AWS disputed.

The European Commission's new Digital Fairness Act could change monetisation in games forever - and not just in Europe

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

"Well, we'll just do this for the rest of the world."

The European Commission has published its proposed Digital Fairness Act, which has the potential to change how video games are monetised - worldwide - forever. A draft of the Act was leaked yesterday and what's been published today appears to be, to my eye, the same, though there's now also a mini-site accompanying it that explores the Act in more detail and uses more understandable language.

And note that while the Digital Fairness Act isn't about video games exclusively - it's much broader than that - it does mention video games specifically, highlighting both their increased importance to society now, and that policy-shaping bodies like the European Commission are prepared to take them seriously.

The Digital Fairness Act is set to become European law, probably sometime between 2028 and 2029, once everyone's discussed it and made whatever amendments they're going to make to it. And at that point, breaching the regulations will come with potentially steep fines (up to six percent of a company's worldwide annual turnover, or up to €2m where an annual turnover isn't available).

The biggest things in the Digital Fairness Act relating to video games are regulations around loot boxes (sometimes referred to as "variable reward systems" or systems with "uncertainty-based rewards") and virtual currencies, particularly with regard to "minors" - anyone under 18 years old.

The Act stipulates paid-for virtual currencies must be disabled in a game by default for minors, then activated by parental and guardian approval, and that paid loot boxes "shall not" be offered to minors at all. Moreover, games must provide a way for everyone to disable paid loot boxes completely, and clearly show the probability of receiving items from them.

It's these changes that have the potential to overhaul how loot boxes work everywhere going forwards, and whether companies use them at all. To understand why, I talked to games business expert George Osborn, who runs the excellent Video Games Industry Memo newsletter, and who's been following the Act closely.

Osborn sees this as a "really big" regulatory change and believes that companies like EA, Xbox, Activision Blizzard, PlayStation, and other big gaming companies, both in console gaming and mobile gaming, will be "really concerned" by this. That's partly because a lot of these things being outlined are not necessarily easy to implement.

For starters, a company now needs a way to work out who's a minor playing its game and who isn't, and it needs to provide "a master switch", as Osborn calls it, for people to switch paid loot boxes off with. These things require not just design changes but perhaps entire alterations to a game's approach; it means theoretically being able to turn paid card packs off in EA Sports FC Ultimate Team, example, and their not being able to be sold to minors at all.

"EA might be looking at it and going, 'This isn't great news for [EA Sports FC] Ultimate Team because people can switch it off,'" Osborn added, "but Ultimate Team is at least hived off in a box." EA could potentially age-gate Ultimate team as a mode and deal with it that way. But what if you're a card game where random paid rewards are central to your premise? "What that now means is you're possibly going to have to find a way to just completely turn that off for users, and it is very difficult to do that when they're embedded into the design of the game."

The virtual currency issue is potentially easier to tackle because existing parental controls can control spending, so those tools could expand to cover it. But another proposal brought forward in the Digital Fairness Act states that a game must clearly show, in a region's local currency, how much an item costs when bought with virtual currency, which adds another layer of complication when considering different currencies and people moving between countries with their devices.

These changes require investment and thought, and they sit alongside broader changes that affect things like subscription services, such as Xbox Live and PlayStation Plus and Nintendo Online, to make them easier to cancel and less able to obfuscate prices and hidden extras. There's even a stipulation that people be able to talk to a human in customer service rather than a chat bot. There are more technicalities for game-making companies to consider.

But why should people outside of the European Union care? These rules are only enforceable there. Because: if a company is selling a game into the European Union, which I expect every game company will want to do - "you don't just want to rule out a market of a few hundred million consumers; it's not a light thing to cut that out", Osborn says - then it will have to comply by these rules. And if it does, how many different versions of a game will it be prepared to make?

Think about the recent global move towards all devices using USB-C chargers: that was a movement started by a European Commission ruling. Think of the new Switch 2s with removable battery packs: that was another change instigated by the European Commission. "Because that market is big enough, you design for it," Obsorn says, "and then your logic becomes, 'Well, we'll just do this for the rest of the world.' You're not going to produce one with the battery sealed in and the battery without. You're just going to find one way to produce things.

"That's likely to be the biggest impact in the UK and the rest of the world," he adds, "as people adjust the designs of their games to follow these rules. There's going to be a strong likelihood that some of the design features will remain within the games because it's easiest to work off a single universal version."

The other thing, which Osborn thinks is more important, is that policy makers around the world are "desperate" for solutions to problems and don't know where to look for them. "So if someone comes up with an answer to a question that's been annoying everybody, like 'what do we do about loot boxes?'," he says, "and it provides a particularly strong - or perceived to be effective - solution, it'll be copied. And it'll be copied because a big regulatory bloc like the European Union, which is known for protecting consumers particularly well, has decided this is a good thing. People trust it."

Then, because companies have already developed a way to comply with those rules, it becomes difficult for them to argue that they can't do it elsewhere. "Because those regulators go, 'Well why not?'" Osborn says. "Because if you build the solution in one place, you can build it elsewhere."

Whether or not this will be the end of loot boxes as we know them, Osborn isn't sure. "My guess would be this will continue to push loot boxes into the fringes of video game monetisation. It was already starting to happen," he says. "I know some really high-profile games have them very prominently, which makes people feel like is there really any change here? But I know a lot of companies behind the scenes started to quietly move away from them in the last few years when they were a reputational problem. If you move to a world in which you're meant to have a massive off-switch in your game for them, that's a world in which fewer companies will go in that direction."

Some will stick with it. "If you're an EA, it's going to be very difficult to move away from the model that has supported your business for years," Osborn says. And card-based deck-building games will struggle to avoid it. "But overall, we're going to see a shift away from it," he adds. "And more broadly what this marks is a much wider crackdown on the way that video games monetise their players. As you start to see that crackdown come into force, game developers are going to have to think much more carefully about how the design decisions they make don't just interact with ethical practices, but actually with legal practices too."

Scientists have proposed a new tabletop device to detect the subtle way Earth's daily spin on its axis twists the space-time around it ‪—‬ an effect predicted by Einstein's general relativity.

If built, the sensor could measure the quantum effect known as frame dragging with the same precision as the best satellite experiments, but in minutes instead of years, the researchers proposed in a new study.

The research, published Sept. 17 in the journal Physical Review Letters, aimed to address a classic mismatch in physics. Picture a ring of clocks bolted to a spinning merry-go-round. You synchronize each clock with its neighbor around the ring. Then, you compare the last clock with the first. They don't match.

"It is impossible to synchronize that collection of clocks when you're in a rotating frame," Kai-Isaak Ellers, a physics graduate student at the University of California, Berkeley (UC Berkeley) and first author of the study, told Live Science.

A photo of scientists working on NASA's Gravity Probe B in 2003. This device measured Earth's frame-dragging from space, confirming Einstein's predictions with an 18% margin of error.

Einstein's theory of general relativity predicts that a massive, spinning object drags the fabric of space-time along with it, just like a spoon twirling in honey. The effect is clearly visible around supermassive objects, like the Milky Way's central black hole. But around Earth, the effect is very faint.

So far, scientists have measured Earth's frame dragging only from orbit. They tracked how the twisting of space-time slowly shifts spinning gyroscopes or the paths of satellites. In 2011, NASA's Gravity Probe B satellite, which carried four gyroscopes, confirmed Einstein's prediction with a margin of error of about 18%. Satellites tracked with lasers have since done even better. In July, researchers reported in the journal Nature that the LARES-2 satellite had shrunk that margin of error to just 0.2%.

However, that took about three years of laser tracking. Researchers also had to account for the fact that Earth bulges at the equator and its mass is spread unevenly, which gives the planet’s gravity small irregularities that tug on satellites far more strongly than frame dragging does. To subtract those tugs, the team relied on detailed maps of Earth's gravity made by other satellites.

Measuring frame dragging more precisely is a way to stress-test general relativity. Several alternative theories of gravity predict a slightly different amount of twisting, and a lab-based measurement would also be free of the modeling assumptions that satellite experiments depend on, Ellers said.

The new device is a loop of superfluid helium-4 — helium chilled until it flows without friction and behaves like a single, giant quantum object. The loop is interrupted by a thin barrier riddled with tiny holes, called a Josephson junction. Superfluid helium can slip through the holes, but ordinary liquid helium cannot.

"That's where the quantum behavior comes from," said K. Birgitta Whaley, a quantum physicist at UC Berkeley and a co-author of the study.

As Earth spins and drags space-time, it nudges the quantum state of the helium flowing through the junction. That nudge shifts the frequency at which the helium sloshes back and forth in the loop, which the researchers could detect.

A visualization of Earth's uneven gravity field taken by NASA's Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment in 2009. The lumpiness of our planet's gravitational field makes it harder to precisely measure relativistic effects.

Superfluid devices, many of them built at UC Berkeley, have detected Earth's rotation since the late 1990s. But the helium-4 versions built around a Josephson junction, like the new design, have worked within about a hundredth of a degree of 2.17 K (minus 455.76 Fahrenheit, or minus 270.98 Celsius), the temperature at which helium-4 becomes a superfluid. At that temperature, a lingering fraction of "normal" helium mixed in with the superfluid adds noise.

Instead, the team calculated how the device would behave at a few thousandths of a degree above absolute zero. Below 0.7 K, the noise changed character, and the team found it drops sharply as the helium gets colder. One key finding in the new paper is the result of the team's thermal noise analysis, which showed how sensitive these devices could be at millikelvin temperatures, Elles said.

The catch is the barrier. Earlier helium-4 devices used holes about 90 nanometers wide. Working at these frigid temperatures demands much smaller holes, about 1 nanometer across ‪—‬ roughly the size of a helium atom. Keith Schwab, a physicist at Caltech and co-author of the study, and his collaborators are developing such materials, but they haven't been built into a working sensor yet.

If they can be, the researchers calculate that the device could measure frame dragging to 0.2% precision in about 35 minutes. If its vibrating membrane were made from the lowest-loss crystals, such as quartz or sapphire, that could drop to about one second, they said.

"This seems to be a very serendipitous discovery that at millikelvin, we can get this high sensitivity," Whaley told Live Science.

The signal corresponds to a time difference of about 10-35 seconds between helium flowing one way around the loop and the other — the same mismatch as the clocks on the metaphorical merry-go-round.

"The interesting thing about it is just how small it is," Whaley said. "It's not so far off from the Planck time," about 10-44 seconds, the shortest slice of time before the known laws of physics are expected to break down. This experiment is still a billion times too large to reach that limit, Whaley said. But future helium experiments could help explore whether time flows smoothly, like a ramp, or ticks forward in tiny, indivisible jumps, like the frames of a movie.

Nvidia board’s idolatry of Jensen Huang could open the door to shareholder lawsuits in Delaware Chancery Court

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There is no doubt that Nvidia founder and CEO Jensen Huang is a visionary genius, whose vision as the ‘Godfather of AI’ has improved billions of lives around the world and contributed greatly to American prosperity and innovation. He has attended our Yale CEO forums and has been revered by CEOs across sectors and around the world for his bold, entrepreneurial vision and his revolutionary technological triumphs, plus he is a showcased contributor to our new, upcoming book, When Machines Act: The Promise and Perils of our Agentic AI Future, published by MIT Press and written with Two Sigma Co-Founder David Siegel.

Yet, amid calls for Huang’s immediate resignation from top GOP voices, including Sen. Kevin Cramer of the Senate Banking Committee and others, and demands that he answer to Congress, there were some blatant red flags in Huang’s recent leadership that were apparently missed by the Nvidia board.

In October 2025, the New York Times reported that Megaspeed, a little-known Singapore firm, had bought billions of dollars of Nvidia chips amid suspicion that it was helping China evade U.S. restrictions by laundering banned chips on the U.S. export restriction list to China via neutral countries. Nvidia said a review found no evidence its chips were diverted, but that reporting was followed by an explosive report by a short seller, Culper Research, alleging “uncomfortably close ties” between Alice Huang, the head of Megaspeed, and Jensen Huang, in May 2026. Jensen described the Megaspeed dealings as normal, and apparently the Nvidia board was content to leave it at that.

Late last month, the Times reported that U.S. intelligence believed that Alice Huang was a Chinese asset. A company spokesperson said the diversion claims had been debunked and described the relationship as nothing unusual. Ten days later, Jensen admitted he had been unfaithful to his wife, in a message that referred to the national security concerns in the press – a troubling sequence of events after all the repeated denials.

The Caremark doctrine, established in 1996 in Delaware Chancery Court, establishes that corporate directors have a fiduciary duty of oversight, and that directors breach their duty of loyalty when they make no good-faith effort to monitor the risks central to the business, or when they consciously ignore red flags.

Under this doctrine, and derivative variants of it, several boards have run into trouble in Delaware Chancery Court for similar sins of omission when it comes to oversight. In 2019, Delaware’s Supreme Court revived a suit against the directors of Blue Bell Creameries, an ice cream maker whose board had no system for overseeing food safety before a deadly listeria outbreak. In 2021, the Court of Chancery let claims proceed against Boeing’s directors, who had no board committee responsible for airplane safety before the 737 MAX crashes. That case settled for $237.5 million. The lesson of both: on risks that can sink the company, the board must watch for itself: the board cannot simply trust management, even when those risks were unforeseeable or unanticipated.

So plaintiffs’ lawyers will ask the questions directors should have asked many months ago. Who conducted the review that cleared Megaspeed? Did it report to independent directors or to management? Did any director know the CEO had a personal relationship with an executive of the customer under review? Did the board ever discuss Megaspeed at all, or Jensen Huang’s relationship with Alice Huang? Did any board members even care to ask any questions about these topics?

The answers are in the board meeting minutes, and alarmingly for Nvidia’s board, shareholders can now get those minutes with nearly complete transparency. Nvidia knows this better than most companies for a very ironic reason. In 2022, it fought a books-and-records demand all the way to Delaware’s Supreme Court. Even after Delaware narrowed those inspection rights last year, board minutes and board materials remain within reach for plaintiffs litigation. That is how the Boeing plaintiffs built their case.

All of the major risks that have now landed on Nvidia in such dramatic fashion were exactly the risks directors should have been most alert to.

First, and most importantly, every board entrusted with protecting minority shareholders should be alert to management conflicts of interest. A Megaspeed affiliate bought about $4.8 billion of Nvidia-powered equipment in under two years. A chief executive in a personal relationship with an executive of a customer that size has an apparent conflict that Nvidia’s own code of conduct would have likely required him to disclose. And Megaspeed was no ordinary customer: by October 2025 both Washington and Singapore were investigating it.

That conflict poisons the compliance record. When the questions first surfaced, Nvidia said it had visited Megaspeed’s sites and had no reason to believe chips were diverted. That assurance came from people who report to a CEO with a personal tie to the customer being cleared, a tie no investor had been told about. Bloomberg reports that Nvidia’s general counsel went so far as to contact a senior Commerce Department official about the government’s Megaspeed probe. Did the board authorize that call? Did it even know?

Then comes national security and export-control evasion, as “mission critical” a risk as food safety at an ice cream maker or flight safety at Boeing. Nvidia makes the most strategically sensitive product on earth; and The Information reported today that companies tied to Alice Huang helped Chinese buyers obtain $7.5 billion of Nvidia chips, including banned ones that were laundered through neutral parties. That report is unconfirmed, and Megaspeed denies wrongdoing. But the penalties for export violations run from fines to criminal prosecution to the loss of export privileges, and a board should not have been waiting for verdicts before it starts asking questions. According to Nvidia’s own proxy statement, the board committee responsible for trade compliance met three times last fiscal year, and the company has no chief compliance officer. How much did they know, and when?

The national security risk is larger still. According to the Times, U.S. officials concluded that Alice Huang was recruited by China’s Ministry of State Security to get close to Jensen and to shape his views on chip sales to China – which of course, Jensen Huang has been very vocal on in Washington and in the media, arguing that the U.S. should continue to sell chips to China so that China is not building their own chips. No one has alleged that Jensen knew Huang was a spy, and Beijing calls the claims a smear. But the president was reportedly briefed in late 2025, and a secret memo circulated among policymakers this spring, the same year Jensen was appointed to the President’s Council of Advisors on Science and Technology. Now three House committee chairmen want him in a closed-door briefing, and the president says the FBI is reviewing the matter. Yet not one Nvidia director, as Semafor noted, has a national security background; what questions were asked and what prudent diligence steps were taken by the board?

Then there is the risk every board dreads: that management did not tell it the truth. On Oct. 29, a company spokesman described Jensen’s interactions with Alice Huang as “not unusual or unexpected,” the routine customer contact of any CEO. The same week, Jensen called the Megaspeed dealings normal. Nine days after that he told employees he had been unfaithful to his wife, and tied the admission to the national security concerns in the press. If directors approved those October statements knowing what Jensen knew, they could be in trouble. If they never asked him, that is the Caremark problem.

Shareholders have already paid. Nvidia closed today at $168.89, down almost 10% in the two sessions since the admission, a loss of more than $400 billion in market value, and about 30% below its early-October record. Nobody can attribute this recent fall merely to just the AI sell-off, as the evidence shows it was an idiosyncratic move. On Monday, as Nvidia fell 4.3%, the semiconductor index jumped nearly 7%, and direct competitors all rose, including AMD.

The last risk is one the board manufactured itself. Jensen has more than 50 direct reports and no identified successor, and one prominent analyst estimates Nvidia would be worth 25% to 30% less without him. That reflects poorly on the board. Succession is a director’s first duty, and these directors let one man become the company with no obvious succession plan until it is too late.

Even now, the reflex appears to be deference. The same statement that launched the board’s investigation declared, before a single interview, that the board had “no reason to believe that either national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised,” and that it had confidence in Jensen. A board that announces its conclusion on the day it opens the inquiry, makes one wonder whether the board has already predetermined what their preferred outcome would be rather than following the trail of wherever facts lead them in a way that protects all shareholders.

Readers of Fortune will know that we have been critics of Vice Chancellor Travis Laster on the Delaware Court of Chancery, who wrongly attacked us in his Cloudflare opinion as “advocates for dual class shares” and for founder control more broadly. Vice Chancellor Laster did not apparently understand that we are not advocates for anything; rather, we call it as we see it as fact-based academics with five decades’ experience as a widely recognized scholar of corporate governance.

We have published extensively on dual-class shares, on when they work, and when they don’t, with extensive academic analysis and quantitative research into performance of companies with dual class share structures.

In our extensive careful research, published widely in academic outlets such as the Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance as well as FORTUNE, Chief Executive Magazine and many other outlets, we have studied instances when dual class shares work well, such as when a founder or family with real money at stake uses the protection to invest through cycles and outlast short-term activists, as at Berkshire Hathaway, Alphabet and The New York Times.

Similarly, we have studied instances when dual class shares should be retired, such as when they shield controllers with ulterior motives from consequence, as at WeWork under Adam Neumann or Paramount under the Redstones.

Critics of founder control tend to treat dual-class shares as the bogeyman behind every entrenched founder, and one share, one vote as the cure. Nvidia is the case study that breaks that outdated, prosaic theory. Nvidia has a single class of common stock, not dual class shares. Jensen owns about 3.5% of it and casts 3.5% of the votes. The board is declassified, every director stands for election every year, and a simple majority of directors could replace him as CEO at any meeting. On paper he is as removable as any hired manager. In practice, no CEO in America has been more immune from proper scrutiny and oversight.

What entrenched Jensen was not the capital structure of the company; rather, it was the apparent idolatry of a board which was in thrall socially and interpersonally to Jensen Huang. He earned the adulation honestly, building a company from a booth at Denny’s into, for a time, the most valuable on earth. But the directors whose job it was to provide effective oversight to guard shareholders’ interests, apparently effectively joined the congregation. No charter provision did that, and no proxy adviser would have caught it. Nvidia has the independent majority, the lead director, the annual elections and the single share class that the checklists of ISS and Glass Lewis reward.

Nor has one share, one vote ever been a vaccine against scandal. Enron, Tyco, Wells Fargo and Boeing all had it. One of us argued in the Harvard Business Review in 2002, after Enron, that failed boards routinely passed every structural test, and that what set great boards apart was a culture of open dissent, in which directors challenge the chief executive and each other. That was true then and Nvidia shows it is true now.

When it comes to providing effective oversight, what matters is the oversight culture of the board and the quality of the board, not the ‘check the boxes’ yardsticks so often misleading propagated by governistas, such as ‘one share one vote’.

Delaware needs no new invented legal theory for measuring whether Nvidia’s board did its duty as a board in providing oversight. If shareholders bring Caremark claims against Nvidia’s directors, the court will ask what the board knew and what it did to find out. We have called out the Delaware Court of Chancery when it overreaches, and we will be again. But failing to question a celebrated CEO about a customer under federal investigation is no business judgment at all. Nvidia’s directors were paid to provide oversight over Jensen Huang, however brilliant. And in that duty, by all outside appearances, they apparently whiffed.

Aristotle famously warned, “There is no great genius without a touch of madness.” Psychiatrists call shared disorders “folie à deux.” It is up to the board to ensure a company’s owners benefit from the genius of founders but are protected by the madness.
