From Files to Cited Answers in One API Call: Managed Ingestion and SearchDocuments in Agent Retrieval

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A guide to the new managed ingestion and SearchDocuments features in Agent Retrieval, for searching PDFs, Office files, and videos without writing an ingestion pipeline.

Before an agent can answer a question about your PDFs, someone has to build the pipeline: parse every file, split it into chunks, embed each chunk, write the chunks to a vector database, and then learn that database's schema to query it. For video it gets harder: transcode the files, cut them into clips, embed the frames, and keep track of which second of which video each clip came from.

Most of that code has nothing to do with your application. Two features recently added to Agent Retrieval (formerly Vector Search 2.0), now in Public Preview, take that work off your hands. Managed ingestion builds the pipeline for you: point a data store at files in Cloud Storage, and the service parses, segments, and embeds them with Gemini Embedding 2. SearchDocuments, the second new feature, then searches the data store with one API call and returns the matching passages with their page numbers or timestamps. In our tests, 18 Alphabet investor PDFs went from a public Cloud Storage bucket to searchable, page cited results in about 9 minutes, and 19 minutes of video became searchable by both visuals and spoken audio in 0.3 to 0.5 seconds per query, with no parsing, chunking, transcription, or embedding code.

This guide covers how to create a data store, import documents and videos, and search them with filters, reranking, and audio-aware video search.

Agent Retrieval Collections already give you semantic, text, and hybrid search over your own data objects. But you have to produce those data objects yourself, and to query them you need to know how they are stored. Here is what changes when you let a data store do the work:

The backing Collection is still a regular Agent Retrieval Collection, so you can query it directly whenever you need full control.

A data store holds your files and the configuration that controls how they are parsed and segmented. The data store and SearchDocuments APIs are currently served over REST at v1beta, so the examples use an authorized session from google-auth:

Two segmentation modes are available. Chunk segmentation (the default) splits text into chunks of 100 to 500 tokens and works with layout parsing, which also detects tables and headings. Page segmentation produces one segment per page. For paginated documents such as PDFs, both modes record page numbers (startPage and endPage) so you can cite pages or filter by page range: page segmentation returns a single page per segment, while chunk segmentation returns the page range each chunk spans.

The import runs as a long-running operation. Re-importing the same files updates existing documents instead of duplicating them, and files whose content didn't change are skipped.

For "How many employees does Alphabet have?", plain hybrid search returned two balance sheet pages among the top five, which don't mention employees at all. With reranking on, every top result reported the headcount: the annual report's "Alphabet had 190,234 employees" and the "Number of employees" row of the quarterly supplemental tables.

The rule of thumb: If your data is files, let a data store ingest them and search with SearchDocuments. Drop down to the Collection only when you need control the data store doesn't give you.

Managed ingestion doesn't change what good retrieval needs: well-segmented content, good embeddings, and the right filters. What changes is who builds it. Instead of maintaining parsers, chunkers, embedding jobs, and a schema, you point a data store at a bucket and get back segments that are ready to cite.

For agents, that means a retrieval tool that returns where each answer came from, a page in a PDF or a moment in a video, with one API call.

New Discovery Could Transform How We Treat Chronic Back Pain

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Restoring a protein that declines in aging spinal discs reduced DNA damage in human cells in the lab, revealing a potential new target for back pain treatments.

For people living with persistent back pain, everyday activities such as walking, bending, or even sitting comfortably can become difficult. New research from the University of Utah points toward a possible way to address one of the underlying causes of age-related back pain by protecting the cells that keep spinal discs healthy.

Spinal discs are cushion-like structures between vertebrae that allow the spine to move. In a study published in Aging Cell, researchers found that older and damaged discs contained lower levels of a protein called BLST, which is encoded by the ZNF865 gene. The study is the first to characterize the function of both the gene and its protein.

To understand whether declining BLST levels contribute to disc deterioration, the researchers used CRISPR, a gene-editing tool that can be used to regulate the activity of specific genes. Restoring BLST production in human disc cells reduced signs of cellular aging and DNA damage, while decreasing its activity in mouse and rat models led to disc degeneration and pain-related behaviors. The findings suggest that BLST helps maintain healthy spinal discs by protecting cells from damage and dysfunction as they age.

“This paper directly shows a novel understanding that DNA damage is a driver of back pain, and that we can regulate BLST therapeutically with CRISPR,” said Robby D. Bowles, the study’s lead researcher and an associate professor of biomedical engineering. “Back pain is the number three leading cause of disability as measured by disability-adjusted life years, only behind cardiac disease and drug use disorders.”

Although increasing BLST activity could eventually offer a way to prevent or treat age-related back pain, additional preclinical studies and, ultimately, clinical trials are needed to determine whether targeting the protein with CRISPR is safe and effective in people.

The research brought together Bowles and members of his laboratory in the John and Marcia Price College of Engineering, specialists in orthopedics and arthritis at the Spencer Fox Eccles School of Medicine, and researchers at Huntsman Cancer Institute at the University of Utah.

Because DNA damage is involved in numerous diseases, the discovery could have applications well beyond spinal health.

“It has been the highlight of my career to work on a protein that was unknown and be the first to identify its function,” Bowles said. “While this work focuses on its application to back pain, the mechanisms identified have the potential to be applied to treat cancer, neurodegeneration, and any disease in which DNA damage is involved.”

Further research is needed to establish whether the protective mechanisms observed in spinal disc cells also play a role in age-related cancers.

“The fact that a single gene can restore normal cell function and reverse aging-related effects is incredible,” said Jason Gertz, a professor in the Department of Oncological Sciences at the University of Utah and co-leader of the Genomics, Epigenetics, and Metabolism Program at Huntsman Cancer Institute. “BLST is widely expressed across tissues and could be a promising target for cancer treatment and prevention, since boosting BLST activity should improve the repair of DNA damage and the overall health of aging cells.”

Reference: “ZNF865 (BLST) a Novel Regulator of DNA Damage and Cell Senescence in Back Pain” by Christian Lewis, Hunter Levis, Jonah Holbrook, Jacob T. Polaski, Timothy D. Jacobsen, Josh Mizels, Molly Czachor, Sarah E. Gullbrand, Brian Diekman, James C. Iatridis, Jason Gertz, Brandon Lawrence and Robby D. Bowles, 9 October 2026, Aging Cell. DOI: 10.1111/acel.70754

7 Most Universally Loved Fantasy Movies Of All Time, Ranked

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Fantasy movies have a peculiar advantage over almost every other genre: they can make somewhere you've never been feel like a place you miss. A fictional kingdom can become as familiar as the neighborhood you grew up in, especially when a piece of music announces that it's time to go on an adventure again.

That's part of what makes the best fantasy movie worlds so difficult to leave behind. Their appeal survives changing special-effects technology and the fashions of whichever decade produced them. Some become even more rewarding with age, revealing details that escaped us when we first encountered them.

The phenomenon is particularly apparent with the Harry Potter movies that feel different when you watch them as an adult. Childhood excitement gives way to a more complicated appreciation of the characters and the dangers surrounding them. That experience extends far beyond Hogwarts, particularly with fantasy films that have become beloved fixtures of popular culture.

But there's an important distinction between the greatest fantasy movies ever made and the most universally loved. A critically acclaimed film can be difficult, divisive, or demanding, while a beloved favorite can inspire extraordinary affection without winning a single major award. The rarest achievements manage both.

This ranking considers critical reception alongside sustained audience enthusiasm, cultural impact, rewatchability, and appeal across generations. No movie can literally please everybody, and this isn't a ranking based strictly on review scores or financial success. The higher placements go to films whose popularity has endured and whose emotional appeal reaches beyond devoted fantasy fans.

These seven films have earned remarkably lasting affection, even if deciding which deserves the top spot invites a little friendly disagreement.

Tim Burton's Edward Scissorhands looks like a fairy tale someone accidentally dropped into American suburbia. Behind the pastel houses and manicured lawns is a lonely young man with blades instead of fingers. The neighbors initially celebrate him, then turn suspicious when his differences become inconvenient. Johnny Depp delivers one of his most affecting performances, relying on expressions and gestures to communicate Edward's desperate desire to belong. His tentative relationship with Kim (Winona Ryder) gives this wonderfully strange fantasy its emotional center.

The film's melancholy makes it particularly memorable, especially when paired with Danny Elfman's haunting score. Burton's visual imagination is unmistakable, from Edward's gothic mansion to the unforgettable sight of snow falling from his ice sculptures. Among Johnny Depp's most memorable performances, Edward remains unusually sympathetic. Its bittersweet conclusion gives the movie a narrower emotional appeal than the more celebratory adventures ranked above it, but that sadness is also what makes it so enduring.

A chocolate river, an everlasting gobstopper, and a man who seems completely uninterested in making children feel safe. What more could anyone want from a fantasy movie? Mel Stuart's Willy Wonka & the Chocolate Factory has delighted audiences for generations without losing its wonderfully unsettling personality. Gene Wilder's performance is the movie's greatest attraction, effortlessly switching between charm and quiet menace. His Willy Wonka is unpredictable, frequently hilarious, and impossible to imagine being played quite the same way by anyone else.

Charlie Bucket (Peter Ostrum) provides the heart, with his impoverished family circumstances making the golden-ticket discovery particularly satisfying. The factory itself is a marvel of practical effects and imagination, while its songs have remained part of popular culture for decades. Although some of its darker moments can frighten younger viewers, the film's combination of childhood wish fulfillment and eccentric humor has made it remarkably difficult to outgrow. Even after learning what's behind every door, Wonka's factory still seems capable of surprising its visitors.

Choosing a single Harry Potter movie for a list about universal affection is practically an invitation to an argument. The first installment introduces Hogwarts with an irresistible sense of discovery, while the later movies deliver some of the franchise's biggest emotional payoffs. Nevertheless, Alfonso Cuarón's Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban arguably strikes the strongest balance between exceptional filmmaking and audience affection. The introduction of Sirius Black (Gary Oldman) and Remus Lupin (David Thewlis) gives Harry's world a more complicated emotional dimension, while the terrifying Dementors suggest that childhood innocence is gradually disappearing.

Cuarón also changes how Hogwarts feels. The castle becomes more lived-in, the changing seasons matter, and the young actors finally have room to develop beyond their established character traits. Even the elaborate time-travel sequence remains one of the franchise's most satisfying narrative achievements. Some fans will always favor the wonder of Sorcerer's Stone, which makes choosing a definitive favorite difficult. Yet Prisoner of Azkaban demonstrates why the series continued to resonate with viewers as they grew older: its magic became more compelling as its characters matured.

Few filmmakers possess Hayao Miyazaki's extraordinary ability to make impossible things feel entirely natural. In Spirited Away, a young girl enters a mysterious world, her parents transform into pigs, and she's forced to find employment in a bathhouse populated by spirits. Somehow, this starts to seem like a perfectly reasonable predicament. Chihiro is an unusually compelling protagonist because she possesses no extraordinary powers or heroic destiny. Her gradual transformation from a frightened child into someone resourceful and compassionate gives the film an emotional foundation that transcends its Japanese folklore influences.

Miyazaki's imagination is breathtaking, although the movie's quieter passages are equally important. The famous train journey across the water is particularly beautiful, allowing viewers to absorb this strange world without another dramatic confrontation. Its Academy Award for Best Animated Feature helped cement its international standing, while its continuing popularity has introduced generations of viewers to Studio Ghibli. Spirited Away is more unconventional than the three films ranked above it, but its emotional clarity makes even its strangest creations feel wonderfully human.

Somewhere over the rainbow, Judy Garland helped establish the language of cinematic fantasy. Nearly nine decades later, The Wizard of Oz remains astonishingly accessible, with an influence extending far beyond the generations that originally watched it in theaters. The ruby slippers, Emerald City, and Yellow Brick Road have become universally recognizable cultural symbols, while Dorothy's companions remain among cinema's most endearing characters.

Yet the movie's longevity isn't simply the result of its historical importance. Dorothy's predicament is timeless: she feels misunderstood, dreams of escaping her ordinary existence, and eventually discovers that what she wanted was closer than she realized. The Scarecrow, Tin Man, and Cowardly Lion are equally appealing because their individual insecurities are so recognizable. Their journey reveals that the qualities they desperately seek are things they've been demonstrating all along.

Among the greatest fantasy movies of the 20th century, The Wizard of Oz occupies an almost untouchable position. Its visual imagination was groundbreaking, and its music continues to resonate across generations. In terms of sheer cultural recognition, it arguably deserves the top spot. However, this ranking also considers the personal attachment and enthusiasm that inspire viewers to return repeatedly, which gives the final two films a slight advantage.

"As you wish." Two words are enough to remind countless moviegoers why Rob Reiner's The Princess Bride has maintained such extraordinary affection since 1987. Adapted by William Goldman from his own novel, the film follows Westley (Cary Elwes) as he attempts to reunite with Buttercup (Robin Wright). Along the way, he encounters an eccentric collection of characters, including Mandy Patinkin's revenge-driven Inigo Montoya and André the Giant's wonderfully gentle Fezzik.

The movie is frequently hilarious, although its willingness to embrace sincere romance is equally important. Reiner understands that audiences can recognize the absurdity of fairy-tale conventions while still becoming emotionally invested in them. The framing device, in which Peter Falk reads the story to his skeptical grandson, cleverly acknowledges the prejudices viewers might bring to an old-fashioned adventure. It's an almost perfect illustration of the film's ability to win over reluctant audiences.

Unlike many fantasy productions, The Princess Bride doesn't rely on spectacular special effects or elaborate worldbuilding. Its considerable charm comes from wonderfully drawn characters and dialogue that only gets funnier with familiarity. There's a reason it belongs among the most comforting fantasy movies ever made. In terms of sheer rewatchability and audience affection, it has arguably the strongest claim to being universally loved. Only one movie surpasses its combination of critical admiration, enduring popularity, and emotional impact on an enormous scale.

Peter Jackson faced an extraordinary challenge when he adapted J.R.R. Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings. He needed to introduce a vast fantasy world without overwhelming newcomers, satisfy readers who had imagined Middle-earth for decades, and convince audiences to follow an enormous adventure across three movies. With The Fellowship of the Ring, he accomplished something remarkable: he made Middle-earth a place millions of viewers wanted to visit again and again.

The opening scenes in the Shire establish the film's emotional appeal long before the adventure becomes dangerous. Hobbiton feels wonderfully lived-in, Gandalf's arrival resembles a reunion with an old friend, and Frodo's departure carries genuine sadness. Elijah Wood gives Frodo an appealing vulnerability, while Ian McKellen finds warmth beneath Gandalf's considerable authority. Even Sauron, one of fantasy cinema's most formidable villains, rarely needs to appear physically to exert his influence.

The sequels feature larger battles and a more definitive conclusion, but The Fellowship of the Ring possesses something neither can recreate: the excitement of entering Middle-earth for the first time. Jackson's film combines extraordinary critical acclaim with mainstream success and the kind of devoted fandom that spans generations. The Princess Bride may be easier to revisit on a casual afternoon, but Fellowship inspires an equally powerful affection while immersing viewers in an entire world. A quarter-century after its release, the invitation to join the Fellowship remains almost impossible to refuse.

Star Trek: Nemesis ended the Next Generation crew's big-screen adventures on a sour note. Thankfully, that wasn't their final goodbye.

Saying goodbye to beloved television characters is never easy. After spending years getting to know them, audiences have expectations about how their stories should end. Some get the send offs they deserve, while others leave fans wondering what might have been. Unfortunately, 2002's Star Trek: Nemesis falls into the latter category, bringing an unceremonious end to the big-screen adventures of the Next Generation crew.

The fourth and final film featuring the Next Generation cast finds Captain Jean-Luc Picard (Patrick Stewart) confronting Shinzon (Tom Hardy), a younger clone of Picard who has seized power in a Romulan coup and plans to destroy Earth with a devastating weapon. It's a promising premise that Nemesis squanders on a convoluted, action-heavy story that forgets what made these characters special.

Now streaming free on Pluto TV, Nemesis remains a disappointing conclusion to the Next Generation film series. Having revisited it recently, I can confirm that nearly 24 years haven't done much to improve it.

The best episodes of The Next Generation aren't necessarily the ones featuring massive space battles. "The Measure of a Man" explores Data's humanity, while "Darmok" focuses on two captains struggling to communicate. Neither requires Picard to jump behind the wheel of a dune buggy and race across an alien desert while shooting at the locals. Yet that's exactly what Nemesis gives us.

Director Stuart Baird, who previously helmed the action thrillers Executive Decision and U.S. Marshals, and screenwriter John Logan, an Oscar nominee for Gladiator, seem determined to turn Picard into an action hero. To be fair, the Enterprise-E ramming Shinzon's massive warship, the Scimitar, is spectacular. Unfortunately, impressive action doesn't make up for a story that barely knows what to do with its characters. Geordi La Forge (LeVar Burton), Beverly Crusher (Gates McFadden), and Worf (Michael Dorn) are reduced to background players. Deanna Troi (Marina Sirtis) endures an unpleasant psychic assault by Shinzon's Reman viceroy (Ron Perlman), while her wedding to William Riker (Jonathan Frakes) gets surprisingly little attention.

Then there's Data (Brent Spiner), who sacrifices himself to save Picard and destroy the Scimitar. It's supposed to be the film's emotional gut punch, but the introduction of B-4, a less advanced android into whom Data has transferred his memories, leaves the door open for a convenient resurrection. Even in death, Data can't escape the possibility of a sequel.

For a crew that spent seven seasons exploring humanity, friendship, and sacrifice, Star Trek: Nemesis was a remarkably unsatisfying goodbye.

One of the more fascinating things about Nemesis is watching a young Tom Hardy play Shinzon. Hardy was still relatively unknown when he landed the role, and according to Stewart, he wasn't exactly the most sociable member of the cast.

In his 2023 memoir, Making It So, Stewart doesn't mince words about the movie, writing that Nemesis "was particularly weak." He also complains about the material he was given, saying, "I didn't have a single exciting scene to play."

As for Hardy, Stewart describes him as a solitary young actor who rarely exchanged greetings with his fellow actors and spent his downtime in his trailer with his girlfriend. Stewart emphasizes that Hardy wasn't hostile, but getting to know him proved nearly impossible.

When Hardy finished filming, he left without much ceremony, prompting Stewart to turn to Brent Spiner and Jonathan Frakes and predict they'd probably never hear from him again. Considering Hardy would go on to star in Inception, The Dark Knight Rises, Mad Max: Fury Road, and the Venom movies, Stewart's prediction ranks among the more spectacularly incorrect assessments of a future Hollywood star. To his credit, Stewart has a sense of humor about the whole thing, writing, "It gives me nothing but pleasure that Tom has proven me so wrong."

For more than two decades, Nemesis remained the final big-screen adventure for the Next Generation cast. Its disappointing box office performance effectively ended their theatrical adventures, leaving fans with a finale that felt less like a celebration and more like a franchise running out of ideas. Thankfully, the crew eventually got another chance.

The third and final season of Star Trek: Picard reunited Picard, Riker, Troi, Worf, Geordi, Beverly, and Data for one last adventure. Picard Season 3 wasn't perfect, and it certainly wasn't lacking in explosions, phaser battles, or threats to the Federation. But unlike Nemesis, it remembered that the greatest strength of The Next Generation was the bond between its characters.

That difference is especially apparent in show's final scene. After saving the galaxy yet again, the crew gathers around a table for a game of poker. No superweapons, no evil clones, and thankfully, no dune buggies. It's a fitting callback to "All Good Things," the 1994 Next Generation series finale, which ended with Picard finally joining his officers for their regular poker game.

Nemesis may have ended the crew's theatrical adventures, but Picard gave them something better: a farewell that celebrated what made them special. And after everything these characters have been through, there's something satisfying about knowing their story ended not with another battle, but with one last hand of cards.

You may remember that Apple made a seeming mistake in the user interface for macOS 26, meaning that attempts to resize windows would often fail. The reason for this has now become clear, and Apple has done something both fun and clever in macOS 27 to resolve it …

I was one of a number of people to note that trying to grab the corner of a window in macOS Tahoe in order to resize it would often fail. Developer Norbert Heger pointed out this is because Apple changed the radius of the corners without changing the grabbable area.

It turns out that my initial click in the window corner instinctively happens in an area where the window doesn’t respond to it. The window expects this click to happen in an area of 19 × 19 pixels, located near the window corner. If the window had no rounded corners at all, 62% of that area would lie inside the window. But due to the huge corner radius in Tahoe, most of it – about 75% – now lies outside the window.

At the time, the radius change itself looked like an inexplicable mistake on Apple’s part, and the company did revert it. However, Macworld’s Michael Simon appears to have discovered the reason for the original change.

The new rounder corners on the MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR are basically a perfect match for the macOS 26 radii.

So if you move a window to the corner of the new machine, it would fit perfectly. However, since Apple reverted the corner radius, it would no longer do so. The company solved this by a clever UI transition.

What’s happening there is Golden Gate’s 20-point corner radius is subtly and gradually expanding when It approaches the bottom corner of the screen to match the exact corner of the display, which is much larger. I can’t be certain it’s exactly 26-point, but it sure looks like it to me. The effect is incredible. I’ve watched it like 20 times and I can barely see it changing, yet when it reaches the corner it’s perfectly expanded to match.

You can see this illustrated by a GIF in the Macworld piece. While the original change annoyed me, Apple’s solution is the perfect example of the company sweating the details.

Roads Less Traveled

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

I explore some alternative routes through the mountains to finish my hike in Pennsylvania. One works out well while the other does not.

As predicted, I was all set on sleep around 5:00AM. Less predictable was the state of my left hip, which I had been sleeping on throughout most of the night and which was experiencing some discomfort. Counterintuitively, my “good” night of sleep with less waking during the night meant I spent too much time sleeping on one side of my body. Normally I wake up a handful of times and roll around to change my sleeping position. Time would tell if that would spill over into my hike. I opened my tent to hang up my water filter to get some water ready for breakfast and noticed that my interior mesh was ever so slightly damp. Indeed, the inside of my rain fly was wet. It hadn’t rained, I figured we must have had an unusually humid night. Not ideal. I was determined to get an early start on the day, I managed to have breakfast in my tent and get packing underway before sunrise. I scrambled onto Table Rock to watch the sun rise over Third Mountain. It was somewhere in the mid 30s this morning, with the humidity the cold was piercing through my shoulder-season apparel like it was barely there. Movement is the best cure so after snapping a few pictures of the sunrise I headed back to my tent, finished packing, and at 7:15AM was heading north on the trail eager to move Pennsylvania into the “Done” column.

It didn’t take long to notice that my left leg was feeling a little goofy. There was a sensation in my hip that made me feel like I was stepping too narrow. I try swinging my leg around to loosen it up, but it didn’t help. My only choice was to hope it would go away once I warmed up.

A darn good spot to camp for the night if I say so myself.

Last evening I studied the topographic map on FarOut and found that the gravel road between Second and Third Mountain is a viable alternative route back to my van. It would be roughly the same distance as the AT, but flatter and with less rocks, assuming gravel doesn’t count as rocks. I love a good alternate route that let’s me get a different perspective on my return leg so it felt like a no-brainer.

The day began to slowly warm up as I hiked along Peters Mountain. By the time I was descending back into the valley the sun was shining directly on the south side of the ridge and providing plenty of warmth. Ahead was the climb up Third Mountain, knowing that I was about to get warm awfully I fast I stopped to shed my sleeping layers and load them into my pack. To connect to the gravel road, I needed to take the Knauber Trail up Third Mountain. It proved a tough, direct route up the mountain. The trail didn’t seem to get nearly as many visitors as the AT. There were leaves piled up several inches thick and I was having to heave myself up the trail with my trekking poles since my shoes were prone to slipping. I arrived at the crest of the ridge in a huff, and promptly felt the temperature drop now that I was between mountain ridges, out of the sun, and no longer climbing. My gloves went back on, and I decided to tough it out in my single layer sun hoodie rather than dig out my other layers.

Sunlight beams onto the trail toward Third Mountain.

The Knauber Trail proves to be a steep, leafy challenge.

The gravel road passed through a mountain gap and descended into the Stony Creek Valley. On the way, I passed an upright stone marker noting the spot where members of the Kabob Hiking Club have been gathering annually to commemorate the founding of their club. Apparently it began in 1934. This location is well off the beaten track. I suppose you could drive down the gravel road easily enough, but be prepared for a long drive.

Once the road settled between Second and Third Mountain it followed Stony Creek for what I imagine is somewhere between 8 and 10 miles. There are stretches of the road where you can see a full mile ahead, maybe more. It was honestly a little unsettling. Imagine hiking 10 minutes and the view ahead hardly looks any different. I found it more comforting to focus on the ground below me, at least that was moving. I enjoyed the steady progress, though there wasn’t much to see or report on, just pine trees and not a soul to be found.

The gravel road descends sharply to the Stony Creek Valley.

Once I had made my way down the gravel road I took a moment to visit a few other markers at Rausch Creek. One sign explained the structures next to the creek were indeed filters as I had guessed yesterday. They use limestone to clean the water, which is why it looked so clear. I suspect the light gray color of the limestone settling on the bottom of the creek also helps the water to look brighter and more clear than if the creek bed were covered in natural rock.

As I made my way back toward Second Mountain I passed back through a flat section of trail that seems to flood regularly. Beavers had dammed some of the creeks and the soft soil caused many of the trees to fall, creating a relatively open area. Oddly, a plaid shirt was hung in the middle of the bog, one can only assume it belonged to one of the beaver’s victims.

With one final push I made my way up to Second Mountain where I made a miscalculation. High on the success of my gravel road alternate route, I decided to double down and take what appeared to be a shortcut. The AT crosses an abandoned dirt road which seemed to lead back toward the road not far from the trailhead where my van was parked. I found it odd that the road bent at a 90 degree angle just ahead on the map, but thought little of it. I forged along the road and did see a turn, but it was slightly too early to be the 90 degree turn on the map. Continuing on, the road continued straight ahead, which wasn’t shown on the map. There was also no 90 degree turn where it should have been. I decided to stick with the road that I was on even though I didn’t know exactly where it would go. Really, there were only two options, it would either end up near the road I was looking for, or it would turn a bit and eventually rejoin the AT.

It turned out to be the latter, albeit after some unwelcome elevation gain that meant I probably lost a bit of time by taking this route. My hip, which had gotten better after the first mile or so, acted up again around mile 20. It is a good thing I have a day off tomorrow for it to bounce back. It was nearly 4:00PM when I was back on the road making my way back to my van. I decided to use the afternoon to drive down to Hagerstown, Maryland so I could spend all day tomorrow focusing on tying up any remaining loose ends before starting off on my final section of the trail.

VanEck is ending its relationship with Gemini across three spot crypto ETFs, leaving Coinbase Custody as its remaining named custodian.

The asset manager disclosed in Nov. 19 regulatory filings that it had notified Gemini of plans to terminate its custody and clearing agreements for its Bitcoin ETF (HODL), Ethereum ETF (ETHV) and Solana ETF (VSOL).

The agreements are scheduled to expire in February 2027, and Gemini will continue to provide services until the termination takes effect. The decision also ends a separate arrangement under which Gemini facilitates Solana staking for VSOL.

The filings did not disclose the reasons for the termination or how much cryptocurrency VanEck currently holds with Gemini.

The three funds have attracted approximately $1.15 billion in cumulative net inflows since their respective launches and collectively managed $934.2 million in net assets as of Nov. 24, according to SoSoValue data.

VanEck's Bitcoin ETF accounted for the bulk of that exposure, with $977.1 million in cumulative net inflows and $848.2 million in net assets. Its Ethereum and Solana funds recorded cumulative net inflows of $151.6 million and $19.2 million, respectively.

VanEck's decision extends beyond asset safekeeping. Gemini also provides clearing infrastructure that helps the funds settle cryptocurrency transactions associated with ETF share creations and redemptions.

Under the existing arrangements, VanEck's funds can use Gemini Clearing to complete trades negotiated with liquidity providers that are also Gemini customers.

The service has supported cash-based orders, which require the funds to purchase or sell cryptocurrencies, and in-kind transactions involving the direct transfer of digital assets.

In The Air & On The Ground: How Premium Demand Has Rewritten Airline Operations

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Surging demand for premium airfare has forced global airlines to rewrite how they operate both inside the cabin and at the airport terminal. Customer demand passed a decisive juncture when Delta Air Lines reported that it made more 2025 Q4 revenue from premium-cabin sales than from main cabin fares for the first time. The landmark shift came as North America and Asia were already tearing out their economy rows while pouring billions into new business suites, satellite WiFi, exclusive lounges, and more perks.

That universal push to capture big spenders collided directly with global supply chain bottlenecks, rising oil costs, and certification delays. United Airlines launched its Elevated Boeing 787-9 with Polaris suite doors locked open pending federal sign-off, while Delta installed 44 domestic first class recliners aboard new Airbus A321neos after planned lie-flat pods missed testing. Overseas, Boeing's delayed 777-9 forced Emirates, Singapore Airlines, and Air Canada to spend billions rebuilding older widebodies to keep premium capacity expanding.

Every carrier has chosen a unique strategy to balance luxury inflight with terminal perks as passenger spending shifts. In another landmark change, United and American Airlines retired international first class while rolling out oversized business cabins and co-branded credit card clubs, while Emirates and Singapore kept enclosed first class suites alive alongside expanded hub lounges. Even US low-cost carriers scheduled domestic first class seats and airport clubs for 2027, leaving basic economy fliers with tighter legroom and fewer perks.

Delta Chief Executive Officer Ed Bastian explained that the second-quarter milestone by confirming nearly all new seat growth now targets business class and premium economy fliers. American CEO Robert Isom echoed that shift on September 16, admitting his carrier sold too much Basic Economy when just 30% of seats generate half its revenue. Isom told View From The Wing: "Those 30% of seats, they're only going to grow in our fleet. That's demand that's more likely to hold up through economic uncertainty."

Delta saw immediate proof of that appetite aboard its accidental 44-seat first class A321neo subfleet flying between Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta, Los Angeles, and Seattle. Even with undersized galleys and a single forward lavatory, paying passengers filled 80% of those recliners by August. As Delta Chief Commercial Officer Joe Esposito put it to From The Tray Table: "It's a really nice surprise that we've got such high demand for our premium products. We're experimenting with how big a domestic premium cabin can be."

American backed Isom's words by removing elite upgrades and frequent-flyer miles from Basic Economy tickets purchased after mid-May, and by using its cheapest fare to direct passengers into more expensive rows. Executives also announced Flagship First's retirement on March 28, 2027, bringing narrowbody premium seating to 40%. Air Canada targeted those same corporate travelers by relocating Boeing 737 MAX 8 jets to Rouge's leisure arm, while reinstating Airbus A320-200 and A321-200 aircraft to regular service.

Air Canada introduced lie-flat beds to single-aisle transatlantic flights in June, when it launched its first Airbus A321XLR between Montreal and Toronto before deploying the jet to Toulouse. Alaska and Hawaiian Airlines joined the narrowbody luxury drive later in the year, beginning in 2028 with lie-flat suites on upcoming Boeing 737 MAX 10 aircraft and upgraded Airbus A330-200s. Putting long-haul comfort on smaller jets allows carriers to serve narrower foreign markets profitably while avoiding widebody fuel use.

Airlines used different strategies to rearrange their cabins to accommodate large spenders on widebody fleets. In September, American launched its 'Project Olympus' Boeing 777-300ER between New York-JFK and Buenos Aires, eliminating international first class in favor of crammed Flagship Suites. In a similar vein, United expanded Polaris business class and Premium Plus capacity by eliminating dozens of regular economy seats on its Elevated Boeing 787-9.

Even as they rebuilt their fleets, foreign competitors refused to give up on the very front of the aircraft. In July, Emirates launched the 100th aircraft from its $5 billion interior makeover in Dubai. It preserved the enclosed first class suites on all of its Airbus A380-800 and 777-300ER planes while making room for premium economy. With the addition of private first class suites to its ultra-long-range Airbus A350-900ULR aircraft operating marathon runs to New York-JFK, Singapore pushed its own widebody rebuild budget to more than $1.56 billion.

Every square inch added to the front of these widebodies forced uncomfortable compromises behind the curtain. Singapore ran into sharp flier pushback after revealing that its retrofitted A350-900s keep older 2017 economy seats without 4K screens or Bluetooth audio. On incoming Boeing 777-9 deliveries, Singapore is also tightening standard economy from nine to 10 abreast in the upcoming 777X, trading away long-praised coach comfort to fund upfront luxury.

Delta Air Lines and United Airlines are producing record results on the back of a surge in premium travel.

As jetliners evolve, airport clubs are becoming increasingly congested as US airlines aggressively entice frequent travelers with co-branded credit cards. In September, Southwest Airlines and Chase announced that their first-ever Icon Lounges would debut in Austin, Baltimore, Honolulu, and Nashville in late 2027. The newly merged Alaska and Hawaiian also announced plans to open new flagship clubs in Honolulu and Seattle in 2028, directly challenging bank-run establishments such as the Chase Sapphire Lounge at Phoenix Sky Harbor.

Alaska Chief Executive Officer Ben Minicucci pitched those ground investments as a core part of the travel experience, saying: "We believe premium is more than a seat. It's how guests feel from the moment they book their trip to the moment they arrive at the airport to when they're in the air with us." On the same thread, to keep top international ticket holders out of packed credit card queues, Air Canada opened a seventh grab-and-go café in Québec City in June while offering apron Porsche transfers from its Signature Suites.

Singapore took a similar approach at its home hub, spending $34.1 million to expand its Changi Terminal 2 SilverKris lounges by 50% and give paid premium fliers dedicated space away from partner cardholders. Singapore Chief Executive Officer Goh Choon Phong said: "Our customers experience Singapore as one seamless journey, and this significant investment is designed to deliver greater comfort, choice, and convenience at every stage."

As construction continues, high-speed satellite internet has become just as essential to winning premium bookings as private terminal clubs. United raced ahead fleet-wide, passing 640 SpaceX Starlink-equipped aircraft in October to lead US carriers in total connected jets. Emirates prioritized raw long-haul bandwidth instead, mounting twin fuselage antennas on every Boeing 777-300ER and three atop each Airbus A380-800, so hundreds of passengers can stream simultaneously above 2 gigabits per second.

Premium cabin demand has only continued to grow.

In February, Capital One stopped offering free guest and authorized user access on its Venture X card. Instead, it now charges $45 per adult guest unless the member spends $75,000 annually. Even though over 70 new lounges started construction this year, banks and airlines tightened admission requirements due to persistent overcrowding. A few days after restricting access, Capital A's 12,500-square-foot (1,161 sqm) facility was opened, arriving at LaGuardia Airport with faster table turnover and custom-made tapas.

Alaska and Hawaiian separated their terminal spaces between domestic crowds and long-haul flyers. Hawaiian broke ground in October on a 13,000-square-foot (1,208 sq m) Honolulu lounge for 200 guests as part of a $600 million overhaul. Alaska simultaneously advanced a 41,000-square-foot (3,809 sq m) Seattle complex for late 2027, splitting a 500-seat standard club from a 200-seat Aurora Lounge for international business-class fliers.

What is MailBaby? Outbound SMTP relay pricing, limits and how it works

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 25 • Originally published at srvscripts.com

Running outbound mail from a shared hosting IP is a reputation game that most providers lose slowly. One compromised contact form can undo months of clean sending, and getting an IP off a major blocklist costs engineering hours every time. MailBaby is InterServer’s answer to that problem: an outbound-only SMTP smarthost that takes every message your server sends, scores it, and delivers it from IP pools whose reputation InterServer manages. This guide explains what the service does, what it costs, and the constraints you need to design around before pointing a production server at it.

Short answer: MailBaby is InterServer’s outbound-only SMTP smarthost: a hosting server authenticates to relay.mailbaby.net on port 25 with STARTTLS, every message is spam-scored, and clean mail is delivered from reputation-managed IP pools while bad mail is bounced back. It costs $1 per month for about 5,000 messages plus $0.20 per 1,000 after that, caps sending at 6,000 messages per hour per address, and leaves inbound mail, MX records and Dovecot untouched. (Reviewed October 2026.)

In short: MailBaby is InterServer’s outbound-only SMTP smarthost: a hosting server authenticates to relay.mailbaby.net on port 25 with STARTTLS, every message is spam-scored, and clean mail is delivered from reputation-managed IP pools while bad mail is bounced back.

MailBaby sits between your mail server and the internet. Exim, Postfix or Plesk authenticates to relay.mailbaby.net on port 25 with STARTTLS, hands over the message, and MailBaby takes responsibility for delivery. Every message passes through a content filter that assigns a spam score. Clean mail is routed out through a sending pool matched to the reputation of the sending account; borderline mail goes through a lower-reputation pool; mail that scores badly is bounced back to your server with a rejection such as “classified as rSPAM” so the sender knows it was not delivered.

Because InterServer has run the platform on its own infrastructure since May 2024, the scoring model, the IP pools and the feedback-loop handling are all in-house. That matters for support: when a message is blocked, the log entry and the score are visible in the customer portal rather than hidden inside a third-party filter.

The service handles the tedious parts of outbound deliverability on your behalf: rDNS on the sending IPs, feedback-loop registration with the large mailbox providers, bounce parsing, and SRS rewriting for forwarded mail. Inbound mail is not part of the product. Your MX records, Dovecot and inbound spam filtering stay exactly where they are.

The account costs $1 per month, which includes roughly 5,000 messages. Beyond that, sending is billed at $0.20 per 1,000 messages. There is no per-server or per-domain fee, so a provider with forty cPanel servers pays for volume alone. Two billing details catch people out:

Ordering is done through the InterServer customer portal at my.interserver.net, where you also find the SMTP username (mbXXXXX style, with no domain part), the password, the logs and the block management tools.

The hard ceiling is 6,000 messages per hour per sender address. Excess messages are discarded rather than queued, so a newsletter script that pushes 10,000 messages from one address in a burst loses 4,000 of them silently at the relay. Throttle at the source: cPanel’s per-domain hourly limits, Postfix’s default_destination_rate_delay, or the sending application’s own batch settings. We set the server-side cap below 6,000 so that the relay limit is never the one that fires.

New accounts are treated more strictly during a warm-up period. Expect a lower effective threshold and more aggressive scoring for the first days of sending. Migrating a busy server onto a brand-new account on a Friday evening is a bad idea; move a low-volume server first.

Logs are retained for 60 days and record the sender, recipient, subject and spam score. Message bodies are removed once delivery completes, so MailBaby cannot recover the content of a message for you later.

STARTTLS is required on the inbound connection from your server. Plain-text authentication on port 25 without TLS is refused, which is why every configuration example on this site sets hosts_require_tls or smtp_tls_security_level to at least opportunistic TLS.

The acceptable use policy excludes several categories regardless of how well the mail is formatted: cold or unsolicited outreach, gambling, cryptocurrency promotion, pharmaceutical sales and debt collection. These are not scored on merit; a customer in one of these verticals will be blocked and can get your account suspended. Screen new signups against this list before you route their mail through the relay, and route legitimate high-risk senders through a dedicated IP elsewhere.

A typical deployment keeps the panel’s mail stack unchanged and adds a smarthost route. On cPanel that is an Exim authenticator, a manualroute router and an SMTP transport with hosts_require_auth, described in our cPanel/WHM setup guide. DirectAdmin uses the exim.*.pre.conf and exim.*.post.conf override files, covered in the DirectAdmin guide. Postfix servers need a relayhost plus SASL credentials, covered in the Postfix guide.

DNS needs one change per customer domain: either add include:spf-c.mailbaby.net to the SPF record or publish a _mailbaby. verification TXT record. The two methods are compared in the SPF and verification guide. DKIM signatures generated on your server pass through untouched, which is the arrangement you want; without them MailBaby signs with its own domain and recipients see “via mailbaby.net”.

Send a test from a mailbox on the server to an external address and read the headers of the received message. The Received: chain should show your server handing off to relay.mailbaby.net, and Authentication-Results should report spf=pass, dkim=pass for your domain and, if you publish a policy, dmarc=pass. In the InterServer portal, confirm the message appears in the log with a low score. Then watch the log for the first 24 hours: a sudden run of rSPAM rejections from one address is the earliest sign of a compromised customer account, and the relay will show it before your local Exim log does.

Am I Bringing My Partner to Too Many Things?

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Should you really have a permanent plus-one?

Ah, the holiday season: A time of good food, holiday decorations, friends and family, and—if you’re in a relationship—adding a plus-one to all of your holiday party invitations.

This year is the first time in a long time that I have a serious partner to bring along to “friendsgivings,” white elephant parties, and New Year’s Eve festivities. And while I’m so excited to bring the person I love around the other people I love (and be able to take cute holiday pictures), I’m a little weary of being that person that has to have their significant other by their side at all times.

Of course, the holiday season is unique: the idea is to bring everyone together and to get to have new experiences with my partner. But by adding my boyfriend as a plus-one to all these events, am I setting a precedent that will translate into the rest of the new year?

“Just because you're in a relationship doesn't mean you're automatically a package deal everywhere you go,” professional matchmaker and dating expert Jill Dunn tells Cosmopolitan.

So how do I find the right balance between including my partner in experiences I want us to have with each other... and maintaining my own independence when it comes to spending time with my friends?

I need to preface this by saying I love hanging out with my boyfriend—I wouldn’t be dating him if I didn’t. That being said, I also love hanging out with my friends. And while my partner gets along with all of my friends, I don’t think that means he automatically gets an invite whenever there’s an event or gathering.

Dunn agrees and says that it’s important to read the room—it really just depends on the situation.

“If your girlfriend invites you out with a few friends, that doesn't necessarily mean your boyfriend should be tagging along,” she says. “But if it's a big birthday celebration or a couples' dinner, that's a different story.”

And while you may fall into the trap of being afraid of being too intertwined with your partner, it’s okay to want to include them in things. “There's a difference between having your own life and keeping someone separate from your life,” Dunn says.

She explains that if you’ve been with your partner for a year, for example, and they have never met any close friends, hasn’t been invited to family celebration, and is consistently left out of important events, you may be sending the signal that they’re not actually a part of your life. And a partner should be part of your life.

When you’re in a relationship, you essentially are gradually becoming part of each other’s lives. And even though you want to have your own separate friends and relationships (which is completely healthy, by the way!) it shouldn’t feel like they’re part of totally different universes.

“You can have amazing chemistry, incredible dates, and a wonderful time together, but if you're not actually integrating into each other's worlds, what are you building toward?” Dunn asks. “You want to feel like you're part of someone's life, not just part of their weekend plans.”

Weddings, holiday parties, or situations where other people’s partners will be present are the perfect time to bring your partner along (if you’ve been given a plus-one, of course).

Now, integrating your partner into your life in a real way doesn’t mean you two have to be attached at the hip no matter the circumstances.

Listen, I get it. Love is so much fun. And when you’re really into someone, you want to spend as much time as possible with them. But that relationship shouldn’t overtake every other aspect of your life.

“I don't think it's healthy for your entire world to revolve around one person,” Dunn says.

If you’re seeing some friends after some time apart, going to an event where no other S.O. is present, or just need a GNO, it is totally fine (and preferable) to let your partner know that you’re going to just be with your friends. In fact, your partner should be hanging out with their friends too! This is especially important to maintain the friendships you already have.

“The dynamic changes when someone's significant other is always there,” Dunn says. “Maybe your girlfriend wants to talk about something personal. Maybe the group wants to reminisce or just have a girls' night. It's not that they don't like your partner. They just want time with you!”

Plus, she explains that it’s not a good habit to expect your romantic partner to fulfill every single need we have. The reason we even have other friendships and relationships is because we need to be able to water different parts of ourselves. You can’t exactly do that if you never leave your house without a plus-one.

And more than anything, it actually benefits your relationship to have a life outside of each other. You know how they say, “distance makes your heart grow fonder”? Well, they’re not wrong. By spending some time apart and living your own life, you become a more interesting, independent person.

“You don't have to share every single experience,” Dunn says. “Sometimes it's nice to go out, have your own conversations, enjoy your friends, and come home with something new to talk about.”

So while there are certain holiday parties I’m excited to bring my boyfriend to, I’m also excited to continue certain seasonal traditions with just my friends. As for the rest of the year, I look forward to learning the balance that works just for us.

Should We All Be Seeing a Concierge Doctor?

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Paying an out-of-pocket fee could deliver faster, more accessible medical care. What’s the catch?

Women's Health may earn commission from the links on this page, but we only feature products we believe in. Why Trust Us?

For as long as I can remember, going to my primary care doctor has been simple: I’d schedule an appointment a few weeks (or months) in advance, commute across town for an in-person appointment, and hope that my insurance covered whatever labs were ordered. But lately, many people have been opting for a membership-based system where they pay a monthly or annual fee for on-demand care. This concept is called concierge medicine.

Over the past few years, the number of concierge medicine services like Tia and One Medical has skyrocketed. Between 2018 and 2023, the number of direct primary care and concierge practice sites grew by roughly 83 percent, per a 2025 study in Health Affairs. (But there are also concierge practices specific to gynecology, longevity medicine, and even emergency medicine.)

“Over the course of my career, I've seen a lot of frustrations that patients have had in terms of access to care, long wait times, lack of continuity of care,” says Jane Zhu, MD, a primary care provider and associate professor of medicine at Oregon Health and Science University, and author of the Health Affairs study. There’s a real need for faster, more on-demand medical care—and concierge medicine is one way that patients are getting that.

Membership prices vary, with some costing as little as $9 a month for on-demand urgent and primary care, and others costing as much as $45,000 a year for top-tier care (at-home doctor visits for urgent care, X-ray imaging from your bed, and even private jets on standby for medical evacuation while you’re traveling). If you don’t live in a major city, many of these programs offer telehealth services all over the country.

So, what’s the catch? Ahead, read up on everything you need to know about concierge medicine, including the pros and cons, and who it’s right for.

The premise is simple: paying a membership fee unlocks extra attention and access to care from your doctor.

At most traditional clinics and hospitals, the goal is for doctors to see as many patients a day as possible. (More visits equals more insurance claims and/or copays, which in turn makes money for the practice.) With concierge medicine, doctors don’t operate within this same construct. Instead, they make money from membership fees, which means they can still turn a profit with a much smaller pool of patients. When you schedule an appointment with a concierge doctor, you’re not competing with hundreds of other patients for a time slot in the same way you would for a traditional doctor.

When Kristen Harvey, MD, MPH, a concierge family medicine physician at Mount Sinai, worked in traditional medicine, she only got about 15 to 20 minutes for each patient visit. Now, she has the freedom to extend her visits to 45 to 60 minutes and form closer relationships with her patients. “I'm able to go into deeper discussions from a more holistic perspective and have a different type of conversation,” she says. Talking more in depth about sleep, nutrition, and lifestyle habits in general is key to helping her patients prevent illnesses in the first place—not just treat them when they come up.

Additionally, getting lab work done through concierge medicine may be a smoother process. While some traditional practices may have you drop by LabCorp to get your blood drawn, for example, many concierge practices will just do it on site, and even go beyond your average complete blood count for in-depth lipid, hormone, and genetic panels. But everything isn't always included in the membership, so check before they draw.

When you need a specialist outside of your concierge practice, they’ll often have health navigators—people whose job it is to schedule your appointments for you, facilitate communication between your providers, coordinate prescriptions, and run point on any insurance claims. You've built a chatbot, and you've learned that chat APIs have no memory: every turn you send the system prompt and the whole conversation again, so long chats get expensive fast. The usual fix is a running summary. Instead of the full history, you send a short summary of what was said so far.

The obvious way to build it is to update the summary after every reply. It's simple and the summary is always fresh. On short chats it's also the most expensive option, because the summary is text the model writes, and written text is billed at a much higher price.

A token is a chunk of text the model reads or writes; APIs bill per million of them. Input tokens are what you send, output tokens are what the model writes back, and output usually costs several times more. A list price is the vendor's official price per million tokens, before any discount. A turn is one user message plus one assistant reply. A summarizer call is a separate API request whose only job is to rewrite the conversation into a shorter text.

I modeled a support chat with round numbers: a 400-token system prompt, 60-token user messages, 250-token replies and a 250-token summary (about 200 words). The summarizer instruction, counted with tiktoken (o200k_base), is 83 tokens. Prices are list prices from the catalog snapshot of the LLM gateway I work on: gpt-5.6 at $2 per million input tokens and $12 per million output.

Total cost of one conversation on gpt-5.6, chat and summarizer on the same model:

The per-reply summary only becomes cheaper than doing nothing at turn 19. Most support chats are over long before that.

The reason is the output price. Each per-reply summarizer call reads 643 tokens and writes 250. Those 250 output tokens cost as much as 1,500 input tokens, so 70% of the call's price is the summary text itself. The whole call costs $0.0043, more than the very first chat turn ($0.0039). On a short chat you pay that every turn to avoid resending a history that was still small.

The batched version has the same per-call problem, but it pays it rarely and only when it buys something. Its first summarizer call costs $0.0081: it reads eight turns (2,480 tokens) and replaces them with 250. From then on every turn sends about 2,230 fewer input tokens, which saves $0.0045 per turn. The call has paid for itself after two more turns: the conversation is 5% more expensive at turn 11, 1% cheaper at turn 12, and 19% cheaper at turn 20. A chat that ends before turn 11 never triggers it and costs exactly what full history costs.

Moving the summary to a cheaper model shifts both curves. With gpt-5.6-mini ($0.20 / $1.20) writing summaries for a gpt-5.6 chat, the per-reply version is still 7% more expensive at four turns but cheaper from turn 6, and the batched one is cheaper from the first summary. The batched version on claude-sonnet-5.5 with claude-haiku-5.5 as the summarizer is also cheaper from its first summary.

A count of tokens works better than a count of turns if your messages vary a lot in length, and the usage field of each response tells you the real input size.

A summary loses detail. The model may drop an order number or a setting the user mentioned on turn 3, and with the batched version that loss happens in one jump, not gradually. I didn't measure answer quality, only price. If your bot must quote exact details from early in the chat, test summaries on real conversations first, or keep key facts in a separate structured field instead.

Prompt caching changes the math. Many vendors bill a repeated prefix at a discount. Full history is a prefix that only grows, so it caches well, while every new summary changes the prefix and breaks the cache. The catalog I used has no cache prices, so none of this is in the table.

The Müller-Lyer illusion looks so simple: two lines, one with arrows pointing inwards from the ends and one with arrows pointing out. One looks longer than the other, right?

A lot of people see it that way, but some people don't. Some people can immediately tell that the two horizontal lines are actually the same length (see below if you don't believe me). But the reason for this difference in perfection continues to be a topic of big debate.

This optical illusion was named after Franz Carl Müller-Lyer, its creator. Many people initially assume that the line with the arrows pointing inwards is longer than the other. But then research published in the 1960s claimed that people in African and Southeast Asian countries weren't so often fooled by the illusion. In that case, there are no lines or angles at all.

Amir and Firestone also turned up flaws in the case studies from the 1960s, and found that later reports had misconstrued the original findings. The evidence that Africans and Asians aren't fooled by the Müller-Lyer illusion appeared to be very scant.

I’ve always loved Italian American food. The result is the Bolognese you know and love, but with a deep, tangy twist. Fish sauce in Italian cooking is not a new idea, actually—Romans have been using salted anchovies and garum, an ancient kind of fish sauce, for centuries. For an extra-special finish, I love garnishing this with baek (white) kimchi for acidity. But it’s optional, and regular kimchi (or no kimchi) works just fine, too.

Start the Bolognese sauce: Heat 2 Tbsp. extra-virgin olive oil in a large Dutch oven or pot over medium-high heat. Stir in 1 medium-yellow onion, finely diced (about 1½ cups), 2 carrots, peeled and finely diced, and 3 stalks celery, finely diced, and cook, stirring occasionally, until the onions have softened and the edges start to brown, about 4 minutes. Stir in 4 cloves garlic, minced, and a pinch of kosher salt and cook for 30 seconds, then stir in 1 cup chopped kimchi and 1 Tbsp. tomato paste and cook until the kimchi pieces start to soften, about 3 minutes. Pour in ½ cup red wine, increase the heat, and simmer, stirring with a wooden spoon to dislodge any bits that have stuck to the bottom, until the wine has nearly disappeared, about 2 minutes.

Add the meat: Stir in 1 lb. ground beef, season with generous pinches of salt (at least ½ tsp.), and cook, breaking up the beef into small pieces with a wooden spoon, until cooked through, 6 to 8 minutes.

Add the tomatoes: Pour one 28-oz. can whole San Marzano tomatoes in a bowl. Using clean hands, squish the tomatoes into small pieces. Pour the tomatoes into the pot and add 1 cup beef broth or water, ½ tsp. dried Italian seasoning or dried oregano, a pinch of salt, and a few grinds of freshly ground black pepper. Bring to a simmer and lower the heat to medium-low. Gently cook, stirring occasionally, until the sauce is just thick enough to stand up on a spoon, about 20 minutes. Stir in 2 Tbsp. fish sauce and taste, then add more salt or fish sauce if needed.

Cook the noodles and serve: In a pot of boiling salted water, cook 1 lb. spaghetti according to package instructions, stirring occasionally to prevent sticking, until al dente. Drain the noodles in a colander, then give the colander a shake to get rid of extra water. Divide the noodles among serving plates and spoon the Bolognese on top. Garnish with 1 cup chopped kimchi (white or regular; if using) and 2 Tbsp. chopped flat-leaf parsley and serve.

Do Ahead: Leftover Bolognese sauce (without the noodles) keeps in an airtight container in the refrigerator for up to 5 days, or in the freezer for up to 2 months.

Archaeologists in Cerveteri, Italy, uncovered Etruscan tombs with stone thrones, red-painted walls and a decorated jar overlooked by looters.

Emergency work at the Monte Abatone necropolis in Cerveteri, the Etruscan city of Caere, has turned up stone thrones, walls painted red, and a nearly intact decorated jar.

The work was carried out by Italy’s heritage authority for the province of Viterbo and southern Etruria, the Soprintendenza Archeologia, Belle Arti e Paesaggio. Some of the tombs had collapsed or become unstable.

Clandestine diggers had entered these tombs before the archaeologists arrived. They left the jar behind.

The collapses and instability meant the tombs had to be investigated, documented and secured. The team cleared the entrances and, as a precaution, removed the fill that had built up inside.

The chambers were dug into tuff, a soft volcanic rock. Excavators have also found places on the hill where blocks of tuff were quarried in several stages.

During a 2019 excavation, a team including the University of Bonn documented a Monte Abatone tomb whose ceiling had partly fallen in along a crack.

The researchers linked such cracks to movements of the tuff layer over a less stable layer of clay. The Soprintendenza has not said what caused the latest collapses, or when they occurred.

One of the chambers still keeps much of its original vaulted ceiling.

In another Etruscan chamber tomb, the funerary furniture was carved directly out of the tuff, including thrones.

A third chamber has extensive red painted decoration on its walls.

Similar furnishings are known from earlier work at Monte Abatone. In 2019, years before the emergency repairs, excavators recorded kline-type death beds, shaped like couches, and narrower benches lining the walls of another chamber.

Cerveteri’s larger Banditaccia necropolis and the Monterozzi necropolis at Tarquinia together form a UNESCO World Heritage property. At Banditaccia, thousands of tombs are laid out in a city-like plan, with streets and small squares.

The most complete find is an olla stamnoide, a jar shaped like a stamnos. It is almost intact. Its surface carries incised figures of animals, with some details picked out in red paint.

The jar will be cleaned and restored, then put on public display. No date or venue for the display has been announced.

The earlier clandestine digging compromised the original context of the burials. Some materials survived it and were recovered.

Looters have missed objects at Monte Abatone before. In the 2019 excavation, Tomb 642 showed signs of their digging, yet it still held a large pithos, a bucchero drinking set, Ionian bowls and Corinthian ointment jars.

Monte Abatone lies immediately south of the ancient city. It flourished in the seventh and sixth centuries BCE, according to a 2022 report on Monte Abatone.

In the 1950s and 1960s, surveys and excavations explored 641 tombs there. That work was carried out on behalf of the heritage authority in response to heavy looting.

Burial types at the site include simple trench graves, chamber tombs and tombs under burial mounds, among them the Campana Tumulus.

That great mound dates to the middle of the Orientalizing period and had not been surveyed since the first half of the 19th century.

Older finds show that burials there continued from the late eighth century BCE into the Hellenistic period and the early Roman Empire.

Researchers also examined the area west and north of the Campana Tumulus. It held tombs of the sixth to third centuries BCE, excavated in the past and partly backfilled, and funerary terraces facing the city.

A research project on Monte Abatone began in the early 1990s. After a new project was set up in 2017, a group of Italian and German universities ran excavation campaigns at the site from 2018 to 2021.

Ground-penetrating radar was used across an area of about 7.4 acres (3 hectares) east of the Campana Tumulus. An earlier geophysical test had revealed a densely packed cluster of tombs.

Below are the four high-impact code patterns introduced in Compose 1.13 that resolve the most frequent findings.

Developers frequently wrap scroll offsets or animation progress checks inside derivedStateOf to prevent a composable from recomposing on every pixel scrolled. However, derivedStateOf allocates and maintains an internal cache object. When the calculation itself is trivial, such as comparing a scroll index against a threshold, maintaining that cache costs more overhead than the boolean comparison itself.

Compose 1.13 introduces computedStateOf, which skips result caching while still invalidating the reading composition only when the computed output changes:

kotlin import androidx.compose.foundation.lazy.LazyListState import androidx.compose.runtime.Composable import androidx.compose.runtime.computedStateOf import androidx.compose.runtime.getValue import androidx.compose.runtime.remember

kotlin @Composable fun FeedScreenWithScrollTopButton(listState: LazyListState) { // computedStateOf recalculates without caching overhead and only invalidates // FeedScreenWithScrollTopButton when the boolean result flips between true and false. val showScrollToTop by remember { computedStateOf { listState.firstVisibleItemIndex > 4 } }

jsx if (showScrollToTop) { ScrollToTopFab() } }

Reserve derivedStateOf for genuinely expensive transformations (such as sorting or filtering a large in-memory list), and default to computedStateOf for threshold checks, boolean conditions, and fast arithmetic over high-frequency states.

With standard AnimatedVisibility, Compose keeps composing the outgoing content throughout the entire exit animation. When a user dismisses a detail sheet by setting selectedItem = null, the exiting composable suddenly sees null mid-animation, forcing you either to cache a stale copy of the state manually or watch the card vanish into a blank frame before the fade-out finishes.

Compose 1.13 adds CapturedAnimatedVisibility, which snapshots the final rendered frame the moment visible turns false and animates that snapshot out while releasing the underlying state immediately:

kotlin import androidx.compose.animation.CapturedAnimatedVisibility import androidx.compose.animation.core.tween import androidx.compose.animation.expandVertically import androidx.compose.animation.fadeIn import androidx.compose.animation.fadeOut import androidx.compose.animation.shrinkVertically import androidx.compose.runtime.Composable

kotlin @Composable fun OrderDetailsSheet(activeOrder: OrderSummary?) { // When activeOrder becomes null, CapturedAnimatedVisibility immediately unmounts // OrderCard and animates a frozen snapshot of its last frame, running 7-8% faster on exit. CapturedAnimatedVisibility( visible = activeOrder != null, enter = fadeIn() + expandVertically(), exit = fadeOut(tween(400)) + shrinkVertically(tween(400)), ) { if (activeOrder != null) { OrderCard(order = activeOrder) } } }

According to Google's Compose 1.13 release benchmarks, freeing the outgoing composition immediately reduces exit frame times by 7 to 8 percent compared to AnimatedVisibility. Keep AnimatedVisibility only when the exiting UI must stay interactive during the transition or when children rely on Modifier.animateEnterExit().

In Compose 1.13, LazyLayoutCacheWindow graduates to stable across LazyColumn, LazyRow, LazyVerticalGrid, and staggered grids, moving cache window configuration directly onto the layout composable rather than burying it inside rememberLazyListState. It replaces legacy single-item prefetching with a viewport-relative ahead-and-behind window. At the same time, FlexBox and Grid graduate to stable while FlowRow, FlowColumn, ConstraintLayout, and MotionLayout in Compose are deprecated in favor of FlexBox:

If You Run Another Round of Promotion and It Still Stalls, Where Do You Go From There?

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Four weeks of promotion. Six short videos, zero views. UTM-tagged social links, zero recorded sign-ups. Yet in week four alone, the email list grew by 24 people.

You've run a campaign, pulled in some traffic, and gotten a few replies, but revenue hasn't budged. Many teams' first instinct is to publish more content or buy more traffic—figuring a customer will turn up eventually.

On the surface, you're deciding whether to run another push. In reality, you're stuck between two very different questions:

1.Did the last round actually reach people who need this product and help them get it?

2.If they did get it, why didn't they take the next step?

Until you separate those two questions, your next move is likely to miss. If you haven't answered the first, pulling the plug on promotion might be too early. If you already have clues on the second, spending more to widen reach can easily send money to the wrong place. For the founder in the original post, the visible cost was hours of work. For a small team about to pay for ads on the next round, it's straight cash out the door.

As long as attribution stays broken, though, another round will likely hand them another report they can't really read.

If your numbers look that murky, hold off on scaling up time or ad spend for a moment. Before buying another wave of reach, test "did it land" and "does it compel" separately. The most direct way is to put your last campaign in front of a few people who have this exact problem right now:

● If they get it and are willing to test it on real work, the core message holds up. Next, it's worth checking whether your usual channels can bring in more people like them.

● If several people get it and nobody wants to try, that's already enough reason to pause paid reach—put the time you would have spent buying impressions toward learning how they handle the problem today, and why switching feels worth it or not.

A handful of conversations won't solve distribution on their own, but they help you pick which thread to pull first.

Sometimes, though, the blocker is already sitting in plain sight—no extra campaign needed to guess.

On Hacker News, a founder building a website tool for small businesses shared that after three months of promotion and plenty of positive feedback, only one customer had paid—and later canceled. A commenter who looked at the site said that what looked like a requirement to connect Facebook put them off right away, since they didn't want to link an account and couldn't tell what data would be read.

That's a concrete drop-off signal. If people who actually want a website keep bailing at that step, buying more traffic before clearing up permission concerns just pushes more visitors into the same dead end. Fixing that friction has far stronger backing than buying another round of reach.

Stagnation isn't a cue to move on by default; it's a business signal that needs an explanation. When promotion doesn't click, you shouldn't keep pouring money in out of sunk cost, nor write off the product over a flat stretch—you can pause the ad spend and let the next piece of evidence decide where the product goes. Separating "is this campaign worth funding" from "is this business worth running" is how you read a real signal instead of gambling on luck.

So before you put down another check or sink another few weeks of hours into promotion, ask yourself one question:

"Even if this next round brings zero orders, what specific thing will I be able to see—and how will that change where I put my next dollar or hour?"

If the last round already paid off on its own terms, running it again as-is makes sense. If the return still isn't there and your current numbers can't explain why, pausing blind spend and putting your energy on the clearest bottleneck right now beats charging ahead in the dark.

Netflix Pulled Off the Impossible With This Unadaptable 3-Book Sci-Fi Series

There’s a classical sentiment that “the book is always better than the adaptation,” but that isn’t always the case. Beyond the very obvious examples of all-time classics based on mediocre books, the transition to a new medium forces certain changes to tell the story in the most palatable way; books with a strong mythology and attention to detail can be more challenging to bring to life on screen, given that audiences don’t want to sit around having information painstakingly explained to them. 3 Body Problem is a remarkable adaptation because it retains the essence of the source material, yet grafts upon a multicultural story with a broader ensemble. Although science fiction has become more popular on prestige television now than ever before, the “hard sci-fi” approach of 3 Body Problem retains its intellectualism without sacrificing its entertainment value.

There has been more than enough criticism leveled at David Benioff and D.B. Weiss for their handling of the Game of Thrones finale, but the suggestion that their efforts adapting the popular novel series did not require any imagination is ridiculous. There are many instances in which great book series are never fully adapted to the screen because of fundamental mistakes within the filmmaking process, and 3 Body Problem shows Benioff and Weiss at their best because of how viscerally it communicates the stakes of Liu Cixin’s Remembrance of Earth's Past books. That the show so delicately blends an exciting ensemble of interesting characters with highly specific mathematical principles is a towering accomplishment, especially since the didacticism of 3 Body Problem is not an impediment for those who haven’t read the books.

The first novel in Liu’s trilogy is primarily told from the perspective of the Chinese nanotechnologist Wang, but 3 Body Problem makes a brilliant decision to divide the protagonist into five main characters, who are referred to as “The Oxford Five.” Wang was an engaging hero in the books because the novel could delve into his internal monologue, but in a show with this degree of complexity, the series required that characters could bounce off one another and express their ideas. Expanding the ensemble wasn’t just a way of populating the cast, ensuring that there were human faces explaining the hard science, but an opportunity to include a diverse set of actors whose ties to the impending crisis were emotional. Alex Sharp gives a particularly moving performance as Dr. Will Downing, a physics teacher dealing with a terminal diagnosis, and it helps to contextualize the theme of eventuality that is so critical to the story.

Benioff and Weiss had already proven that they can toggle between multiple locations and sets of characters, leading to instances where main cast members aren’t in every episode, but 3 Body Problem is successful at balancing different timelines. Even though 3 Body Problem is an English-language show, it does not forget how critical the Chinese Cultural Revolution was as an inciting incident in the novel, and recreates the pivotal events of the Communist government’s 1966 purge of intellectuals vividly on screen. The non-linear narrative structure is also helpful in masking some of the twists, specifically when it comes to the character of Mike Evans. But although the turn that Evans takes is one of the most shocking within all three novels, the series makes it more understandable through the combined efforts of Ben Schnetzer and Jonathan Pryce to create a consistent character arc told across several decades of time.

Waiting for more ‘Silo’? Netflix’s sci-fi series turns a scientific mystery into a fight for humanity’s survival.

Knowing the ending ahead of time was something that Benioff and Weitz obviously weren’t gifted with on Game of Thrones, but the mapping out of a three-season arc allows for 3 Body Problem to strategize its plot points in a way that will complement its future. The Wallfacer Project, which is an important aspect of the second novel The Dark Forest, is introduced at the end of Season 1, and it doesn’t feel like a burden on the story because of the great performance by Jovan Adepo as Dr. Saul Durand, a member of the “Oxford Five” who is brought into the project. Benioff and Weiss were also able to make compelling creative decisions when it came to the design of the 3 Body video game itself, as they were able to adjust to all the advancements within artificial reality technology that had developed within the real world in the time since Liu’s first novel was published.

No adaptation will perfectly fit within what the readers imagined when reading the novel, but 3 Body Problem makes bold decisions in committing to a specific aesthetic that builds techno-paranoia, existential moral questions, and moments of sheer horror. The mathematical concepts are explained in a way that makes sense to those with varying degrees of expertise about the subject, but Benioff and Weiss also handle Liu’s breakthroughs with the appropriate gravity. There is a level of craftsmanship within 3 Body Problem that makes it even more rewarding upon multiple viewings, but its success as a traditionally entertaining high-concept adventure ensures that it will offer some nice surprises for those expecting to see just another feat of escapism.

As one of the biggest entertainment platforms in the world, Netflix has countless original series across practically every genre imaginable. From romance, sci-fi, horror, crime, the streaming giant has built a catalogue that has something for just about everyone. Of course, not every Netflix original is worth the binge, but every once in a while, the platform delivers a show that reminds us just how incredible episodic storytelling can be.

Here, we take a look at the best of the best in Netflix' vast library of shows. These series demonstrate the incredible range of storytelling and showcase great talents in front of and behind the cameras. Some will keep you glued to the screen with unexpected twists, while others will leave you thinking about their characters and themes for days. Based on John Steinbeck's novel, East of Eden follows the Trask family across multiple generations, starting with brothers Adam (Christopher Abbott) and Charles (Mike Faist) whose fortunes differ when their dad favors one over the other. East of Eden is relatively new compared to the other series on this list but it is obvious that this show is one of the best things Netflix has put out in years. the search for a home without a fixed address, from prefabricated houses to floating dwellings

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

from postwar demountable houses to floating neighborhoods, architects have repeatedly questioned the assumption that buildings must remain tied to one site. the answers reveal different ideas about permanence, ownership, and the future of domestic space.

For decades, architects have imagined homes that can be packed up, transported, and assembled somewhere new, designing alternatives to the idea that housing must be tied to a permanent address. From prefabricated dwellings designed for mass production to experimental houses conceived for remote landscapes, these projects explore how architecture might exist beyond the limits of a fixed site.

One early expression of this ambition came from American architect and inventor Buckminster Fuller. In 1928, he developed the concept for the Dymaxion House, a prefabricated dwelling conceived for mass production and delivery as a single unit by aircraft. Its lightweight aluminum structure reflected Fuller’s interest in applying industrial manufacturing to housing. Although the project never developed into a commercially viable housing system, it proposed a home manufactured away from its eventual site and transported there for assembly.

The idea took a different form in the late 1960s with Finnish architect Matti Suuronen’s Futuro House. Designed as a portable ski cabin, the fiberglass dwelling could be dismantled and transported to remote locations, including by helicopter. Its rounded silhouette and synthetic materials reflected the period’s fascination with space exploration and technological progress.

Mobility, however, does not necessarily mean moving an entire building. It can also involve designing structures that can be taken apart, reassembled, extended, or adapted as needs change. This distinction became particularly important in the work of French architect and designer Jean Prouvé, who explored industrialized construction as a way to make buildings faster and easier to assemble. In the final years of the Second World War, Prouvé developed demountable houses for people whose homes had been destroyed in eastern France. His 8×8 house used a structural system based on a central steel portal frame, with components designed for assembly and disassembly. The project responded to an urgent need for housing, but it also demonstrated how prefabrication could make construction more flexible.

By the 1960s, the question of impermanence had expanded from individual dwellings to the scale of the city. British architectural group Archigram imagined urban environments in which buildings and infrastructure could be continually replaced rather than treated as finished, unchanging compositions. Peter Cook’s 1964 Plug-In City proposed a large structural framework into which housing, offices, shops, and other units could be inserted or removed according to changing needs.

Mobile and temporary architecture is not always driven by a desire for a different lifestyle. In many cases, it responds to an immediate need for shelter. Following the 1994 Rwandan genocide, Japanese architect Shigeru Ban began developing paper-tube structures for refugee accommodation, experimenting with inexpensive, readily available materials that could be used to build temporary homes.

The shelters were designed to be assembled with simple construction methods, making them suitable for situations where building resources were limited. Ban’s work brought architectural thinking into a humanitarian context, showing that even temporary structures require careful consideration of how people live, rather than simply where they sleep.

Another reason to reconsider fixed construction is the instability of the environments in which buildings are placed. Rising water levels, flooding, and pressure on urban land have encouraged architects and communities to explore ways of living on or alongside water. Floating architecture changes the relationship between a structure and its site, allowing the building to respond to water. In Amsterdam, Schoonschip offers an example at the scale of a neighborhood. Developed by its residents with urban planning support from architecture studio Space&Matter, the floating community comprises 30 water plots and 46 homes. Shared infrastructure connects the dwellings, while solar panels, batteries, and a smart grid allow residents to generate and exchange electricity.

Other projects approach the same question at a smaller scale. NOA’s DeKruif House, for example, is a prefabricated timber residence conceived for a family that does not want to commit to one location. Designed to adapt to different waterfront settings, it considers how a house might respond to changing views, orientation, and surroundings. David Rule’s TinyTanic takes mobility onto the water itself, compressing basic domestic functions into a miniature houseboat designed for short journeys. While their scale and ambitions differ, both projects treat the relationship between home and location as something that can be reconsidered.

It is easy to see the appeal of a house that can move. It opens up the possibility of taking a home somewhere new, adapting it to a different setting, or using it for a limited period of time. But moving a building does not automatically make it cheaper, greener, or easier to live in. It still needs materials, energy, water, electricity, and waste systems. A house may be able to travel, but it cannot escape the practical needs of everyday life. These projects also raise a question about why homes are expected to stay in one place in the first place.

From prefabricated houses and temporary shelters to floating neighborhoods, architects have explored different ways of building for changing needs and surroundings. Not every structure needs to be mobile, but more buildings could be designed to adapt, come apart, or be used again rather than remain fixed forever. The Futuro House is a good example of how this idea can come full circle. Designed to be transported and assembled in different locations, it is now a rare piece of architecture that people want to preserve. A house once imagined for life on the move has become something that must stay put. Perhaps the question is not whether we will all live in houses that travel, but whether our homes can change along with the way we live and the places we inhabit.

Instead of regulators chasing harmful content after it spreads, the law makes platforms find and reduce the risks they can see coming before people get hurt. Low-risk services fall outside the law, and Wells gave a café or a bookkeeper’s website as examples of sites unlikely to harm their users.

The bill passed the lower house in October with 23 amendments, but needed Greens votes in the Senate after the opposition Coalition came out against it.

The Greens’ votes came at a price: individuals and groups of Australians will be able to sue platforms that breach their duty of care. A separate external complaints body will give people a faster route alongside the courts.

The penalties also grew in the bargain, with companies that breach the duty now facing fines of A$109mn (about €62mn) or up to 5% of global revenue.

Wells said that figure sits between Canada’s 3% and the EU’s 6%. She noted that European courts tend to award only part of the maximum, depending on the case.

For everyday users, the most visible change is a switch to turn off algorithmic feeds, which social media platforms must offer and keep easy to find.

The 10 Most Overrated Rock Songs of All Time, Ranked

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

There will never be a better genre of music than rock and roll. Rock is the basics, with a band of guitar players and a drummer singing songs they wrote themselves, free of anything synthetic. Rock is real, a genre free of restraints. The listener feels it all, from the energy of the beat, to the emotion behind the lyrics. Today, even as newer rock songs lose their hold on mainstream popularity, classics from the past continue to reign. Still, some rock classics get more credit than they deserve. While none of them are bad by any means, being overplayed, or simply being unworthy of their all-time greatness status, has made these 10 rock songs overrated.

Putting The Beatles on such a list is just wishing to be hated. Let's get one thing clear right up front. The Beatles are the best band of all-time. But this status has also made it so that every song they ever released is put on a pedestal, and not all of them deserve it. Among them is "Come Together." The first song from their iconic Abbey Road album, "Come Together" is written and sung by John Lennon. While it has a cool opening riff and hook, the song repeats itself with lyrics which make absolutely no sense. Gone is any emotion, replaced instead with lines like "He wear no shoeshine, he got toe-jam football." What?

This song refuses to die. Being overplayed over the decades has caused listeners to cringe any time Journey's "Don't Stop Believin'" comes on. The piano intro is pretty standard, and there's nothing all that impressive with basic lyrics like, "Just a small-town girl livin' in a lonely world." The beat feels like a throwaway song without much effort put into it, and the theme is so spot-on that it's more cheesy than anything else. Throw in how it now will forever make us immediately think about the unsatisfactory ending to The Sopranos, and this song is one that can please stop.

Cheap Trick's "I Want You to Want Me" has two versions. The studio version is completely lifeless and forgettable. The entire band sounds bored, and Robin Zander is twisting his voice in ways that don't work. The song only became a classic because of the live version. There, Cheap Trick comes to life, a band set free. Still, there's only so much you can do with such a dull original song. Zander's voice is much better here, and we won't deny how catchy it is, but a live version of a song no one wanted isn't worth the hype.

The Police are one of the greatest bands of the 1980s, and it can't be understated what Sting means to rock history. The band's final album, Synchronicity, is one of the best of the decade, and it's carried by the second single, "Every Breath You Take." The song lacks any real energy that The Police had on other big hits. What really makes it annoying is how the song was treated by the public. Despite being a song about a man's obsession with the woman who got away, so many listeners refuse to look at the deeper meaning, instead treating it like a love song worth getting married to. Come on, people!

Folk rocker Bob Dylan was the voice of America and the forgotten man and woman in the 1960s. What he means to music deserves to be celebrated. This doesn't mean that every song of his was some genius recording worthy of his reputation. "Lay, Lady, Lay" is an unusual one. Gone are the powerful political protest songs Dylan was known for. In its place is lyrical simplicity about either true love or an affair. And missing from it all is Dylan's well-known nasaly whine.

Many lists will recognize "Hotel California" by the Eagles as one of the greatest rock songs of all-time. And that's why it makes this list. This is an American classic, but it's also hyped beyond belief and has been overplayed to death. At six plus minutes, it's much too long. The lyrics, about the excess of fame and living it up in southern California, are a little too vague. As for the music itself, outside of the usual guitar solos, "Hotel California" seems stuck in first gear. It doesn't deserve the hate it gets, but this is not one of the best songs ever made.

Thanks to albums like The Joshua Tree and Achtung Baby, the Irish band U2 were one of the biggest and best bands of the late 1980s and early 1990s. Then they got experimental, and in the late 90s, as rock changed, U2 was left behind. The 2000 album All That You Can't Leave Behind was seen as a comeback return to form. It's carried by the first single, "Beautiful Day," a song that was everywhere to begin the 21st century. Although it's a nice song, it can't live up to their classics. Rather than being a challenging song, "Beautiful Day" is overly cute, the lyrics a far cry from what the band once was.

Green Day are one of the better modern bands still out there and going strong, but oh man, if you grew up in the late 90s like this writer, did it get old hearing "Good Riddance (Time of Your Life)." The punk pop power the group is known for is stripped away in favor of an overly sappy acoustic number. At just two-and-a-half minutes, the song doesn't overstay its welcome, but the millions who heard it weren't paying attention. Billie Joe Armstrong is saying goodbye to an ex, trying to be heartfelt while also saying good riddance. Yet, for years after, it was part of every damn high school and college graduation in the country.

"In-A-Gadda-Da-Vida" by Iron Butterfly is one of the most well-known rock one hit wonders. It's also a song that doesn't hit as hard the more you listen to it. Just the title itself, a mispronunciation of "In the garden of Eden" is annoying. That opening hook, with an organ leading into a sick guitar riff and drum beat, is admittedly badass, and lead singer Doug Ingle sounds a bit like Jim Morrison. But once you get past the first minute, this one starts to fall apart. It's the same riff and basic structure repeated over and over. If you have the patience, check out the whopping 17-minute album version.

In the late 1990s, as rock was trying to figure out what came next in the post-grunge era, along came Semisonic and their song "Closing Time." It's a catchy song from a decent band, but it's basic verse-chorus-verse style isn't anything original. If it stood on its own as a minor hit, this one might not be on the list. It's how it was received that makes it so overrated. Not only was it played on every Top 40 and rock radio channel non-stop, but good luck being at a bar at closing time in 1998 and the years after. Drunken sing-a-longs turned a fun little song into something rather annoying.

FDA clears DeepHealth’s AI that predicts breast cancer risk 5 years ahead

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Mammo Risk was trained on 220,000 studies and tested on more than 65,000 US screening mammograms. It flags women at higher future risk rather than looking for a tumour.

The US Food and Drug Administration has cleared DeepHealth’s Mammo Risk. The AI software estimates a woman’s chance of developing breast cancer within three, four or five years. It works from a routine 3D mammogram, with no extra scan. The FDA cleared it on 23 November, the RadNet subsidiary said in a press release on Tuesday.

DeepHealth calls it the first FDA-cleared tool that predicts breast cancer risk from 3D mammogram images. It also covers combined 3D and 2D workflows. It is now on sale to healthcare providers across the US and works with systems from several vendors. 3D mammography is available at more than 90% of US mammography facilities, the company said, citing FDA figures.

Mammo Risk does not look for an existing tumour. It is meant to flag women whose scans show no signs of cancer. Some of them may still face a higher risk within five years. Providers can then adjust their screening, for example by offering extra imaging. DeepHealth sells a separate detection product, Mammo Dx.

American Cancer Society figures cited in the release put this year’s US cases of invasive breast cancer at about 321,910. Traditional risk models rely on long patient questionnaires and clinical information. Mammo Risk instead uses AI trained on 220,000 studies to read the risk from the mammogram itself.

“By launching the world’s first and only image-based risk assessment software for 3D mammography, we are moving beyond just detecting cancer towards enabling preventive pathways,” said Niccolò Stefani, business and product leader for clinical AI at DeepHealth.

An independent multicentre study covered more than 65,000 screening mammograms at 10 US clinical sites. Mammo Risk showed “strong predictive performance” in it, DeepHealth said. Results were consistent across subgroups, including Black women and women with dense breasts. Both groups have historically had poorer outcomes. Traditional models have also been less accurate for them, the company said. The release cites the study to the FDA clearance file, K262709, and gives no accuracy figures.

The software builds on an earlier DeepHealth model that predicts risk up to two years ahead. That model is CE-marked and on sale in Europe. Karolinska Institutet researchers led a 2022 study of it in Science Translational Medicine. It drew on more than 154,000 US women screened with 3D mammography. For the 12% of women at highest risk, the model showed about 2.4 times the sensitivity of the traditional Tyrer-Cuzick model.

“Everyone’s biology and breast cancer risk are different, yet too often we take a one-size-fits-all approach to screening and care. Better approaches have been long overdue,” said Victoria Smart, senior vice president of mission at the breast cancer charity Susan G. Komen.

The American Medical Association has set up a Category III billing code for image-based breast cancer risk tools. It takes effect on 1 January 2027. The code tracks how often such tools are used, DeepHealth said. It also gathers evidence that may inform future payment decisions.

Mammo Risk will join RadNet’s own screening programmes in the coming months. DeepHealth will show it at the RSNA radiology meeting in Chicago, from 29 November to 2 December. A talk on 2 December will present a study of its image-only risk model, according to an earlier DeepHealth release.
