10 Classic Rock Albums To Listen to Before Kicking the Bucket

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

​​​​​​Joining the choir invisible. Shuffling off this mortal coil. Ceasing to be. Biting the dust. Wearing wooden pajamas. Vacating the earthly meat prison. No matter how it's said, it all means the same thing: death. Death is the great equalizer, the one thing that is inevitable regardless of one's race, religion, income or social status. Every day brings us closer to hopping the twig.

But hey, you're still on this side of the grass, right? Unless you're a well-read zombie, but that seems unlikely (but not impossible... discuss). So you have a choice to make: sit idly by, paralyzed by the thought you may have had your last Slurpee; or make the most of the time you have left. And if — hopefully — you've selected the latter, before kicking the bucket you've got things you want — nay, need — to do, see, and hear. Thankfully, your friends here at Collider have compiled a list that speaks to the latter: 10 rock albums you need to listen to before going belly up, ones that are not only awesome but offer guidance as you prepare for the inevitable. We're dying, literally, to share those with you now.

There comes a point in everyone's life where it's time to drop the charades and get real. That means raw, emotional honesty with others and yourself, and there is no better album that wears emotional honesty on every track like Fleetwood Mac's 1977 classic Rumours. The story behind the album is legendary at this point, recorded as the interpersonal drama among the group was akin to a soap opera, with separations, outright spite and heartbreak, making the atmosphere in the studio, shall we say, tense. A lesser band would have buried that under upbeat pop/rock songs, recording in separate sessions if needed. Not Fleetwood Mac: they owned that energy and put it all on vinyl, creating a timeless masterpiece that is relatable, real, and, in the case of "Don't Stop," even hopeful.

Getting real with ourselves potentially opens up a proverbial Pandora's Box of questions about life, death, time, money, or even one's mental health. While it may not provide any answers to these questions, Pink Floyd, with their iconic album Dark Side of the Moon, sure as all hell wax philosophical about those same themes. Like Rumours, the album has an emotional honesty that resonates only with the fluid cohesion that comes from being a concept album. As a result, Dark Side of the Moon is less a record than it is an experience, one that should be made before going up to the "Great Gig in the Sky."

Speaking of experiences, seeing KISS in concert is one that is an absolute must. Or at least was, given the band gave their last performance at Madison Square Garden on December 2nd, 2023. So if that's on your bucket list, you now only have two options: wait for the KISS virtual avatars to hit town, or listen to their 1975 album Alive! Opt for the latter. Their breakthrough album, consisting of cuts from their first three albums (KISS, Hotter Than Hell and Dressed to Kill), achieves something that other live albums have mostly failed to accomplish: the feeling of being there. Alive! captures the bombastic, take-no-prisoners energy of a KISS live show and puts it on vinyl. Twice, that is, with that raw power requiring containment on a double album. Good luck replicating that, avatars.

The "when" of kicking the bucket is assured. The "how," on the other hand, is another story. There are millions of ways one could go, from horribly tragic to an eternally looping end as a Darwin Award winner. You could, as alluded to in the aforementioned Rumours, even "Go Your Own Way." And if that's the case, as Van Halen cheerfully suggests on their 1984 album, you "might as well jump" (but in all seriousness, please don't — help is always available). The last album with long-time frontman David Lee Roth (until 2012's A Different Kind of Truth) is also, arguably, their best, with "Panama," "Hot for Teacher," "Drop Dead Legs" and "Jump" the standouts. Really, though, the album is so good it doesn't really matter where you "jump" in.

It's ironically fitting that the last album the Beatles recorded before their demise, one that includes "The End," would show up on a list like this, no? As Collider explains, Abbey Road is arguably the greatest record of the 20th century. It's high praise, and even if you may disagree — you shouldn't, it's pretty damn good — then it still stands tall amongst the crowd. Noted as much for its iconic album cover as its tracks, Abbey Road has contributions from all four of the Beatles, with George Harrison and Ringo Starr each providing their strongest entries ("Something" and "Here Comes the Sun" for Harrison, "Octopus's Garden" for Starr). Even the much-maligned "Maxwell's Silver Hammer" is far better than its reputation. The album has a blend of genres that work as a whole, serving as a snapshot of the band at the apex of their creativity.

The Beatles fell apart after recording Abbey Road, while Australian rockers AC/DC could have done the same after the death of original frontman Bon Scott, and even toyed with the idea of disbanding. Instead, they stayed together, with Scott's father encouraging them to do so. They recruited Brian Johnson and set out to record what would be the definitive AC/DC album: Back in Black. It was created as a tribute to Scott, with the all-black cover a sign of mourning, while simultaneously launching a new era for the band. That new era came with a commitment to elemental, arena-ready guitar riffs and straight-forward rock, with a frontman who brought his own energy instead of being a Scott-lite. To say it worked out pretty well is an understatement: Back in Black hit the top of the UK album chart, became the best-selling album that never reached number one in the US charts, and is currently the second best-selling album of all time in music history. There is legitimately not a single track that sucks, from opener "Hells Bells" (which, hopefully, aren't what you hear on the other side) to "Rock and Roll Ain't Noise Pollution."

These classic rock albums are masterpieces from beginning to end.

Another album that showcases the band's mastery over a wide range of genres is Led Zeppelin's Led Zeppelin IV. "Black Dog" and "Rock and Roll" are straight bangers, "Going to California" has an acoustic folk vibe, while "When the Levee Breaks" takes the original blues song by Kansas Joe McCoy and Memphis Minnie from 1929 and reinterprets it as a slow-groove, blues-rock masterpiece. Every member of the band is at their best, which comes through the album's most prominent track: "Stairway to Heaven." The delicate acoustic softness of its opening melds seamlessly into its hard-rock crescendo and a fiery solo from Jimmy Page that arguably stands as his finest, with Robert Plant owning the track with his uncanny vocal range. And hey, when you do say goodbye to this world, it's nice to know you don't need to waste your time finding the elevator to Heaven.

And if you make that trek to the top of the stairway, you'll find "Paradise City," which is also a track from one of rock's all-time greatest debut albums, Guns N' Roses' Appetite for Destruction. Neither the album or the band entered the music world quietly, but rather with a raw, explosive danger. They took the makeup, tights and hairspray that marked the hair-metal era and made it irrelevant overnight, ditching the sexualized, party-time dynamic in favor of a gritty, punk realism. The album balances radio-friendly(ish) tracks like "Sweet Child O' Mine" and "Welcome to the Jungle" with darker cuts like "Mr. Brownstone," making it an album of all killer, no filler. Why waste the time you have left listening to crap?

Knowing that this life is fleeting, you may want to get familiar with the fella that'll be hosting you for eternity after you've bought the farm: God. To that end, you'll want to throw on Stryper's game-changing 1986 album To Hell With the Devil. With it, the holy-roller band did the unimaginable by making Christian rock music actually good. Like, MTV good, where they were in heavy rotation with the likes of Mötley Crue and Poison. They had all the bombast and flash of their secular hair-metal peers, even better than most, with a message that was radically different. "Honestly" is as good a power ballad as you'll ever hear, while standout tracks "The Way" and "To Hell With the Devil" showcase the blistering fretwork of Oz Fox and vocalist Michael Sweet's uncanny pipes. In a word, To Hell With the Devil is heavenly.

As we learned from It's a Wonderful Life, every time a bell rings, an angel gets their wings. Assuming the bells you hear on side B of life aren't one of Hell's, then congratulations, you get your wings! But why wait until you take the big Adios, when you can pick up Aerosmith's second album, Get Your Wings. First, a little context: Aerosmith's self-titled debut in 1973 was not a success, unaided by Columbia's promotional efforts being diverted to tout Bruce Springsteen's debut album Greeting from Asbury Park, N.J. That, and as Tom Hamilton points out, "a lot of people took a look at us and said, 'They're like a Kmart version of the Stones.'" They were given one more chance: "Unless your next album is really, really good, you're not going to be recording artists anymore."

Suffice it to say, Get Your Wings was, is, really, really good. The biggest sin of Aerosmith was that it simply didn't sound like Aerosmith, and Get Your Wings reflected the band's emergence from the blues roots that dominated the first album into their own hard-rock identity. Joe Perry and Brad Whitford's dual-guitars were accentuated by producer Jack Douglas, who also helped frontman Steven Tyler hone his songwriting to reflect the now-iconic, swaggering persona he was developing as a frontman. The album would define the distinct Aerosmith sound from that point on, with tracks like "Same Old Song and Dance," "Seasons of Wither," "Train Kept a-Rollin,'" and the playful sleaze of "Lord of the Thighs" particular standouts.

Steve Sarkisian vs. Lane Kiffin is a fun undercard to the main event of Texas at LSU

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AUSTIN - Steve Sarkisian matches wits with Lane Kiffin on Saturday, which offers an intriguing subplot to the main story on the field.

It could be argued that these two polarizing coaches are the top narrative, and the game itself is secondary. Kiffin, as the declared King of Social Media until he got off it, might not be opposed to that.

The LSU coach does like attention. No, wait. He demands it. Sarkisian is fine with it and, given some of his offseason quips, it seems some of his good buddy's antics have rubbed off on the Texas coach.

Sarkisian and Kiffin might be mirror images of each other. That especially holds for the LSU coach with the boyish charm because he has never met a mirror he didn't like.

Sarkisian also has no shortage of self-confidence - should we say arrogance - which is yet one more common denominator for these two premier head coaches in college football.

The two will go head to head as head coaches at their current jobs for the first time Saturday night in Baton Rouge.

Not sure there has ever been two head coaches playing in the same conference that share such similar backgrounds and common ground - or uncommon ground, considering their paths - than these two men barely in their 50s.

Both are head coaches in the SEC making $11 million or more. Kiffin somehow gets by with $13 million.

Both were quarterbacks in college, but Sarkisian actually played. Kiffin didn't play a snap.

Both called plays under Pete Carroll for USC.

Both got fired as the head coach at USC, but Kiffin got the axe barely after he got off the airplane from a road game.

Both have spent abbreviated time in the NFL before being fired.

Both coached for the Oakland Raiders, Kiffin as head coach and Sarkisian as quarterbacks coach.

Both are really good friends who share insults and insights on the same group text threads.

Both coach at programs with near $50 million rosters.

Both have been panned for less than perfect seasons since they've lost a combined five games.

Both have overcome alcohol addiction. "On a personal level, a major change in my life was getting sober almost six years ago, and he was a big part of that," Kiffin said of Sarkisian. "I owe him a lot for that and will always be appreciative of that."

Sarkisian is a native Californian. Kiffin is Nebraska-born but California-cool. No truth to the rumor he came out of the womb tanned and wearing sunglasses.

In fact, these two have so much in common that they rehabilitated their reputations and repaired their careers in Nick Saban's halfway house for wayward coaches.

They're both front and center in the Saban docuseries although Kiffin got the most verbal spankings.

"Lane and I do go way back," Sarkisian said Monday, noting he was a grad assistant at USC when Kiffin was the tight end coach. "I think Coach Carroll had a big impact on both of us. Then there's this influence of Nick Saban on both of our careers. You love competing against your friends, and there's nothing more gratifying than beating your friends."

Lane Kiffin's first season at LSU is off to a 6-3 start after last week's loss to Alabama. (Gus Stark/LSU/University Images via Getty Imag)

Sarkisian has to beat Kiffin to ensure a spot in the College Football Playoff. Kiffin has to beat Sarkisian to keep a For Sale sign from being planted in his front yard.

But they also have their differences, too.

When I asked Sarkisian about them Thursday, he demurred, "I don't know. I know this. I have a ton of respect for Lane and the job that he does. He's always had very talented teams. They play well. He's a really bright football coach. Thinks outside the box."

Thinks it? No, Kiffin invented out of the box.

No coach has angered more fan bases at his exits than Kiffin whether it was from Tennessee after one season to return to USC or from Ole Miss in the middle of the season. That was a new one, even for him, but it added to the growing list of fan bases who hate him. He might be leaving Bobby Petrino in his wake.

Both were unceremoniously fired as USC head coaches although Kiffin wins for drama since he was let go on an airport tarmac on the very night after giving up 62 points to Arizona State.

Both were quarterbacks, but Sarkisian actually played. He was a record-setting star at BYU while Kiffin was a backup at Fresno State and quit the team during his senior season to be a student assistant coach. Wise move.

Steve Sarkisian was the USC offensive coordinator/quarterbacks coach at USC when Lane Kiffin was assistant head coach/quarterbacks in the 2005 season. (Erich Schlegel)

The duo combined to call plays for a 2004 Trojans team that put up half a hundred points in almost half its games and obliterated Oklahoma for a national championship. Then-USC offensive coordinator Norm Chow, for whom Sarkisian played at BYU, claimed in Monte Burke's "Men of Troy" book that Sark undermined him with Carroll and wrote that Sark and Kiffin were young, cocky and living high.

Kiffin spent a season and a quarter as a 31-year-old head coach of the Oakland Raiders before going 4-12 and getting fired after a 1-3 start by owner Al Davis, who called him "a flat-out liar." Sarkisian called plays for the Atlanta Falcons for two years before getting fired for his red-zone failures. Hmmmm.

The two are very tight and don't mind exchanging barbs. They share a lot of group thread texts that Kiffin always starts.

"I do appreciate that Lane has the humor and ability to do that," Sarkisian said. "He's humble enough to joke about himself, honestly, and all of us. And so I think that part's kind of fun."

Both are super smart, maybe too smart for their own good. Kiffin at least trusts his offense to play-caller Charlie Weis Jr. Sarkisian, analyst and former Longhorn Rod Babers told "On Second Thought," has "nobody on his staff he trusts to call plays, and that scares me."

Alabama owns Kiffin, who is 0-6 against the Crimson Tide, while Sarkisian is 1-1 against Alabama and would be 2-0 if not for a missed sack and a last-second field goal at DKR.

Saban hired Kiffin as his offensive coordinator, only to fire him and promote Sarkisian in the middle of the playoffs when Lane took the Florida Atlantic job.

Sarkisian has a very strong personality but is extremely guarded about his private life. Kiffin has a personality that's out of control. Kiffin doesn't do subtle.

Lane Kiffin doesn't mind a little showmanship such as having Kevin Costner join the "Victory Walk" before last week's game vs. Alabama. (Gus Stark/LSU/University Images via Getty Imag)

"It doesn't have to do with winning or losing games and who walks in. We'd won every game at home so far, so who walked in wasn't the reason we didn't execute in the red zone," Kiffin said. "Obviously, recruiting is the number one part of the whole thing that you create a program with. People watch from far away, and not just 12th graders, but eighth graders, and they see it and go, "That place is cool. I want to go there."

Sarkisian, however, doesn't feel the need to draw as much attention to himself. He understands the Longhorn brand doesn't need to oversell.

"Sark is great and very transparent," SEC Now host Peter Burns told the "On Second Thought" podcast. "He's not going to rock the boat a whole lot, and they're winning ballgames. So are you trying to win the social media war? Or are you trying to win football games?'

Kiffin doesn't mind whom he trolls. Sarkisian rarely said an unkind word until this offseason when he poked fun of Texas Tech's schedule and promoted Ole Miss's basket-weaving curriculum.

"Lane tries to convince himself that all of the headlines help the branding," said Burns, a Baton Rouge native who's attended more than 50 games at Tiger Stadium. "You probably had to do that at FAU. You probably had to do that at Ole Miss to get on everybody's radar. LSU is like Texas. You're already on everybody's radar. So the question is is the juice worth the squeeze? So you probably can't ask a tiger to change his stripes."

It's definitely what LSU signed up for. Its 6-3 record, not so much.

Another thing these two coaches have in common is that neither has won a national championship as a head coach.

Sarkisian's definitely come closer and might have played for one in 2023 but could never get a hand on Michael Penix in the Sugar Bowl and in 2024 because of a stupid blitz before halftime and a more stupid toss sweep in the red zone against Ohio State in the Cotton Bowl.

Kiffin has a lacking record against Top 25 teams. He's 15-27 versus ranked teams, including 0-3 this season. Sark's 25-35 but 18-7 in his last five years as well as 4-0 this year.

Kiffin has a better sense of humor. He once joked at Ole Miss that the Rebels were coming for Alabama and fans had "better get their popcorn ready." The Tide smoked Ole Miss. The next week, Ole Miss offered free popcorn at its game.

Sarkisian almost certainly could have had the Alabama job and succeeded Saban but didn't want to live in Tuscaloosa. Or follow the legend.

Kiffin almost certainly has coveted the Alabama job, but the Tide wanted no part of the Kiffin circus. (See: Saban butt-chewings.)

The simple truth is both are very good at recruiting. Both are damn good coaches. Both have egos that can land them in trouble but gets enormous clicks.

Neither really minds being in the spotlight, but just one absolutely craves it. Maybe almost as much as he does wins.

Dai Brooklyn Nets alla Big 12 Conference, passando per Roc Nation e NASCAR. Adesso una nuova esperienza a Milano, al fianco di Gerry Cardinale. Nominato amministratore delegato della nuova società, Yormark risponderà direttamente a Cardinale e si occuperà di sviluppare la strategia del gruppo, supervisionando le attività in portafoglio e valutando ulteriori opportunità di investimento. Non si tratta quindi di una nomina all'interno dell'organigramma del Milan, che resterà comunque il principale riferimento sportivo di MSE. Attualmente Yormark ricopre l'incarico di commissioner della Big 12 Conference, che lascerà alla fine di gennaio 2027. La scelta nasce da una conoscenza professionale di lunga data: "Conosco Brett Yormark e lavoro con lui da 25 anni, e non riesco a immaginare un dirigente migliore nel settore dello sport internazionale per contribuire a realizzare questa strategia", ha dichiarato Cardinale.

Il nuovo CEO di MSE ha oltre trent'anni di esperienza nello sport e nell'intrattenimento. Una parte importante della sua carriera è legata a Brooklyn Sports & Entertainment, società di cui è stato presidente e amministratore delegato per quattordici anni, supervisionando le attività dei Brooklyn Nets e del Barclays Center. Ha avuto un ruolo centrale nel trasferimento della franchigia NBA a Brooklyn e, nel 2007, nella conclusione dell'accordo ventennale per i naming rights dell'arena con Barclays. Dopo l'inaugurazione del 2012, l'impianto ha ospitato circa 200 eventi e accolto oltre 2,1 milioni di visitatori nel primo anno, con circa 11.000 abbonamenti stagionali venduti e l'80% delle suite assegnate. Sotto la sua gestione sono cresciute anche le attività commerciali dei Nets, mentre il portafoglio degli impianti si è ampliato con strutture come il Brooklyn Paramount e lo storico Webster Hall di Manhattan. Prima di questa esperienza, Yormark aveva lavorato per sei anni in NASCAR; successivamente è stato per tre anni Chief Operating Officer di Roc Nation e Co-CEO di Roc Nation Unified.

Più recentemente, alla guida della Big 12 Conference, Yormark ha seguito l'espansione della lega universitaria americana attraverso l'ingresso di Arizona, Arizona State, Colorado e Utah. Ha inoltre concluso un accordo per i diritti televisivi con ESPN e Fox valido fino alla stagione 2030-31, lavorando sullo sviluppo commerciale e sulle partnership della conference. Un curriculum particolarmente significativo rispetto alle ambizioni di RedBird, che con MSE intende ampliare le proprie attività milanesi oltre il calcio. Tra i progetti figurano infatti la gestione di stadi e arene, una possibile franchigia nella futura NBA Europe, lo sviluppo di un polo per sport e intrattenimento a San Donato e nuove opportunità nel tennis internazionale. "Nel corso della mia carriera ho contribuito all'evoluzione delle organizzazioni sportive, ad ampliarne la portata e a creare nuove opportunità. Non vedo l'ora di mettere a frutto questa esperienza per realizzare la visione di RedBird per MSE", ha dichiarato Yormark, che sarà chiamato a trasferire a Milano il modello di sviluppo commerciale sperimentato nello sport americano.

Ongoing Microsoft outages have led companies across Japan to dust off fax machines, with employees now using the old-school tech to complete tasks during the widespread blackout.

A widespread Microsoft 365 outage has disrupted email and workplace services around the globe, leaving many without access to the everyday tools they need for work.

While some services have started to recover, local governments and firms across Japan have turned to a familiar sight in offices during the 1980s and 1990s: fax machines.

These chunky devices are used to send copies of documents over telephone lines, allowing staff to communicate and confirm transaction details while email remains unavailable.

So, despite the tech being outdated compared to more modern devices, it turns out that sometimes relying on old-school equipment can be the perfect modern solution.

On November 13, companies across Japan began using fax and other alternatives to keep communications moving. Disruption has also reached government offices, with some asking people to contact them by telephone or fax while their email systems remain unavailable.

Since then, Japanese users across social media have been praising fax machines and other analogue tech for enabling them to continue communicating throughout the outages.

“Unbelievable, faxes are saving the day yet again…it reminded me of when faxes were getting renewed attention after the recent cyberattack. When you’re in a bind, you can always count on analog,” wrote one user on X.

“Our company wasn’t affected by the Outlook issue, but since other companies were, emails were only going one way to our phones. We used to exchange photos by texting them to each other, but in the end, even in this day and age, fax was just unbeatable,” added another.

What's Wrong With Amazon.com (AMZN) Stock, and Is It a Buy in November?

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Amazon has a few issues to keep in mind, but it's still growing briskly.

Shares of Amazon (AMZN +0.48%) have averaged annual gains of 4% over the past 15 years and 22% over the past decade. But over the past year, they're up only 9%. So what's wrong with Amazon? I would argue not much, but let's take a look.

First off, know that the long-term average annual gain for the stock market is close to 10%, and a 9% gain is quite respectable. IDFA Artistic Director Isabel Arrate Fernandez On Festival’s Competition Lineups, “Huge” Interest In Elizabeth Holmes Doc, And AI In Documentaries

The 39th edition of IDFA – the world’s biggest nonfiction film festival – is underway in Amsterdam, gathering the documentary field’s top talents, Oscar seekers and emerging filmmakers with dynamic work.

“It’s hectic, all the last-minute things,” artistic director Isabel Arrate Fernandez told Deadline just before the kickoff. “From the lineup competitions to also the industry program we have, there are many exciting things going on.”

The opening night slot Friday evening goes to White Tiger Column, a world premiere directed by Robert Mentov, Dwight Jantzi, and Bryan Dickie that’s set within the resistance movement opposing Myanmar’s military-dominated regime. The festival calls it “a striking example of documentary cinema… The film offers a moving portrait of a courageous generation.”

White Tiger Column is programmed in International Competition, along with films from Morocco, Madagascar, Burkina Faso, Brazil, France, Germany, the Republic of Georgia, and other countries. An Incomplete Day in the History of Georgia 10.06.93, directed by Salomé Jashi, examines a critical moment for Georgia in 1993 when the country, recently freed from Soviet domination, debated measures to replace the Russian ruble. Considering Vladimir Putin’s attempt to reassert Russian dominance over much of the old USSR – including Ukraine – it’s a film with immediate relevance. Watch on Deadline

A baker’s dozen of features is programmed in the festival’s Envision section, which platforms films with bold stylistic choices. “The way we look at both of our competitions,” Arrate Fernandez notes, “they are of equal value… In Envision, we are really looking at filmmakers that are challenging documentary, are challenging the form.” Arrate Fernandez adds, “If we don’t have that, then how does a genre, how does cinema evolve?”

One of the Envision titles, Ragazzi al fiume, examines the experience of three young Afghan immigrants to Switzerland. The IDFA program notes the documentary directed by Pablo Briones “gradually evolves into an observational portrait of isolation, loss, and the longing for a peaceful future.”

Fellow Envision Competition documentary The Black Box Is Orange focuses on the Israeli town of Shlomi that “was built on land belonging to the destroyed Palestinian village of Al-Bassa, near the Lebanese border,” according to the IDFA program. “Beginning with the Nakba mass displacement of Palestinians in 1948, its residents were forced out and citizens of the newly established state of Israel were settled there. All traces of the previous inhabitants were to be destroyed, including all Palestinian archives.”

That film is directed by Yuula Benivolski, an Israeli Jewish filmmaker born in Canada and raised in the village of Shlomi. (Two years ago, she posted a video on YouTube discussing her decision to renounce her Israeli citizenship. “It wasn’t a decision I made lightly… Yes, I did it in response to Israel’s horrific assault on Gaza,” she explained, before adding, “Whether Israel realizes it or not, all this death harms its own people as well.”).

More than 250 films are programmed across the festival’s sections. Among them are some of the strongest contenders for Oscars, including To Hold a Mountain, the Sundance winner directed by Biljana Tutorov and Petar Glomazić; Once Upon a Time in Harlem, directed by David Greaves and based upon material shot by his father, William Greaves (this festival is separately screening a large selection of William Greaves’s work); Musk, directed by Alex Gibney; Closure, directed by Michał Marczak, and You Can See Everything, the box office sensation about Elizabeth Holmes that’s directed by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim. Do they know the imprisoned Theranos founder in the Netherlands? Maybe so, maybe not, but enthusiasm for the film has certainly crossed the Atlantic.

Interest in You Can See Everything “has been huge,” Arrate Fernandez affirms. “The festival screenings sold out very quickly… It’s definitely generated some attention, to put it mildly.”

IDFA has become an increasingly important stop for filmmakers in Oscar contention, in part because of the plethora of Academy documentary branch voters who are expected to attend. (“It’s difficult to count them, I can tell you,” Arrate Fernandez notes. “Let’s see.”). Last year’s winner in IDFA’s International Competition, A Fox Under a Pink Moon, has become a leading contender for Oscar consideration this year. (That film’s protagonist and co-director, the young artist Soraya Akhlaghi, designed this year’s IDFA’s poster and contributed artwork used in the festival trailer. Watch it below).

Arrate Fernandez mentions other films in the 2026 IDFA lineup making a strong bid for Oscar recognition.

“The winner of Cannes, Rehearsals for a Revolution; Traces, a Ukrainian film talking about sexual assault as a war crime against women; Closure, NAZA… there are many interesting films there that I hope will make it,” she says. “Even though they are not the films with the bigger budgets and everything for their campaigning, I really hope they get far.”

14th, another top Oscar contender that will screen at IDFA, is among the documentaries that make use of AI – in the case of Ava DuVernay’s film, to animate vintage photographs. For the first time ever, the artistic director tells us, IDFA asked filmmakers to complete a form indicating if they had incorporated AI into their film’s production.

“Last year, we noticed that sometimes we were wondering while watching films and now we have this, we can go back and see what they filled out just to understand,” Arrate Fernandez comments. “But also, it’s part of the conversations that are taking place with regards to how to use [AI], what is acceptable? There are so many questions, so I think that really [speaks] to conversations that are being held within the industry.”

To that end, IDFA will be hosting two panel discussions on AI, part of a robust lineup of industry talks. A panel titled “Fighting Back – Cinema as an act of resistance?” will feature Oscar winner Laura Poitras, who is bringing They’re Here to IDFA, a short film she directed with Rachel Lauren Mueller that examines the fatal ICE surge in Minneapolis. Fellow panelists include Sofía Bordenave, director of Forest Up in the Mountain, and Mohanad Yaqubi, director of Revolutionaries Never Die. Fernandez is slated to moderate a conversation with this year’s IDFA Guest of Honor, filmmaker Tatiana Huezo.

This is the second year of Arrate Fernandez’s leadership as artistic director. Last year, she faced questions after IDFA revealed it would not invite to the festival what it considered to be Israeli state-supported organizations, such as DocAviv. On its website, IDFA spells out its position under a section titled “Principles and guidelines” governing the 2026 festival.

“For films, makers, and organizations from countries where freedom of expression is under pressure or human rights are violated, it is essential to assess and investigate their independence carefully, alongside the artistic selection process,” IDFA writes. “In this way, we aim to prevent governments and organizations involved in and/or suspected of serious human rights violations from gaining a platform at IDFA.”

The IDFA policy statement continues, “Films, makers, and organizations are never excluded on the basis of their origin and/or nationality, but rather on the basis of any possible relationship with regimes or organizations held internationally accountable for serious human rights violations.”

Arrate Fernandez tells Deadline, “We also really looked at what we did last year, how we’re implementing things, what are our internal processes. And basically, for… a film work or a film project, everything just went through the regular route of being evaluated by our different teams of programmers on the creative criteria. And the moment that films make it through the different phases and end up on a short list, then in cases of countries… that are known for human rights violations, we check the independence of this film or of this project in the sense of also going about checking credits, who are financiers. At the same time, it’s not a blanket approach in the sense of, ‘Oh, this financier, it’s out.’ We also look at the film, so we still have our case-by-case approach.”

She continued, “Then in terms of accreditations… we have the similar line in the sense of having this case-by-case approach.”

American Israeli filmmaker Rachel Leah Jones (Advocate) will be presenting her new project, Podium (You Have Three Minutes), at IDFA Forum, the event’s pitching, co-financing and co-production market for nonfiction and new media projects. Logline: “What does resistance sound like when delivered from the parliamentary podium? Since Israel’s founding, Palestinian lawmakers have taken the mic to appeal, demand, denounce, and warn — articulating a powerful counter-history of the state itself.”

Avi Mograbi, described in the IDFA program as “one of Israel’s most distinguished filmmakers,” heads to the festival for the Dutch premiere of his short documentary Wild Asparagus (Halayon), which bowed at the Venice Film Festival. In Collapse, Israeli director Anat Even returns to Kibbutz Nir Oz, where she lost her home and friends in the Hamas sneak attack of October 7, 2023. She films what remains there and also attempts to enter Gaza, documenting “a gathering of settlers who plan to build in Gaza and proclaim this by chanting, ‘Occupy, expel, settle,’” the festival program reads. “An introspective and revealing film essay on loss, grief, revenge, dehumanization and indifference.” (The program description includes this note: “This film contains content on potentially sensitive topics.”).

The production company for Collapse is listed as France.

“You won’t find films that have as a production country Israel” in this year’s festival, Arrate Fernandez indicates, “but there are Israeli filmmakers. The thing is also that we don’t ask people for their nationality… We have several films that are taking place in Israel and others that are taking place in Palestine or in the Occupied Territories.”

Just about every corner of the globe is reflected in the festival lineup. IDFA runs through November 22, a chance for documentary filmmakers and lovers of the form to gather in person in one of the world’s great cities.

Says Arrate Fernandez, “In a world where storytelling is being formatted by decision makers, AI, algorithm, numbers, I think it’s important to treasure these spaces.”

On Sunday evening, Mauricio Pochettino got the call he had been waiting for.

He and long-time assistant Jesus Perez had frequently asked about Manchester United over the last decade. They would often contact friends in the game who were connected to Old Trafford, looking to find out what was happening, whether there could be an opening.

Pochettino came very close to taking one of those openings on at least three occasions: 2018, 2021 and 2024. Now, elements finally have aligned.

It is a call that others at Old Trafford have long waited for, too. Back when they had a much publicised dinner in 2016, Sir Alex Ferguson essentially told Pochettino he should be the United manager. The Argentine was enthralled at that meeting by stories from one of the game’s great glory eras, and a sense of magic he had felt just standing in Old Trafford had been deepened.

Pochettino had been especially taken by Ferguson’s account of rebuilding United from the 1980s, initially drawing comparisons with what he was doing at Tottenham Hotspur. Now, he’s set to take on that exact same job, if one that has become so much bigger than in 2016. It is arguably even bigger than in 1986.

Pochettino, however, is fired by the idea of being the man to finally fix United. It has become one of the great managerial quests. The United hierarchy have ultimately gone for him because of that personality, and his standing. They don’t want a coach who hasn’t worked at this level. They want someone ready. Both the Glazers and Sir Jim Ratcliffe can sense that, since the two sides of the United ownership have known Pochettino for years.

If this is an appointment that always felt destined, though, that does raise one big question. Is this the right moment for it? Is it like Jose Mourinho, in that it came too late? Is it typical of modern United in that sense? Is it the same old “magical thinking” of hoping a famous name saves them, when the game has changed and the actual problems are now so structural?

Pochettino probably should have gone to United in 2016 or 2018, when he was at a peak in terms of being one of the most exciting coaches in football. The club did consider a move in the summer of 2016, but sources say an exit clause was allowed to expire. How different things could have been.

Old Trafford insiders are now talking about the value of “a name”, but the hierarchy did pass on Pochettino in 2024, when they initially considered sacking Erik ten Hag before ultimately deciding to stick with the Dutchman. His interview wasn’t seen as convincing as that of Thomas Tuchel or Roberto De Zerbi. Those who know him also say he is closer to being “a manager” now, in terms of a wider outlook, rather than the on-the-grass coach that so drove Spurs.

The very fact United had to negotiate to pry him from the United States national team, without wishing to be disrespectful, also illustrates how his stock has recently dropped. Some close to Pochettino would nevertheless put that down to necessary choices at certain moments. Taking charge of the hosts of the biggest World Cup ever, that had so much pomp around it, was too enticing. Before that, he probably should have pushed to go to United or Real Madrid in 2018 but he was convinced he could do something special with Spurs. He’d put too much into it.

And that does point to one of the other great twists of fate in modern football, where Pochettino’s profile could be completely different. His Tottenham team were genuinely one of the best in Europe over 2016-18, and one of the great Premier League teams. To achieve 86 points in 2016-17, on what was then only the sixth highest wage bill in English football, was genuine alchemy. It was immense overperformance, fostered by a side that could just overrun you.

We also now know that the wage bill of the eventual champions for that season, Chelsea, had in part come from signings that had been secured by payments against the rules. The punishment from that guilty verdict still looks weak. There is a strong argument that Chelsea should have been stripped of the 2015 and 2017 titles. Pochettino himself said as much in a wide-ranging interview with The Independent and other media in September: “I’ve said that, that we should win one title, one Premier League. We should have been awarded it… for sure, we deserve to be champions for that year.”

Had that justice been done, Pochettino would not just be a “name” who created an exciting Spurs once. He would be an English champion, and one responsible for one of the most historic titles given the much wealthier competition. A great institution would have won just their third title, and first in 56 years.

Both he and Spurs could have had their futures transformed, as well as their place in history. It would have been a modern equivalent of what Ferguson himself did with Aberdeen.

Now, he wants to follow Ferguson in a different way. This is the job he has been waiting for.

A certain apathy towards the US job was displayed in that September interview when he spoke about how his contract didn’t necessarily mean he’d stay the full term.

For all of that, and how their World Cup campaign ended in the chaos of Donald Trump’s disruptive interference in the Folarin Balogun case, there were very impressive spells when the US really looked like a classic Pochettino team. The way they ripped into Paraguay was reminiscent of Spurs 2016-18, right up to some of the flourishes. And that was in a much more limited and restricted environment than a club team.

This is the other thing about just taking the job, too. Regardless of United’s current problems, and perceptions of where Pochettino is in his career, he is energised by this. He has that bite. As one figure who knows him well says, “he has got the IP, he has got the brand, he has got the experience”.

Having already worked for Chelsea and Paris Saint-Germain, he’s been at big clubs with demanding owners. He has the mettle for that. And he now has the big job he’s always wanted.

Skipping doses topped the list of changes, with 28% of users saying they did it. Among users who made changes, cost was the reason for 38% and availability for 36%.

The data come from the CDC’s Rapid Surveys System, which collected responses from 8,118 U.S. adults in July and August 2025. Overall, an estimated 9.8% of adults said they had used a GLP-1 drug at some point over the past year. The 976 users in the sample received follow-up questions about their use.

Younger adults reported the most changes. Modification rates were 54% among users ages 18 to 39 and 30% among users 65 and older. Delayed prescription fills were reported by 37% of the younger group and 6% of the older group, and 58% of younger users who made changes cited cost, compared with 20% of older users.

Compounded formulations were used by 22.6% of GLP-1 users, and another 8.8% could not say if theirs was one. The Food and Drug Administration does not vet compounded GLP-1 medications for how safe, effective or well made they are before they go to market. Modification rates were 62% among compounded-drug users and 36% among other users.

About 12% of users reported reducing or stopping their medication because of side effects or symptoms. The CDC said more evidence on the safety and effectiveness of these real-world practices could help guide clinical guidelines.

Cost has been a barrier for some time. Without insurance or discounts, FDA-approved weight-loss drugs carry a sticker price that can top $1,000 a month, which helped drive demand for compounded versions during shortages of approved products.

Inside the $3.25 Million Pasadena Glass Pavilion That’s Still Studied in Architecture Classes Today

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A three-bedroom midcentury modern home designed by Case Study architect Craig Ellwood has just come on the market in Pasadena, CA, for $3.25 million. It features a notable cantilevered glass pavilion.

Built in 1965, the Kubly House was originally constructed for less than $45,000. The 2,227-square-foot home has since been thoughtfully updated.

“What makes this house special is its architecture and design, and how true it has remained to the original,” says Chris Suarez, who is co-listing the property with Kimberly Benz of Suarez + Benz at Compass. “Nothing was significantly altered. Everything done was intended to restore the house to its original condition. The footprint, the floors, the glass—everything has remained true to the original design.”

Located in the Linda Vista neighborhood, the home has an iconic redwood, cantilevered glass pavilion that opens to a single-level floor plan. The private half-acre lot includes a pool and spa surrounded by lush landscaping with eucalyptus and oak trees.

With exposed post-and-beam construction, clean horizontal lines, and expansive walls of glass, the home was designed to give the illusion of floating above its natural surroundings. The deck was recently restored using specially milled wood.

“It was sourced from northern Washington and brought down here,” Suarez notes. “Everything was done painstakingly to avoid altering the house’s integrity, architecture, and design. That being said, significant work has been done, including updates to the roof, infrastructure, air conditioning, and electrical systems. All the major systems have been updated, but you wouldn’t necessarily know it because the work was done so carefully.”

Originally commissioned by ArtCenter College of Design alumni and educators Don and Sally Kubly, the home represents their understanding of design and the culture of the college community. It is still studied in classes there.

“Don was ArtCenter’s first president in Pasadena,” Suarez explains. “There’s also a connection through Craig Ellwood, who designed ArtCenter’s main campus building. The main campus is an incredible steel-and-glass building designed by Ellwood. Don played a significant role in moving the school from Los Angeles to Pasadena, where he served as president for many years. He’s very well known within that community, and this house is significant to the college’s history and culture.”

Based on available architectural records, the Kubly House is said to be Ellwood’s only documented residential project in Pasadena.

After remaining in the Kubly family for nearly six decades, the home changed hands for $3.6 million in 2022. Since then, the sellers have undertaken a preservation-minded restoration with Carl Bronson of Bronstruction, updating major systems to contemporary standards.

“A house that helped usher in much midcentury modernism was the Glass House, designed by Philip Johnson around 1948 or 1949,” Benz notes. “That design was definitely something Ellwood loved. You can clearly see that house’s influence here, which is important because it reveals some of the influences behind Ellwood’s work.”

Benz adds that the home’s design was also influenced by Ellwood’s peers, including midcentury designer George Nelson.

“A furniture plan developed for the house clearly referenced Mies van der Rohe and George Nelson, both important figures in midcentury modern design,” she says. “Even the deck and the steps leading to the house resemble a George Nelson bench. There’s actually a bench built into the concrete wall near the pool that resembles a George Nelson design. I don’t know whether Nelson designed or made it, but the influence is certainly there.”

The house is special because many of the original details have remained intact, she adds.

“You can see the influence of the people Ellwood admired throughout the house,” Benz says. “That significance is what makes this house important when you look at the midcentury modernist movement. All these examples are visible in the house and remain there.”

Some of the built-in furniture has a distinctive style, too.

“There’s a stunning floating credenza that looks like a George Nelson piece,” she notes. “We think it might be one of his designs. It’s in the living room. There’s also a built-in console that might be a George Nelson design as well. So, some of the furniture is actually built into the architecture.”

While some pieces are originals and others are reproductions, Benz says, “the most important thing was following the original plan. The history of this house is everything.”

When staging the home, she says they were “fortunate to discover an original interior design study among the blueprints, plans, and documents that came with the house. We were able to look at that study, and it became a guide and a road map for staging the property. Sourcing the furniture was a challenge because we had to find the specific pieces.”

The city of Pasadena has also identified the property as eligible for individual listing on the National Register of Historic Places.

A hopeless thriller is one that keeps you grabbed by the neck but still ends in a painful manner. Someone in a thriller says, “We just need to find the truth,” and I immediately start worrying about them. Because finding the truth is one thing. Surviving what it does to your life is another. I've watched characters sacrifice everything to uncover a secret, only to discover that knowing the answer doesn't give them the slightest power to change it. It's infuriating. It's heartbreaking. And when a show earns that feeling, I cannot look away.

I think helplessness becomes truly frightening when you can understand every decision that leads somebody there. You wouldn't necessarily have behaved differently. You might have trusted the same person, followed the same evidence, or convinced yourself that one more sacrifice would finally put things right. These six thrillers are merciless about that very human tendency.

Sarah Linden (Mireille Enos) is preparing to leave the Seattle Police Department when the disappearance of teenager Rosie Larsen interrupts her plans. She works alongside detective Stephen Holder (Joel Kinnaman) and investigates a murder that tears through Rosie's family and becomes entangled with a political campaign. The Killing makes the investigation painfully personal, particularly for Rosie's parents, Stan (Brent Sexton) and Mitch (Michelle Forbes).

What gets under my skin is how little the truth can actually repair. Mitch can barely function inside the home where her daughter should still be living. Stan's grief keeps twisting into anger, leaving him vulnerable to decisions that could destroy what's left of his family. Meanwhile, Linden's inability to abandon the case damages her relationship with her own son. Everybody wants justice, but nobody can promise that justice will make living any easier. I find that distinction absolutely devastating.

Paul Spector (Jamie Dornan) has a wife, children, and a job as a grief counselor. He also stalks and murders women in Belfast. Detective Superintendent Stella Gibson (Gillian Anderson) arrives to investigate the killings, gradually closing in on a man who appears disturbingly ordinary outside his crimes. The Fall makes no secret of the murderer's identity. We know who he is, and the awful suspense comes from watching him move through a city full of people who don't.

Dornan's most disturbing moments aren't necessarily the violent ones. Sometimes Paul is simply behaving affectionately toward his children, and I want him as far away from them as possible. His domestic life offers camouflage without making his affection straightforwardly false, which is a sickening contradiction. Gibson is formidable, but intelligence and determination cannot undo what his victims have experienced. Even catching him means confronting the lasting damage he's inflicted. This is a thriller where the prospect of victory never feels remotely satisfying enough.

Mr. Robot begins with a thrilling fantasy of rebellion. Elliot Alderson (Rami Malek) works in cybersecurity while secretly hacking people and organizations he believes deserve exposing. But Elliot's loneliness is even harder to endure. He can access almost anybody's private information while remaining terrified of genuine human intimacy. Sam Esmail, the show's creator, pushes that contradiction to its most painful extreme during Elliot's conversations with therapist Krista. The man determined to expose the world's hidden cruelty has spent years struggling to confront his own suffering. Mikkelsen can turn a quiet dinner invitation into something sinister, while Dancy makes Will's desperate need for connection painfully convincing. The second season's confrontation at Hannibal's home is among the cruelest emotional disasters I've watched on television. What destroys me is that neither man can simply dismiss their attachment afterward. Understanding one another has become another form of imprisonment.

“The statistics tell one story. We tell another,” the film’s opening frame reads.

Structured as a six-part anthology, Facing The Numbers explores these disparities through the real-life stories of First Nations people using a bold mix of dance, poetry, and spoken word.

Satour and Perkins wrote and directed the film together. Perkins also served as the DoP. Satour produced alongside Ramona Telecican. Below, Satour and Perkins speak with Deadline about how they developed the anthology film and their bold plans for its release.

DEADLINE: This film has a very unique structure. How did you develop this approach to the material?

TYSON PERKINS: This kind of subject matter is often portrayed in a very academic way on film. It tends to take the form of talking-head interviews and fairly basic re-creations, which don’t get close to the emotional point of view—or the emotional suffering—that many victims of police brutality go through. We started with that as our aim, and this little, unusual thing came out of it.

DEADLINE: I read this started as a short project and then expanded. Is that right?

PERKINS: It was as much about treating the subject matter in a fresh way as it was about using a fresh format. For us, that format was short-form and shareable online. It came as a response to the Black Lives Matter movement. We were watching what was happening in America from Australia and the artistic responses that came out of it—not the news coverage, but the things that appeared on our Instagram feeds. It prompted Kieran and me to think about Australia. Some of our statistics on incarceration, youth incarceration, male suicide, and deaths in custody are among the worst in the world. We wanted to take some of those subjects and statistics and frame them through the short-form, viral storytelling many African American artists in the U.S. were using. By “viral,” we didn’t necessarily mean that it had to go viral; we meant something short, shareable, and to the point.

As happens with films, once we delved into the individual subjects and the stories, they expanded. We realised there was a way to tell the story of this country—its institutional racism, its history of oppression and its ongoing oppression—through these personal, first-hand stories. We thought we had six separate subject films, but when we put them together, they formed a truthful picture of what Aboriginal people face in this country.

DEADLINE: You incorporate several different art forms in the film. There’s dance, spoken word. But it feels seamless. How did you get it to work?

KIERAN SATOUR: One of the amazing things about Facing the Numbers is that so many First Nations artists came together to tell this story. The spoken word, poetry, and dancing feel seamless because everyone is telling the same story. We’re telling multiple separate stories, but they represent a collective Aboriginal experience. Tyson and I were lucky to work with all these incredible creatives.

DEADLINE: How have people been responding to the film, especially in Australia?

SATOUR: People have been responding really well. We premiered at the Melbourne International Film Festival and had a great turnout. About 75% of tickets were sold for both screenings. We had standing ovations, and the Letterboxd reviews came in almost immediately after the first screening. Nothing was under four stars, and the reviews were glowing. We screened at the Darwin International Film Festival. My sister lives in Darwin. She’s also a co-founder of the production company and an executive producer on the film. She told me that people have been stopping her in Darwin to talk about Facing the Numbers because they’d been at the screening. It’s been amazing.

DEADLINE: Where did you find your participants?

SATOUR: During the global movement after George Floyd’s murder, people were sharing their stories online, and we found some of these stories through social media. Geoffrey Johnson is one example. Otherwise, we were doing research and development, trying to find stories that spoke to this collective experience. It came down to meetings and phone calls.

PERKINS: It’s important to mention that it would not be difficult to find thousands of stories like these. Thousands of Aboriginal people have direct descendants who were taken during the Stolen Generations or sent to schools like Kinchela Boys’ Home. Probably every Aboriginal family knows someone who has been a victim of police brutality, whether in their own family or a family they know. The stories we chose are told by extremely courageous people. It takes strength to repeat your story on camera, allow it to be filmed, and make it public. But one of the key messages of the film is that these experiences are extraordinarily common—in people’s lives and statistically.

DEADLINE: How did you build this production? I can’t imagine the Australian public funding system was jumping to invest in something like this. Or maybe I’m wrong.

SATOUR: It was funded through a digital-series, online-production fund at Screen Australia, our national public screen-funding body. That fund doesn’t even exist anymore. The project was never meant to be a feature film. We made the anthology into a feature partly because that helped us unlock extra funding and become eligible for a significant tax offset. Production went through a few phases. At the end of the first phase, we had two strong rough cuts, which we used to secure more funding. We received a little more from the national broadcasting funding body, then a broadcaster attached through a pre-sale, and we brought in philanthropic support. It was a struggle, but we got there. A film like this doesn’t get made without the goodwill of philanthropists, but also without all the work everyone put into those first two rough cuts. They were strong, and I think the project sold itself from there.

DEADLINE: How will it be distributed? I know you’re screening at the Sydney Opera House, which is wonderful. Do you have plans for international screenings? In the UK and U.S., people involved in solidarity movements would be intrigued to hear from you and see a film like this.

SATOUR: Totally. In terms of craft, we were inspired by global Black filmmakers. Steve McQueen’s Small Axe was a huge reference for us, as was Spike Lee. We both admire the American director Khalil Joseph. We’re connecting with global Black and brown movements because we’re facing similar issues everywhere: over-policing, incarceration, and child removal. We’re still waiting to find out where the international premiere will be. In Australia, we’re doing a film-festival run, and then the film will be broadcast on NITV, the National Indigenous Television Network, which sits under SBS, our multicultural public broadcaster. The digital release will follow once the holdbacks for the national TV broadcast are over. The films will also be released online through a microsite.

A key partner is Common Ground, an Aboriginal-run and Aboriginal-led organisation focused on education. They’re running our impact campaign. The organisation’s founder, Rona Glynn-McDonald, is Warwick Thornton’s daughter, so filmmaking is in her blood. We’re excited to get these stories out digitally and reach younger people who may not watch television or attend film festivals, and to connect with an audience that isn’t already converted.

PERKINS: For me, the online release is what I’m most excited about. In film and television now, you see people who have built audiences on YouTube or Instagram bringing those audiences into more traditional film and TV. The ideal outcome would be for some of the storytellers to experience justice—whether for a family member’s death or, in Uncle Widdy’s case, by having more of the public learn what happened to him at Kinchela Boys’ Home. Not many people know that thousands of children went through that school or what happened to them. The survivors are trying to reclaim the land where the school stood. Once the films have been shared and completed their festival runs, I hope some of those outcomes can begin to happen. I’m really excited about that.

Three central banks have completed tests of a cross-border foreign exchange settlement system connecting traditional payment infrastructure with distributed ledger technology.

In a joint statement on Friday, the Bank of England, Monetary Authority of Singapore, and Bank of Thailand said they conducted the experiments using a prototype developed by Partior, which coordinated payment-versus-payment (PvP) settlement and multilateral net FX transactions across their test environments.

The three central banks are working under Project Meridian FX APAC, announced last November. The project encompassed multiple currencies, technologies, settlement infrastructures, and time zones, with the Reserve Bank of Australia and Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas also participating as observers, the banks said.

According to the statement, Partio's system matched settlement instructions, coordinated the reservation of funds, monitored payment readiness, and initiated settlement.

The experiment also examined multilateral netting, which allows banks to offset payments against one another to reduce the liquidity needed for settlement.

However, the accompanying policy report identified questions around operational resilience, settlement finality, governance, liability, and cross-border data sharing. It also warned that additional functions, including holding funds or calculating net positions, could change an operator's regulatory responsibilities.

Tom Mutton, the BOE's executive director of payments, innovation and fintech, said the project provided "practical evidence of how synchronized settlement could help reduce risk and improve efficiency in cross-border FX transactions."

Many leafy plants we now grow as houseplants evolved in hot and humid rainforests which is why, now fall’s here and the heat’s turned on, the dry air in our homes is leading to less-than-lovely foliage. So, if you're wondering how to keep houseplants healthy in winter so you can enjoy lush, glossy leaves, you're not alone. Thankfully, a humidity tray is the quick and easy DIY solution that will speed your houseplants back to happy in time for the holidays, and it won't cost you a dime.

It’s a cruel fact that it’s the plants with the biggest or boldest leaves that struggle most in the dry air of our winter-heated homes: adapting to life in a rainforest is exactly why the foliage is so fabulous in the first place. Beneath a dense forest canopy, sunlight is a precious commodity so some plants like Elephant Ear (Alocasia) grow huge leaves to catch as much light as possible. Others such as peace lilies has foliage densely packed with chlorophyll to absorb as much of the scarce light as possible, and that's what creates their intense, deep green hue. Some like Calathea have purple or red undersides to their leaves precisely so that precious light gets bounced back into the leaf.

So, if you want to enjoy these dramatic leaves at their best, you have to provide these houseplants with the humidity level they need to flourish. Any plant likely suffering from a lack of humidity at this time of the year is easy to spot, starting with any houseplants with brown leaf tips or edges, smaller or discolored leaves, or slow growth. Now, to restore these ailing houseplants to lush, glossy growth, you could treat them to a small humidifier like the Homedics Humidifier from Amazon. Or you could simply DIY a humidity tray with stuff you’ve probably already got in your backyard.

A humidity tray, also called a pebble tray, is a shallow container that’s wider than the plant pot it serves, filled with small pebbles and water. The plant pot sits on the pebbles above the water level so instead of water being pulled up into the potting soil, it evaporates into the air. The higher the heat in your home, and so the drier the air, the more water evaporates to create a humid microclimate around your houseplant.

An old plant saucer is perfect, or if you’d like something more decorative for your houseplant display, look in your kitchen cupboards for a deep plate or shallow dish. Fill this with fine gravel or small pebbles – pea gravel is perfect and you can buy bagged pea gravel pebbles from Home Depot very cheaply if you don’t have a gravel path or driveway to raid.

Then simply stand the plant pot on top of your DIY humidity tray and keep the water topped up, making sure the water level stays below the base of the pot.

Grouping your houseplants together will also help to raise the humidity. Clustering plants together creates an indoor microclimate because the water vapor that’s lost through the leaves diffuses more slowly – and this effect is amplified if even one or two of the grouped houseplants have humidity trays.

Many more adaptable houseplants won’t be as dramatic about a less-than-ideal humidity level, but add a little more moisture to the air and they’ll grow faster and produce bigger leaves with a better color and shine. Making a DIY pebble tray will help all these plants to flourish:

These plants all thrive in humidity levels of 40-60%. While that's likely within the average humidity of a home in summer, it's a different story in winter. Because cold air can't hold as much water, and home heating dries it further, home moisture levels typically drop to 20-40%. While that lack of humidity isn't enough to kill these adaptable plants, it will certainly prevent them from looking their best.

Leaves with brown tips and outer edges are the most common sign that a houseplant is struggling in low humidity levels and is losing moisture from its foliage faster than the roots can replace it. All plants release water vapor from tiny pores on their leaves and, in dry air, that moisture is sucked out at a faster rate. The plant parts furthest away from the roots supplying the water are the first to run out of moisture, which is why leaf tips are the first to dry out to a crispy brown. At this time of the year, it's very common to see houseplants with long, narrow leaves like spider plants with brown leaf tips. Low humidity is also an easily-avoided reason why peace lily flowers turn brown.

There are other symptoms to look out for too, such as slow growth and smaller new leaves. While many plants naturally produce aerial roots, if you spot a sudden escalation in their number, the houseplant is likely seeking more humidity. And if you find that the potting soil dries out super-quickly after watering, too-dry air may be the cause.

Misting houseplants provide a quick fix but the spike in humidity only lasts for a very short time, whereas a humidity tray delivers constant moisture to create a more permanent microclimate. To match the humidity levels provided by a pebble tray, you’d need to mist houseplants every hour, every day, every night. Misting can also create a breeding ground for fungal and bacterial disease by leaving water sitting on foliage.

In dry or arid zones like Arizona, Nevada, New Mexico or Colorado, your houseplants may need humidity trays topped up year-round. You’ll likely see even plants that don’t require very high air moisture levels developing brown tips, which is a sure sign that they’ll benefit from a humidity tray.

At the other end of the scale, if you live in a high-humidity climate like Louisiana, Mississippi or Florida, only the houseplants that are highly dependent on humidity need a pebble tray – unless your home is equipped with an efficient air-conditioning system that dehumidifies, and dependant on your winter home-heat level, of course. Unnecessary standing water in a warm, damp home puts plants at a higher risk of pests and fungal disease.

Gifting many of your houseplants a DIY humidity tray will help them to flourish in our heated homes through fall and winter, and happy plants grow leaves that are larger and lusher, greener and glossier. Along with a cheap thermometer that displays air moisture percentages, your DIY pebble tray makes it very easy to adjust humidity levels, too. Keep the trays topped up with water for maximum humidity, and gradually reduce if needs be once your home's heat is turned off. You can even leave the trays water-free over summer if natural humidity is high enough in your climate, and let your DIY pebble saucers serve as stylish houseplant decor for a personal touch, until the heat's turned back on.

Many series opt for a cathartic wind-down, a safe and comfortable ending that offers closure to the audience. However, some series do something a little different, opting to strive for more ambitious conclusions that have a more dramatic effect on viewers.

Many of the series included ended with bold and brash big swings that completely reframed how viewers regarded the show. Others misfired as underwhelming resolutions to iconic titles, the backlash of which completely changed how television as a medium regards the art of delivering a climactic episode and season. All of these finales have made an undeniable impact on the format and its fans, and all stand as noteworthy chapters in HBO’s impressive and extended stay as the flagship brand of television excellence in the 21st century.

It is a conclusive shift that isn’t necessarily restricted to the series finale. After all, a tone of realism and uncertainty permeates the entire five-season run, but The Wire’s conclusion does reframe the series quite substantially. Revolving around the complex criminal ecosystem of Baltimore, spanning from the street-level drug dealers to the politics of City Hall, The Wire delivers an all-encompassing, novelistic epic of modern American crime, one laced with dozens of focal characters on both sides of the law.

Towards the end of the final season, it begins to become clear that creator David Simon has no intention of wrapping character arcs up in a neat little bow that will give viewers a sense of closure. Instead, The Wire ends as a grim meditation on the cyclical nature of organized crime, institutional corruption, and bureaucratic inefficiency. In the context of the series itself, it’s a thought-provoking conclusion about the nature of society and the way things both change and stay the same over time. Pertaining more to television at large, however, The Wire’s resolution proved that major series don’t need to end with clarity or cliffhangers to be compelling.

The air of infamy that surrounded Game of Thrones thanks to its final season has begun to dissipate — the success of House of the Dragon and A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms has been influential in this — but the impact of what the epic fantasy series actually changed is still being felt. This isn’t a reference to the plotting of the story, but rather to the way television as a medium changed in the wake of Game of Thrones’ finale and the outpouring of audience disgust.

It shifted the paradigm of television storytelling. Suddenly, it wasn’t enough to run with an enticing premise and figure it out on the go. Showrunners and writers had to approach major, ongoing series with a mind to how the story would end, or risk facing strong backlash from the fanbase. Granted, some major series haven’t learned the lesson in the ensuing years, and the nature of television storytelling was already evolving due to the rampant rise in streaming services, but Game of Thrones’ conclusion has proven to be a cautionary tale of negligence and subversion, with most shows since opting to conclude in a manner that honors central themes and character dynamics of their respective stories.

Barry is one of those series that has come out since the fallout of Game of Thrones, one where the lessons HBO learned from its failure have been most evident. After four seasons following the violent hitman struggling to transition from a life of crime to a career on the stage, Barry ends with a poignant sense of inevitable tragedy that dismantles the antihero redemption trope, then closes with a 10-year time jump that sees Barry’s (Bill Hader) life being recontextualized and completely misinterpreted in an adoring Hollywood biopic.

The thematic notes that Barry flaunted throughout the four seasons are beautifully handled in “wow,” which also adds a healthy dose of social commentary at the expense of glamorizing entertainment and the audiences that embrace it. The series as a whole is redefined in its final minutes, pivoting from a black comedy crime thriller to a scorching indictment of Hollywood sanitization. With every major character also facing appropriate ends to their arcs, Barry’s finale is a masterclass in how to end a story with thematic punch and emotional deftness.

There have been plenty of series that have completely changed their own nature in their finale; some have even altered the fabric of television with their resolutions, but few have done what Curb Your Enthusiasm did with its swan song outing. The hit comedy series follows the sensationalized life of Larry David, the producer of Seinfeld, as he drifts through Hollywood aimlessly. After 12 seasons and 120 episodes, it ends with Larry on trial in Atlanta, with “No Lessons Learned” delivering a meta recreation of Seinfeld’s infamous and divisive finale.

It’s clever, endearing, and cathartic for all who felt undermined by Seinfeld’s finale, especially as it ends on a twist that sees Larry walk free, leading him and Jerry Seinfeld to declare that would have been a better conclusion for their hit ‘90s sitcom. It’s an admission that Seinfeld was flawed that also serves as a perfect finale for Curb Your Enthusiasm in its own right, making it possibly the only TV show ever made with a finale that has directly changed the perception of a separate series.

Criminally underrated, Show Me a Hero is a breathtaking political drama that uses the tragic true story of the youngest mayor of Yonkers, New York, to explore the complexities of desegregation, the cost of political conviction, and how grassroots humanity clashes violently with institutional power. Oscar Isaac is brilliant as Nick Wasicsko, with much of the miniseries revolving around his desire to embrace a low-income housing project that many of his constituents are viciously opposed to.

The miniseries adamantly rejects the comfort of white savior heroics, delving into the grim reality of the true story that sees Wasicsko grow paranoid after losing an election and eventually commit suicide. It shatters the tone of an idealistic, progressive politician navigating the red tape of city hall, highlighting Wasicsko’s egotistical knack for defining his self-worth through his career as a damning fault, while also illustrating the callous, arduous nature of true systemic change.

The Leftovers is one of those series that feels like everything changes every episode. While it is a slow and meditative show, the depth it invests in its characters constantly brings new revelations to the fold, not only in their own lives as they navigate heartache, frustration, and rage, but in accordance with overarching story themes of grief, trauma, and the human condition as well. Overall, The Leftovers unfolds in a world reeling from the “Sudden Departure”, an inexplicable event in which two percent of the world’s population vanished in an instant.

The series finale, “The Book of Nora,” transpires years after the rest of the series, exploring an uneasy reunion between Nora (Carrie Coon) and Kevin (Justin Theroux). It revels in the surrealist intensity and compassionate dramatic might that defined the series at its best, ending on an unproven explanation as to what caused the Sudden Departure, then leans on empathy and choice in its final moments, rather than a traditional, plot-centered resolution. It’s a bold decision, but it perfectly suits the series’ overarching tone and brings touching closure to Kevin and Nora’s relationship.

Blending corporate satire with engrossing, high-stakes drama, Succession fast became one of HBO’s greatest ever series in its exploration of the vying for power that erupts between three siblings when their father, the aging founder and head of a multi-billion-dollar multimedia company, signals his intent to retire. It is a manic fight for power and position with no blow being deemed below the belt, and yet the fourth and final season still ends in a manner that leaves viewers numb and shocked.

The kinship the Roy siblings display as they stand against Lukas Matsson’s (Alexander Skarsgård) efforts to buy Waystar RoyCo gives way to total desperation and betrayal. It proves that the inherited wealth and influence the Roys flaunted made them incapable of ever following in their father’s footsteps, and their festering animosity with one another, as their shared childhood trauma went unresolved, was instrumental in them fragmenting and losing everything they wanted. It is quite brilliant how a series that features such unlikable and conniving characters leaves the audience feeling hollow, if not completely rattled, at their failure.

Based on Gillian Flynn’s novel of the same name, Sharp Objects is a methodical, meandering crime thriller that anchors its drama in Camille Preaker’s (Amy Adams) investigation of the murder of two young girls and the inner turmoil she experiences as she returns to her hometown of Wind Gap and is confronted with the ghosts of her traumatic childhood. It holds a captivating Southern Gothic atmosphere, a litany of exceptional performances, and a macabre balance of character drama and murder mystery, but its greatest asset is the festering sense of dreadful unpredictability it exudes throughout the entirety of its eight-episode run.

Even with viewers accustomed to its volatile plot twists, Sharp Objects completely changes the nature of its story in its final episode. In fact, it does so many times. The most striking of which comes in the final minute of the series, revealing a horrifying truth that shatters Camille’s sense of self-growth and safety. Accompanied by a credits sequence intercut with shots that force the audience to bask in the terrifying truth, Sharp Objects delivers an unforgettable ending that elevates it from a gripping psychological thriller to a masterclass in nightmarish suspense.

Six Feet Under is essentially a tale of mortality and the human condition wrapped up in a dark comedy about the dysfunctional Fisher family and their management of a funeral home in the aftermath of their patriarch’s death. It’s funny, often poignant and sad, always thought-provoking and compelling, and it ends with what is arguably the greatest series finale in the history of television, a feat of storytelling steeped in catharsis, closure, and beautiful thematic touch.

Within the context of the series itself, it recalibrated all the death and drama as being a simple fact within the wonder of life, transforming the show from being an acidic satirical dramedy to a powerful ode to family and existence itself. Its flash-forward montage and evocative use of Sia’s “Breath Me” only add to the emotional resolution. Through this, Six Feet Under even redefined the commercial possibilities for television, with the episode marking a major breakthrough for Sia that proved a perfect needle-drop moment could define a series’ legacy for decades to come.

It should come as no surprise that The Sopranos’ finale altered the course of television history. After all, the series itself did so from its very first episode. Whether it was through presenting such a complex and challenging antihero in Tony Soprano (James Gandolfini), pioneering long-form serialized drama on the small screen, or simply through its stronger appetite for graphic material, The Sopranos revolutionized the modern age of television drama, and its finale marks another major evolutionary step for storytelling in the medium.

It is iconic in its own right. The diner, the uneasy tension, Journey’s “Don’t Stop Believin’”, and, of course, the cut to black. It was divisive at the time, but it has come to be heralded as a stroke of genius. Almost 20 years on, people still discuss what they think happened to Tony, but from the perspective of television drama, it proved that series didn’t necessarily need to end with neat, conclusive closure to be rewarding. It proved ambiguity had a huge part to play in 21st-century television; it redefined how audiences engaged with television (especially as it coincided with the rising popularity of the internet and social media), and it remains one of the best and boldest TV finales of all time.
