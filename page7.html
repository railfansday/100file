MusiCares’ 2026 Wellness in Music Survey: 5 Statistics That Paint a Sobering Picture

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

The situation is even worse for emerging professionals (aged 18 to 24).

MusiCares shared key findings from its 2026 Wellness in Music Survey on Tuesday (Nov. 10), revealing that financial instability, limited access to healthcare and persistent mental health challenges remain closely interconnected across the music industry.

More than 3,300 music professionals responded to this year’s survey in English and Spanish, representing every U.S. state and Puerto Rico and more than 40 professions across the industry. The annual survey examines more than 100 indicators, including financial security, physical and mental health, access to health insurance and preventive care, substance use and confidence in sustaining a career in music.

MusiCares’ takeaway from the study: “Taken together, the 2026 findings point to a need for the industry to look beyond individual wellness interventions and more closely examine the underlying conditions affecting whether music professionals can build healthy, sustainable careers.”

This year’s survey also included dedicated outreach to emerging music professionals aged 18 to 24 with support from Live Free 999, a nonprofit founded by Carmela Wallace in honor of her son, the late rapper Juice WRLD, offering a closer look at the challenges facing the next generation entering the industry.

Since its founding in 1989, MusiCares has distributed more than $146 million in financial assistance to the music community. Findings from the Wellness in Music Survey help MusiCares identify where needs are most urgent.

In recent years, survey findings have helped shape expanded services, including access to women’s and family care through Maven Clinic, increased education around cannabis use, and scaled preventive care services. Following last year’s Wellness in Music data showing persistently high rates of suicidal ideation among music professionals, MusiCares also expanded its mental health resources through Tune Into Mental Health, developed with The Jed Foundation (JED) to provide mental health and suicide prevention resources tailored specifically to the music community.

Between 46% and 50% of music professionals who responded to the survey earn less than $50,000 annually, while only 30% say they can comfortably cover their expenses through work in music. Finances remain the leading single source of stress, anxiety and depression across demographics.

Approximately 84% to 85% of music professionals who responded to the survey report having health insurance compared with 93% of the general U.S. population. Coverage is lower among Black and Latino music professionals who responded at 79% and 80.6%, respectively.

Even for those who are insured, high deductibles, costly out-of-pocket expenses and limited coverage can make it difficult to access the care they need. Among those without insurance, more than 60% cite affordability as the reason they do not have coverage. Preventive care access also differs sharply by insurance status. Nearly 80% of insured respondents reported receiving an annual exam compared with just one-third of those without coverage, with similar disparities across dental, vision, hearing and cancer screenings.

Despite increased attention on mental health, the proportion of music professionals using available care has not substantially changed. Over the last five years, the percentage of music professionals who responded to the survey who report having accessed therapy has remained relatively steady at 57% to 59%. Among those interested in therapy who have not received it, 45% cite an inability to pay as the primary barrier. Suicidal ideation among respondents has also consistently measured two to three times higher than the U.S. general population.

Within the overall survey sample, 203 respondents were emerging music professionals (defined as those aged 18 to 24). With the support provided by Live Free 999, MusiCares was able to analyze this group more closely to compare their experiences with those of the broader respondent pool. Among them, 68.8% earned $25,000 or less in the previous year and 86.4% earned $50,000 or less. Seventy-nine percent said they could not comfortably cover their expenses through work in music, compared with 70% of respondents overall, while 72.7% reported low or very low confidence in their ability to cover future expenses through music work alone.

Health coverage among this younger group presents another important consideration. While 89.7% of emerging professionals who responded to the survey currently have health insurance, 57.4% receive that coverage through a parent. MusiCares’ summary: “As these professionals age out of family coverage, many may enter an industry where employer-sponsored insurance is far from guaranteed, underscoring the need to help early-career music professionals prepare for health care costs and coverage as their careers evolve.”

Posted November 10, 2026 | Reviewed by Gary Drevitch

"I like my therapist, but sometimes I wish they would just ask me more open-ended questions."

An amazing colleague, who is also professional coach, recently said this to me. This colleague shared how her therapist is brilliant and helpful, but often shifts too quickly into problem-solving mode. I inquired if my colleague had ever given her therapist this feedback and she shook her head. I asked her what stopped her and she replied that she just never thought of doing so, but felt that her therapist would likely respond positively if she did. I encouraged her, in the same way that I encourage you, dear reader, to regularly be sharing feedback in your sessions.

Licensed therapists have years of training, including learning about evidence-based practices and getting supervised training in providing therapy. Your therapist shows up to your sessions with a lot of knowledge and skill that can help you recognize patterns, process significant life events, and so much more. But no matter how amazing your therapist is, one thing that they can never know, unless you tell them, is how helpful you find their therapeutic approach and the information that they’re sharing with you.

Therapists try to build a strong rapport with you, as they know that having a strong therapeutic alliance is important for you to feel safe and fully engage in your session. Research tells us that the stronger a therapist’s interpersonal skills are, the more likely their patients are to return for another session.1 Nevertheless, the therapist cannot know if you do not understand Information that they are trying to share with you, or if it just doesn’t feel aligned with your perspective, unless you tell them. You, your therapist, and your work together will benefit from this information.

It can feel awkward, giving feedback—especially if after reading this article, you’re the one who’s prompting such a discussion in your sessions. (Feel free to share this article as a jumping off point for discussion in your therapy session!)

If part of the reason that you don’t want to give feedback to your therapist is because you are afraid that they will stop liking you, that is also really important information that you need to share with your therapist. There could be many reasons for you feeling this way, and they are all really important to identify and discuss in therapy.

It’s normal for you to want other people to like you. This is especially true of your therapist, with whom you have shared a lot of sensitive information about yourself. Therapists have an ethical obligation to do no harm, to try to benefit you with their care, to build trust, practice fairly and justly, and respect the dignity and privacy of all people. This means that the therapist needs to be making decisions based on what they feel is healthiest for you and the therapeutic relationship. Therefore, even if your feedback is somehow given in such a way that your therapist could feel negatively about it, that is not a reason that you would not share it.

A therapists job is to take in the information that you’re sharing and to consider how the therapy session could be augmented to better meet your needs. They will not stop liking you if you provide constructive feedback, and if you perceived that were somehow to be the case, that would be a really important area for discussion and ultimately may signal that you and your therapist are no longer a good fit.

The feedback you provide can help the therapist engage in shared decision-making with you to see if you need a new treatment approach, if they need to refer you to a colleague so you can meet with someone who specializes in a different areas (e.g., couples counseling or EMDR sessions). This could mean engaging with different clinicians for different treatments at the same time or pausing your current treatment while receiving this care.

As a therapist, avoiding feedback doesn't mean you can assume a client is completely satisfied with the therapy: it more likely means you won't have a good understanding of the reasons why clients discontinue their sessions with you. Asking for feedback routinely normalizes the practice, helps you provide better care, and helps you learn and grow as a clinician so you can build your skills and continue benefiting your clients.

Bilt CEO Ankur Jain is betting AI and sports fandom can drive local spending—starting with free beers when the home team wins

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sports has never been bigger—team valuations are skyrocketing, fanbases are diehard, and the money in the industry is growing faster than it has before. Now Bilt, the online rewards platform, wants to make going to a sporting event about more than just what happens in the stadium.

The company is expanding what it calls a “neighborhood operating system,” allowing teams and local businesses to connect with fans through personalized rewards, loyalty programs, and AI according to a press release Tuesday.

The expansion is another step in Bilt’s effort to move beyond its origins as a rent-rewards platform and become an operating system for local commerce—under the proposed system, local restaurants and small businesses can use Bilt’s platform to pair with sports teams and drive local traffic through personalized rewards. The company has already partnered with multiple professional sports teams to make it happen—including the Phoenix Mercury, Phoenix Suns, Houston Comets, Houston Rockets, Houston Texans, Sacramento Kings, and Chicago Bears.

“We think of it as expanding game day into the neighborhood,” Bilt founder and CEO Ankur Jain told Fortune. Teams could offer fans restaurant reservations before games, transportation to stadiums and watch parties at nearby businesses—alongside exclusive discounts and other benefits, he said.

Bilt is also bringing sponsors into the equation. Jain described an arrangement with Constellation Brands, the company behind Corona, in which fans participating in a team’s loyalty program could receive a complimentary round of the beer at a local bar if their team wins. The sponsor would fund the offer directly through Bilt and the bar’s point-of-sale system without needing access to individual customers’ private information, Jain explained.

As pro sports have expanded past television screens in the modern day, they’ve become a source of community: fans congregating at sports bars, stadium tailgates, and camaraderie.

According to S&P Global Market Intelligence’s 2026 Consumer Insights survey in July, approximately 74% of Americans watch live sports. Of those fans, 53% of Americans watch the NFL or college football, 36% watch baseball or basketball, and 20% watch hockey.

And that large fanbase means larger commercial opportunities that can extend past ticket sales. Sports teams have created opportunities to monetize their audiences through merchandise, sponsorships, memberships, and experiences past game day.

The global sports market generated an estimated $463 billion in revenue in 2024 and is projected to reach $610 billion by 2028, according to a 2024 study from investment bank Houlihan Lokey. Sports-related travel alone generated $111.2 billion in direct spending in the U.S. in 2025 as 339 million people traveled to attend sporting events, according to the Sports Events and Tourism Association’s 2026 State of the Industry Report.

According to Deloitte’s 2026 Global Sports Industry Outlook, AI and changing ownership models converged with sports, media, and entertainment as major forces shaping the industry—which is “inclusive of leagues, teams, federations, mega-events, media partners, investors, and more,” according to the report. The firm highlighted the importance of sports venues as year-round commercial destinations.

“AI has outgrown its role as a trick play,” the report read. “For many sports organizations, it’s becoming a whole new game plan. Moving into 2026 and beyond, sports organizations looking to compete shouldn’t ignore AI.”

The rollout of the new platform also impacts small businesses, according to Bilt. Jain said AI is an integral part of making the system work, especially for small businesses that struggle to manage multiple software platforms.

Bilt’s “Neighborhood OS” is at the center of the new system that rewards users for visiting local businesses, an AI-powered platform helps local businesses attract customers, build loyalty, and grow without replacing their existing systems—“designed to unlock” a $6.6 trillion opportunity in “local commerce.” According to Bilt, approximately $6.6 trillion of annual U.S. retail and food-services spending occurs in person.

Bilt’s AI Agents have completed more than 2 million real-world actions across more than 29,000 businesses, with 91% completed without human intervention in the past 30 days, according to the company. In one example, Jain described a restaurant potentially using Bilt’s system to recognize returning customers, identify previous orders, provide special offers, and combine existing software into one app.

“To me, the way a lot of these technology companies are looking at AI, they’re all talking about digitizing everything and taking away jobs and living online,” Jain said. “But our belief is that the biggest beneficiaries of it will be small businesses who can now use AI to help them run their fragmented business.”

The Impact of a Psychopathic Mother

Posted November 10, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

When my children were young, I remember reading aloud children’s books that delighted both them and me. One book that especially comes to mind is Diane Goode’s Where’s Our Mama? 1 The author dedicated this book to her own mother, and her feelings for her mother are expressed through the two young children in the story. In the story, a gust of wind blew the mother’s hat off her head. When the mother went to fetch her hat, the two children became separated from her and began to cry. A policeman approached them as they searched for their mama and asked them to describe her. They quickly said she was the most beautiful and bravest person who always wears the prettiest hats, has a voice that everyone loves, and is the best cook in the world. Of course, they were all soon joyfully reunited.

As in the story, “mama” elicits an image of a loving, giving, selfless person. But some people never had such a mother. Many were born the children of psychopathic mothers. For those in such circumstances, what can they expect?

According to psychopathy experts, Paul Babiak and Robert D. Hare, in their book, Snakes in Suits, “Psychopaths may push the envelope of acceptable human behavior … . Some psychopaths live in society and do not technically break the law—although they may come close, with behavior that usually is very unpleasant for those around them. Some may lead seemingly normal lives, not hurting people in ways that attract attention, but causing problems nonetheless in hidden economic, psychological, and emotionally abusive ways. They do not make warm and loving parents, children, or family members.”2

Having had a mother and sister who were highly psychopathic, I had eye-opening experiences from when I was young. I noticed early on that both my mother and sister were different from other mothers and other children that I knew. But it took years before I was able to understand the term that described their personality disorder. Their behavior displayed the psychopathic traits of lovelessness and guiltlessness, which sociologists William and Joan McCord have characterized as fundamental to the disorder.3

In realizing this, I came to terms with the fact that my mother was the antithesis of the iconic mother. This realization has helped me to make sense of much of what I experienced and observed growing up. She consistently exhibited strange and unusual behavior. Previously, I could never understand why she hid and disregarded treasured family photographs. One day, I happened to find them hidden in a dark, clammy basement behind a piece of plywood on a cold, damp cement floor. They had not been placed there by accident. The age-old photographs were damaged by mildew and could not be replaced. When I asked her about it, she shrugged and was expressionless, showing her “I don’t care” attitude. She had no affection or regard for the warm memories the photos embodied. Instead, photographs of complete strangers were showcased in prominent places in the house for no apparent reason.

My mother had many strategies to get her way by creating conflicts and division between family members and relatives. I witnessed these conflicts all my life at different times and in many situations. One day, I happened to call my mother, only to find out that my niece had recently been married. My mother’s voice could not have sounded more delighted to tell me that I was the only person not invited. She perpetuated ill will and division. According to psychologist Neville Symington, “One of the most evident signs of psychopathy is the presence of confusion and bad feeling. One person is set against another and suspicion is rife, but the cause is never rooted out.”4

The brush-offs and the slights were countless, and the cold treatment never changed, even during a time of great personal distress. In such a time, even a stranger can sympathize with someone going through hardship. Instead, my mother and sister went to an astrologer to find out if my life would be ruined and, on a telephone call, they blurted out in high-pitched laughter exactly what the future had in store for me. They tried to get me to believe I would be ruined. I didn’t believe, and it never occurred, but their diabolical scheming was another sign of their disorder.

In dreaming of what a mother is, I came to grips with the fact that I never had a mother like "mama," and that my mother could never be trusted.

5 Years Later, One of Anime’s Biggest Mysteries Has Officially Been Solved

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Okarun's backstory is Dandadan's saddest and best chapter

It's hard to explain briefly why Dandadan is one of the best manga and anime that have come out in the past five years. The series' eclectic nature and mix of genres means it escapes easy categorization. "The author is a genius" and "the anime adaptation is fantastic" could be reductive when recommending it to someone. Luckily, chapter 252 of the Dandadan manga, published on Nov. 9, 2026, does a much better job of explaining why this series is a masterpiece.

One of Dandadan's oddities is that, until now, author Yukinobu Tatsu has refrained from showing his readers anything about the background and past of one of his two protagonists: the sweet, spectacled, alien-obsessed Ken Takakura, nicknamed Okarun. This did not hurt the series nor Okarun's characterization, which was developed through his interactions with the rest of the cast, especially co-protagonist Momo Ayase. Now, we finally know that Tatsu had a long-term vision for this character — and it's a brutal one.

This article contains mentions of suicide and bullying.

Spoilers for Dandadan beyond the anime follow.In the current Dandadan arc, Okarun has lost his supernatural powers after fulfilling his pact with Turbo Granny. The foul-mouthed yokai has disappeared, and Okarun was left without means to defend himself when new enemies attacked him, Momo, Aira, and Jiji during a trip to a theme park. Okarun had to rely on the Ogre Club, a mystical weapon provided by Momo's grandma, Seiko, but it turns out that the tool has unpleasant side effects. Okarun lost himself to rage and attacked friends and foes indiscriminately, until Turbo Granny made her triumphant return. In chapter 252, "Ken Takakura," the spirit enters Okarun's mind to bring him back to reason, and in the process, she witnesses the boy's traumatic past.

Ken Takakura's parents split up after his mom found a new partner. She asked little Ken if he wanted to go with her, but he decided to stay with his dad. In a gut-wrenching sequence, Okarun remembers realizing his mom was actually relieved he didn't follow her, since he could have spoiled her fancy new life. Things kept getting worse for young Ken from that moment onwards. He was beaten up by other kids, bullied, and sexually humiliated. Meanwhile, his father was crushed by debt and experienced angry outbursts, leaving poor Ken a lonely, traumatized child.

One day, as he was running away from some bullies, Ken found an occult magazine that became his lifeline. He hoped and prayed every day that aliens would one day show up to "save his heart." They didn't. Okarun kept getting bullied as he grew up, until he couldn't take it anymore. In another chilling sequence, Ken is shown on top of a building, ready to jump off. However, thinking about distant, happy memories with his family made him lose courage (or rather, he found the courage to live), and he broke down crying.

After that suicide attempt, Ken kept getting abused at school. In the present, the influence of the Ogre Club mixes his memories with nefarious thoughts of murder. However, a voice (likely Turbo Granny) tells him that he has forgotten something: His prayers were answered. Someone did show up. The chapter ends with a panel of Momo sitting in front of Okarun's desk, shielding him from the bullies, a callback to the manga's first chapter.

At the beginning of Dandadan, in the much-discussed scene where Momo and Okarun are held by the Serpoian aliens, the boy said that, as he didn't have any friends, he looked up to the skies every day hoping that the aliens would show up. But they never did. Instead, Momo stood up for him. At that moment, this motivated Okarun to fully transform into Turbo Granny, breaking the Serpoians' bonds. It was a pretty traditional setup for a story that mixes romance and action, and it was hard to predict that the author would wait five more years before revealing the true depths of his protagonist's trauma.

Choosing to withhold his main character's backstory that long is a bold move for Tatsu, proving that he has confidence in his storytelling and character work. It also recontextualizes the current arc, which saw Momo lose her recent memories, including those of Okarun, which left Ken struggling to remind her of the bond they shared. Now, fans know that Momo isn't just Okarun's first crush or the girl who stood up for him at school. She's literally the person who saved his life.

Bullying, abuse, and suicide are difficult themes that are often present in manga and anime, but they're not always treated with the sensitivity required. Dandadan's long-term storytelling set up a big reveal about Okarun's traumatic past, but Tatsu has already shown that, despite his talent for action and comedy, he's not afraid to touch on other, more difficult themes. Vamola's backstory was, until now, one of the best and most traumatic parts of the manga (and one that I can't wait to see adapted in the anime). Okarun's backstory hits those same powerful emotional notes that make Dandadan a masterpiece and his author one of the best mangaka currently active.

Sundrop turns your endless baby photos into recaps, time-lapses and more — without AI

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

New parents take a lot of pictures of their children, but those photos often end up languishing in photo galleries, never to be seen again after the initial snap and share.

A new app, Sundrop, aims to make those pictures more useful by turning them into weekly recaps, highlights, time-lapses of the child growing up, and other cute scenes, like putting the child’s photo on a magazine cover or a rookie card.

While Google Photos and Apple Photos already provide extensive tools to organize and manage photos, Sundrop is taking aim at a particular niche: Parents with a new baby (usually their first!) who take an overwhelming number of photos and want to do more with those pictures.

The app was developed over the past few months by the same team behind the read-it-later app, Matter: Ben Springwater, Sky Lan, and Rob Mackenzie (all dads themselves).

Notably, Sundrop claims it is designed to be private, and processes all photos locally on the device it is installed on. To make this possible, Sundrop taps into Apple’s Vision framework and on-device Core ML (machine learning) models instead of uploading photos to a cloud server or some other hosted AI service.

Both of the child’s parents can use Sundrop together by linking their accounts, which allows the app to sync their shared photos via Apple’s iCloud service. This feature, the company says, means the pictures aren’t sent to any external servers it runs.

In addition, the team notes that no generative AI is used in the creation of its scenes or highlights.

This differentiates it from many photo editing apps on the market, including Google’s, which are increasingly tapping generative AI to create new versions of your photos.

To find highlights and recaps, Sundrop scans pictures in the parents’ respective Camera Rolls on their iPhones, skipping things like screenshots or duplicates. It then creates highlights or fun scenes, which it calls “drops.”

One of these is a recap of the week every Sunday, announced as a Lock Screen notification. Others can put two siblings side-by-side at the same age, generate a time-lapse series of photos or a video of a kid growing up, or cut out a photo of the child and place it into a layered template.

Parents can also manually edit the photos Sundrop uses to make its creations, or use the app to automatically organize the best photos into galleries with other similar shots.

The app costs $39.99 per year, and offers a week-long free trial. The annual plan also includes Family Sharing, so only one parent needs to purchase it.

Half of New Games on Steam Are Now Made With AI. How Does This Reflect the Gaming Industry?

Steam, the digital marketplace for PC games from game studio Valve, has hit a major milestone, sparking pushback from parts of the gaming community: Approximately 50% of new Steam games in October were made with the help of AI.

This underscores a growing trend in gaming, where more games are made with some form of AI assistance — specifically, generative AI, which developers have used to generate code, art assets, voice lines and more. Developers are adopting the technology in increasing numbers.

The numbers come from a Reddit user who goes by Ploz, and who used Steam’s publicly available data to see how many newly released games relied on AI in some capacity. This was possible because of Valve’s AI disclosure policy for newly released games, which requires developers to state whether they used AI in making their game. The disclosure is part of the storefront’s publicly accessible records, allowing anyone to view it.

A Valve representative did not respond to a request for comment.

The data is a snapshot of the tidal wave of AI-made games flooding storefronts, most prominently Steam. But crucially, Valve has chosen not to limit this flood, washing its hands of the problem and leaving gamers to fend for themselves in finding quality games. Developers had already been struggling to get their games noticed, and Valve’s limited curation seems completely unprepared for the firehose of AI-made games churned out on an increasing scale.

Valve implemented the AI disclosure policy in January 2024 to increase developer transparency. At the time, only 1.4% of newly released games used AI. This increased to 13.4% by the end of 2024, then to 24% by the end of 2025. It now sits at 49.9%.

There are some caveats. The AI disclosure is only required for games that use AI to create player-facing content, such as artwork, sound, character dialogue and localization. The 49.9% figure is specific to these types of games and doesn’t account for developers who use AI for behind-the-scenes tasks such as coding and bug fixes. A Google Cloud survey conducted in August 2025 showed that 87% of developers use AI for backend work, which doesn’t require disclosure on Steam.

So it’s likely that the number of games on Steam built with AI assistance is greater than 50%. Ploz notes that the only way users can avoid AI-generated games is to manually check for AI disclosures.

According to Ploz, the most common use of AI in game development is for artwork, which was mentioned in 63% of disclosures. Text and translations gobbled up 30%, while music (21%), code (16%) and voices (7%) rounded out the list. Many developers had more than one of those areas named in their disclosure, and about a quarter of developers said they were using AI for their Steam store pages and marketing.

About 3% of developers used AI directly as a game mechanic. One such example is a driving simulator called Teach My Little Sister How to Drive. Developers use AI in the game to interpret a player’s voice commands and generate the sister’s responses. To keep a playable demo of the game available to the public, the development team, Easy Fox, is paying $1,000 every day just to cover these AI operating costs — an expense so high that it was forced to take out a loan simply to keep the demo running.

For development, there are loads of options. The Unity 6 engine came out in October 2024 and includes AI code assistance, AI asset generation and third-party integration with Claude Code, Gemini and Grok. Anthropic’s Claude is a fairly popular option among smaller developers, since it supports the ability to vibe code whole games, with ChatGPT, Gemini and GitHub Copilot as other options.

Many of the games being released with the AI disclosure on Steam can be classified as slop — a derogatory term gamers have applied liberally to titles that heavily lean on generative AI for development and look derivative of other popular games. According to Ploz, most AI-enabled games are small: Around 47% of new games with fewer than 10 reviews have an AI disclosure. Among developers who released more than 10 games in a year, 68% of those games included the disclosure.

Games with AI disclosures received fewer positive reviews on average than those without. And among all newly released games with more than 1,000 reviews, only 17% include an AI disclosure. That means gamers are still gravitating toward games without an AI disclosure and pushing back against games that use the technology.

Developers seem aware of this. CNET reached out to two dozen well-known developers, including Epic Games, EA, Activision Blizzard, Ubisoft, PlayStation Studios and many others. All of them declined to comment.

Bigger studios may be using AI in their development, but it’s hard to tell the scale. There’s no such thing as an AI audit yet, and the industry seems to be relying on voluntary disclosure for now. Steam’s disclosure policy has outed smaller developers who are either worried about violating the policy and being taken off the digital storefront, or aren’t worried about the negative effects of disclosure.

A lot of dominoes had to fall for this to happen. The availability of AI tools and the increasing ease of use are definite contributors. Subreddits like r/AIGameDev have plenty of examples of people putting together games with just a few days or weeks of vibe coding, which is a lot shorter than the standard video game development cycle, which can stretch over years depending on the scale, production level and graphics quality. This practice has exploded over the last couple of years, resulting in a lot of games getting launched that are made mostly or entirely with AI.

Many of these games end up on Steam, which doesn’t have a policy limiting AI-developed games.

“Digital storefronts like Steam have already struggled to moderate low-effort and poor-quality games, as well as copycats stealing other games’ ideas,” said CNET Managing Editor David Lumb.

Valve openly admits its role in this, stating that the problem definitely exists but that the company doesn’t “want to be gatekeepers telling developers which tools they should or shouldn’t use.”

It takes a similar stance on AI cloning, with Valve’s Tom Giardino telling developers that “you must continue to grow your game by adding features, personality and creativity that stem from your own perspective as a developer.”

These policies essentially allow any AI-developed game or clone to reach the Valve storefront without pushback, which certainly contributes to the flood of newly released games with some form of player-facing AI involvement in their development.

Rival store Epic Games has a similar stance, allowing basically any game that wants to be there. The company declined to comment further about this, but CEO Tom Sweeney is an avid supporter of AI in video game development, stating that it was “perfectly logical” for developers to rely on AI to handle the mundane grunt work that takes up most of a developer’s time.

While Steam and Epic Games tend to take a more lax attitude toward AI slop, other storefronts like Itch.io are not. Itch.io has a whole policy against AI slop and the company has willingly, and at times gleefully, deleted terabytes of AI slop from its storefront. It also requires developers to disclose if they used AI but takes a much more consumer-friendly approach by letting users filter those out of search results if they don’t want to see them.

Some measures are being taken to address the proliferation of AI slop, but none will entirely stop the problem. Cloning has been a problem for many years. Mobile gamers of a certain vintage may remember the flock of Flappy Bird clones that appeared on app stores after the original game went down, and those weren’t even made with AI.

AI makes cloning much easier and faster, and some AI companies are addressing it. Anthropic recently changed Claude Opus 5.5 to prevent it from generating copies of existing games. Anthropic engineering leader Addy Osmani posted on X that using Claude to copy games infringes on intellectual property rights and violates Anthropic’s policy.

However, during CNET’s testing, we found we could still make games resembling popular titles, so cloning remains possible with a little creativity.

“This has come up over and over on the subreddit r/GameDev, and there’s no real solution if Steam and other digital storefronts don’t stem the flood of low-quality AI games drowning out the labors of love that entertain and innovate,” Lumb said.

Many developers openly oppose AI and refuse to use it in their own work. However, as the Steam stats show, that crowd’s output appears to be getting smaller and smaller every year amid the unending stream of AI games.

It was too much. This year, the town built an electric fence around the site, hoping the bears would move on. They didn’t. Some started coming into town looking for food. Others just hung around the fence, searching for a way in.

Inuvik, population about 3,500, was the first community in that part of the Arctic to try to wean the bears off their dependence on dump food with such a fence, which cost about $700,000. The problem was, the bears had not learned to depend on any other source of food.

“We’ve probably had three to four generations of bears that have been using that landfill,” said local mayor Peter Clarkson. In the end, the town took a drastic solution — but one that was probably unavoidable in the circumstances: They shot 28 bears.

Tragic though this is, officials hope that within the next couple of years, the remaining bears in the area will move on and learn to eat more natural fare.

Don't throw away your old phone box — you might need it in an emergency

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

That cardboard box in your closet can come in handy more often than you’d think.

There's a specific type of anxiety that comes with deciding what to do with a box. Do you immediately throw it away? Do you stash it in a closet with about 20 other boxes that store little more than your own guilt? If you chuck it, you might need it for some unknown purpose in the future. But if you don't, that box might just sit in your home indefinitely.

It doesn't help when it's a nice box, either, like the type your phone might have arrived in. It's likely made of thick cardboard, with convenient compartments. Luckily, or perhaps unluckily for those who have finally gathered the nerve to go on a recycling tear, there are a few ways to help you decide the fate of your phone's box. Whether you're ready to resell your phone or need to report a stolen one, here's why you might want to keep your phone's box.

Hopefully, you and your phone will never part ways without your consent. However, if it does get stolen, you'll need its IMEI (International Mobile Equipment Identity) number. An IMEI is a 15- 17-digit number unique to your phone. Carriers can use it to identify the device and block it if it's lost or stolen. AT&T describes it as your phone's "fingerprint."

One place to check for your IMEI number is the box your phone came in. You should also be able to find it through your Apple or Google account, depending on the device you have. Still, in the heat of the moment, grabbing that box from a drawer might be the easiest option.

Some less stressful scenarios might also lead you to keep your phone box. If you purchased an iPhone from Apple directly, for example, you have 14 days to return it from the day you received it. It must also be in the original packaging. Likewise, Samsung requires original packaging. Other retailers might have different policies, so at the very least, it's a good idea to check before you trash the box. Or keep it until after the window has closed, just to be safe.

Additionally, if you're thinking of reselling your phone at some point down the line, having its original box could come in handy. For one, you might have saved additional cables or something like a SIM ejector tool that a new owner will want. And while there's no hard data, some experts believe that having that original box can boost your odds of selling your phone at a good price.

Consumer Reports suggests the box increases the market value of the item and shows that you've taken good care of it. If you saw two identical iPhones for the same price, but only one was in a box, would you really choose the one without the box? The article also notes that you'll already have a box if you need to ship your phone.

So, if you're waffling on the eternal question of whether to save your phone's box, you can consider if any of those situations apply to you. And if not, there's likely a recycling bin somewhere waiting for its next deposit.

Brand may well have gotten away with it had it not been for a joint investigation by Channel 4 documentary strand “Dispatches” and The Sunday Times of London. Their reporting, published in Sept. 2023, contained numerous serious allegations, including that the former comedian had sexually assaulted four women between 2006 and 2013. Brand denied any wrongdoing (none of the women who made the allegations in the documentary were involved in the recent criminal trial although it is understood the Met Police are now investigating at least one of those complaints and Brand may face yet more charges).

The former comedian, who’d appeared in Hollywood movies and had a short-lived marriage to Katy Perry at the height of her success, had long cultivated an air of degeneracy as part of his persona, enabling him to operate in plain sight. Yet it wasn’t until the Channel 4/Sunday Times investigation was published that police opened an investigation. Whether it’s because the reporting had prompted more women to come forward – as Brand’s lawyer in the trial, Ian Winter, repeatedly argued — or because the media spotlight gave the authorities the resolution they needed to investigate a celebrity will probably never be known.

But Brand is not the only celebrity to have found themselves in the dock after being accused of wrongdoing in the media. In 2023 news anchor Huw Edwards, once the best-known and highest paid journalist at the BBC, was the subject of an exposé by The Sun, who revealed he had been in an inappropriate text-based relationship with a teenager during which he’d paid for nude photographs.

Although he had not acted unlawfully in that instance, Edwards was arrested less than a year later and eventually convicted of making indecent images after police found videos of children as young as seven being abused on Edwards’ phone. None have resulted in any finding of liability or criminality and Spacey has always denied any allegations of sexual misconduct but his career has never recovered. In the U.K. Spacey was, like Brand, tried at Southwark Crown Court, where he was on trial for nine charges of sexual assault against four separate men. He was acquitted on all counts. After the trial, some of the jurors asked to meet the Oscar-winner and shake his hand.

So it’s unsurprising that David Walliams, a comedian, bestselling children’s author and former “Britain’s Got Talent” judge, sought an injunction last month to prevent the same newspaper from publishing an investigation into his alleged sexual misconduct. Lawyers for Walliams told London’s High Court he would be likely to commit suicide if the story was published. In his sentencing remarks, Justice Bennathan accepted the former comedian’s fame was “an intrinsic part of each offence” as it allowed him to carry out his crimes. But, he noted, “I also need to be careful I am not punishing you more because you are famous.”

Coddled by their managers, obeyed by their assistants and adored by their fans, some celebrities have been able to abuse their exalted status. But when an intrepid journalist turns that spotlight to expose alleged misconduct, whether criminal or otherwise, fame can also be their undoing.

U.S. Marine Calls Alaska Airlines Employee “Anti-Military” After Being Denied A Free Upgrade

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A United States Marine Corps member says an Alaska Airlines employee was “anti-military” after declining his request for a free extra-legroom upgrade. He also alleges that the employee was rude and accused him of lying about previous upgrades. Those are separate complaints, and I think they deserve separate answers. Everyone deserves courteous service. Military service, however, does not entitle someone to a better seat.

The Instagram algorithim placed Taylor Yontz at the top of my feed this morning, where he describes an encounter at Portland International Airport. According to his account, he saw roughly 30 unassigned seats on the seat map and requested a complimentary move to an extra-legroom seat. He says he is 6 feet 4 inches tall, regularly makes this request, and receives an upgrade about nine times out of 10.

This time, he says, the employee told him free military upgrades were against policy. He alleges the agent spoke with disgust and accused him of lying when he mentioned previous complimentary upgrades. The employee offered a paid move, which he declined at $150.

Alaska publishes a substantial list of military and veteran benefits. These include:

Complimentary upgrades based solely on military service are not among the listed benefits. So at the outset, there is no policy for upgrading service members when space is available, which even Yontz concedes.

I don’t blame him for asking, especially because he is 6’4″. Sitting in economy class is difficult enough for an average-sized person…it’s brutal for someone that tall.

But “it can’t hurt to ask” needs to leave room for the employee to decline. Otherwise, it becomes a test the agent can pass only by giving something away. Yontz strikes me as highly-entiteld to suggest that he should be given this upgrade because he has in the past, as if it’s standard procedure.

We’ll talk about the Alaska Airlines employee below, but by arguing that you’ve been upgraded in the past and therefore you should today, entitlement is on full display.

If an agent actually accused him of lying about previous upgrades, I would find that inappropriate (I’m not convinced, but it is certainly possible). The employee should have politely explained the current policy without assumptions about past interaction the passenger has had with the airline.

There is no contradiction between “another employee upgraded me before” and “I cannot provide that upgrade today” and Alaska should examine his complaint about insulting language on its own merits.

But even if the employee handled the conversation poorly, that would establish poor service, not necessarily hostility toward military members. And even if it was one agent being hostile (again, certainly possible), it’s still a different matter than whether the upgrade should have been denied.

I appreciate the sacrifices military members and their families make. Airlines can recognize those sacrifices through practical benefits, and Alaska offers several. But I hate to see that appreciation reduced to whether an airport employee hands over a seat upgrade…Yontz’s conduct is unbecoming of a member of the U.S. military (and I was one too, thank you very much).

If one agent makes an exception, the next agent should not face an accusation of prejudice for following the published rules. That creates an incentive to stop offering discretionary favors altogether. Accepting a kind gesture with gratitude leaves room for someone else to receive one later. Treating it as the minimum acceptable response makes it harder for employees to exercise generosity without creating expectations their colleagues must then manage.

The practical result of this whole brouhaha should be a memo to gate agents that no one is to be upgraded for free.

If the Alaska Airlines employee was insulting, the passenger has every reason to complain about that conduct. He did not need to receive an upgrade to deserve a respectful conversation.

But declining a complimentary extra-legroom seat does not, on its own, make an employee anti-military. Alaska’s published benefits do not promise that upgrade, and previous exceptions do not change that.

To all travelers: ask politely, enjoy the upgrade if it happens, but if it does not, a declined upgrade is not evidence that someone disrespects you, no matter what uniform you wear.

The US Supreme Court issued an unsigned decision on Monday allowing the Trump administration to resume building border barriers and installing surveillance equipment in the Big Bend region of southwestern Texas, including inside Big Bend National Park.

The ruling lifts a temporary halt on construction ordered in early October by US District Judge Kathleen Cardone in El Paso. While litigation over the legality of the 500-mile border project continues in the lower courts, construction crews can now move forward with work. Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson dissented from the decision.

For visitors and locals in the remote desert region, the physical ramifications of the project began appearing over the summer. In August, construction crews brought bulldozers near Santa Elena Canyon (one of Big Bend National Park’s most famous landmarks) and graded more than a mile of land. According to administration officials, Customs and Border Protection plans to install four- to six-foot-tall vehicle barriers, surveillance gear, and an access road in the area.

Opponents of the construction, which include a bipartisan mix of Texas ranchers, local landowners, and conservationists, warn that barriers will permanently alter the desert. Critics say the project threatens to cut off access to the river, disrupt livestock and crops, and interfere with the area’s status as the largest dark sky reserve on the planet.

Legal opposition to the project began in April when a coalition consisting of an environmental organization, a river guide, a church preservation group, and local landowners filed a lawsuit to stop the project. The coalition argued that federal officials had exceeded their authority by waiving environmental and conservation laws, such as the National Environmental Policy Act and the Endangered Species Act, without approval from Congress.

In early October, Judge Cardone temporarily blocked the construction after finding that the coalition was likely to succeed on at least one of its legal claims. After Judge Cardone denied the administration’s request to pause her order during an appeal, the government filed an emergency application with the Supreme Court, describing the lower court’s block as “baseless.”

The federal government argued that halting work would leave a gap of more than 300 miles in border defenses, which he said could draw cartels and other bad actors.

Still, the coalition urged the justices to keep the block in place. They argued the administration sought emergency relief to immediately restart bulldozing, blasting, and road building across an ecologically and culturally sensitive landscape, warning that continued work would permanently damage historic resources and private ranch lands.

In Monday’s order, the Supreme Court majority acknowledged the plaintiffs’ argument that construction in the Big Bend Sector would “diminish their ability to use and enjoy the area.” However, the majority concluded that federal law grants the Homeland Security secretary the power to waive conservation and environmental statutes, noting that the law shows “a clear preference for expeditious border construction over the very harms” cited by the challengers.

The decision marks the first major policy victory for President Trump at the Supreme Court following last week’s midterm elections, as the underlying lawsuit proceeds in the lower court.

Your driving record may be spotless, but if you have poor credit, car insurance companies view you as a risk — and they’re significantly raising your rates.

Over the past three years, drivers with poor credit have seen auto insurance premiums surge 37.7%, according to NerdWallet’s 2026 Auto Insurance Rates Report. These price hikes were even steeper than those faced by drivers with good credit who caused a crash or got a DUI.

In January 2023, someone with poor credit and a clean driving record would pay a median of $2,864 per year for full-coverage car insurance, NerdWallet’s analysis found. By January 2026, premiums for this driver had risen to $3,944 — an increase of $1,081 per year. By comparison, the same driver with good credit saw an increase of only $576.

“Credit scores are one of the most harmful things that results in you paying higher auto insurance premiums,” says Michael DeLong, a research and advocacy associate with the Washington, D.C.-based nonprofit Consumer Federation of America. “The penalties are extremely severe.”

While higher premiums after a wreck or DUI may not be a surprise, DeLong says many drivers aren’t aware their credit scores will affect their car insurance rates. To further complicate things, building credit can take months.

But there are ways to get relief more quickly. DeLong suggests comparing car insurance quotes from multiple companies, because some insurers penalize poor credit less than others. Raising your deductible, evaluating your coverage and avoiding small claims can also help.

NerdWallet’s analysis found that insurers raised rates more sharply from 2023 to 2026 for drivers with poor credit than for those with good credit — even if the poor-credit driver had a flawless record while the good-credit driver had a DUI, speeding ticket or at-fault crash.

When our sample driver with poor credit also had a recent DUI or speeding ticket, rate increases were even greater. Prices for a full-coverage policy went up by more than 40% for drivers with poor credit and a recent DUI, the biggest increase of any profile we analyzed.

Drivers often think it’s unfair for credit to affect car insurance prices, DeLong says, since it isn’t directly linked to driving behavior. Some states agree; California, Hawaii and Massachusetts don’t allow credit as a factor in setting auto insurance rates.

Working to build your credit over time may help lower your car insurance premiums, but it’s not a sure thing. A credit-based insurance score is just one of many factors (along with things like location, age and driving history) that insurers use to rate a person’s risk.

Building credit “can produce a noticeable premium decrease in some cases, while producing little or no change in others,” Niami said, depending on whether the new score causes an insurer to move you into a better rating tier. Each insurer sets its own score thresholds, so a score viewed as “poor” by one insurer might be considered “fair” by another.

At the same time, consumer advocates argue it’s difficult for many people to take the steps needed to strengthen their credit scores.

“Most people probably have some idea that you can improve your credit score by trying to pay your bills regularly and reliably, and using different kinds of financial resources and credit cards,” DeLong says.

“But again, that’s easier said than done," he says. "If you’re struggling at a low-wage job and trying to juggle bills, or if you don’t easily have access to a credit card, that advice may not be especially helpful."

Drivers with poor credit or no credit may be able to take other steps to lower their premiums.

Comparing car insurance quotes from different companies is one of the best ways to ensure you have the cheapest rate possible. Insurers set their own pricing formulas, and some are more forgiving of poor credit than others. A NerdWallet analysis of October 2026 rates found that in about half (49.3%) of U.S. ZIP codes in states that allow credit scoring, the cheapest insurer for a driver with poor credit was a different company than the cheapest for a driver with good credit.

Although you can’t improve your risk profile overnight, “there’s a bunch of small things you can do that over a two- to three-year span make you look a lot better” to insurers, says Bradley Flowers, founder of Portal Insurance agency in Mobile, Alabama.

Consider a higher deductible. Raising your car insurance deductible — the amount of a future claim that you agree to cover yourself — lowers your premium and signals to insurers that you’re less likely to file a claim. But be sure you can afford to pay the higher deductible if you have to make a claim.

Adjust how you’re billed by insurers. By signing up for automatic payments and ensuring you don’t miss insurance payments each month, you’ll get a small discount while improving your risk profile for insurers.

Avoid minor claims if you can. Flowers recommends thinking twice about filing minor claims, such as windshield cracks. “When you’re in one of these situations, let’s save our insurance for the really bad stuff,” he suggests.

Lastly, be sure your coverage fits your vehicle. If you could afford to replace your car on your own, consider dropping collision and comprehensive coverage to bring your premiums down. Your trimmed-down policy would still provide you with liability coverage after a crash, while leaving you to cover damage to your vehicle.

In late October, we got our annual Halloween push of ducks here in the Midwest, and a friend and I timed it just right. We set up on a big agricultural retention pond with winds gusting to 30 mph. I threw out three dozen goose floaters, and we set another dozen full bodies in the shallows. We added a few mallard blocks right at the water’s edge in the landing hole in front of the blind. It’s best to put ducks upwind of geese, as geese don’t like flying over ducks, while ducks will fly over geese.

The first ducks in the spread were greenwings, and we shot a pair. Not long after, a big group of mallards worked us, then dropped into the hole. And so it went, until an hour and a half later, I called the shot on a pair of greenheads at ten yards. We stood up, the blind blew down, and we shot our last two ducks of the day.

We added a couple of geese and passed on the shovelers and ruddies that came into the spread. A flock of bluebills landed at the edge of the geese, but they were too bunched up for a clean shot. The point is, everything liked the goose spread that morning. We had a nice bag of mallards, a couple of teal, a gadwall, and some geese. It proved that a spread of goose floaters can work on ducks just as well as a spread of goose silos and full bodies does in a dry field. And adding floaters to your collection of goose decoys lets you target geese on ponds and rivers, where there may be less competition than in dry fields. If you still aren’t convinced, here are four reasons big Canada floaters will improve your success on ducks and geese.

It doesn’t take a huge decoy spread to fool geese over water. Sometimes, as few as four decoys can be enough to pull geese on small ponds or marshes. Plus, geese feel comfortable enough coming to water that even mediocre (that’s putting it kindly) callers like me can honk and cluck a few times to get their attention, and then let the decoys do the work.

The best time and place to hunt geese over water is a loafing spot, which can be whatever pond, marsh, or stretch of river birds go to after breakfast. Find one of these loafing spots and hunt it at “goose time,” which starts about 8:30 and runs through the middle of the day. There’s no need to get up early to hunt a loafing spot, and shooting the roost in the evening is generally frowned upon, as it might move birds out of the area for good. Save a late afternoon roost shoot for the last day of the season. If you’re after a mixed bag of ducks and geese, however, set the alarm early.

If you’ve gotten this far, you’re probably a serious waterfowler, and I don’t have to explain the adrenaline rush of ducks and geese decoying from high altitudes. This leads me to my next point on why you should use goose floaters: geese will lock up as dots in the sky and drop down to the water as if on a giant, invisible escalator, in a way they rarely do in fields. I remember honking at a bunch of four passing over from behind me and seeing them stand on their wingtips, drop almost to the water, spin, and come into the decoys, barely skimming the surface as they crossed the pond. It was a sight mesmerizing enough that I turned what should have been an easy triple into a single bird on the water. Watching geese decoy over water is more fun than watching them decoy in dry fields.

You will shoot far more ducks over goose decoys than you will ever shoot geese over duck decoys. Up to a 90/10 goose-to-duck decoy ratio can work surprisingly well on ducks, and it pulls more geese than a few goose floaters with a large duck spread will. Goose decoys are big, highly visible, and serve as a confidence bird for other waterfowl. This makes your spread not only more appealing and visible to ducks, but also a more secure place for them to land.

Shanghai next? City’s art scene leans into an uncertain future

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Since 2014, West Bund Art & Design (shown in 2025) has expanded to include multiple venues Courtesy West Bund Art & Design

Shanghai is a restless city. Its impatient hustle may spare its art scene from the plateau of middle age as it navigates a crisis of transition after the Covid years derailed its late 2010s momentum. Going into Shanghai’s annual art week (10-15 November), with two established fairs anchoring an explosion of art events across the city, its galleries, artists and institutions are assessing how the city’s art market can weather China’s economic slowdown and whether the cultural momentum of the past three decades can be sustained.

“Shanghai has so many galleries, so many museums, so many eccentric little spaces that you could come in the middle of summer and still see art non-stop,” says Lorenz Helbling, who founded ShanghArt in 1996. One of the first contemporary art galleries in China, it now has locations in Shanghai, Beijing and Singapore. The Swiss transplant says the volume of artistic activity in the city of 25 million people can be compared to New York’s, though the scene can be harder to find. “You can make a lot of fun things here.”

The city’s two large-scale fairs, Art021 Shanghai (11-14 November) and West Bund Art & Design (10-15 November), established in 2013 and 2014 respectively, set the barometer and prime the mood. So far, Shanghai’s core galleries have largely survived China’s property crash and deflationary economy, but they need young artists and new collectors to continue buoying their businesses.

Lise Li, who founded Shanghai’s Vanguard Gallery in 2004, says the city is “still in an upward phase, just growing much more slowly than before”. During art week, Li’s gallery will participate in the second iteration of the boutique fair Hangover Shanghai (11-15 November) and in Art021, while showing the artist Tang Chao in the gallery and collaborating on a group show at the artist-run 33ml Offspace. Several artists represented by Vanguard are having big moments nearby: Liao Fei will simultaneously be showing at Hangzhou’s Imagokinetics and the artist group known as Jiū Society has an exhibition at Nanjing’s Beiqiu Museum of Contemporary Art.

Established in 2013, Art021 Shanghai (pictured in 2025) is one of the most successful art fairs in the city Photo: Lisa Movius

Though the buzz of the 2010s has quieted, Li says, “there are quite a few more art institutions, and more newcomers are getting involved. The number of young artists is increasing rapidly; we see a lot of new faces every year. But most exhibitions are relatively conservative, which is rather a pity.”

The market has “changed considerably” compared to what now “might be called the ‘golden 15 years’ of China’s contemporary art market: the shift in the broader economic environment over the past few years has been impossible to ignore”, Otto Neu says. In 2022 he co-founded Nan Ke Gallery, which has a group show about art and literature on view during the fair week. “For artists and galleries alike, it is certainly a challenging moment. We probably have to work twice as hard as we did before simply to survive and keep things moving.”

Change, Neu adds, does not have to be negative. He cites the Chinese expression “great waves wash away the sand and leave the gold behind”, adding that “difficult periods can force an industry to become more focused, more rigorous and perhaps more honest about what really matters. In that sense, this may also be an important stage of maturation for Shanghai’s art scene.”

In addition to this month’s two international art fairs, Shanghai’s many resident artists—ranging from established figures like Ding Yi, Zhang Enli and Yang Fudong to mid-career and emerging talents—can rely on year-round programming at a healthy variety of homegrown and imported galleries. Western outfits like Almine Rech, Perrotin and Lisson maintain spaces in Shanghai, as do Asian powerhouses including Japan’s Ota Fine Arts and Korea’s Arario Gallery. ShanghArt has been joined by several influential local galleries, among them Antenna Space, Gallery Vacancy, Capsule Gallery and Don Gallery.

Major state-backed museums like the Power Station of Art, Museum of Art Pudong and West Bund Museum (which is partnered with the Centre Pompidou in Paris) have a complement of private museums that have proliferated since the 2010s. The Rockbund Art Museum, Long Museum and Yuz Museum are the best known, but smaller, newer, specialised or further-flung institutions like Fotografiska and the Museum of Glass, and non-profit spaces like Pond Society, Longlati and Prada Rong Zhai, have also enriched the Shanghai art ecosystem.

Vanguard Gallery’s Li credits Shanghai’s “open-city vibe” and its large base of collectors and visitors, along with the museums, galleries and artists, plus a citizenry curious “about fashion and new things, a strong history of business, and a diverse cultural structure. The main downside is that the cost of living is too high.” The lack of art information platforms, even in Chinese, is the other main challenge, she says.

The sense of openness is what drew the collector Cherry Xu to open her art-meets-fashion space Cheruby in the city last year. With new initiatives, “the boundaries between artists, designers, musicians and people working in fashion are becoming increasingly blurred”, she says, opening the art world to new audiences. “This kind of natural crossover between different fields is something very particular to Shanghai,” Xu says. “Art here doesn’t operate as an isolated system; it is constantly in conversation with design, music, fashion, technology and a broader youth culture, with these different communities influencing one another.”

Compared to Beijing’s art scene, Neu says, Shanghai’s creative community maintains a “healthy sense of distance. There is a certain independence between galleries, institutions, artists and collectors, which has shaped a rather distinctive ecosystem here”, a happy, pragmatic chaos of sorts. Beijing’s art scene is built on personal connections, Neu says, while in Shanghai, “between institutions, between galleries and even between galleries and collectors, there is often an understanding that business is business, friendship is friendship, and collaboration does not necessarily require complicated personal obligations or relationships”.

However, Neu adds that those divisions could become vulnerabilities in the current economic upheaval. “Perhaps this is precisely the moment when Shanghai’s art community needs to become a little more connected and cohesive than it has been in the past—not by giving up its independence, but by finding better ways to support one another,” he says.

Helbling says that Beijing is “more split up into scenes which work on their own”, while Shanghai has various circles that interact and are more permeable. He likens the city’s art community to its fairly compact geography, with the art neighbourhoods all within comparably easy reach of each other.

Twenty years ago, Shanghai was the upstart artistic challenger to Beijing, the political capital home to China’s leading art academy. Hong Kong’s concurrent cultural rise proved complimentary to Shanghai’s, and many in Shanghai say the expanding art activity in Korea, Japan and Singapore—as well as in mainland Chinese cities like Chengdu, Shenzhen and nearby Nanjing, Hangzhou and Suzhou—have been net positives for the local scene.

“This situation didn’t happen overnight; it’s the result of years of collaboration between Shanghai’s art institutions, galleries and fairs, as well as the collectors and artists living here,” Li says. “It’s not simply due to fairs or art weeks alone. The rise of art in new Chinese cities has also become developed, likely influenced by Shanghai and Hong Kong.”

Shanghai “was one of the earliest cities in China to develop a contemporary art market, and its relatively open culture and international outlook have given it a particular advantage”, Neu says. “Whether we are talking about collecting, aesthetic awareness or public understanding of contemporary art, Shanghai has accumulated more than two decades of experience and audience development. That kind of cultural infrastructure takes time to build.”

He adds that growing art scenes in other mainland cities represent “something very positive for Shanghai rather than a threat. A stronger regional art ecosystem ultimately creates more audiences, more conversations and more opportunities for everyone. Healthy competition and exchange between cities can only make the broader Chinese art scene more dynamic.”

Regionally, Xu says, Shanghai “has a very distinct local culture, its own audiences and creative communities, [while] its relationship with other Asian cities is becoming much more direct. It feels increasingly natural for artists, curators and other creative practitioners to live and work across different cities in Asia, and for us to look to what is happening in Tokyo, Seoul, Hong Kong, Singapore, Bangkok and elsewhere in the region, rather than instinctively treating Europe and the US as the only points of reference.” For Shanghai’s art week, Cheruby is showing a solo exhibition by the multidisciplinary Thai artist Tanat Teeradakorn.

“Shanghai has an interesting place within this network,” Xu says. “It has a relatively mature art infrastructure, but also very strong fashion, design, commercial, technological and popular cultures, and these worlds exist very close to one another in everyday life. It also has a large and increasingly young audience, so when an artist comes to Shanghai, they are encountering more than an established ‘art world’; they are entering a complex and fast-moving urban environment that is constantly being reshaped by new forms of consumption, technology and youth culture. That complexity creates a lot of possibilities.”

Zhang Ruyi, whose 2022 exhibition at Beijing’s UCCA Center for Contemporary Art is shown here, is among Shanghai’s mid-career artists to watch Photo: Sun Shi; courtesy UCCA Center for Contemporary Art

After helping to build the careers of some of Asia’s biggest art stars, Helbling now focuses on cultivating rising talents through more group shows and dialogue. “You always look at what artists are doing,” he says. “Many of them now operate between here and the West—educated here and in the West”, with networks and roots in both cultures.

After suspended mobility during the Covid years, dual-culture artists in Shanghai and across Asia have resumed their frequent travels between regions. Often the children of artists or collectors, young Chinese artists are very well versed in both Western and Asian artistic traditions, giving them a sophisticated perspective. “They have an interesting job to do to figure out what kind of art makes sense today,” Helbling says.

He recalls the “loneliness” of Shanghai’s 1990s avant-garde, now rendered “impossible” by contemporary art’s citywide popularity. Still, he views the 2000 internationalisation of the Shanghai Biennale, the first contemporary art fair SH Contemporary (2007-14), and the flowering of galleries and museums as all part of one era.

“There was from the 1990s when Shanghai opened up until the Covid time, and there is after Covid time,” he says. “The first 30 years, and now the second 30 years.”

Your ‘Healthy’ Soda Alternative May Not Be So Healthy for Your Liver

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

WE'RE LONG PAST having to make a decision between just Coke or Pepsi. You've got a lot of options if you're bored of water—from fizzy nonalcoholic drinks to flavored seltzers. But while a lot of these drinks market themselves as healthy alternatives to sodas, research suggests they could be silently doing a number on your liver health.

If you've done the work to switch to diet sodas or have learned to love prebiotic sodas like Olipop or Poppi, the news can be pretty hard to hear. So it's important to get this out of the way upfront: If you have a "healthy" soda every once in a while, the experts we spoke to say the risk of having liver problems is unlikely. The real danger to your liver is if you’re tossing these back on a daily or near-daily basis. Here's everything you need to know.

THE STUDY PRESENTED at the United European Gastroenterology (UEG) Week 2025 used data from 103,251 people who enrolled in the UK Biobank and didn’t have liver disease at the start of the study. Participants were asked to fill out questionnaires about what they ate and drank throughout the day. The researchers then paid close attention to how much sugar-sweetened and low- or non-sugar-sweetened beverages they drank.

The major takeaway from the study is that it pretty much linked any drink that wasn’t water to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). You might know it by its former name, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). But whatever you call it, it's no joke.

MASLD happens when too much fat builds up in your liver from inputs other than alcohol. It can lead to serious liver scarring or end-stage liver disease. It can also put you at high risk for heart disease and diabetes. According to the American College of Gastroenterology, heart disease is the number one killer of people living with MASLD.

DAILY DRINKING OF nearly 9 ounces of any type of drink that was not water was associated with a significantly higher risk of developing MASLD. That risk jumped 50 percent for sugar-sweetened beverages and was surprisingly higher—60 percent—for those who drank low- or non-sugar-sweetened beverages. (Though some of these prebiotic sodas have more sugar and less fiber than marketing claims might imply).

After a 10-year follow-up, 949 people developed MASLD and 103 died from liver-related causes. Both types of beverages were significantly connected to increases in liver fat.

Replacing nearly 12 ounces of sugary beverages with water lowered MASLD risk by 14.4 percent and by 13.5 percent with low- or non-sugar-sweetened drinks. But if you switched completely from a sugary beverage like soda to a low-sugar beverage like fruit juice, the authors found that this did not help lower a person's risk of developing MASLD.

Exactly which beverages raised risk is hard to say because the authors did not directly state what low- or no-sugar drinks people were drinking. That said, there are lots of sodas that are marketed as being healthier alternatives that are not as good as they claim, according to Danbee Kim, MD, nutrition expert and assistant professor at Rutgers New Jersey Medical School.

“Even beverages marketed as ‘natural’ or ‘health-oriented’ may still fall into this category if they rely on sweeteners instead of sugar,” Kim says. “The key commonality is that they provide sweetness without significant calories. However, they may still affect liver health.”

A FEW THINGS may be going on here. “Low/non-sugar sweetened beverages, although low in calories, may still affect liver health through several pathways,” says Lihe Liu, a graduate student researcher at The First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University in China and lead study author. “They can alter the gut microbiome, interfere with feelings of fullness, increase preference for sweet foods, and in some cases stimulate insulin secretion.”

When you have something that’s sweet, even if it doesn’t contain much or any sugar, it sets off a chain reaction in your body the same way as if you were to actually have sugar, says Albert Matheny, RD, CSCS, registered dietitian and co-founder of SoHo Strength Lab. “You’re basically tricking your body,” he says. “Your body still reacts as if it has sugar.”

As a result, Matheny says your body produces more insulin (a hormone that helps regulate blood sugar), and encourages the liver to take in more sugar from your bloodstream. This can lead to metabolic disruption.

“Some artificial sweeteners may alter how the body handles glucose and insulin, contributing to insulin resistance, which is a key driver of metabolic dysfunction and fat accumulation in the liver,” says Kim. This process may even mess with your appetite, making you think that you're hungry when you're not.

Another reason has nothing to do with your gut, but with your mind. Kim says we can't discount the possibility that people who drink these low- and non-sugar-sweetened beverages may subconsciously compensate for the “saved” calories by eating more sugary or fatty foods later.

Keep in mind that all this is speculation. The explanation for why and how artificially sweetened beverages are linked to MASLD is still under investigation since Liu says her team didn't look too deep into this.

NOT NECESSARILY. “THEY’RE definitely an improvement over traditional diet sodas,” Matheny says. “That’s not to say that they’re a totally healthy option.”

When given a choice, water reigns supreme. And even then you still don't want to go too overboard.

If you still want to crack open a cranberry fizz prebiotic soda or even an unsweetened iced tea, Matheny says that’s probably fine, too. “In moderation, kind of everything is OK.”

The money behind that demand is enormous. A recent Stateline analysis called data centers “the largest financial bet in US history.”

The shift shows up in Team Group’s own business. Business-to-business sales made up 64.6% of revenue in the first three quarters of 2026, up from 24.6% a year earlier. Chen attributed the change to market demand rather than a strategic decision by the company.

The holiday hosting season is upon us, and while you’re planning your tablescape decor and seasonal trimmings, you might want to take a hard look at your guest bedroom.

“Most guest rooms get treated like overflow storage, and guests feel that the second they walk in,” says Marie Cloud, principal designer at Indigo Pruitt.

Even if you host only occasionally, your guest room should give visitors a comfortable place to unwind. No need for a guest book or fuzzy slippers: Designers say these four thoughtful touches can make the room feel luxe without costing a fortune.

Regardless of the design aesthetic, the ambiance of a room always hinges on lighting. “I always pay attention to the lighting,” says Louis Lin, founder of his eponymous New York City–based interior design studio. “A beautiful bedside lamp with a warm, flattering glow can transform the atmosphere of a room. Layering different light sources, rather than relying on overhead lighting, can do that. It's a relatively simple gesture that makes a room feel much more intimate and refined.” This also gives guests the freedom to tailor the air of their space to their liking. It’s one of the few details they can truly control in their temporary lodging.

While a pile of pillows might look inviting, designer Marie Cloud says the bedtime legwork promptly cancels out the aesthetics.

“Skip the stack of eight decorative pillows your guest has to relocate before bed,” says Cloud. Her perfect formula for a top-notch guest setup? Two sleeping pillows, plus one lumbar pillow, all set atop a good set of sheets.

“Sheets with real hand-feel, like a crisp percale or washed linen,” Cloud suggests. “Finish with a throw folded at the foot in a color pulled from the room. It looks considered, and it’s actually comfortable at 2 a.m.”

Most guest rooms get the all-white wall treatment in the name of neutrality, but all it really says is that your space lacks intention. Just because this room is (mostly) reserved for guests doesn't mean it has to lack character. In fact, Cloud says you should not hold back.

“Drench the room,” says the designer. “Walls, trim, and ceiling in the same color, ideally something deep and warm. Continuous color reads custom, it makes a small room feel wrapped rather than cramped, and it tells your guest the space was designed for them. A guest room is also the lowest-risk room in the house to be bold, so take the swing.”

The best guest setups make visitors feel anticipated, even when they show up unexpectedly. To achieve the boutique-hotel feel, designers recommend investing in small luxuries that leave a big impact. A few items Cloud and Lin always recommend having on hand are a small luggage rack, real hangers, fresh towels, and a select set of toiletries reserved especially for guests.

“The real luxury is making guests feel that the room has been prepared just for them,” says Lin.

High oil prices have led to a dip in global carbon emissions

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Bucking recent trends, fossil fuel emissions look to be down 0.4 percent.

Evaluating the annual global temperatures each year requires context. There is a fair amount of natural variability from one year to the next, so it’s important to focus on the trend over time. Famously, global warming did not end in 1998, as some claimed a couple of decades ago—we just had a run of La-Niña-dominated years after a very strong El Niño.

Annual greenhouse gas emissions are much less variable, having increased fairly steadily, but many complicating factors can still muddy the picture. Sometimes those factors conspire to throw a year off the trend. It looks like 2026 will be one of those years.

A new analysis led by Corinne Le Quéré at the University of East Anglia estimates that fossil fuel emissions have fallen about 0.4 percent from last year. This probably isn’t a peak that symbolizes heartening progress on climate change, though; the researchers think it’s a short-term dip driven by the war in Iran and its effect on oil prices.

This team works on the Global Carbon Project, which compiles stats on fossil fuel use and things like deforestation to track carbon emissions globally and by country. In this case, they looked at the stats and tried to pull out key underlying trends that show us what to expect over the next couple of years.

Last year, fossil fuel emissions increased 1.2 percent, a bit above the 2019–2025 average of 0.8 percent per year. This year’s decline featured a 1.9 percent drop in oil emissions, a smaller 0.3 percent drop in emissions from natural gas use, and a 0.6 percent increase in coal emissions.

Unlike the pandemic-driven drop in 2020, this wasn’t driven by a widespread decrease in energy demand. Demand did increase, but additions of electric vehicles and solar and wind generation covered almost 70 percent of the growth. Electric vehicles can account for only about 20 percent of that consequential decline in oil emissions, meaning the bulk is due to high prices.

One simple way to break down emissions is to connect it to economic output. Given a country’s gross domestic product (GDP), energy use per unit GDP, and carbon emissions per unit energy, you can calculate total carbon emissions. GDP might go up, but if that’s due to less energy-intensive industries or a cleaner energy system, carbon emissions might not rise with it.

Between 2019 and 2025, 59 countries cut their emissions despite economic growth, even as global emissions increased by 0.8 percent per year. The researchers estimated that, had the economic growth not been accompanied by the growth of electric vehicles and solar and wind generation, the increase would have been much higher, at 1.9 percent per year.

Given the International Monetary Fund’s projection for GDP through 2028, the researchers extrapolated these trends forward as well. From that, we would expect global fossil fuel emissions to continue increasing. That includes the present year—without the shock to oil prices, we would be seeing emissions increase in 2026 as well.

So while we could be close to peak emissions, we’re probably not there yet. The growth of electric vehicles and renewable energy is offsetting most of the growth of emissions, but not all of it. That would take just a bit more juice.

The researchers write, “If further acceleration of clean technology deployment can be supported through time, a sustained peak and decline in global CO2 emissions could be a few years away.”
