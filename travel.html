RALEIGH, N.C. (AP) — Jordan Martinook scored two of Carolina's four first-period goals in the Hurricanes' 9-2 win over the Chicago Blackhawks on Saturday night.

The Blackhawks have a six-game losing streak and have lost nine of 10. Chicago goalie Spencer Knight was pulled with 9:24 remaining in the second period after allowing six goals on 17 shots. Arvid Soderblom stopped 12 shots in relief.

Trailing 7-0, Roman Kantserov and Connor Bedard scored in the third period for the Blackhawks, with Alex Vlasic assisting on both goals. Kantserov has an eight-game points streak.

Jarvis scored 1:34 into the game. After Blake's goal, Martintook completed the first-period scoring.

Shortly after the midway mark of the second period the Hurricanes led 6-0 while the Blackhawks had eight shots on goal.

Nikishin, a defenseman who was unsigned when the season began, had a shot make it through traffic for Carolina's seventh goal and he later added an assist. He was named to the NHL All-Rookie team last season with the Stanley Cup champions.

The Hurricanes have outscored their last two visiting opponents by a combined 16-2 after defeating the Winnipeg Jets 7-0 on Sunday.

Turn left, take a few steps forward, and you will find a tree with a crew member sitting under it.

You can find the second crew along the path to the Temple, and you’ll need a few air dashes to reach the spot.

To find the third crew member, you have to defeat the zone’s boss. You’ll find him near the top of the teleporter. From there, turn right, and continue along the path that goes downwards. Go straight, and you’ll find an opening on the floor. Drop, and you’ll find the crew member.

To reach the final crew member, head to the Cemetery Precipice teleporter at Isle of Neasied. Go past the teleporter, continue straight, and then turn right. You’ll come across the structure shown on the image above.

Substitute captain Federico Valverde struck in stoppage time as Real Madrid twice came from behind to beat AEK Athens 3-2. The win keeps Mourinho's side perfect in the league phase and takes them past his qualification target, days before a trip to Valencia without Vinícius.

Federico Valverde came off the bench in Athens and settled a match Real Madrid had trailed twice. The captain’s long-range strike arrived in the second minute of stoppage time. It completed another away comeback in this Champions League campaign. Mourinho’s side remain perfect after four matchdays.

AEK struck first. Koita put the hosts ahead in the 12th minute. Bellingham had a goal ruled out for offside after a review just before the half-hour. He did not have to wait long. In the 32nd minute he equalised with a backheel from Cucurella’s cross.

AEK went ahead again with 15 minutes of normal time left. Stavros Pilios ran onto a pass from Koita and shot through Courtois’s legs. Madrid answered in less than three minutes. Denzel Dumfries played a low ball across goal and Mbappé made it 2-2.

The night ended badly for Koita. AEK’s opening scorer was sent off at 90+5, after the final whistle. He had kept protesting to the referee despite repeated warnings.

Madrid’s European record now reads four wins from four. They beat Inter 2-1 at home, came back to win 4-3 at Roma and beat Leipzig 4-1 at home before this trip. Their tally of 12 points takes them past the 11–12 point mark Mourinho set as the target for qualification. The next aim is a top-eight finish.

The pattern is notable. Both the Roma and AEK wins came after Madrid had fallen behind away from home. In Athens they had to respond on two separate occasions.

Attention now turns to LaLiga, where Madrid remain without Vinícius. On 16 October, the RFEF Appeals Committee rejected the club’s appeal and upheld his ban. Two games were for pulling Nathan Saliba’s hair against Villarreal. The other two were for confronting the referee afterwards.

The Best Hiking Child Carriers for Travel, Day Hikes, and Long Trails

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

If you buy through our links, we may earn an affiliate commission. This supports our mission to get more people active and outside.Learn about Outside Online's affiliate link policy

Before our daughter AJ arrived, my wife and I braced for the end of an era. We figured our adventurous lifestyle would be long gone (or at least forever changed); our family trips would revolve around nap schedules instead of trails; and our days of spontaneity and exploration would grind to a halt. Turns out, we couldn’t have been more wrong.

At eighteen months, our daughter has probably logged more trail miles than the average adult. She’s joined us on ridgeline hikes in the Blue Ridge Mountains, alpine lake loops in the Rockies, coastal walks in New Brunswick, red-rock rambles in Arches and Zion national parks, and plenty of urban outings around Chicago.

And all the credit goes to the unsung hero of our gear closet: the hiking child carrier.

While she loves to explore the trail on foot in short stints under a mile (for now), we’ve found that she’s most content at longer distances taking in the view from above our shoulders. And when we’re lucky, she’ll even doze off for an afternoon nap mid-hike. We’ve tested several kid carriers on dozens of hikes, looking for the ones that balance comfort, fit, and storage. These are the ones we reach for again and again on our hiking adventures.

We take a lot of hiking-centric trips, and packing for them with a toddler is already a puzzle. Between a Pack ’n Play, a car seat, and a week’s worth of diapers, there’s not much bandwidth to lug along a bulky carrier. The Osprey Poco LT is the exception. At five pounds, nine ounces, and a whopping twenty-five liters of gear storage (nearly double what you’ll find in the Kelty and Deuter carriers below), its weight-to-capacity ratio stomps the competition.

Its internal aluminum frame keeps the pack light but provides enough structure and strength to carry a toddler up to forty pounds. There’s a massive lower zipper compartment that fits extra layers or a change of clothes, a soft cooler with snacks, and diapers with room to spare. And a built-in UPF 50 sunshade unfolds and clips to keep the sun off our kid’s sensitive skin.

But the real magic is in how it packs down. The Poco LT folds completely flat and can easily fit into an airplane’s overhead bin or other tight spaces like a jam-packed trunk on a road trip. I can’t say that about any other child carrier I’ve tested. Since getting this pack, we’ve completely ditched the stroller at the airport in favor of this pack as a lightweight transport option for our kiddo.

The tradeoffs? Thinner shoulder straps and a minimalist design mean it’s not the pack for very long days on the trail. Its shorter hip belt and slim profile also don’t fit larger bodies as well as some of the alternatives below. But for traveling to a place you expect to hike or walk five miles or less, it’s the ideal pick.

For smaller babies not big enough for a full-blown backpack carrier, Osprey also makes the Poco Soft Child Carrier. It cradles your young’un on the front of your body, and once they’re big enough, they can face outward to enjoy the view.

Gearing up for a day hike (especially with a kid) means bringing plenty of extras along for the ride: snacks, layers, sunscreen, bug spray, more snacks, and diapers—to name a few. Two pounds heavier than the Poco LT, this pack certainly feels bulkier. But what it lacks in packability it makes up for in convenient features. If you care less about cutting weight and more about pockets, comfort for your kiddo, and space, the Kelty Journey PerfectFIT Elite is your best bet.

Nine total pockets—including three front zippered pockets in different sizes, dual zippered hip pockets, and large mesh side pockets—offer ample storage for a day hike. We’re big fans of the zippered “dirty” compartment on the underside of the pack to stash soiled clothes, like when an unexpected diaper blowout strikes mid-hike. Six chunky grab handles make it easy to hoist the carrier (with some help from a partner or friend) onto your back or set it down gently. Once it’s down, an oversized kickstand keeps the pack sturdy and balanced, even when your kiddo’s kicking around inside. Kelty clearly cut no corners on the details with this pack.

A pack suitable for sleep is non-negotiable. Who wouldn’t want the serenity of their kid snoozing mid-hike? More miles, fewer meltdowns, as I like to say. AJ zonks out in the Journey PerfectFIT Elite more than in any other carrier. I suspect it’s the roomy seat and oversized chin pad (perfect for resting her head like a pillow), which seem to make settling in for a nap that much easier. We took her on numerous day hikes in the Blue Ridge Mountains around Asheville this fall, and she fell asleep on nearly all of them when we timed our outings around her usual naptime. Now, I’m not promising this pack will put your kid to sleep. But I’m confident its cozy, supportive setup will make a mid-hike nap that much more likely.

Backpacking families and peak-bagging parents who want to take on bigger mileage need a carrier that balances weight, capacity, and comfort. For longer hikes, I grab the Deuter Kid Comfort because it fits like a classic backpacking pack. Think of it as an overnight pack that happens to carry a kid instead of your tent, sleeping pad, and stove.

Fit is where the Kid Comfort shines. A sliding torso adjustment lets whoever’s wearing it (Mama, Momzie, Grandma, or Uncle Daniel, in AJ’s case) dial in a precise fit. And as a notoriously sweaty hiker, I appreciated that the back panel’s raised and ventilated mesh kept air moving without trapping heat on my back. The Kid Comfort’s height-adjustable seat has a cushion that widens as your kid grows. We started AJ in it at ten months old, and at eighteen months, she still rides comfortably with plenty of room for continued use over the next couple of years.

It’s eight ounces heavier than the Journey PerfectFIT Elite, but its greater adjustability and plusher padding make it that much more comfortable on longer, more technical routes. On a recent trip to Arches and Zion national parks, we spent long days exploring the trails. I noticed far less shoulder and hip fatigue with the Kid Comfort compared to the Osprey Poco LT and Kelty Journey PerfectFIT Elite packs, especially given all the scrambling over obstacles or on longer hikes. Ergonomic hip wings secure the weight of the load above your hips. Because the hip belt locks the load in place and the pack doesn’t shift or sway with every step, I felt confident scrambling up and down sandstone, squeezing through slot canyons, and crossing exposed slickrock with AJ on my back.

The tradeoff is its limited storage. At fourteen liters, it holds roughly half as much as the Kelty and Osprey packs. Two stretchy side pockets, hip belt pockets, a large stretch pocket on the back, and a zippered bottom compartment hold the essentials but not much more. It’s enough for a day-long outing, but one of us always also ends up carrying a daypack in addition to the Deuter so we can have everything we need. All to say: This pack is by far the most secure and comfortable on technical trails, especially over longer distances.

Flock Won't Take No For An Answer, Keeps Cameras In Service After Communities Cancel Contracts

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Cities across the country have been dropping their Flock camera contracts left and right as people object to the surveillance state they enable. Canceling these contracts isn't always a straightforward process, however. The Verge reports that Flock is not only delaying the removal of their equipment from communities that have requested it, but also reactivating it when officials take matters into their own hands.

One of these cities is Plymouth, Minnesota, where Joel Feder of The Drive was wrongfully accused of driving a stolen car based on a bad Flock hit. The city council voted to cancel its contract with Flock on September 22, and officially notified Flock the following day. From The Drive:

[Plymouth Police Chief Eric] Fadden said, "We did not hear any reply from Flock Safety Corporate or Legal, other than some communication with our local representative." Flock's silence and refusal to comply with the canceled contract forced the Police Chief to take matters into his own hands. "After hearing nothing for more than a week, I directed our staff to disconnect the power cords from the cameras in order to be sure that they are not functioning or recording plates," Fadden said.

It's rather ironic that the chief of police ended up instructing his officers to do exactly the same thing some vandals have done to disable the cameras. With Flock not responding to multiple attempts to cancel the service, this appeared to be the only way to be sure the cameras were off, as the city council's vote required.

Other cities have taken this process a step further, removing the cameras and storing them until Flock wants them back. From KARE 11:

"Once we made this decision, I personally went to the locations and removed the cameras and poles myself," Crystal police chief Pete Underthun told KARE 11 in a statement. "We are still working with the company to close out our contract and for them to pick the cameras up from our garage."

I guess what would otherwise qualify as vandalism is fine if the police do it themselves. Dayton, Minnesota, also physically removed its cameras, but even that didn't shut down the service. As the city explained in a Facebook post:

On September 17, 2026, the Police Department was made aware of a maintenance work order produced by Flock. The City notified Flock on September 18, 2026, that the program was on hold, and no work orders were to be completed. Unfortunately, the City's direction was not followed, and the camera was placed back into service without authorization. Following the report, City staff reviewed locations throughout the community to determine whether any additional cameras had been reinstalled, and one additional location was identified. The Police Department has been notified, and both cameras will be removed promptly.

Like so many things these days, Flock is a subscription service. Communities pay for the service, but Flock owns all the hardware and is responsible for installing and maintaining it, as well as removing it when requested. Technically, by the book, the police shouldn't be disabling or removing cameras, either. But we're not going to complain when they do, especially when Flock seems to be dragging its feet on the matter.

Other communities have resorted to covering cameras with trash bags while waiting for Flock to do its part. Even this strategy hasn't always been effective, though. The Verge also reports that Elgin Community College found the bags over its Flock cameras removed, presumably by Flock contractors sent out to "fix" the cameras they had covered while trying to cancel the service. This happened several times between December 2025 and April 2026. Only when the original contract expired did Flock arrange to remove the unwanted cameras.

Flock's only comment on this matter to KARE 11 was: "We are proud of the impact our technology had in Plymouth and Dayton. We respect the cities' decisions and are working with the customers to have the cameras removed." It gave no status of the cancellation requests or timeline for camera removal. Zach Doud, Dayton city administrator, told KARE 11 that Flock told him it would take 60 days to remove the equipment. It's hard to tell whether Flock is deliberately dragging this out, or simply overwhelmed by the large number of cancelation requests it's getting.

Either way, I wonder if whoever wrote Flock's cancellation policy was listening to "Hotel California" by the Eagles at the time. It seems like you can check out any time you like, but you can never leave.
