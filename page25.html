The MCU Ends Its Best Trilogy Since James Gunn's Guardians Of The Galaxy

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Trilogies work best when there’s a single, unifying vision behind them — George Lucas’ Star Wars trilogy, Sergio Leone’s Dollars trilogy, Peter Jackson’s Lord of the Rings trilogy — but there are so many cooks in the kitchen at Marvel Studios that singular visions are rare in the MCU. Typically, a series of solo movies will switch hands between a bunch of different directors with a bunch of different styles, going off in whatever direction the larger cinematic universe dictates. Thor’s trilogy started off with the Shakespearean heft of Kenneth Branagh and ended with the ironic self-awareness of Taika Waititi. Captain America’s trilogy is a pulpy World War II actioner followed by a paranoid conspiracy thriller followed by Avengers 2.5.

It’s rare that the MCU is able to deliver a perfect trilogy with a clear beginning, middle, and end, and no weak links. The Thor trilogy is let down by The Dark World. The Ant-Man trilogy is let down by Quantumania. The Deadpool trilogy is spread across two separate franchises. Tom Holland’s Spider-Man trilogy never had a weak link, but Far From Home is pretty unremarkable.

As it stands, the best trilogy in the MCU is James Gunn’s Guardians of the Galaxy series. Gunn wrote and directed all three Guardians movies, so they’re all held together by the same creative voice. The second movie has some pacing issues, and some not-so-subtle symbolism in the father-son story, but that’s the worst thing you could say about it. The first film was a breath of fresh air, the second one maintained the emotional weight to complement the comic book spectacle, and the third one was the best of the bunch, with a much deeper and darker story to tell.

With the release of the VisionQuest finale, the MCU has now completed its best trilogy since Guardians of the Galaxy. The WandaVision trilogy of streaming shows has been much more consistent and satisfying than most of the MCU’s movie trilogies.

WandaVision was a massive success, both critically and commercially, when it premiered in 2021. Marvel put its best foot forward in the brave new post-Endgame world, and audiences responded with such enthusiasm that this little sitcom homage became a cornerstone of the Multiverse Saga. WandaVision remained a one-and-done limited series, but it was followed up by two sequel shows.

After WandaVision told the story of Wanda Maximoff processing her grief over Vision’s death (in a roundabout way), Agatha All Along wondered if her witchy arch-nemesis Agatha Harkness could be redeemed, and VisionQuest wondered if sentient A.I.s and fabricated memories can enjoy lives and identities of their own. All three shows have been spectacular in their own way, and they’ve each made for a worthwhile chapter in the overall story.

Jac Schaeffer humanized Wanda and Vision in WandaVision — she took this witch and this robot who have fought supervillains with the Avengers and live in a magical sitcom fantasyland, and really dug into their emotional conflicts — and that spirit carried through into the subsequent spinoffs. Agatha All Along is as much about the fraught relationships between the witches in the coven as it is a campy homage to horror classics, and VisionQuest is as much a meditation on artificial intelligence as it is a quirky buddy comedy about bickering androids.

All in all, the WandaVision trilogy has been one of the strongest stories that the MCU has ever told, and it’s not over yet. The trilogy has ended, but VisionQuest’s post-credits scene seems to be setting Wanda up for a major role in Avengers: Doomsday. That final stinger showed that, after Wanda made the ultimate sacrifice to destroy the Darkhold in Doctor Strange in the Multiverse of Madness, Doctor Doom showed up to steal her powers. So, we could be seeing the Scarlet Witch under Doom’s control.

The partnership was announced in May 2025, with reciprocal loyalty earning and redemption launching five months later. The arrangement gives JetBlue an opportunity to increase the value of TrueBlue while providing United Airlines with access to another established customer base. JetBlue and United Airlines announced their Blue Sky commercial partnership in May 2025, bringing together two carriers with different network strengths. Netflix's New 12-Part Sci-Fi Mystery Already Feels Like a Masterpiece Just 3 Episodes In

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Even in a sunless world, something has to bloom

Even in a sunless world, something has to bloom

Some of my favorite science fiction stories take one seemingly impossible premise and explore all the ways it ripples through everyday life, from Gattaca to The Leftovers. Netflix's latest original anime is another perfect example of that trope.

From a surprise collaboration between two iconic anime studios, Sunrise and Shaft, Fool Night imagines a future world where the sun has long disappeared, leaving humanity with the unusual solution of turning volunteer humans into plants for oxygen. It's a bleak setup, made all the more gripping by a visual identity that constantly juxtaposes human decay with budding flora. Netflix only provided us with the first three episodes (out of 12 total) for review, and I'm entirely hooked. Between its sharp character work, unsettling world-building, and melancholic atmosphere, Fool Night is shaping up to be one of the most striking new shows of Fall.

Based on the manga by Kasumi Yasuda and directed by Atsuyuki Yukawa, Fool Night is a surprisingly intimate story set against an enormous environmental catastrophe. Rather than throwing viewers into an expansive science fiction mystery, Yukawa takes his time introducing its main characters and how their lives become intertwined by circumstances neither could have anticipated. It's a more methodical introduction than some might expect, offering plenty of room to establish the people at the center of a world literally steeped in darkness and comforted only by the discarded remains of the volunteers transformed into grotesque flora.

That’s the kind of setting Fool Night presents, a miserable environment governed by survival. With the sun gone, oxygen increasingly scarce, and most plant life dying off as a result, humanity has turned to a process called transfloration, allowing terminally ill volunteers to undergo a procedure that implants a seed inside their bodies for a payout of 10 million yen. Over the course of two years, that seed gradually grows until that person becomes what’s called a “spiritflor,” essentially a sprout that helps replenish the oxygen humans need to survive. It's an extraordinary solution to an extraordinary problem, although the implications are considerably more disturbing when you see what it actually looks like.

Spiritflor are everywhere in Fool Night. Their bodies litter the streets, fill abandoned alleyways, and even occupy the stairwells and corridors of apartment buildings. Some look almost beautiful, with sprouts and foliage emerging from what remains of their human bodies. Others are considerably more grotesque, their former appearances barely recognizable beneath the vegetation. They're like haunting reminders of the people who once lived there, transformed into something simultaneously beautiful and deeply unsettling. The worst part, of course, is that they're still alive.

The series introduces its grotesque imagery ingeniously in its opening sequence, establishing how accustomed the world’s inhabitants are to their surrounding environment. Standing outside a guitar store, a young child named Toshio Kamiya insists to his friend Yomiko Horai that he’ll be a musician one day. It’s pure wishful thinking, and Yomiko tries to ground him, even as Toshio beams at the thought of future stardom. As they’re walking down an alleyway, with Toshio making little guitar noises and pretending to play as he twirls around, the lighthearted moment is juxtaposed with the bodies of spiritflor lining the fences around them.

It's an especially eerie scene, with the children paying little mind to the grotesquery around them. They've presumably walked past these bodies countless times before, and that's perhaps the most disturbing part of the entire sequence. This is simply what life looks like now. It's an incredibly effective way of introducing the series' central conceit without stopping to explain every little detail. The world has adapted to something that should be horrifying, and the children have grown up knowing nothing else.

What makes Fool Night immediately compelling isn't just its grim premise and setting, but how sharply it uses the childhood friends Toshio and Yomiko to expose the class divide at the heart of its dying world. Now adults and reunited after years apart, the two represent opposite ends of transfloration’s bleak economy.

Toshio is trapped at the absolute bottom of society. A disillusioned factory worker crushed by poverty, systemic debt, and an abusive home life, he’s left with no viable future other than to sell his body to the state. In doing so, Toshio trades his remaining two years of humanity for a 10-million-yen payout that he barely gets to keep — only to get robbed almost immediately following the procedure. Yomiko, by contrast, sits comfortably on the clinical side of the glass as a researcher at Transflora Medical Facility 4, approving candidates and overseeing transfloration procedures, all while the morality of what she’s doing hangs over her head like a creeping vine gathering blight.

Their contrasted situations give the series its strongest dramatic friction. Early on, Yomiko is defined by a chilling professional detachment. When a colleague admits he feels like they killed someone after performing a procedure, she coolly insists they’ve "extended" a life, hiding behind the rhetoric of personal choice. It’s easy for her to rationalize transfloration when the patients are strangers, and that’s precisely why Toshio’s sudden appearance in her waiting room hits so hard. Reuniting after years apart only to find someone she genuinely cares for cornered into becoming a plant shatters her clinical distance, turning an abstract ethical debate into an immediate personal crisis.

Rather than bogging down in misery, however, the show smartly pivots their reunion into an intriguing partnership. When Yomiko discovers that the procedure has left Toshio with the bizarre ability to hear the voices of spiritflor, she pulls him into the facility's orbit. Alongside sour-faced Unsection Chief Jin Hiragishi, the two begin untangling the darker mysteries behind the program. It’s a sharp, character-first hook that grounds the show's high-concept sci-fi in genuine human stakes, one saddled against the backdrop of a looming murder investigation that could change the world forever.

If there's one thing that immediately separates Fool Night from other science fiction anime, it's the sheer confidence of its visual presentation — though, it definitely helps that two of the most legendary animation studios collaborated on the production. Sunrise, best known for its work on Gundam and Cowboy Bebop, and Shaft, the same studio behind the Monogatari series and Virgin Punk, have more than enough history behind them. Seeing them come together on a project of this caliber is mindblowing, and the results so far more than live up to the occasion.

What impresses me most isn't necessarily the scale of the animation, but how much attention the series pays to composition. There are so many shots that deliberately position characters in unexpected places, sometimes leaving enormous portions of the screen occupied by otherwise unimportant scenery. Rather than constantly following characters with the camera, Fool Night lets them exist within the environment, allowing the world around them to communicate just as much as the dialogue.

There's a particularly good example when Yomiko speaks with the front desk clerk about midway into the first episode, asking whether Toshio had gone through with the transfloration procedure. It's a relatively somber moment, with Yomiko and the desk clerk occupying just a small portion of the left side of the screen. Taking up almost the entirety of the composition is a plant just out of focus, filling the surrounding space rather than simply centering the shot on Yomiko’s back.

The same approach appears at the very start of Fool Night. As the child versions of Toshio and Yomiko walk down a fenced-off alleyway, Toshio launches into a short sprint like a musician would on a stage. Instead of directly following him, the camera tracks the fence on his left, moving along the cold steel as the jogging Toshio only takes up the right side of the frame. These are small decisions, but they help communicate the darker implications behind this captivating sci-fi dystopia and give the show a striking visual identity unlike anything I’ve seen before.

Fool Night isn't in a rush to lay all its cards on the table. While early episodes drop breadcrumbs of the wider conspiracy surrounding an investigation into bizarre murders involving plant-based lacerations and a shadowy organization attempting to stop transfloration procedures, the series largely holds its burgeoning mysteries at bay until the final moments of episode three. Rather than feeling sluggish, that restraint pays off by offering you a firm grasp on how transfloration has warped the show’s society and why these two leads are so deeply entangled within it.

Three episodes in, it's still too early to say where Fool Night will ultimately land, particularly with so much of its sunless world left unexplored, but what I've seen so far has me hooked. Its unsettling premise, wonderfully complicated characters, and gorgeous animation have already made for a compelling start. If the series can maintain this level of visual ambition while continuing to build on its central mysteries, I can't wait to see where Fool Night goes next.

The Design Fiend’s Guide to Tangier

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Moroccan city, a complex, culturally kaleidoscopic crossroads between Europe and Africa, is home to a savvy community of creatives whose stylish impact continues to grow. Word is out.

Above: The pool terrace at Villa Mabrouka, a 12-room hotel that was once the home of Yves Saint Laurent and Pierre Bergé, with gardens designed by Madison Cox.

Writing about Tangier in the 1950s, Truman Capote observed that “the days slide by less noticed than foam in a waterfall.” Decades later, this city on Morocco’s coast, across the Strait of Gibraltar from Spain, is significantly changed from Capote’s time, when it was an exotic retreat for café society’s demimonde, people such as Barbara Hutton and Malcolm Forbes. In recent years Tangier’s mosaic of villages—from the medina’s warren of whitewashed lanes to the Ville Moderne’s blocks of Art Deco towers to the hillside villas and gardens on Jebel Kebir—has been reinvigorated, in part by an enterprising mix of Tanjawis (the locals) and expats who continue to infuse the city with a fresh creative spirit.

Yet there is much that remains the same: the dreamy light that seduced artists from Eugène Delacroix to Henri Matisse to Mariano Fortuny, the overgrown gardens of casbah courtyards, and the cooling breezes coming off the confluence of Atlantic and Mediterranean waters. And there’s still plenty of the languorous lifestyle that captivated Capote.

“Tangier is all about settling in,” says Charlotte Claydon, whose whitewashed Moroccan Art Deco house has been in her family for more than a century. Known as Dar Sinclair, the residence sits in a grove of papyrus and lilies between thick palms and weeping willows. It’s one of numerous historic villas and townhouses renovated by international designers and other creatives who live here that can be rented. There’s arguably no better way to ease into the rhythms of daily life in Tangier. Pin Affleck, who arranges travel and accommodations through the Tangier Bureau, says that “going into a private house, you get the sense of being inside Tangier.”

When Claydon and her sister Jacquetta Wheeler began renting out Dar Sinclair, they received messages from guests worried about filling up a week. “We spent hours coming up with lists of activities, knowing full well they might only get to one or two,” Claydon says, noting that most guests make their own discoveries and end up on unplanned diversions.

Among the sisters’ can’t-miss activities: tea at Café de Paris, Sunday service at historic St. Andrew’s Church, and a camel ride, especially with kids, at Achakar Beach. A typical morning in Tangier might begin with breakfast under a banyan tree at El Morocco Club. Around 11 a.m. the markets buzz to life, including the legendary fish market in the Grand Socco, the Fondouk Chejra (weavers’ market), the antiques mall on Rue de Hollande, and the Casabarata flea market, where a sharp eye is required to spot the gems amid the seedy furniture and cheap fabrics.

Lunch in town at Alma Kitchen or El Dorado is a must for crispy falafel and plates of lamb chops. Later, as the sun sinks into the sea, it’s hard to beat the views from the roof of Villa Mabrouka, the onetime home of Yves Saint Laurent and Pierre Bergé that Jasper Conran transformed into hotel with gardens by Tangier resident Madison Cox. After a martini, the hotel’s L’Orangerie restaurant is a smart spot for a dinner of thistle tagine and grilled sole; down the street, the atmospheric Hamadi serves standout harissa soup and sizzling skewers.

Claydon and Wheeler, for their part, also advise guests to reserve time for relaxing and being transported at home, one of the great pleasures of staying in a design-centric private residence. Other places available to rent include the red-and-white-striped riad owned by decorator Gavin Houghton and photographer Boz Gagovski (who took most of the photos in this story), as well as antiques dealer Gordon Watson’s large ochre house. Both weave together Moorish and European details, with objects from Tangier and around the world that reflect the city’s cosmopolitan history. As Hamish Bowles noted in his foreword to the book Inside Tangier, “The magic of this place was that everyone who had chosen to make a home here brought their own fantasy with them.”

That book was produced by my husband, designer Nicolò Castellini Baldissera, and our home here, a 1929 townhouse named Casa Tosca, is filled with a worldly mix of objects and furnishings that we designed. Outside, our terraced garden was overseen by Umberto Pasti, whose own Tangier residence is a collection of 1930s bungalows set within meandering gardens of datura trees, rambling roses, and alocasia. For us, so much of the pleasure of this place is being at home and having people over. Creating the setting with local craftspeople and market finds becomes a pastime in itself.

It’s the same for Marco Scarani and Jamie Creel, whose house, Dar Zero, has had an illustrious lineage of owners who have contributed to its rich layers. The home is in the casbah, with a bushy fig tree and bougainvillea spilling from its courtyard over the front wall. Inside, iron klismos chairs from when designers Yves Vidal and Charles Sevigny lived there sit on well-worn bejmat and zellige tiles. There are also pieces of their own creation, such as brass bird-foot candlesticks they make in Tangier and sell in their New York shop, Creel & Gow. “There is a lot of transmission from one generation to another,” Scarani says. “Friends here inherit objects and even homes. We live in houses that have the spirits of the people who came before.”

For designers Frank de Biasi and Gene Meyer, it was the relationship with artisans that prompted them a few years ago to open Habibi Burton, their boutique where they offer pillows, painted tables, and a rotation of decorative objects, all sourced locally. “Making things for the shop gives me more freedom than making things for clients,” says de Biasi. “Because we aren’t designing to a deadline, I can have four artisans contribute to a single piece.” He also recommends Las Chicas for fashion, Kasbah Collective for regional crafts, and the bookshop Les Insolites.

Down the street, artist and curator Hicham Bouzid oversees Kiosk, a cultural salon and shop that hosts talks and exhibitions bringing together creatives from Africa, the Arab world, and Europe. It has a selection of books and artist prints, as well as a restaurant and live music. Bouzid is one of the native Tanjawis who are animating the city’s cultural life. Another is Yto Barrada, perhaps Morocco’s best-known contemporary artist, who founded the popular Cinéma Rif, where screenings of contemporary and classic films share the program with talks and other cultural events.

Across town Clos Nadir, the house shared by designer Jean-Louis Deniot and William Holloway, exists in a different world, perched among pines and looking out across the strait. Focusing on spaces for entertaining, Deniot placed a 30-foot dining table on a roof, while his living room is outfitted with a large fireplace, plush carpets, and a welcoming mix of furniture, mostly made locally. “Tangier is a land of artistic opportunity, a place for aesthetes, where stylistic adventures, contemplation, and the freedom to create are central to daily life,” says the ELLE Decor A-List designer, who is opening an office here.

Tangier’s close-knit community of creatives comes alive during the summer, which is when Veere Greeney, another ELLE Decor A-List designer, hosts an annual theatrical performance in the gardens of his mountaintop villa, Gazebo. Producer Rob Ashford assembles actors from Broadway and the West End (last summer it was Dames at Sea, featuring Darren Criss and Julianne Hough), and the shows are open to the public, though tickets are limited. It’s a full sensory experience colored by the scent of jasmine and gardenias, one that captures the eccentric artistic allure of Tangier.

The energy and interest in the city continue to grow. Deniot is creating a hotel with theater producer Jordan Roth, the Four Seasons recently announced a new property on Malabata Bay, and King Mohammed VI is behind a luxurious renovation of the historic Hotel Continental.

And while there is excitement around these new projects, what appeals most about Tangier is something older. Over lunch on Grenney’s terrace, looking out at Spain, we discussed the city’s past lives. “What makes Tangier unique is its varied history and strategic position,” he observed. “The strait made it a city of diplomacy, openness, and inclusiveness. That is its bedrock.”

For the past two years, stargazers have waited with bated breath for a "new" light to appear in the night sky, born of one of the most cataclysmic events known to occur in the cosmos — a nova explosion. But what is the "Blaze Star" T. Coronae Borealis, and why is it so difficult to predict when its overdue brightening will occur? We turned to the experts to find out.

T. Coronae Borealis — or T CrB for short — isn't a single star, but rather a binary system containing a dense white dwarf star and a bloated red giant. Over a period of around 80 years, the white dwarf's gravity strips material from the red giant until it hits a critical threshold. Next comes a dramatic nova explosion that causes the star to flare to 1,000 times its usual brightness before gradually settling down and repeating the cycle.

"T CrB *will* erupt sometime soon," Bradley Schaefer of the Louisiana State University Department of Physics and Astronomy told Space.com in an email. "Everyone is very confident on this. But we cannot make high-accuracy measures of the rate of gas falling onto the surface of the white dwarf, and we do not have a high accuracy measure of the trigger mass for the big hydrogen bomb." Schaefer continued.

Historical observations reveal that the last nova eruption reached Earth in 1946, which places us well inside the window for another dramatic brightening of T. CrBb. However, the unpredictability of the white dwarf's feeding makes it difficult to pin down exactly when the explosion will become visible from Earth.

"The exact date of the upcoming T CrB eruption depends primarily on how fast the gas coming from its companion star is accumulating on the surface of the white dwarf," explained Schaefer. "When some specific trigger mass has accumulated, then T CrB will erupt. For the last 80 years, we have been watching the gas pile up on the white dwarf surface, and we know that it must be near the trigger threshold."

Astronomers have tried to predict T CrB's brightening by comparing a recent fluctuation in the light of the distant star system to a similar event that occurred in the runup to the 1946 event.

"There have already been some dimming and brightening episodes that caused excitement but did not yet turn into the main event," Elizabeth Hays, lab chief at NASA's Astroparticle Physics Laboratory at the Goddard Spaceflight Center, told Space.com in an email. "It is likely that the nova will happen in the next few years but we still don't know exactly when. I can't give you a guaranteed prediction, but I will be a little surprised if it gets to 2030, or 84 years past the last eruption."

Hays notes that the intense light from the red giant dramatically outshines the dimmer white dwarf, making detailed observation challenging. There is also no way to precisely measure the temperature and pressure of the matter lurking near the surface of the smaller star. "Predicting nova explosions are a little like predicting the weather; conditions may be right but the timing of the storm can be uncertain."

There'll be no visual warning of the eruption before it reaches Earth, though according to Hays there could be an early flash of light in the X-ray spectrum.

"The world's amateur astronomers have been doing an awesome job of keeping T CrB under hair-trigger monitoring roughly every minute or two, so when T CrB has brightened by around one magnitude, then alerts will spread around the world within minutes," said Schaefer.

Once the call goes out, telescopes and observatories around the world will kick into gear, running pre-prepared observation programmes to capitalize on the rare and cataclysmic event. "When the nuclear runaway process starts, it will take off and peak very quickly, in less than a day."

The nova will see the star jump from its normal brightness of magnitude +10 — which is beyond the detection limit of the naked eye — to magnitude +1.7, making it appear as if a star brighter than the North Star Polaris has suddenly burst to life in the night sky.

"The brightness will fall below naked-eye visibility in dark places within a couple of days and become too faint for binoculars within about a week," said Hays. "Outside the visible band, for example, in radio, X-ray and gamma rays, light caused by the eruption will be visible for months or even years. "

T CrB can be found glowing close to the magnitude +4.28 star Epsilon Coronae Borealis in the constellation Corona Borealis during the early evening in November, with the constellations of Hercules and Bootes on either side.

It will dip below the horizon for viewers in the U.S. just a few hours after sunset, before re-emerging into the northeastern sky in the predawn hours.

Why your laptop fans spin up even when you're not doing anything

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It could point to many things, including aggressive power profiles, background software updates or even dust building up.

Laptops generate heat. You can't really avoid it. Even energy-efficient models are still producing some level of heat. To try and keep things cool, your laptop fans should kick into action when you're doing something that pushes it hard, like gaming. By design, most laptops will ship with adjustable fan speeds that keep temperatures cool without too much noise. You'd usually expect the fans to quiet down, although they won't necessarily stop completely.

That isn't always the case, though. You might hear your laptop fans spin up even when you've closed all your apps. Power settings can have an impact, but so can dust and debris clogging up your case. You'll need to work out whether the noise is normal, because if it isn't, you'll need to fix it.

Closing the windows on your desktop doesn't stop everything happening on your laptop. Apps run in the background, with running processes that continue performing tasks under the hood without you realizing. These all create demands on your processor, RAM and storage. More power means more calls for the fan to spin.

Dell, for instance, recommends reviewing your startup apps to keep the loads down on your CPU, which should help prevent your fans from spinning up unnecessarily. You can review these in Task Manager by pressing Ctrl + Shift + Esc, selecting Startup apps, and disabling anything non-essential.

Other essential maintenance tasks will cause demands while your PC isn't active, too. Windows Update will do it, as will search indexing, which catalogs your files so that you can easily search for them later. That initial indexing process can take a few hours, according to Microsoft. The same applies to MacBooks, and you can use Activity Monitor to see which background apps are using the most power.

Imagine a set of scales. You can tip them one way or another, or get them perfectly balanced. Your laptop power settings work the same way. On a Windows laptop, for instance, you can switch between modes that prioritize energy efficiency, performance or a balance between the two. The most aggressive option, for best performance, will prioritize speed over saving power.

This can allow for higher power consumption under load, which'll produce more heat and make the fans more likely to kick in. You can change these settings on Windows, for instance, via Settings > System > Power & battery > Power mode. These settings will change when you've plugged in your laptop compared to when it's running on battery, too.

Your laptop might come with an app that lets you set your own cooling settings, even if you've changed Windows' own settings. For example, some Dell laptops ship with an ultra performance mode that'll increase processing power and cooling fan speeds. Switching to quiet mode reverses it, although that will reduce performance and push the temperatures up when you're using your laptop.

You'll only know what settings work best, as far as cooling is concerned, by trying them out. Try each setting at a time, then push it through your usual use. You'll want performance mode if you're doing something like gaming, because you'll want maximum power and cooling. For lighter work like typing up documents, you might manage better by switching to balanced or energy efficiency modes.

The amount of work you're doing on your laptop is only really part of the explanation. Your surroundings can have an impact. Apple, for instance, suggests that higher ambient temperatures will force fans to spin up sooner and run faster. A warm room makes it harder for your laptop to stay cool, and it'll produce different fan behavior as a result than a laptop in a cooler space.

It isn't just the air, though. Where you place your laptop is important, because you need good ventilation for your fans to lower temperatures and, as a result, work properly. Working from bed isn't great for the air from your laptop fans to circulate, for instance. With restricted airflow, temperatures will rise and the fans will keep working harder to try and compensate.

A hard, flat desk is a better option for the vents on your fan, but even better is if you can raise it up a little to give more space for the air to flow. Side and rear openings matter, too. Manufacturers like Dell and Apple recommend keeping any ventilation openings unblocked for the best performance.

Then there's dust and dirt. Air flowing through your laptop isn't just bringing in the air; it's bringing in the dust that might be in the air. This builds up over time. It collects around the vents and inside the cooling fans, and around the fan blades themselves. It raises the baseline, meaning your fans have to work harder and harder, even when you're performing the same tasks. If you keep your laptop fans clean from dirt, it'll help the air flow freely so the fans don't have to work as hard.

A good can of compressed air will help. You might be able to take the bottom panel from your laptop off to give it a more thorough clean, but in most cases, you might not need to. Use the can liberally to push as much of the dust out of your fans as possible to clean your laptop fan.

Changing the settings on your laptop isn't a miracle fix. If your fans are still spinning hard, even when your laptop isn't doing much, then you'll need to investigate a little deeper.

Don't rule out a hardware issue. If the laptop maintenance has fallen behind, you might start to hear persistent grinding or rattling, which would point to a worn-out or damaged fan. Cleaning won't help if that's the case. You'll need to check whether your laptop is still under warranty, or if it isn't, switch the fan out yourself.

Check that there aren't any software issues, though. Get the BIOS updates installed and any manufacturer-issued apps, like Dell's Dell Power Manager, to give you full control over the power settings. HP recommends that you have the latest BIOS update installed, along with chipset and graphics drivers, as your GPU can impact fan speeds.

Apple users can reset their System Management Controller if they're seeing unexpected heavy fan usage on an Intel MacBook, for instance. For newer Apple silicon MacBooks, the advice is a little simpler: just close and reopen the lid, or give it a restart.

There are some manufacturer-issue tests you can run through. For example, some Dell laptops have fan tests that you can work through during preboot diagnostics, which you can access by restarting the laptop, pressing F12 when the Dell logo appears, and then selecting Diagnostics.

You don't need to worry every time your laptop fans make themselves heard, though. Background tasks can kick them into action, but unless the noise is distracting, or you're hearing grinding or rattling, then you can usually leave them to do their job, as long as your laptop isn't overheating. Changing the settings and cleaning the vents could help, but if it doesn't, give your warranty a check and see if you can get your laptop checked for damage.

Companies attempting to go it alone in the stablecoin race could be left behind. For that reason, I'm also concerned about PayPal (PYPL +1.68%). The company has a rapidly growing stablecoin, PayPal USD (PYUSD -0.02%), but is not part of either financial consortium.

Both banks and credit card issuers are clearly responding to the threat posed by stablecoins. If stablecoins are able to offer highly attractive yields, they might be able to siphon away traditional bank deposits. If stablecoins become a mainstream form of payment, they might pose a threat to the big credit card giants.

That's one reason why there was so much debate over the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act ("Clarity Act") earlier this year. Banks and traditional financial institutions are doing everything they can to protect themselves against the rise of new rivals. From this perspective, both Project Faro and Open USD can be seen as defensive reactions to the exploding popularity of stablecoins.

How long will your NVIDIA GPU be supported?

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

NVIDIA can support GeForce GPUs for years, but Game Ready updates, security patches and OS support don't all end at once.

Graphics cards do not have an expiration date on the box. That would make it easier to upgrade, but much more depressing.

NVIDIA also does not guarantee a specific number of years of driver support for all GeForce GPUs. Rather, support is largely dependent on the architecture your card uses. At time of writing, Turing, Ampere, Ada and Blackwell GPUs are still on the normal Game Ready Driver track, which includes new features, game optimizations, performance improvements and bug fixes. That means that even though the RTX 2060 was released in 2019, it continues to receive regular driver updates.

Older cards have started moving down the ladder. In October 2025, Maxwell, Pascal and Volta GPUs stopped receiving Game Ready updates, but NVIDIA will continue to offer critical security updates until October 2028. Pascal and Volta had nine years of Game Ready support, whereas Maxwell lasted for 11 years.

That's a pretty respectable run, but what comes next and how could it impact your system?

NVIDIA does have a neatly defined three-phase software support lifecycle. Unfortunately, it's about NVIDIA vGPU software; not about the GeForce card in your gaming PC.

NVIDIA vGPU is a virtualization solution, mostly for enterprise use (data centers and professional deployments), that requires an active software or support subscription to receive software support. Phase I, also known as Full Support, includes new features, performance enhancements, security patches and bug fixes. Phase II is the next three years of Extended Full Support, with the same coverage, and Phase III is the final three years of Maintenance Support, where the GPU remains on its last supported software branch and receives maintenance, security fixes and bug fixes as NVIDIA sees fit.

GeForce support is far less ceremonial. NVIDIA has moved older architectures off its primary Game Ready branch, but doesn't publish a single lifespan for all generations.

That can also make model names confusing. The GTX 750 Ti is a Maxwell card and will continue to receive security updates until October 2028, whereas the more powerful GTX 780 Ti is based on the older Kepler architecture, whose published security-update window ended in September 2024. A good rule of thumb: bigger number equals older architecture. GPU naming is weird that way.

The end of Game Ready support sounds a lot more dramatic than it normally is.

Your graphics card does not suddenly refuse to boot up Windows or stop running all of the games you already have installed. Existing drivers continue to work. What goes away is the constant flow of optimizations for new games, feature additions, performance tweaks and regular bug fixes, which can become more noticeable as newer software expects newer drivers.

Security-only support is still meaningful though. NVIDIA has already issued dedicated Maxwell, Pascal and Volta security drivers during 2026, so these cards haven't simply been abandoned.

Another clock is ticking too: your operating system. The last full Game Ready and Studio driver for Windows 10 will be available in October 2026, according to NVIDIA. Windows 10 systems will get quarterly security updates and critical bug fixes until October 2029. So even a newer RTX GPU might lose the normal Game Ready update stream due to Windows and not the hardware.

For an RTX 2060 owner, driver support alone is not much of a reason to start looking for a new GPU. Performance and VRAM limits or a lack of access to newer features may become bigger issues first. NVIDIA hasn't confirmed a new GeForce generation for 2027 either, despite reports previously pointing to late-2027 mass production for its next architecture.

It wasn’t the plane’s first flight. The CE-25A had already completed 19 autonomous test flights with nobody on board since February 2, and China’s aviation regulator is now reviewing it for a type certificate.

The CE-25A is a T-tail commuter plane with 10 electric ducted fans on the wings, five per side, instead of propellers. COMAC lists a 300 km/h cruise speed and a 400 km (250-mile) range.

The CAAC accepting COMAC’s type certificate application starts the airworthiness review. It doesn’t mean paying passengers are close, and COMAC hasn’t given a date for commercial service.

The program has been quiet for a long time. COMAC showed a CE-25A model at the China International Import Expo in Shanghai in November 2023, with a placard listing 400 to 500 km of range and a 400 km/h top cruise speed. The full-size aircraft, registered B-0EFH, only became public when photos leaked on Chinese social media on October 28.

The obvious battery candidate is CATL. In July 2023, COMAC, CATL, and Shanghai Jiao Tong University formed a joint venture with CNY 600 million in registered capital to develop electric aircraft, as we reported when CATL launched its aviation unit.

CATL had just unveiled its “condensed” battery at the time, claiming up to 500 Wh/kg at the cell level, and said it was testing the cells to aviation standards. Neither company has confirmed what’s inside the CE-25A, though.

~500 Wh/kg has long been seen as the target for battery energy density to enable electric flight on a commercial scale.

COMAC isn’t alone in flying a large electric demonstrator. In August, Heart Aerospace flew its X1 in Plattsburgh, New York, for 27 minutes. It’s a 25,000-lb aircraft with a 106-foot wingspan, which Heart calls the largest battery-electric aircraft ever flown.

Why do birds flap before landing? It may be about more than slowing down

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

New calculations suggest birds flap during landing not just to control speed, but to stop within limited space.

Most birds flap their wings as they come in to land, but those final wingbeats may do more than slow them down. New calculations suggest they also help birds stop within a tight space.

A bird the size of a pigeon could glide to a complete stop with its wings held still.

To manage it, though, the bird would have to begin its landing maneuver at about 80 mph (36 meters per second). It would also have to climb roughly 65 feet (20 meters), the height of a six-story building.

Flapping, the researchers propose, lets a bird stop inside the space it has.

Tom Rottier carried out the study at the University of Manchester and the University of Toronto Institute for Aerospace Studies. He worked with Philippe Lavoie of Toronto and Ben Parslew of Manchester.

No birds were flown for the work. Instead, equations were written for a bird gliding on fixed wings.

Only gravity, lift, and air resistance acted on this model bird. The team then solved the equations for the slowest possible touchdown.

The forces on a wing depend on how fast the wing moves through the air. Those forces shrink as a bird slows down. Its control over where it goes shrinks with them.

Arriving fast is no better. Whatever speed is left at touchdown is usually absorbed by the legs. A bad landing can damage muscle and bone, or it can kill the bird.

The difficulty, said Parslew, an aerospace engineering lecturer at Manchester, is that “landing isn’t as simple as just slowing down.”

A wing also has a minimum working speed. In level flight, the bird’s whole weight has to be carried by lift. A wing that moves too slowly cannot make enough of it. Engineers call that minimum the stall speed.

The stall speed is not fixed, however, as the team’s equations show. A bird that climbs more steeply puts less of its weight on its wings. The stall speed drops as a result. In a climb straight up, it is zero.

A climb that ends straight up is how a gliding bird could stop without flapping. The bird comes in fast and pulls up. It then climbs more and more steeply while gravity and air resistance slow it down.

At the top, the bird is pointed straight up and has no speed left.

More lift also means more drag, and drag takes away the speed the climb depends on. For that reason, the tilt of the wings has to keep changing on the way up. By the end, the wings are turned broadside to the air.

The team calculated what such a landing would take for a rock pigeon weighing 14 ounces (400 grams).

Starting from level flight, the bird would need that 80 mph speed. This is more than double a pigeon’s typical cruising speed.

The bird would travel about 200 feet (60 meters) horizontally, roughly two-thirds of a football field, while climbing 65 feet.

City pigeons fly between buildings. A landing of that size is not feasible in many of those places, the team wrote. Bigger birds would need even more room.

The researchers therefore propose that flapping is not simply a way to slow down. A glide can do that. Flapping does it in a small space.

A perch can be approached from below, but the ground cannot. That changes the answer.

The researchers then tested landings in which the model bird could not climb at any point. No path brought it to a stop.

The slowest landing allowed by the equations came in at an angle of about 34 degrees below level.

A bird that glides down to the ground therefore arrives with some speed left. That speed has to be removed by flapping, by braking with the legs, or by both.

The legs need enough muscle and bone to absorb the impact. But that adds weight the bird must carry in flight.

The researchers suggest this trade-off may have shaped how birds evolved. The need to withstand landing impacts may have set a minimum strength for their legs.

Leftover speed may also help explain why ducks, geese, and swans land differently on water than on solid ground.

More of a bird’s energy is absorbed by water than by the ground. The bird can therefore arrive faster.

Some birds of prey do finish a landing without flapping.

A 2022 study observed Harris’s hawks flying between two perches. They learned to dive low and then glide up to the perch.

A wing that is tilted very steeply is stalled, meaning air no longer flows smoothly over it.

The authors of that study proposed an explanation for the swoop. In their view, it keeps the distance a hawk flies with stalled wings as short as possible.

Rottier’s team tested the idea by giving their model bird one goal at a time. For this test, the model bird was allowed to flap during the dive, as the hawks did.

First, it was told only to spend as little time stalled as possible. The model bird climbed and then came down onto the perch. It arrived at speeds that would harm a real hawk, the team wrote.

Next, the only goal was to land as slowly as possible. This time the model bird swooped deep.

A mix of the two goals generally matched the real hawks’ paths best. Touchdown speed did more than stall time to produce the swoop.

Diving builds speed, and speed allows a steeper climb. A steeper climb ends in a slower arrival.

The pigeon figures come from equations, not from a bird in the air. In its main form, the model has no wind, no body or tail, and no flapping.

A wing gets a brief boost in lift and drag when it tilts up quickly. The wings of landing birds do this, yet the boost is also left out of the model. To see how much it could matter, the team doubled the model’s maximum lift.

For a slow touchdown, the starting speed the bird needed fell by 44%. The forward distance fell by 65% and the climb by 55%. When the maximum drag was doubled instead, the speed needed went up.

A living bird may therefore be able to glide to a stop in less room than the pigeon numbers suggest. How much less cannot be said from the model.

The equations were written so that wind can be added later. The team also described the model as a base for studies of single species.

The same physics may apply to animals that never flap, such as flying squirrels and flying snakes. It may also apply to drones built to land on perches.

The full study was published in the journal Proceedings of the Royal Society A.

The 8 Best Soft Sci-Fi Movies in Film History

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The label 'soft sci-fi' refers to sci-fi stories that shift the focus away from strict scientific plausibility and instead place a premium on people, culture, and society. They're not that interested in explaining the nitty-gritty of their advanced tech or alien creatures. The speculative elements exist to bring out the themes or reveal something about human nature.

With that in mind, this list looks at some of the best movies in this subgenre, from intimate romances grand to space operas, the wacky time-travel of Back to the Future to the existential cybernoir of Blade Runner. Bangers, all.

“Meet me in Montauk.” Although this one has a speculative premise involving fictional technology, it's really a story about relationships. After discovering that his former girlfriend Clementine (Kate Winslet) has undergone a procedure to remove her memories of their relationship, main character Joel (Jim Carrey) decides to do the same. Most of the film then unfolds inside his collapsing memories as the procedure progresses, which writer Charlie Kaufman and director Michel Gondry (both masters of the offbeat) cleverly present in non-linear order.

Joel revisits arguments, disappointments, tenderness, embarrassment, and ordinary moments that seemed insignificant when they happened. In the process, he begins to realize that losing the painful parts of the relationship also means losing everything meaningful about it. As a result, Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind winds up being a madcap but surprisingly touching psychodrama.

“The heart's not like a box that gets filled up. It expands in size the more you love.” This is one of the most perceptive movies ever made about AI, precisely because it avoids the usual Skynet-like 'evil robot' storyline. Instead, we get something more wistful and intimate. Joaquin Phoenix leads the cast as Theodore Twombly, a lonely writer living in a near-future Los Angeles who installs an advanced operating system that names herself Samantha (voiced by Scarlett Johansson).

Samantha learns extraordinarily quickly, developing curiosity, humor, insecurity, desire, and an increasingly complex relationship with Theodore. Director Spike Jonze refuses to treat that romance as a simple joke about a man falling in love with his computer. Ultimately, Her paints a compellingly prescient picture of isolation in the digital age, as well as a cautionary tale about the inability of any machine to truly replicate human connection.

“Roads? Where we're going, we don't need roads.” Back to the Future devotes remarkably little effort to making time travel scientifically credible. Doc Brown (Christopher Lloyd) invents the flux capacitor after hitting his head on a bathroom sink, installs it inside a DeLorean, and discovers that traveling through time requires reaching 88 miles per hour. That's essentially all audiences need to know, because director Robert Zemeckis understands that explaining the physics would only slow down the endlessly entertaining, ingeniously constructed plot.

The solution requires our likable hero Marty McFly (Michael J. Fox) to play matchmaker for two teenagers who will eventually become his own mother and father, while simultaneously finding enough power to return home. The time travel itself makes no real sense and functions more like something out of a fairy tale than a typical sci-fi flick, but that's all part of the charm.

“Everything that comes true is an expression of your true wishes.” This cryptic gem from Andrei Tarkovsky bears some sci-fi trappings, but approaches them with a meditative sensibility. In Stalker, three men (Alexander Kaidanovsky, Anatoly Solonutsyn, and Nikolai Grinko) are sent into an eerily abandoned landscape known as the Zone, where the normal rules of reality may no longer apply. The Stalker makes his living illegally guiding people into this forbidden territory.

His latest clients are known simply as the Writer and the Professor, both of whom want to reach a mysterious Room supposedly capable of granting a person's deepest desire. Their journey gradually becomes less about reaching a physical destination than confronting what each man actually believes. In other words, this is philosophical sci-fi where clear answers are in desperately short supply. It's poetic and elliptical, leaving the viewer to draw their own conclusions.

“Dreams make good stories, but everything important happens when we're awake.” The original Dune novels are landmarks of soft sci-fi, almost science fantasy, really, and Denis Villeneuve did a great job of carrying that over to his film adaptations, while still making them feel modern. He weaves a colorful, thematically rich tale of prophecy and personality cults, focused on Paul (Timothée Chalamet), the scion of House Atreides, ordered to take control of Arrakis.

Although the story takes place in a far-flung future, the vibe is mythological rather than coldly high-tech. (Indeed, we don't see any digital devices at all). We get grnithopters, shields, spice harvesting machines, and interstellar travel, but the movie doesn't get bogged down in explaining how it all works. Instead, the emphasis is on imperialism, political manipulation, ecological exploitation, religion, and Paul's growing awareness that the visions surrounding him may have horrifying consequences.

“Unfortunately, no one can be told what the Matrix is. You have to see it for yourself.” The Matrix contains enough philosophy, martial arts, cyberpunk imagery, religious symbolism, and leather coats to fuel decades of interpretation. Keanu Reeves turns in perhaps his most iconic performance as Neo, a programmer who learns that humanity has been imprisoned by machines inside a simulated reality, all so they can be exploited for the machines' ends.

It's a killer premise that draws on real problems from philosophy, which the Wachowskis gleefully fuse with ideas borrowed from anime, Hong Kong action cinema, cyberpunk literature, and mythology. So much of the movie has become world-famous (like the red and blue pills), and its vision of a future where people live entirely in an online, digital world manipulated by bots now feels uncomfortably close to home.

“Do. Or do not. There is no try.” Few sequels build so well on their predecessor's foundation. A New Hope gave us a galaxy of Jedi, droids, smugglers, and impossible spacecraft, and The Empire Strikes Back gave those ideas a darker, deeper treatment. Here, the heroes lose repeatedly, and those failures make their eventual journey more meaningful. After the Rebel Alliance is driven from its base on Hoth, the central characters are separated.

Han (Harrison Ford) and Leia (Carrie Fisher) flee the Empire aboard the Millennium Falcon while Luke (Mark Hamill) travels to Dagobah to train under Yoda. Again, the aesthetic verges on science fantasy. Faster-than-light travel, giant walking tanks, artificial intelligence, and inhabited asteroid fields exist alongside the mystical Force. What matters is how those elements serve the characters. Then comes one of cinema's most famous revelations, completely altering Luke's understanding of both his enemy and himself.

“It's too bad she won't live! But then again, who does?” The plot of Blade Runner has noir bones in a futuristic skin: former police officer Rick Deckard (Harrison Ford) is tasked with hunting replicants, bioengineered beings created for dangerous labor and prohibited from living on Earth. This mission unfolds in a future overflowing with extraordinary technology, yet Ridley Scott is far less interested in how any of it works than in atmosphere and theme.

The key question is: what does humanity mean once technology can reproduce something almost indistinguishable from a person? That idea turns a noir detective story into one of the most haunting sci-fi movies ever made. A big part of that is thanks to Rutger Hauer's brilliant performance as replicant leader Roy Batty. Roy is frightening, charismatic, and ultimately deeply tragic, making this a story of ideas rather than gadgets.

Enterprise blockchain is going to come back from the dead

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

With scalability, privacy tools and onchain liquidity improving, businesses may finally have the infrastructure to make enterprise blockchain work.

Opinion by Paul Brody, founder and chief executive, Nightfall Networks

Do you remember Pets.com? It raised $110 million from Amazon and others to build a business where people ordered pet toys and dog food online.

The company crashed and burned, and everyone learned an important lesson: you can’t profitably ship a 50-pound bag of dog food to a customer’s door.

As online retail entrepreneur Ed Baum asked in a 2001 postmortem: “Did Pets.com’s business plan assume the invention of transporters to magically move bags of pet food without shipping costs?”

Please, whatever you do, don’t share this with the people at Chewy; they might not realize their business model doesn’t work. Chewy reported $12.6 billion in sales and $223 million in net income in its latest fiscal year.

The real lesson of Pets.com wasn’t that shipping dog food was a bad idea. It’s that the idea arrived before the logistics, customer habits and unit economics required to make it work.

As it was with chew toys and dog food, so it may be with enterprise blockchains.

Collectively, the crypto industry probably incinerated just as much money trying that between 2017 and 2023 as Pets.com. Maybe more. But the dozens of companies that went after enterprise blockchain were not delusional; they were just too early.

It’s not hard to understand the appeal of enterprise blockchain solutions, at least superficially. Nearly all enterprise systems today tend to fail at the boundaries between companies. There are two failure modes: mode 1, the OG failure mode, where collaboration information is constantly out of sync.

In this world, enterprise IT systems are islands, communicating asynchronously with each other by digital text messages and emailed spreadsheets. It’s cheap and easy to set up, if you don’t mind always having slightly wrong information.

Related: Privacy is missing ingredient for business blockchain adoption: EY exec

Failure mode two is the costly, real-time proprietary integration. Powerful industry leaders politely request their key suppliers and partners to join their closed but always-on networks.

Chewy reported $223 million in net income in its latest fiscal year. Source: Chewy.

These are very costly, but they work well and leave everyone with a kind of business version of Stockholm syndrome, where you come to appreciate the thoroughness and efficiency of the digital choke-hold you are in.

Blockchains offered a better path forward: shared information and business logic across an open ecosystem. All the benefits of a proprietary system in terms of real-time visibility and efficiency, without the lock-in.

Sometime around 2017, this started to look like a very obvious good idea. Investment poured in, consortia launched, press releases were drafted and released, and then pretty much everything crashed and burned. TradeLens, We.Trade, Contour, B3i, ASX Chess, Everledger. All came and went in just a few years.

Though there is a lot of nuance to the details of each of these stories, they all largely have one thing in common: they had to do unnatural things to make up for the lack of privacy available natively in blockchains.

No company wants their competition to see what they’re doing in detail. Mostly, this meant building a private/permissioned chain and then doing lots of non-blockchain-like things to it and in it to try to keep information private. The results were uniformly slow and expensive and failed to gain traction.

It’s hard to understate the bloodbath of companies and startups that tried and failed to make enterprise blockchain work. A quick web and history search turned up 18 different ones, some of them well-known.

Many had big-name backers, from major banks like Deutsche Bank and HSBC to technology companies like IBM. Pedigreed parents and big brands made no difference — privacy didn’t work and the results were dismal.

What few survivors of this are left have “de-chained” themselves — giving up the blockchain theater and reverting to a classic software-as-a-service (SaaS) model. Many companies that tried and tested these permissioned enterprise systems as users came away from their experience with a clear learning: we tried blockchain, it didn’t work.

That’s unfortunate because everything is different now.

Blockchains have scale. Ethereum in 2020 handled 1 million transactions a day and costs per transaction periodically spiked to $30-50 when demand was high. Today, most transactions get done under a penny and capacity is ramping toward billions of transactions a day. Nobody talks about the “trilemma” anymore. Ethereum is decentralized and it is scaled.

Related: Ethereum’s Glamsterdam upgrade launches on Sepolia testnet

Privacy is different now, too. While privacy is still not the default setting, a huge range of privacy tools and systems are now available, from privacy pools to zero-knowledge roll-ups and full privacy chains.

On top of that, Ethereum is building a range of tools that will add basic elements of privacy at multiple levels in the network. The result: it’s now possible to run business operations at scale with a high degree of privacy.

Finally, there’s the money. It’s all moving onchain. Regulators around the globe have blessed stablecoins and the result is a tsunami of liquidity, with transaction volume reaching almost $1.8 trillion in June.

If we combine the operational benefits of blockchains with the financial ones, we get something that enterprises have never really had before: a single ecosystem where you can not only buy, sell, and move assets, but get paid for them as well.

There was just under $1.8 trillion in adjusted stablecoin transaction volume in June. Source: Visa

The return on investment from every single component of this equation is big. Combined, they can yield colossal advantages. McKinsey partners even found that digital supply chain tools could make inventory reductions of 35-75% possible through better planning.

More conservatively, from my experience in the field, just switching away from the existing cascades of spreadsheets and electronic data interchange (EDI) messages can allow firms to run most supply chains with about 20% less inventory at the same service level. That’s worth billions in working capital in most industries.

It gets better from there. Smart contracts reduce the time required to handle many business contract rules from days to minutes and cut the costs of administration in half.

Microsoft began moving its Xbox video game network onto smart contracts for handling royalties and cut the time to access monthly royalty statements from 45 days to four minutes. All that while handling more than 2 million transactions a day.

And once you have inventories, contracts, purchase orders and invoices onchain, you can go after working capital directly as well.

When you can prove your purchase order originates from a top-quality buyer, you can access credit for less. And when you have full visibility into inventory and delivery status, you can factor receivables immediately and efficiently.

The possibilities and the returns are big. The use cases are obvious. It’s time to try again.

These are some of the strongest 3D printer filaments for durable parts

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Stronger filament doesn't automatically mean a stronger print, and the toughest materials can require better hardware too.

If you're looking for one winner, it's not exactly convenient that a 3D print can be "strong" in a few different ways. A rigid bracket should resist bending, while a part that gets knocked around may last longer if it can flex and recover instead of breaking. In this article, we're going to hone in on rigidity and the types of materials that work well for structural parts.

Carbon-fiber-reinforced nylon (PA-CF) and polycarbonate (PC) are two of the strongest practical materials for the kind of filament-based 3D printers that are accessible to the average maker. Both materials can handle demanding functions, higher temperatures and considerably rougher treatment than everyday polylactic acid (PLA) or glycol-modified polyethylene terephthalate (PETG).

Sure, there are stronger, more expensive specialty materials that could vie for the title of heavyweight filament champ. But let's stick to the pros and cons of carbon-fiber nylon versus polycarbonate, so you can decide which material packs the most punch for your projects.

Carbon-fiber-reinforced nylon (PA-CF) combines nylon with short carbon fibers — hence the name. Nylon already handles demanding functional parts well, and the added fibers make the finished material stiffer and more resistant to bending under load. How resistant, you ask?

Bambu Lab's dry-state test puts its carbon-fiber-reinforced nylon 6 (PA6-CF) at 151 megapascals (MPa) of bending strength. Its heat-deflection temperature is 186 degrees Celsius, meaning it resisted deformation under a specified load until around that temperature. Those figures help explain why carbon-fiber nylon is used for brackets, fixtures and other working parts instead of for items that simply sit pretty on a shelf.

A strong filament, however, does not necessarily make a strong print. How you orient the object on the print bed, plus wall thickness, infill and part shape, can significantly change how much abuse the project can take. Bambu Lab does not claim the 151 MPa result applies to every finished print. Pricing is another factor to note, as Polymaker currently lists a 1kg spool of its Fiberon PA6-CF20 at $52.99 at time of writing

There is some hardware housekeeping to contemplate too. Carbon fibers are abrasive, so your old faithful brass nozzle may not last long. Bambu recommends a hardened steel nozzle, requires PA6-CF to be dried before use, and lists a nozzle temperature of 260 to 290 degrees Celsius with an enclosed printer when using this material.

The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) says fused-filament desktop printers can emit respiratory irritants and ultrafine particles, so good ventilation or filtration is important to consider.

Unlike carbon-fiber composites, polycarbonate (PC) produces strong parts without chopped fibers. It's a stiff engineering plastic with good heat resistance, making it suitable for housings, brackets and other parts that need to withstand more than light use.

Once again, you'll need to consider your equipment and workspace before deciding if PC is the best and strongest filament for you. Similar to PA-CF, Polymaker recommends a nozzle temperature of 250 to 270 degrees Celsius, a 90 to 105 degrees Celsius heated bed, and an enclosed printer. The company says large, dense parts require a heated chamber, which helps reduce uneven cooling and warping. Good workplace ventilation or exhaust is recommended.

When it comes to strength, print direction matters, too. Polymaker measured 69.1 MPa of tensile strength across the printed layers and 52.8 MPa between them. PC's bending strength clocked in at 106 MPa, a drop compared to the 151 MPa logged for PA-CF.

The big difference with PC versus PA-CF is the price. Polymaker lists PolyLite PC at $29.99 for a 1kg spool and PolyLite PLA at $19.99at time of writing. The bigger pain point could be your printer, since lower-cost models may struggle to maintain the temperatures PC prefers.

Search for a free “independent assister” on healthcare.gov to better understand your insurance options.

Shrago started by asking Mikunda about his income. How much money you expect to make in the coming year determines whether you’re eligible for reduced monthly payments. The system uses a sliding scale for households making less than 400% of the federal poverty rate — which means, for 2027 enrollees, an individual with no children can make up to $63,480 and still qualify for a discount.

A person in their mid-20s making $50,000, for instance, would get an average 23% discount, according to KFF’s subsidy calculator. For someone making $30,000, the tax credit would cover nearly three-quarters of the cost.

It’s important to estimate next year’s income as accurately as possible. If you end up making more than anticipated, you may owe money back to the government.

Short-Term Affordability vs. Long-Term Needs

It’s a personal decision about financial planning and risk tolerance, balancing up-front payments with out-of-pocket costs. Shrago also recommends starting the selection process early enough to step away and think.

“If you’re dealing with something that is uncertain enough and you feel really awkward about it, you don’t need to make a decision today,” she said. “Take your time and think about it.”

Assisters tend to have the least availability right before the federal marketplace’s Jan. 15 deadline, so reach out early.

The Trump administration cut funding for independent assisters and navigator nonprofits by roughly 90% in 2025. When making that announcement, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services said the program was inefficient, costing more than $1,000 per person enrolled. The reduction in funding means fewer people helping. In Ohio, for example, the number of paid navigators serving state residents dropped from 50 to five at the start of open enrollment in 2025.

Gen Z believes using AI is making their colleagues dumb and lazy—but may paradoxically see it as key to their own promotion, Wharton says

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Last year, researchers from Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon University revealed startling evidence about the impact of using AI on how—and how hard—people think, finding that among more than 300 knowledge workers, leaning too much on AI tools like ChatGPT was associated with diminished critical thinking skills.

The study, mirrored by results from MIT-led research, suggested that even using AI for low-stakes tasks such as proofreading “can lead to significant negative outcomes in high-stakes contexts,” like writing legal documents, the study authors wrote.

For the young generation of digital natives navigating AI anxiety around keeping up with peers using the technology and AI displacing them from jobs, the fear of the technology making people dumber is dominant. But that hasn’t stopped them from using AI—even when they’re explicitly told not to.

A Wharton-led survey published earlier this year, conducted in partnership with Gallup and the Walton Family Foundation, found young people are ramping up their AI use, even as their concerns about it causing lazy thinking persist. A survey of nearly 2,500 U.S. adults between the ages of 18 and 28 completed in October 2025 found 79% of respondents believed AI makes people lazier, and 62% said they had concerns it makes people less smart.

“What we find is deep ambivalence on how Gen Z is thinking [about] using AI,” Benjamin Lira Luttges, a postdoctoral scholar at Wharton who led research for the report, told Fortune.

Despite these fears, Gen Zers have increased their AI usage. Pew Research Center data published in June found 66% of people ages 18 to 29 have ever used an AI chatbot, such as ChatGPT, up from 55% in 2024. However, 37% of those young adults said they believed the impact of AI on them over the next 20 years would be negative, the most pessimistic attitude of any age group surveyed.

The paradox of Gen Z’s willingness to use AI at the office, even amid persistent worries about the technology’s impact on critical thinking, lays bare the young generation’s complicated feelings toward AI, according to the report’s authors. After all, Gen Z’s fraught relationship with AI runs deep. Nearly one-fifth of the generation is worried about AI displacing them at work, yet they lead the workplace in AI adoption.

Though they require some decoding, Gen Z’s tangled attitudes toward AI can be critical in designing a path forward for the technology, more broadly, to be best integrated into the workplace, Lira Luttges suggested.

“Young people lead the adoption of new technologies, and a lot of things that are often seen as fringe, as not mainstream, are adopted by young people and eventually become part of the mainstream,” he said. “So in a sense … looking at Gen Z is a way of looking towards the future of work.”

Lira Luttges speculates the biggest mental factor informing Gen Z’s attitudes toward AI is simply a bias toward immediate gratification, a disposition more prominent in younger, developing minds.

“There’s a legitimate tradeoff between benefits and costs that you get from using AI,” he said. “Our brains are wired to prefer smaller, immediate rewards versus long-term, delayed rewards.”

As Gen Z grapples with finding or keeping jobs, as well as scaling their career ladders, job performance bolstered by an AI boost may hold more appeal than the less tangible threat of critical thinking skills loss. Similarly, even if an employer does not want an employee using AI for certain tasks, those workers, particularly if young, might consider getting their tasks done efficiently as more important, particularly if the risk of getting caught is slow, Lira Luttges noted.

Anyone—not just Gen Z—could also fall victim to the better-than-average effect, a statistically impossible phenomenon of most people generally believing they are above average at a certain task. Gen Z survey respondents, for example, may see themselves as AI power-users, Lira Luttges said. Sure, AI could atrophy critical thought capabilities and make other people lazy, but not those filling out the survey.

To maximize how AI is used in the workplace, employers should not ban AI, but rather embrace ambivalence toward it, the report authors argued. According to the survey, respondents who reported using AI more frequently worried less about its impact on intelligence and motivation, indicating AI anxiety may resolve over time.

But resolving AI anxiety doesn’t address the question of AI use impacting critical thought. Some future-of-work experts, including Mark Beasley, professor and director at North Carolina State University’s Poole College of Management, believe a critical thinking gap, not an AI skills gap, will pose a serious threat to organizational pipelines and business operations. Beasley told Fortune the threat AI poses to entry-level jobs could mean insufficient training and experience for middle- and eventually upper-tier positions in the near future.

“The biggest risk organizations face is just being stagnant,” he said.

But as long as workplaces are intentional about how they implement AI, Lira Luttges said the technology won’t have a significant impact on critical thinking.

“For every task, there are two kinds of efforts,” Lira Luttges said. “There is effort that is germane to the task, that is intrinsic to the thing that you’re doing, and that kind of effort is the effort that you put in, and gets translated into learning. But there’s a lot of effort that is just there, that’s just like friction, that doesn’t really teach you anything.”

“You should outsource the crap, not the craft,” he added.

“It’s got to be a pretty high bar to keep me away from the family.”

Clippers rookie Keaton Wagler draws praise from Shai Gilgeous-Alexander

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Keaton Wagler continues to find his footing in his first season with the Los Angeles Clippers.

The fifth overall pick in the 2026 draft has found a way to contribute to coach Tyronn Lue's team, averaging 12.4 points, 3.4 rebounds and 3.2 assists through 11 games played this season.

The rookie has not made his first official start but has averaged 22.7 minutes per game off the bench.

Wagler modeled part of his game after Shai Gilgeous-Alexander, who spent his rookie season with the Clippers in 2018 before he was later traded to the Oklahoma City Thunder.

Being in the NBA has afforded Wagler opportunities early in the season to play against some of his idols with the Clippers having played the Thunder twice this season.

"It was a cool experience," Wagler told reporters about being able to play against Gilgeous-Alexander. "Growing up I really liked Shai, his game and being able to go up against him."

Wagler has drawn praise from Gilgeous-Alexander after those two meetings.

"He has good feel. Can really shoot," Gilgeous-Alexander said about the rookie after the Clippers' 113-112 victory against the Thunder on Friday, Nov. 13. "Usually when you have those things, the game will slow down for you eventually.

"He's in a good situation where he can play a lot. There are good coaches over there, so he'll learn the game the right way. As long as he works hard, he should have a long runway to be whoever he wants to be."

The Clippers have struggled to start the season, winning just three of their first 11 games, including Friday's victory against the Thunder.

Earlier this month, Lue apologized to the rookie for not getting him more playing time.

"That's just what I felt like," Lue said about Wagler's minutes after a 117-116 overtime loss to the Miami Heat on Nov. 4.

Wagler played a season-low 16 minutes against Miami. He finished with 11 points and a team-high +15 while on the court.

Lue told reporters on Nov. 8 that he had apologized to Wagler after having rewatched the film from the game, telling him he earned more playing time than he received.

Wagler averaged 19.6 minutes per game over the last three games since Lue's apology.
