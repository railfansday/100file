GSK won U.S. approval for its second lung cancer drug from the Nuvalent deal

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The FDA granted accelerated approval to neladalkib, sold as Nelkarmi, for ALK-positive non-small cell lung cancer

GSK won accelerated approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration on Tuesday for neladalkib, a lung cancer drug sold as Nelkarmi.

The FDA cleared the kinase inhibitor for adults with locally advanced or metastatic anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK)-positive non-small cell lung cancer. Eligible patients have received one prior ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitor consisting of alectinib, or two or more prior ALK inhibitors and platinum-based chemotherapy, the agency said.

GSK gained neladalkib through its $10.6 billion acquisition of Nuvalent, according to Bloomberg. The deal closed in July, according to Reuters.

The acquisition also brought Jideytro, which the FDA approved on July 22 for adults with locally advanced or metastatic ROS1-positive non-small cell lung cancer who have received at least one prior ROS1 inhibitor. Nelkarmi is the second of the two lung cancer drugs from the deal to win approval, according to Reuters.

The FDA granted the approval on an accelerated basis because an early-stage trial found notable anti-cancer effects and a safety profile resembling that of current ALK-targeting drugs, according to Bloomberg. A later-phase trial meant to establish how much the drug ultimately helps patients is still ongoing, Bloomberg said.

The approval gives patients who have run through the available targeted therapies another option, according to Reuters. For those patients, the principal fallback has frequently been chemotherapy.

"I think we're making great progress with the oncology portfolio," Hesham Abdullah, GSK's head of oncology R&D, said in a briefing, according to Bloomberg. He said GSK is assembling a franchise of lung cancer drugs, and that it has additional medicines in development for blood and gastrointestinal cancers.

GSK reported higher third-quarter sales and core operating profit last month, with oncology sales up 23% to £600 million ($786 million). CEO Luke Miels cited Jideytro among three new U.S. specialty medicine approvals in recent months, alongside Hibsago for chronic hepatitis B and Jemperli for rectal cancer.

Oncology is a business GSK exited a decade ago, according to Reuters. Miels is leading its return to the field, and the Nuvalent purchase marks the boldest step GSK has taken so far toward re-entering oncology.

5 Legendary Rock Bands With Only One Perfect Album, Ranked

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

It can take years for a rock band to finally get radio play, but for a lucky few exceptionally talented acts, all it takes is one album to make a lasting impression. It's not every day that a band comes together, puts out a single project, and then leaves it all behind. Whether it's a side project, an inability to maintain the same chemistry beyond the debut, or simply a matter of unfortunate circumstances, there's something fascinating about bands with only one album to their name.

Arguably, a one-album discography can represent some of the most honest work in a band's career, seizing its artistry at its rawest. There's also the pressure of making that one record count, leaving listeners with something truly memorable. Without further ado, here are five of the greatest rock bands with only one perfect album, ranked.

Following the massive MTV success of Enema of the State and Take Off Your Pants and Jacket, it was only a matter of time before fans (and their record label) expected Blink-182 to follow up with another hit-making album built around their tried-and-true three-power-chord formula. But for members Tom DeLonge and Travis Barker, they needed an outlet to explore heavier sounds and darker subject matter—something that contrasted with Blink-182's rambunctious energy, immature antics, and crass humor. Enter their side project, Box Car Racer. Originally conceived as a creative outlet during a break from touring, the short-lived project gave DeLonge and Barker room to branch out musically and experiment with a different sound, something they would continue exploring in later Blink-182 releases and with DeLonge’s future band, Angels & Airwaves.

Inspired by DeLonge's interest in post-hardcore bands like Fugazi, Refused, and Quicksand, Box Car Racer ditches the fast, chord-driven riffs he was accustomed to playing in favor of heavier guitar textures, distorted riffs, and drop-D tuning—all channeled into their self-titled album. While traces of his pop-punk roots remain unmistakable, the band isn't chasing catchy hooks designed to dominate radio airplay. Instead, explores darker themes ranging from apocalyptic destruction to Freemasonry—definitely a far cry from singing about teenage break ups and personal insecurities.

The Los Angeles hardcore punk scene owes a lot to The Germs. Their original run may have lasted only four years in the late '70s, but the band helped lay the groundwork for the scene, leaving an influence that would carry on for decades. The brainchild of lead vocalist Darby Crash and guitarist Pat Smear (who would later play with Nirvana and the Foo Fighters), The Germs drew inspiration from a surprisingly wide range of artists, including Iggy Pop, The Ramones, and The Sex Pistols. Their only studio album, GI, is regarded as one of the earliest full-length hardcore punk records and is notably produced by Joan Jett of The Runaways.

GI proves that hardcore's unrestrained chaos doesn't have to come at the expense of structure or artistic expression. Rather than reducing the genre to theatrical antics and one-minute bursts of noise with barely intelligible lyrics, The Germs showed that hardcore could be just as expressive and musically thoughtful as any other genre. Hardcore punk may have been at the heart of their sound, but it was their frenetic aggression, both on record and during notoriously chaotic live performances that sometimes got them kicked offstage, that cemented their status as legends of the first generation of Los Angeles punk bands.

Their time in the industry might be short-lived, but The La's got the world singing in karaoke thanks to their hit track "There She Goes," which has become an anthem for many romantic comedies. For a bunch of Brit lads making jolly, Merseybeat sing-along tunes à la The Kinks, The Who, and The Rolling Stones, The La's were surprisingly perfectionistic. This not only became a huge obstacle during the production of their debut album but also put a strain on the band's relationships, eventually contributing to their breakup. It's quite a contrast for a band whose repertoire sounds so raw and organic to have gone through a recording process filled with rejected takes, endless re-recordings, and rotating producers.

While The La's weren't happy with their self-titled debut album (which took a total of three years to record), its sound became a blueprint for Britpop, thanks to its emphasis on guitar-driven music and 1960s British rock influences. The album's legacy can still be heard in the music of Oasis, Arctic Monkeys, and Fontaines D.C. While it's a shame The La's never released another studio album, they at least had the chance to create something that would stand the test of time. The recording process may have been a pain, but the fruits of their labor are unmistakable.

Them Crooked Vultures takes the term "supergroup" to another level, bringing together a trio that's a match made in rock heaven: Josh Homme of Queens of the Stone Age, John Paul Jones of Led Zeppelin, and Dave Grohl of Nirvana and the Foo Fighters. Their sound feels like a more laid-back take on Homme's work with his own band, but with a catchier, more modern rock edge. The project took years to come together, with the trio making its debut in 2009. All three musicians bring their own playing styles to the table, but somehow, everything clicks.

Their self-titled album is a real treat for rock fans, especially when you consider the heavyweight talent packed into this tight trio. There's no shortage of heavy guitar riffs, but the album isn't all about loud guitars and flashy musicianship. It also throws in funk influences, piano arrangements, and plenty of catchy vocal hooks from Homme. There's a little bit of everything here, and that's what makes the record such a familiar yet fresh listen. Rather than sounding like three rock legends simply showing off what they can do, Them Crooked Vultures brings their individual strengths together without the need to overpower each other.

Britain's biggest troublemakers have long sparked debate over whether they're true punk pioneers or just cynical posers who were more interested in the lifestyle than the music itself. It doesn't help that bassist Sid Vicious reportedly could barely play his own instrument, with Steve Jones having to step in and re-record the bass tracks. Still, whether you buy into their authenticity or not, there's no denying that the Sex Pistols left a lasting mark on anti-establishment culture. Their rebellious attitude was a sharp contrast to Britain's prim and proper image, particularly the kind of regal etiquette expected in a society still shaped by its monarchy.

Their only studio album, Never Mind the Bollocks, Here's the Sex Pistols, is pretty much the epitome of their bad-boy attitude. Dropped by record labels, plastered across tabloids, and met with concert cancellations, the album attracted plenty of public scrutiny for its outrageous content. Few songs were as controversial as "God Save the Queen" and "Anarchy in the U.K.," both of which captured the spirit of punk at its most rebellious. The album was packed with political undertones, and at a time when censorship was still rife in an uptight British society, the Sex Pistols came in swinging with a sound that refused to follow the rules and lyrics that could give the royal family a headache.

OHSAA public-private playoff split headed for statewide vote

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Suburban League, a conference of 16 schools in Northeast Ohio, has officially gotten the ball rolling on a public-private divide for high school playoffs.

The league submitted a petition for a membership vote on separate tournaments for public and private schools to the Ohio High School Athletic Association on Nov. 16. The petition received 152 signatures, well exceeding the required 75-signature threshold.

Now, the question of whether public and private schools should compete in separate postseason tournaments will go to a vote for OHSAA member institutions. The OHSAA's next referendum period begins in May 2027, with each high school voting on all ballot proposals from May 1 to May 15. There are 691 public-school and 121 private-school OHSAA members, each of whose principals cast a vote in the referendum period.

If approved in May, separate state tournaments would likely be implemented for the 2028-2029 school year. According to the National Federation of State High School Associations (NFHS), only Alabama, Maryland, Texas, and Virginia currently hold separate public and private high school state tournaments.

This isn't the first time the question of public vs. private athletics has been brought to the OHSAA's members for a vote. Petition attempts in 1978 and 1993 both failed, but renewed discussion over disproportionate private-school titles led to the development of the OHSAA's competitive balance plan in 2014.

OHSAA member schools adopted competitive balance measures beginning in 2017, placing schools in appropriate postseason divisions for baseball, basketball, football, soccer, softball, and volleyball.

The competitive balance formula looks at a school's enrollment and assigns each rostered player a tier based on considerations like which school district the student lives in or whether they come from an assigned feeder school. The OHSAA then adjusts the enrollment count accordingly, with more local players lowering the enrollment number and more out-of-district players raising it. That adjusted enrollment figure then determines the school's tournament division.

Competitive balance has had some success at bringing state titles closer to a proportionate public-private split. In football, for example, private schools won over 50% of the state championships in the 16 years before the plan's implementation. Since 2017, private schools have won just 20% of the football titles.

While nearly every sport has seen more public schools win championships since competitive balance was adopted, some sports still seem to favor private schools. Girls volleyball has hardly been affected as private schools have won over 64% of state titles since 2017, with three Greater Cincinnati-based private schools winning this year alone. Both boys soccer (48%) and girls soccer (42%) also see high rates of private schools titles.

In OHSAA fall 2026 state finals so far, public schools won four of seven titles in volleyball, four of five titles in boys soccer and three of five titles in girls soccer.

9 Classics Where the Audiobook Version Is Better

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Audiobooks were technically born as far back as 1877, with Thomas Edison's invention of the phonograph. However, it wasn't until 1932 that the American Foundation for the Blind officially named them "Talking Books," introducing them as a medium for visually impaired people to enjoy literature as an alternative to Braille. Throughout the entire 20th century, and even well into the 21st, many classics have been turned into audiobooks so phenomenal that they're arguably even better than the original book.

Audiobooks and physical books are, of course, vastly different media that fulfill vastly different purposes, so comparing them can feel a bit like comparing apples to oranges. Still, sometimes, an audiobook is so good that it becomes entirely reasonable to say it provides a better experience than the original. These are audiobooks that anyone who enjoys classic literature should consider essential listening material. "Classic" is defined for the purposes of this list as any book that was originally published before the 21st century.

Folkloric vampires predate Bram Stoker's Dracula by hundreds of years. Inspired by such stories, as well as by Irish folklore and historical figures like Vlad the Impaler, Stoker wrote the 1897 classic that is nowadays widely remembered as the most foundational pillar of modern vampiric fiction. It's undoubtedly one of the best horror books of all time, which made the announcement of an original Audible audiobook-slash-audio-drama all the more worthy of skepticism.

In the end, however, Bram Stoker's Dracula ended up being one of the greatest horror audiobooks that readers have been treated to in years. With a stacked cast that includes the likes of Ella Purnell, Jonathan Bailey, and Aimee Lou Wood, the audiobook takes the classic epistolary novel and makes it feel even more immersive, cinematic, and well-paced. It's worth noting that it's an abridged audio drama rather than a full reading of the original novel, which is not ideal, but those interested in such a concept should love it.

Originally serialized between 1875 and 1877, Russian author Leo Tolstoy's Anna Karenina is widely regarded as one of the most foundational pieces of world literature in history, a must-read masterpiece whose daunting 1,000-page length shouldn't deter anyone from considering it essential reading. Since it was one of her favorite books of all time, Maggie Gyllenhaal agreed to narrate an Audible original audiobook that really does justice to one of the best epic books of all time.

Tolstoy's novel was such a monumental artistic achievement that the author was notoriously drained and exhausted by the whole experience by the time he was done, and reading such a vast book can itself also be a massive undertaking. With the raw immediacy and many emotional variations of Gyllenhaal's narration, listening to Anna Karenina can be a far more engaging and far less tedious experience than many may initially think. At over 30 hours long, it's definitely a time commitment, but oh so worth every minute.

Upon publication back in 1997, J. K. Rowling's Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone (published in the US as Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone) was an immediate commercial and critical hit, kicking off one of the biggest multimedia franchises in history. The book's first audiobook version came out in 1999, and the performers varied by region. Whereas Jim Dale narrated the book in the US and Canada, it was Stephen Fry's voice that was heard in the British and international releases. If there is any one Harry Potter book that's better heard than read, it's Fry's performance of Philosopher's Stone.

As a whole, this is one of the most essential fantasy books to read before kicking the bucket, but there's nothing quite like hearing it through Fry's rich, velvety baritone voice. On the page, Philosopher's Stone is beautifully imaginative and cozy, but at times feels like an overly simple debut novel in terms of Rowling's prose. Fry elevates every word and makes the entire story feel like a wonderful fairy tale being told by the fire on a cold weekend night outdoors.

One of Stephen King's first novels was 1983's Pet Sematary, published before he had fully established and cemented himself as the true King of Horror. Fans had been begging for an audiobook for years, and once the project finally materialized, it was Michael C. Hall of Dexter fame who publisher Simon & Schuster picked as the right voice to narrate the story. The result is one of the best Stephen King audiobooks to date, and considering just how many great ones there are, that's saying a lot.

Pet Sematary was already absolutely legendary well before 2018, widely regarded by many—including King himself—as the author's most genuinely terrifying work. However, once the horror aspects really pick up steam after a relatively slow-burning first few pages, Hall makes it hit even harder. Getting an actor well-known for projects as death-related as Six Feet Under and Dexter to narrate a horror novel all about death and grief was not only brilliant meta-casting, but also hugely beneficial for the story as a whole.

Multiple books are almost always brought up when the endless debate of what "The Great American Novel" is gets brought up, but John Steinbeck's East of Eden is the most noteworthy. This family saga was likely key to Steinbeck being awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1962, and it more than lives up to its reputation as one of the most perfect American books ever written. The first audiobook version of the classic ever recorded was narrated by respected character actor Richard Poe.

East of Eden was already widely recognized as one of the most perfectly written masterpieces of all time, and it's definitely a must-read, but there's something about the way Poe complements the narrative that makes his version arguably even more unforgettable. Steinbeck wrote East of Eden in such a sweeping, almost Biblical voice that it almost feels like the narration of a legend, and Poe's resonant yet deeply human voice allows that narration style to feel all the more intimate and personal.

The book that earned Harper Lee a Pulitzer Prize was 1960's To Kill a Mockingbird, one of the most essential and widely discussed American classics of the 20th century. Though certain elements of its narrative are undeniably dated by modern standards, its heart will forever be in the right place, with its critiques of racial prejudice and systemic injustice being as timely and hard-hitting today as they were back in the '60s. In 2014, legendary actress Sissy Spacek was tasked with narrating the audiobook version of the story.

The reasons why this is one of those great books that are even better as audiobooks are aplenty, and even though the Pulitzer-winning original remains absolutely foundational, Spacek brings a level of nuance and authenticity to the book that feels like a much-welcome sense of modernity. The actress' beautifully melodic Texan drawl makes the small-town Alabama setting feel all the more evocative and atmospheric, and the way she flawlessly captures Scout's personality as both a child and an adult is incredible.

Stephen King's 22nd book was 1986's It, still remembered by many as the author's best and most iconic work to date. At over 1,100 pages in length, it's an absolute brick of a horror novel, but its epic scope still works marvelously all these many years later. To narrate the 2016 audiobook, Simon & Schuster decided to go for an actor who already had a pre-existing tie to the Stephen King universe: Steven Weber, who played Jack Torrance in the 1997 three-part miniseries version of The Shining.

It was already one of those horror books where every chapter is perfect—something quite admirable for such a thick novel—but Weber took that literary doorstop and somehow turned it into an even bigger, more immersive 45-hour juggernaut of an audiobook. The actor gives each of the Losers a distinct voice both in their child and adult versions, and he goes all in on Pennywise without ever teetering too far into distracting camp. Perfectly paced and intense to the core, It is a must-listen-to audiobook through and through.

American poet, essayist, and civil rights activist Maya Angelou was a towering figure in modern American literature, completely reshaping how Black American identity was expressed in the written word. The first of her seven published memoirs, 1969's I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings, broke new ground and became one of the first major autobiographies by an African American woman to achieve global bestseller status. When an audiobook version was released in 2011, it was Angelou herself who narrated it.

Taking one of the best memoirs of all time and turning it into an audiobook worthy of its legacy was always going to be a tough task, so who better to achieve it than the author herself? By having Angelou narrate her own story with her unmistakably theatrical contralto voice, I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings feels even more honest and intimate than it already does on the page, balancing poignancy and humor to absolute perfection.

Well before he wrote what many still consider the greatest and most influential fantasy epic of the 20th century, J. R. R. Tolkien published the fantasy children's classic The Hobbit, almost inarguably the most perfect fantasy novel of the 1930s. There are multiple audiobook versions of The Hobbit available, but one reigns supreme as perhaps the audiobook that most elevates the experience of the original text: the 2020 version narrated by Andy Serkis.

It's easily one of the best audiobooks of all time, with Serkis not only delivering a much-expected powerhouse performance as Gollum, but also giving each of the other characters a distinct voice that makes them even more memorable than they are on paper. The Hobbit is also filled with songs and poems, and Serkis brings them to life in a way that reading the physical book simply can't match. In Serkis' voice, The Hobbit really does feel like the wonderful myth that it was always meant to be.

The biggest value proposition of a foldable is that it’s a 2-in-1 product, letting you have a standard phone but also a tablet-sized display that fits in your pocket.

I totally get the appeal, and fully expected to love that hybrid design. But after two weeks of trying, I still struggled to find times when I actually wanted to unfold the Duo. And to be clear, that’s a me problem, not an issue with the Duo itself.

I tried a bunch of apps on the inner display, and struggled to find use cases that—for me—made it worthwhile to open the Duo. But based on the feedback I’ve seen from most Duo owners, I’m very much in the minority.

I know lots of people love the Duo for video, but I don’t watch much video on my iPhone. I almost never watch YouTube, TikTok, or Instagram Reels. And for shows and movies, I stick with my TV screen and an Apple TV 4K.

Multitasking support on iPhone Duo works really well. But when I found myself needing to multitask, I still reached for my iPad Pro with Magic Keyboard attached. Last year I bought an iPhone Air and absolutely loved it. Initially, the Air’s less capable camera system didn’t bother me, it was “good enough.”

After a few months though, I started missing the Pro camera system more. Then I learned that my wife was pregnant with twins, and I knew I wanted the best possible iPhone cameras to capture their early weeks and months. So I bought an iPhone 18 Pro in September.

I initially planned to dual-carry the two models, using iPhone 18 Pro solely as a camera. But over and over I found myself wishing it was my only iPhone.

Is the iPhone Duo camera good enough for most people? Probably so. But for this year at least, with my twins aging rapidly, it’s not quite right for me. If I loved the Duo for other reasons, I would just use both models. But since I didn’t, I’m happy to stick with the iPhone 18 Pro.

Apple created its most unique new iPhone model since the original, and there’s plenty to love about it. I’m very curious to see how popular foldables become over time compared to slab models, because each clearly offers pros and cons. The failure of a Vulcan Centaur rocket carrying a full load of internet satellites for Amazon was caused by an anomaly in its first-stage booster, rocket maker United Launch Alliance said today (Nov. 17).

That failure — which led to the loss of 40 Amazon Leo internet satellites — was recognized about 10 minutes after liftoff, but the problems began much earlier than that, ULA said in a statement.

"While we appear to have lost telemetry and the mission at approximately 10 minutes into flight, early indications are that just after liftoff the launch vehicle experienced low booster performance, resulting in stage separation occurring when the vehicle was low in altitude and below minimal velocity to reach orbit," ULA wrote in today's update.

The two-stage Vulcan Centaur is the successor to ULA's workhorse Atlas V rocket, which will retire after just six more flights. Vulcan debuted in January 2024 and now has a total of five launches under its belt.

The new rocket has experienced problems on three of those missions. On both its second and fourth flights — which launched in October 2024 and February of this year, respectively — the nozzle of one of Vulcan's strap-on solid rocket boosters (SRBs) fell off about 30 seconds after liftoff.

On both occasions, the rocket overcame the problem and successfully delivered its payloads to orbit. However, after the second occurrence, the U.S. Space Force paused national-security launches with Vulcan until ULA addressed the SRB nozzle issue. (The February 2026 flight was a Space Force mission.)

That work led to "design and process improvements to the nozzle," ULA said in an Oct. 28 update. And those tweaks apparently worked.

"All indications are that the solid rocket booster performance was nominal, following nozzle improvements implemented for this launch," the company wrote in today's update.

Vulcan's Centaur V upper stage, which features two RL-10C engines, did its job as well "but was unable to recover from the [booster] performance loss, resulting in loss of mission," ULA added.

Vulcan's first-stage booster is powered by two BE-4 engines, which are built by Amazon founder Jeff Bezos' Blue Origin. Today's update did not identify the exact problem with the booster; doing so will require more time, as the investigation has just begun.

If that investigation finds a serious issue with the BE-4, there could be broad ripple effects across the American space program. After all, that engine also powers the first stage of Blue Origin's New Glenn rocket, which is a key piece of hardware for NASA's Artemis program of moon exploration. (And New Glenn is currently recovering from a failure of its own — a massive explosion on the test stand that occurred on May 28.)

NASA plans to integrate the Centaur V upper stage into its Space Launch System moon rocket, the vehicle that launches Artemis astronauts to space. So the fact that the Centaur performed nominally on Sunday is a bit of good news for the space agency.

Vulcan's grounding is a big deal, no matter what the anomaly investigation ends up finding. The U.S. doesn't have a surfeit of powerful rockets, after all, and two of them — Vulcan and New Glenn — are out of commission at the moment.

There's considerable pressure on both Vulcan and New Glenn to bounce back strong, for the nation faces an impending launch crunch in the next few years. SpaceX plans to retire its Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy rockets and shift its launch load over to the fully reusable Starship megarocket. SpaceX is expected to prioritize its own needs in Starship's early days, using the vehicle primarily to build out its next-gen Starlink broadband constellation and Starmind AI data center network.

The Vulcan failure also impacts Amazon — specifically, the company's plan to build out Amazon Leo, which will compete with SpaceX's Starlink. Amazon has booked about 100 launches on a variety of rockets to build its constellation, which will eventually consist of more than 3,200 satellites. Thirty-eight of those bookings are for Vulcan; Sunday's launch was the first of those.

The other rockets Amazon has booked are the Atlas V, New Glenn, Falcon 9 and Arianespace's Ariane 6. About 400 operational Amazon Leo satellites have reached orbit to date across 14 launches (not including Sunday's failure).

The Cologne-based Lempertz auction house said it still planned to sell two works by Gothic painter Johann Koerbecke after the heirs of a German Jewish art dealer who was persecuted by the Nazis claimed the pieces were “stolen.”

Dealer Max Stern held the paintings in Düsseldorf between 1936 and 1937, the year the Nazis shut down his gallery. Both paintings—The Annunciation and The Vision of Saint Bernard—are likely from Marienfeld high altarpiece, which was completed in 1457. They are being auctioned in a sale of Old Masters art. They carry high estimates of around 300,000 euros each, or about $329,000 apiece, making them the auction’s most expensive works. “For art historical reasons,” the Lempertz lot entries read, “the two important panels (Lots 1005 and 1006) will first be offered individually and then together.”

Potentially complicating the matter is the fact that, prior to Stern’s possession of the Koerbecke paintings, they were owned by Ellen Funke, who, during the Nazi era, was classified as a Mischling, or “mongrel,” because she had Jewish ancestry through her mother. She sold her collection in 1936 to fund her emigration from Germany, and works she once owned have also been the subject of restitution claims—two of which happen to involve the Koerbecke works.

In 2025, Bavaria’s state museums restituted Koerbecke’s Vision of Saint Bernard to Funke’s heirs. At the time, the Bayerische Staatsgemäldesammlungen noted that The Vision of Saint Bernard and The Annunciation were at one point part of a single work, with both images appearing on either side of the same panel, and that Stern had taken them apart with the hope of selling them both. Funke’s heirs are listed as the sellers of The Vision of Saint Bernard on the auction house’s website.

Bitcoin Lightning developers needed two patches to stop a potentially costly attack

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The initial July update blocked one crash method but missed a persistent vulnerability that could leave affected nodes unable to restart.

Bitcoin's Core Lightning software required two security patches to eliminate a vulnerability that could repeatedly crash payment nodes and potentially expose funds held in other channels.

A Nov. 13 security disclosure revealed that an initial repair released in July failed to address a second attack method that could keep affected nodes offline even after they restarted.

The vulnerability, tracked as LNF-2026-0004, affected Core Lightning, one of the software implementations used to operate nodes on Bitcoin's Lightning Network.

Security researcher Nishant Bansal discovered that attackers could exploit how Core Lightning assigned identifiers to payment channels, creating duplicate records that caused the software to crash.

The weakness originated in the network's channel-opening process, where each payment channel receives an identifier derived from the Bitcoin transaction output that funds it. Core Lightning could accept multiple channels referencing the same funding output, producing identical identifiers that conflicted with assumptions in its internal software.

In the first attack, a malicious peer could create duplicate pending channels and crash a victim's node simply by reconnecting. The attack required no Bitcoin transaction fees because the fraudulent channels did not need on-chain funding. Each subsequent reconnection could trigger another crash, allowing attackers to repeatedly interrupt payment processing.

However, researchers identified a more persistent variation involving previously closed channels.

By reusing an identifier associated with a closed channel and subsequently closing another channel with the same identifier, attackers could trigger a software failure that persisted across restarts.

Unlike the initial attack, this method required on-chain transaction fees but could leave the node unable to recover without a software upgrade.

Core Lightning developers addressed the original vulnerability in version 26.06.6, released on July 22, after researchers privately reported the reconnection attack earlier that month.

The update prevented new channels from reusing funding outputs associated with existing live channels. It also modified connection handling to prevent duplicate records already stored from triggering the original crash.

However, the protection did not extend to identifiers associated with closed channels, leaving the second attack route available.

Researcher Crypt-iQ independently identified the remaining weakness, prompting developers to introduce broader safeguards in version 26.06.8, released Sept. 22.

The second update expanded duplicate-identifier checks across active and closed channels belonging to different peers. It also allowed nodes containing previously stored duplicate records to recover without repeatedly crashing.

Although I caught the marathon bug almost instantly, I also felt defeated after New York. I knew I could have done better. Between New York City in 2022 and Paris in 2024, I never ran more than 10 miles at a time, but I was running enough that I knew I could do it again if I really wanted. I followed a similar approach for Chicago in 2024: Build up to one long run about a month out, then fill the rest of the weeks with shorter runs and one medium-length run. It wasn’t much of a plan, but it worked for me. My times kept improving, and I broke four hours for the first time in Chicago.

Looking back, I realize that my no-plan approach was partly a way to protect myself. If I never said my goal out loud, I couldn’t feel disappointed if I missed it. Breaking four hours in Chicago changed that. For the first time, I wanted to see what I could do with a real plan.

I was now living in the city I had expected to call home ever since I left college, so I really wanted this race to feel meaningful. For the first time in my marathon career, I downloaded a Runner’s World training plan, the 16-week Break 3:45 plan to be exact, printed it out, and started tracking all of my runs. I wanted to beat my time in Chicago and get as close to 3 hours and 45 minutes as possible.

In the beginning, this felt super daunting. I had never done an interval run in my life nor had I ever intentionally sought out hills to make my runs feel harder than they already did. Nonetheless, I found my old Garmin Forerunner 55 and learned how to upload intervals to run up and down the West Side Highway. When the plan called for hills, I made my way back to the Queensboro Bridge for the first time since 2022, ready to face it with excitement instead of fear. Even while pretending to be excited, the incline is no joke!

For a while, I really felt like I had gotten the hang of things. I actually started to recognize what different paces felt like during the intervals, instead of sprinting and pulling back and looking at my watch while I ran. And, I could now confidently run over many of the bridges in New York City, notably the Williamsburg and Manhattan, even though they aren’t even part of the marathon course. I wasn’t following the plan exactly, but I was able to reorganize the days of the week to hit all the workouts.

Near the end of August (roughly week eight of the plan), it became hot all the time, I was constantly traveling for weekend trips, and I had started to lose the excitement of running. So while I was making sure to hit all of the long runs, the interval training fell off completely. I was a little disappointed in myself because the interval training runs were workouts that made me feel like I was getting stronger each week. I had never done this type of workout before, so I could tell I was getting better each time.

To find the joy of running again, I really started to lean on my friends. Every week, I’d send out a few texts to see who was available and wanted to go for a run with me. I ran the loop in Central Park almost every week and met up with my friends in Brooklyn to run every Friday morning. I started to explore parts of the city I had never been to before, which helped ground me in my training and remember that running is supposed to be fun and is an exciting way to explore the city.

From my previous experience, I knew that running the long runs was the most important thing for me to feel confident on race day. So much of marathon running is mental, so knowing that I could run 20 miles (and have run 26.2 before), was enough for me. So, while I started with a plan, I started reverting back to my tried-and-true method—the just make sure you run the long runs plan.

Outside of the training plan itself, I leaned on Runner’s World a lot. I didn’t have access to this in the past so I didn’t feel confident about planning for prerun meals or even how to take gels during the race. I also found that being able to read runner stories online made me feel more connected to the community, which helped me mentally with my training.

Plan or no plan, I’m proud of myself! My official time was 3:57:39, so while I did not achieve my goal of breaking 3:45, I did run a sub-4 race, and following the Runner’s World plan helped me get there. The last time I ran this race, I was a senior in college and thought I had it all figured out. I was going to move to New York City, get a real adult job, and start the life I had been imagining. Similar to my experience following the Runner’s World plan, this is not how everything went. Instead, I moved to a different city after graduating, eventually found my way back to New York, and my life is so much more full than I ever expected because of it. Maybe things aren’t always supposed to go to plan, and that’s a good thing!

Thankfully, there was no drama this time in the sauna!

Federal prosecutors urged jurors Monday to convict Chinese technology company Huawei, arguing that the company had grown by repeatedly committing crimes. (Photo from Shutterstock)

Federal prosecutors urged jurors Monday to convict Chinese technology company Huawei, arguing during closing statements in the ongoing 11-week trial involving trade secrets theft and sanctions evasion that the company had grown by repeatedly committing crimes.

According to a Monday story from Law.com, Meredith Arfa, the assistant U.S. attorney for the Eastern District of New York, told jurors that Huawei spent two decades pursuing growth “by any means possible.”

To illustrate the trade secrets allegations, Arfa played a video of a former Huawei employee removing part of Tappy, T-Mobile’s robot for testing phone touchscreens, from the carrier’s lab. She also showed side-by-side images of Cisco’s and Huawei’s router source code, saying the two matched exactly—down to an engineer’s stray comment mentioning someone named “Bob.”

“This simply couldn’t happen without copying,” Arfa said in her summation.

She further alleged that Huawei managers encouraged theft by paying bonuses for obtaining confidential competitor information, and that employees boasted about it on an internal forum.

On the sanctions compliance allegations, prosecutors said Huawei secretly controlled Skycom, a subsidiary that supplied sensitive technology to Iran’s government. Arfa said the company repeatedly lied about owning Skycom. She also pointed to admissions by Meng Wanzhou, Huawei’s chief financial officer, who signed a deferred prosecution agreement after spending three years under house arrest. Meng acknowledged that Skycom’s operations violated U.S. sanctions, and that she misled HSBC, Huawei’s primary bank, about its compliance, Arfa noted.

Huawei, one of the largest telecommunications companies in the world, denies the allegations, contending that the case rests on the independent actions of rogue employees who were disciplined.

The government announced the case in 2019. The company is represented by Jenner & Block, Sidley Austin and Steptoe.

Does It Matter That We’ve Taken the “Mormon” Out of the Mormon Wives?

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There is something about hearing “come ye saints, come ye sinners” that sets off a Pavlovian response in my brain; a physical trigger to sling the (dirty) Diet Coke back and turn the volume up. It means The Secret Lives of Mormon Wives is back, this time with its OC-set spinoff: The Secret Lives of Mormon Wives: Orange County.

In the show’s marketing, Hulu leans into the California setting as the clear divide between SLOMW: OC and its Utah predecessor. SLOMW: OC isn’t as ensconced in the shadow of the Mormon church as the Utah show is. Instead, it offers a more laid-back version of Mormonism (they drink! they get tattoos! they’re in throuples!). But in taking the “Mormon” out of the “Mormon Wives,” are we missing out on what makes the show so interesting?

It’s a bold choice to greenlight a spinoff about Mormons without actually casting any Mormons. In fact, of the eight new cast members (Jen Affleck is the only familiar face), only four identify as practicing Mormons at all: Mayci Neely’s identical-looking sister McCall DaPron, McCall’s bestie Jordan McClanahan, YouTuber Madison Bontempo, and Ashleigh Pease.

Meanwhile, the other half of the cast have either left the church altogether, as is the case with influencers Aspyn Ovard and Salome Andea, or were never religious to begin with like podcasters Avery Woods and Bobbi Althoff. It’s the “Molly Mormons,” or traditional Mormons, vs. the “Black Cats,” the rebels of the group. What connects these women is not so much a shared religion as a shared career path: influencing and podcasting.

SLOMW Utah’s strength was its portrayal of the contradictory rules of Mormonism. For example, what do you mean it goes against the church to drink caffeine but it’s totally fine to get high off laughing gas at a Botox appointment?? The rules only got more interesting as the show progressed and the women went from housewives to reality TV megastars overnight. Suddenly they were grappling with bigger religious conundrums: how to be both subordinate in the church and a breadwinner at home.

SLOMW: OC takes a different approach by focusing more on influencer mom drama, shifting away from the central tension of the show: women striving for independence and equality while navigating deeply patriarchal relationships. Take Mayci in season 5 when she fends off a fourth pregnancy request from her mother, sister, and husband (keep in mind, she just popped out baby #3!). Or Jessi, whose husband Jordan felt emasculated by the number of zeros in her paycheck.

In the Orange County-set spinoff, the men are curiously absent. Madison, Salome, and Aspyn are the only women to feature their romantic partners on screen—and even then it’s only a few moments of the show’s eight-episode run. The absence of the men feels like a strategic response to the DadTok hate that plagued the Utah show. But Hulu may have mistaken our TFP relationship fatigue for not wanting to see messy relationships at all.

Instead the show has channeled all that messy relationship tension into the women’s dynamics with each other. Their fights originate from the different ways they interpret feminism, religion, money, and power. Take McCall and Bobbi, for instance. McCall, who sees the group’s religious divide as her “McCalling” to convert, is the clear villain of season 1 because she tries to center Mormonism in the show. It’s Bobbi, hoodie pulled up and strings pulled tight, who brings McCall back down to earth and challenges her to see her faith through different perspectives.

“Show me the [bible] verse that says to be a bitch and talk shit on people, please,” Bobbi throws out in episode 4 after learning McCall made a dig about her comedy show and threatened to have another cast member replaced for not piling on Bobbi too. Later in the season, Bobbi confronts McCall over her drama with Aspyn Ovard and urges her to rethink her mean girl internet campaign. She points out that McCall might not agree with all of Aspyn’s life decisions, but Aspyn has a terminally ill child; McCall should be the bigger, more religious woman and show her some grace.

If the show wants to lean away from messy marriages, then it needs to lean into the messiness of religion in friendships. What are the boundaries that divide? What are the boundaries that unite? People hate McCall but, sorry, she is very good mess! Watching her try (and fail) to convert Bobbi into wearing pastels and saying a singular nice word about the church is the most fun I’ve had in a while. Without the religious tension, the show becomes hot, rich women fighting in public, and there are stronger contenders in that genre (hello, Real Housewives).

Maybe taking the “Mormon” out of “Mormon Wives” will rejuvenate a flailing brand, but the religious tension has to stay a priority regardless of the women’s religious affiliations. Taking the “Mormon” out of “Mormon Wives” is bad for the show—and bad for the mess!

Cursor Traces Malicious Claude Code Extension to Third-Party Breach

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A compromised key issued to AI infrastructure provider Fireworks was used by threat actors to access Cursor data and credentials.

Cursor has traced the origins of a security incident involving a malicious version of Anthropic's Claude Code extension in its marketplace to a breach at AI infrastructure provider Fireworks.

As many as 5,000 users may have downloaded the tampered extension during the roughly 11 hours it was live on Oct. 29. It was a reminder of the risk organizations face when they rely on third-party marketplaces for software applications and extensions. It also illustrates how one security lapse can lead to a cascade of subsequent breaches and supply chain attacks.

When Cursor first disclosed the incident, the company said attackers had placed malicious versions of Anthropic's Claude Code extension in a cache serving its Visual Studio (VS) Code extension marketplace. Users who requested either of two specific cached versions of the extension (2.1.312 and 2.1.313) during the exposure window may have downloaded the malicious builds, the company said. The malicious extensions attempted to collect information about infected systems and establish persistent remote access, including by installing an attacker-controlled SSH key.

Related:Grok Bots Attempt to Access Corporate Data Without User Approval

Cursor quickly removed the malicious extensions, temporarily shut down its marketplace, and audited the store for signs of further tampering. Cursor also rotated its keys, rebuilt the marketplace, and worked with the cloud provider hosting the attacker's server to take it offline. The company's initial advisory, however, did not explain how the attacker might have gained access to its environment and planted the malicious extension in its extensions marketplace.

In an update on Nov. 13, Cursor filled in that blank, with a detailed description of what its investigation had unearthed in the days following the Oct. 29 incident.

The intrusion, according to Cursor, began when an attacker obtained an access key the company had issued to Fireworks, an AI startup based in San Mateo, Calif. Cursor said "the key was exposed by Fireworks," and the attacker used it to copy data from a read-only storage bucket containing historical coding-agent session records belonging to Cursor users. Most of the records were from 2025 and involved sessions that were not in Privacy Mode and that Cursor was permitted to use for model training purposes.

Some of the stored session records contained credentials that had not been properly redacted. The attacker used those credentials to access what Cursor described as select SaaS tools for accessing non-production source code repositories, customer support data, emails, usage metrics, and other data.

Related:F-Secure Identifies 26 Ways AI Can Harm Consumers, and Where to Stop Them

On Oct. 29, the attacker subsequently used one of the compromised credentials to tamper with the cache that Cursor uses to deliver extensions through its VS Code marketplace. The attacker inserted unauthorized copies of Anthropic's Claude Code extension in the marketplace where they remained available to users for about 11 hours.

"Anthropic did not publish this copy, and this was not a vulnerability in Anthropic's product," Cursor stressed in its update. "The marketplace source, Open VSX, never had the malware. At most 5,000 users who requested specific cached versions of the extension on October 29 potentially received the malicious build."

Cursor estimated that API credentials belonging to fewer than 0.01% of its customers were exposed in the incident. Historical coding-agent session records, or traces, belonging to fewer than 0.05% of users were accessed, the company said.

However, the supply chain attack goes back even further than Fireworks, which itself was a victim of a third-party breach. In a Nov. 9 update, Fireworks said a cloud provider hosting the AI company's R&D systems "experienced a security incident on Oct. 23." The attackers used the access to the R&D systems to obtain sensitive data, including API keys and access tokens.

Related:AI Makes Reverse Engineering Mainstream Software & Malware Far Easier

According to Fireworks, the available evidence linked the breach of its environment to a financially motivated cybercriminal group seeking to monetize unauthorized access to large language models. It's a practice known as LLMjacking, which Google and others have described as a rapidly emerging threat for organizations across multiple sectors in North America, Asia-Pacific, and other regions.

Following the incident, Cursor said it has implemented a broad security overhaul to reduce the risk of similar compromises. Immediate steps the company has taken include cutting off Firework's access to all its shared data sets, revoking and replacing potentially exposed credentials, and rotating internal credentials. The company has also tightened restrictions on how its systems and third-party service providers can communicate with one another and expanded automated scanning for secrets across its code repositories and internal data.

The Cursor incident is another indication of how the rapid adoption of AI-powered development tools and increasingly interconnected software ecosystems has exacerbated software supply chain risks. Recently, for instance, researchers uncovered more than 550 exposed secrets in Microsoft VS Code and Open VSX extension packages, including over 100 publishing tokens that could have allowed attackers to distribute malicious updates to legitimate extensions. Similarly, earlier this year researchers identified dozens of malicious GlassWorm VS Code extensions seeded in the Open VSX marketplace to steal developer credentials that attackers could use to spread poisoned software packages.

The Game Awards has announced its nominations slate for 2026, revealing that Grand Theft Auto 6 is this year's most-nominated game. Rockstar's hotly anticipated crime epic has secured eight nominations including Game of the Year, for which it'll be competing with games including Resident Evil Requiem, Control Resonant, and The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time.

Now you—and the countless other people preparing to argue about this online—may have noticed that GTA 6 has been nominated for a number of awards despite the fact that it hasn't been released yet. In a press release, The Game Awards notes that jurors were in some cases provided with early access to enable awards consideration.

"The nominees for The Game Awards are selected by a global jury of over 160 media publications and creator outlets around the world, who may be provided with advance or pre-release access to games for critical evaluation," The Game Awards said.

In a Variety interview last week, Game Awards host and co-creator Geoff Keighley refuted speculation that TGA 2026 eligibility deadlines had been sculpted to allow GTA 6 to have a shot for this year's nominations.

"There was a thing a few weeks ago where everyone thought that we had changed our dates to accommodate GTA, and it's like, no, actually our dates have been very set every year. It's the same calendar that we have," Keighley said. "I don't know this, but I'm sure some of these companies consider that when they're looking at their release calendars."
