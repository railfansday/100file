Medicare beneficiaries who remain in their current stand-alone Part D drug plans could face sharply higher premiums in 2027 if they don’t switch to lower-cost plans during open enrollment, according to a KFF analysis.

The average monthly premium for stand-alone prescription drug plans, or PDPs, is projected to rise 50%, from $34 in 2026 to $51 in 2027, if current enrollees remain in the same plans, according to the analysis from KFF, an independent source for health policy research, polling and news.

The increase would widen the gap between stand-alone drug plans and Medicare Advantage plans that include prescription drug coverage. KFF projects that drug coverage through Medicare Advantage plans that offer Part D coverage, or MA-PDs, will average $9 a month in 2027, compared with $51 for PDPs. The average PDP premium would rise by $17, compared with an increase of less than $2 for MA-PDs.

For Medicare Part D prescription drug coverage, the average beneficiary has 28 Medicare Advantage prescription drug plans (MA-PDs) and 9 stand-alone prescription drug plans (PDPs) to choose from, with varying costs associated with these different options. KFF analysts examined premiums and cost-sharing requirements for Part D coverage in 2027 and how these costs have changed over time, based on Medicare plan landscape and benefits data from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS).

The $51 figure assumes current PDP enrollees remain in their existing plans. The actual average premium could be lower as beneficiaries switch to less expensive plans during open enrollment and new beneficiaries select coverage.

CMS projected a much smaller increase in the national average Part D premium, from $35 to $36. KFF noted that CMS, which based its estimate on September 2026 enrollment, did not explain its calculation methodology.

Among PDP enrollees who do not receive low-income subsidies, 76%, or 10.4 million people, would pay higher premiums if they keep their current plans. About 2.4 million would see their monthly premiums increase by more than $50.

That's a sharp increase from 2026, when only 38,000 enrollees faced monthly premium increases exceeding $50, according to KFF. The Trump administration’s decision to discontinue the Part D Premium Stabilization Demonstration after 2026 ended the additional premium subsidies that were provided to PDP sponsors and the cap on the maximum allowable monthly PDP premium increase, which was set at $50 for 2026, according to KFF analysts.

Another 4.1 million beneficiaries without low-income subsidies are enrolled in zero-premium PDPs this year, but no zero-premium PDPs will be available in 2027.

“With some plans increasing premiums substantially, the stakes are higher this year for people with Medicare stand-alone drug coverage if they sit on the sidelines during open enrollment,” Juliette Cubanski, a vice president and director of the Program on Medicare Policy at KFF, said in a statement. “There are relatively low-premium PDP options, but people facing large premium increases need to take action if they want to avoid paying significantly more.”

According to KFF's analysis, if PDP enrollees who are not receiving Part D Low-Income Subsidies (LIS) keep their current plan in 2027, more than half (55%), or 7.5 million people, will pay premiums of less than $20 per month, but nearly 30%, or 3.9 million people, will pay at least $100 per month.

Average monthly premiums for the eight national PDPs are projected to range from around $6 to $204 in 2027. Among national PDPs, the two lowest-premium plans are Centene’s Wellcare Value Script Rx, an enhanced PDP with an average premium of $6.50 for 2027, up from $5.80 in 2026,  and Wellcare Classic, a basic PDP with an average premium of $10.90 for 2027, up from $8.60 in 2026, according to the analysis. The national PDPs with the highest average monthly premiums are United’s AARP Medicare Rx Preferred at $204.50, up from $126.00 in 2026, and Humana Premier Rx Plan at $137.40, up from $119.60 in 2026.

The analysis also finds that most Part D enrollees will face a deductible of several hundred dollars for drug coverage in 2027, including 85% of MA-PD enrollees, up from 80% in 2026, and virtually all PDP enrollees, 99.8%, up from 96% in 2026, based on current enrollment. The average Part D deductible in 2027 will be $643 for PDP enrollees, up from $540 in 2026,  and $489 for MA-PD enrollees, rising from $360 in 2026. The standard Part D deductible in 2027 is $700, up from $615 in 2026.

Most Part D enrollees will also face coinsurance rather than fixed copayments for preferred-brand and non-preferred drugs, potentially making their out-of-pocket costs less predictable. Virtually all PDP enrollees will face median coinsurance of 25% for preferred brands and 35% for non-preferred drugs, according to KFF.

Everyone On US-Bound Flights, Including Crew, Must Now Declare They Haven’t Been In Congo In The Last 21 Days

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) within the Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) issued an order on travel for the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) which has now come into effect. It requires all air travelers, including crew, flying into or connecting through the United States to sign that they haven't been present in the DRC in the last 21 days.

The order comes into effect at 11:59 PM EST on November 3. It will be applied to all flights that depart for the US at or after this time. The order comes in response to the outbreak of the Ebola disease in some remote areas of the DRC. Simple Flying has contacted the CDC for further comment.

An attestation order is a requirement for a traveler to confirm something before they come to the United States. The CDC initially issued the order on October 2. It was clarified on October 28, confirming that it applies to crew and passengers on any aircraft, including cargo services. The order also applies to crew on "deadhead" status, meaning that they are onboard a flight but not carrying out duties. Despite the severity of these new restrictions, the CDC insists that "The overall risk to the American public and travelers remains low."

The 21-day restriction has been chosen because that is the maximum incubation period for the disease. Being present in the DRC means physically entering or traveling through the country, either by land, air or sea. It still applies even if someone doesn't leave the airport or aircraft. Day zero of the period is when the individual leaves the DRC, with day one being the first full day outside the DRC. Impacted people can only enter the US from day 22.

Airlines are responsible for collecting and retaining the attestations. The CDC then has the right to request any attestations to confirm compliance and assist with public health investigations should there be any Ebola cases within the US. The required retention time for carriers is at least 42 days.

When you fly to America, you'll be asked to sign the attestation prior to boarding. One authorized individual, such as a parent or guardian, may sign individual attestations on behalf of each member of a travel party. Anybody refusing to sign the document will be denied boarding. You can also be prosecuted and fined if you submit false information.

The current plan is for the order to run until December 18 unless the CDC changes the rules before then. The regulator may also extend the order should the DRC Ebola outbreak worsen or continue.

The flight was forced to land in Montreal after US authorities identified a passenger from Ebola-stricken Congo.

Airlines have a degree of freedom to decide how they will deal with the attestation process. Make sure to check before you travel as processes will vary between carriers. The Travel has reported that Delta Air Lines' guidance states the following:

"Travelers may complete the attestation upon check in via the app or Delta.com. Travelers will also receive a link to the attestation via the check-in reminder sent 24-hours before departure."

Meanwhile, if you are flying with American Airlines, you can complete the attestation during check-in on the carrier's website, on the app, at the airport or with a printable PDF. United Airlines has fewer options, with the process only available as a confirmation step during check-in. Things are easier for large groups flying with JetBlue, as one person can complete the form for multiple passengers in their party so long as they are flying under the same booking.

NY gov. pointed to Kennedy’s long-standing anti-vaccine work as spurring outbreaks.

A child in New York has died of measles, health officials in the state announced late Monday. It marks the first reported measles-associated death in New York in more than 30 years. It is the seventh measles-linked death reported in the US this year; all six of the other deaths were reported in Pennsylvania, which is experiencing a dramatic measles outbreak that has stricken over 1,430 people this year. Prior to the deaths, Pennsylvania hadn’t seen a measles-related death in 35 years.

The boom in vaccine-preventable outbreaks, cases,  and tragic deaths is being fueled by slipping vaccination rates nationwide as health misinformation and anti-vaccine sentiments have taken hold. One of the most prominent anti-vaccine activists in the US currently serves as the US health secretary; Robert F. Kennedy Jr. spent decades prior to taking office attacking vaccines and spreading misinformation. He and his anti-vaccine organization, Children’s Health Defense, have been one of the most prolific sources of anti-vaccine content.

Since taking office, Kennedy has waged a multi-front war on vaccines that has included dismantling and obstructing federal vaccine recommendations; launching dubious studies to look for alleged, unspecified harms of vaccines; promoting alleged vaccine injuries; and continuing to spread misinformation about vaccines and vaccine-preventable illnesses.

In a statement, New York Governor Kathy Hochul directly tied the state’s death to Kennedy’s policies and anti-vaccine activism. She called the child’s death “a devastating reminder of what’s at stake when Washington deliberately erodes decades of progress in protecting Americans from preventable illnesses.” She also said she was “heartbroken” by the death, sending her condolences to the family. “As a mother and grandmother, I cannot imagine what this family is going through,” Hochul said. Overall, New York has reported 170 measles cases in over a dozen counties.

Days before the seventh preventable measles death, The Washington Post reported on Kennedy’s latest push to thwart public health efforts: Career scientists at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention—which is under Kennedy’s control—were blocked from publishing an urgent health alert to clinicians regarding the risk and management of measles cases during pregnancy.

Given the previous success of vaccines and vaccination campaigns, many clinicians have never seen such cases in their careers. But measles infections during pregnancy pose severe, life-threatening risks to both pregnant people and fetuses. As such, CDC scientists wanted to issue an informational advisory through the CDC’s Health Alert Network.

But the CDC Director’s Office declined to provide the required approval for the alert, sources told the Post. The scientists have since moved to trying to publish the information in the CDC’s publication, The Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report (MMWR). But the peer-review process for the publication is longer than that of an advisory. Further, vaccine-related studies already approved for publication in MMWR have been suspiciously delayed, the Post reported. The scientists are now considering publishing the information outside the agency to avoid onerous clearance. Health officials in Pennsylvania, meanwhile, released their own alert on measles in pregnancy and congenital measles on October 19.

New York has not released the age of the child who died or any other details about the case. The other sixth deaths reported this year have included babies, including one with congenital measles. The six deaths in Pennsylvania included a newborn with congenital measles, whose mother was severely ill at the time of birth; a 6-week-old who was too young to be vaccinated; a “perfectly healthy” 18-year-old; a 20-year-old with leukemia; a 40-year-old with underlying conditions; and a 39-year-old with underlying conditions. All the deceased were unvaccinated.

Pennsylvania’s health department is currently only reporting five deaths; the department confirmed to Ars Technica on Monday that its investigation into the sixth death, reported by the Franklin County coroner on October 12, is still pending. A Franklin County judge has ordered that information about the death be sealed from public review, and The Philadelphia Inquirer has taken legal action to obtain certain death record details.

The CDC, meanwhile, has reported only three of the seven deaths this year. Kennedy reportedly ordered the CDC to delete some of the early deaths and has since changed how the CDC counts deaths. Instead of the long-standing practice of relaying reports from states, the CDC now relies on federal death records, which can take weeks to come through and are not designed to capture the real-time data needed to respond to an active outbreak.

Overall, the CDC reports over 4,850 measles cases across 47 states and jurisdictions. At least 511 people have been hospitalized. This year’s tally is more than double the total from all of 2025, which was 2,289 cases with three deaths. The 2026 tally is also the largest the country has seen since 1991, prior to measles being declared eliminated from the US in 2000 amid high vaccination rates.

Why Does Everyone Else's Weight Loss Feel So Personal?

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Posted November 3, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

A friend tells you she's lost 20 pounds. A coworker shows up looking noticeably smaller. Your sister mentions that she's started taking a GLP-1 medication for weight loss. You recognize that what they do with their bodies is their decision, but before long, you're thinking about your own appearance. You start wondering whether you've gained weight, studying yourself in the mirror, or questioning what you ate that day. Nothing about your body has changed since yesterday, so why are you suddenly feeling dissatisfied with it?

Weight loss is so widely celebrated that congratulating someone for becoming thinner has become a normal and socially acceptable response. Yet weight loss can happen for many reasons, including illness, depression, stress, inadequate nourishment, or an eating disorder. Even when someone has intentionally lost weight and feels good about it, praising the change reinforces the idea that a smaller body is a better body.

We rarely consider what these compliments might mean to someone who later regains the weight. After hearing how wonderful they looked when they were thinner, they may worry that others now find them less attractive or assume they've let themselves go. Nobody has to make a negative comment about their weight gain for them to feel ashamed. The earlier praise has already communicated which version of their body was considered more desirable.

These messages also reinforce the stigma people with larger bodies face, whose appearance is often used to make assumptions about their health, behaviors, and self-discipline. We have become so accustomed to associating thinness with health and attractiveness, and have been told that everyone can and should control their weight and shape. These beliefs end up pathologizing the natural diversity of human bodies.

I think there's more to these reactions than jealousy, particularly for people who have struggled with eating disorders or chronic dieting. Seeing someone else lose weight can bring back memories of when changing your body felt like the solution to so many things you were unhappy about.

Think about all the things you may have missed out on because you felt bad about your body. Perhaps you avoided swimming with friends because you didn't want to be seen in a bathing suit, made excuses not to go to parties because you felt uncomfortable with how you looked, or refused to buy clothes you liked until you went down two sizes. You kept promising yourself that you'd do these things once you were thinner. Looking back, there's sadness in recognizing how many experiences you missed while waiting to feel comfortable in your body.

For someone recovering from an eating disorder, watching another person receive praise for losing weight can bring back memories of the approval they once received. You may remember feeling accomplished when the number on the scale went down, even though maintaining that weight required behaviors that were harming you. It can be upsetting to miss those feelings, especially when you remember how miserable you were at the time.

Eating disorder recovery involves questioning beliefs about food, weight, and self-worth that are deeply embedded in our culture. Sometimes those old thoughts resurface, particularly when the people around you celebrate the very thing you've worked so hard to stop pursuing.

Talk about weight loss has always been difficult to avoid, but the widespread use of GLP-1 medications has brought even more of these conversations into everyday life. People share stories about medications, compare how much weight they've lost, and discuss changes in their appetites. Even a simple dinner can turn into a conversation about portion sizes or how little someone is eating.

In my work as a therapist specializing in eating disorders and body image distress, I hear how frustrating this has become for people who have spent years trying to improve their relationship with food. After working to become less preoccupied with eating and weight, they're surrounded by people discussing medications and the pursuit of smaller bodies. Some describe feeling pulled back into a way of thinking they've worked hard to leave behind.

GLP-1 medications have also changed how people think about losing weight. With weight loss increasingly presented as something that can be achieved through medication, those who have stopped pursuing it may begin questioning their decision. There's a growing sense that if changing your body is possible, you should at least consider it. For someone who has worked to accept their body, that pressure can be difficult to resist.

If hearing about someone else's weight loss makes you want to start dieting again, think about what your previous attempts to lose weight were actually like. Maybe you lost weight and felt good about yourself for a while, only to regain the weight, sometimes more than you initially lost. Each time, you blamed yourself for not having enough willpower or discipline and promised you'd do better with the next diet.

Weight regain is common, in part because our bodies respond to weight loss by increasing hunger and conserving energy. Yet when a diet fails to produce lasting results, people tend to blame themselves rather than question the diet. After years of losing and regaining weight, that repeated sense of failure can seriously affect how someone feels about themselves.

It's worth remembering what those years of dieting actually involved. Perhaps you constantly thought about food, felt guilty after eating, or judged yourself based on what the scale said that morning. If you've decided to stop dieting or are recovering from an eating disorder, you likely had good reasons for wanting something different for yourself. Another person's weight loss doesn't change those experiences.

It's understandable to feel uncomfortable when a friend talks about losing weight, especially if you find yourself comparing your appearance to hers. You might even feel envious of the attention she's receiving and then guilty for having those thoughts. Given how much importance our culture places on thinness, these reactions aren't surprising.

Sometimes the way to cope with those feelings and protect your eating disorder recovery is to first pay attention to what happens afterward. Are you checking your body more frequently, reconsidering foods you've been comfortable eating, or thinking about restarting a diet? Recognizing these changes early can help you avoid slipping back into behaviors you've worked to move away from.

It's also reasonable to limit conversations about dieting and weight loss. You can change the subject or tell someone, "I'm trying to spend less time thinking and talking about weight, so I'd rather hear about what else is going on in your life." You don't owe anyone a detailed explanation of your relationship with food or your body.

We can't prevent other people from pursuing weight loss or control how much attention their changing bodies receive. But we can question why someone else's weight loss has become a reason to criticize our own appearance. You don't have to return to dieting, change how you eat, or become smaller simply because someone else has. Their body is theirs, and yours doesn't need to change in response.

We interviewed game director Mike Daly after the launch of Insomniac’s recent Wolverine game to get his take on what makes Wolverine and Spidey different to work on, his thoughts on breadcrumb trails, and why Logan can’t ever seem to get the girl.

Insomniac had extensive experience working on well-known superhero characters before starting on Wolverine. Early on, what traits or qualities of Logan as a story character and playable avatar did you know you wanted to focus on, distinct from the Spider-Man games and character?

From a story perspective, we wanted Logan to be a man with centuries of experiences that he can’t remember, creating a mix of being worn, wizened, and cynical. His resilience makes him fearless, which comes through in his candid manner, and he can’t ignore his sense of justice, as much as he would like to.

For gameplay, it was important that players felt the difference made by his healing factor, claws, berserker rage, animalistic strength, enhanced senses, his adamantium skeleton, and corresponding weight.

In both story and gameplay, emotionally and physically, Wolverine is a hero who gets hurt and feels that pain deeply but always gets back up again and presses on. This dogged resilience was really important to us and a central theme of story and gameplay.

Wolverine is more of a linear adventure than the open-world exploration of a big city seen in Spidey. In my playthrough, that added to the sense of relentless pacing that characterizes Wolverine as a fictional lead. Was that part of the thinking behind why the game was built that way, or was the more linear path there for other reasons?

Ultimately, we made a game suited for a Wolverine story that had comic book pacing – action and meaningful story development on every page.

We experimented with different structures and stories, evaluating from the perspective of: “Is this the best way to do Wolverine justice?” As the various things we tried didn’t feel right, we had to challenge our assumptions and look to our favorite Wolverine stories for inspiration, which led us to where we ended up – a story with momentum in which every beat felt authentic to the character.

Scenes and gameplay sequences that might have been fine in other games but didn’t strongly evoke Wolverine undermined the consistency of the fantasy we were trying to create, so we tried to keep things very focused.

After the game’s release, there was an active online conversation about some of the breadcrumb-trail guidance the game offers to lead you to pickups, or to mark the continued golden path of progression. What was the thinking internally about that design decision, and your take on the debate after the game launched?

Our thinking was that it was essential to make the player feel like they were empowered by Logan’s enhanced senses, a canonical mutant ability, enabling players to do things they could not with other characters. We wanted to weave that power into as many parts of the game as we could, so that if Logan could smell or hear something relevant, the player could see it too.

For collectibles, we wanted players to enjoy seeking out and spotting senses cues, enabling you to find things that would have been obscure to other characters. Using the cues, players can cover a lot of ground without feeling the need to scour, maintaining story and action momentum.

After the game launched, we saw feedback from players that interpreted senses as abstract designer guidance, along with a desire for options in how they approached exploration and discovery. Fortunately, we have been able to incorporate this feedback from players in our post-launch patches. One aspect we’ve improved is the scent trail’s organic presentation, so they give a stronger impression that they belong in the world rather than being an abstraction. For players that want to do more manual exploration, we’ve added options to remove the sense cues for optional collectibles, and one to make scent trails dimmer until the player requests a hint.

Ultimately, enhanced senses are still essential to creating an authentic Wolverine, and that’s still reflected in the cinematic and environmental storytelling of the game. I think it’s great that on top of that, players now have more flexibility in how they experience them.

As a longtime comics fan, I found Logan’s combat system especially exciting, particularly for the relentless forward momentum it provided. What do you think were some of the keys to nailing that dynamic, as distinct from other high-quality third-person action games?

There were a few key elements we wanted to get right - Logan’s resilience, rage, claw feel, and overall lethality.

For resilience, Logan’s healing factor needed to feel important for survival - a prominent element of the system. A natural starting point there was to try continual regeneration so you could outlast enemies, but that quickly fell apart as it incentivized the player to slow the pace of combat or even retreat for maximum advantage. Even though it was incredibly effective at enabling survival, it was also hard to notice by the player so it didn’t feel prominent.

The key to solving this problem was making a connection between rage and healing factor. The Last Stand mechanic is a healing surge that can happen when you lose all your health, themed as using adrenaline to keep Logan’s heart beating. This incentivized aggression and made a space for an up-close look at visceral healing.

Speaking of rage, it’s the backbone of the combat system where being an indicator of momentum is key. At the low end of rage, you don’t have the safety net of Last Stand, so your decision-making naturally biases towards survival; ensuring you prioritize threat assessment and defense. As you climb into the middle tier, you have the leeway to push your luck for longer combos or barrages that let you build up rage and get kills faster. Finally, you get to rage tier 3, where the quick decay of rage makes your rate of kills the top priority, so you might prefer to attack groups over ranged stragglers just for the improved kill density. Beyond the mechanics, we change the presentation of combat, especially through Logan’s animations and exertion to reflect his state of mind. The presentation and systemic incentives drive the player into a frenzy, just like Wolverine.

We invested a lot in the details of claw feel to make sure it was distinct from general game attacks. Claws shred, so we added locational body damage to our enemies to reflect that, along with a dynamic blood system where it globs together as it is spilled, splatters on contact with characters or the environment, drips down the walls, diffuses in the water, and other details. Many kills throughout combat are punctuated with custom animations with cinematic camera work that pushes in and frames the puncturing of claws into the body for a strong impression of the connection and weight of the puncture. Severing limbs on these attacks is an occasional reminder of how sharp Logan’s adamantium claws are. Reinforcing this is top-notch sound and haptics design, so each hit registers.

The last aspect I’ll touch on is lethality, the rate at which Wolverine is able to dispatch enemies and vice versa. We found that fights where enemies were more technical and could survive a lot undermined what players expected to experience as Wolverine, which was aggressively and lethally tearing through a lot of enemies. On the other hand, combat was less exciting if the enemies didn’t feel like a serious and deadly threat, and you didn’t feel like Wolverine if a little bit of caution could enable you to defend perfectly and avoid all damage.

These aspects led us towards encounters where you confronted large groups of enemies that were highly aggressive, to the point of feeling unfair. Enemies can’t believably go toe-to-toe with Wolverine alone, even world-class mercenaries, so their strategy is in group coordination, which creates a counterweight to Wolverine’s own rage-based momentum.

Marvel’s Wolverine draws in a number of characters from across the X-Men comic mythology, but notably makes no mention of some foundational figures of that fiction – Professor Xavier and Magneto. That, in turn, leads to dramatically different lives for characters like Jean Grey or Logan. Is the narrative you crafted an intentional exploration of a world where those figures weren’t present in the early days of mutant emergence?

I would say it was more of an exploration of the character than the world. We wanted a story where Logan is pulled into conflict not because he’s a crusader, but because there’s no other option. There are no X-Men to help. His growth as a person should be a challenging journey, which could go unearned if he was surrounded by wise, supportive, heroic characters pushing him in the right direction. In contrast, his dynamic with the members of Team X evokes his struggle. The adversity to personal growth is what makes it interesting.

Jean does end up being the person that pushes him in the right direction, and that works because they can relate to each other, having had similar struggles.

In several versions of Marvel storytelling, Jean and Logan share a special bond, but Jean ultimately chooses Scott Summers as a partner. But even without that barrier in your story, Logan still hits some roadblocks to love. Is Wolverine fundamentally a better character when he just can’t get the girl?

The way I would put it is that a Wolverine story is fundamentally better when you empathize with Logan’s pain and admire him for pressing on despite it. That goes for his emotional pain as much as physical. He’s a tragic character in this regard – all he wants is to be at peace but can never find it for long.

Wolverine gets to take on a lot of the “heavy hitters” of his comic mythology in different boss fights throughout the game. When you began development, do you recall who the very first character was that you decided he simply had to fight in your game?

Sabretooth. No character is a better foil for Logan. His inner turmoil and chaotic behavior helps illustrate Logan’s own inner beast and his struggle to resist it. Sabretooth’s strength and mutant powers also provide a mirror to Logan in combat, and his savagery creates shocking moments that leave an impression. We knew an epic confrontation with Sabretooth was going to be a thing from the start.

The RESET for Remission trial, presented at the 2026 European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD) Annual Meeting and published in The Lancet Regional Health Americas on October 1, found that more than half of adults with early onset type 2 diabetes entered remission after six months on a low calorie diet and supervised exercise program.

Early onset type 2 diabetes is when the condition is diagnosed before age 45.

Those with early onset type 2 diabetes may be at a higher risk of complications like long-term damage to the capillaries (microvascular complications), adverse cardiovascular outcomes, and earlier death.

Typical treatment for early onset type 2 diabetes is often a combination of lifestyle changes, like diet and exercise, and medications.

However, this new study suggests that a supervised low calorie diet and exercise program may help some people with early onset type 2 diabetes enter remission.

The RESET for Remission trial included 96 adults from England and Canada who were between the ages of 18 and 45, had early onset type 2 diabetes, obesity, and were not on insulin treatment.

The participants had an average age of 38 and an average body mass index (BMI) of 35.2 kg/m2. They were randomized into a control group that received usual care alone and the intervention group, which ate a low calorie diet and participated in supervised exercise programs.

During the first 12 weeks, participants in the intervention group followed an 800–900-calorie-per-day diet and completed twice-weekly supervised aerobic and resistance exercise sessions, along with one independent exercise session per week.

For weeks 13–24, they transitioned to a weight-maintenance diet and independent exercise.

Both groups were supervised by physicians, and the intervention group was also closely supervised by licensed dietitians.

The low calorie diet consisted of 30% protein, 50% carbohydrate, and 20% fat. Participants were also encouraged to drink at least 2 liters of calorie-free fluids per day and given access to a fiber-based laxative as needed.

In the first two weeks, all meals were replaced with specially formulated nutritional products (Optifast). Then, in weeks 3–12, the participants maintained the same calorie intake but had more flexibility in their food choices.

This second phase of the diet included one conventional meal per day, with daily intake targets that included protein, fruit, and vegetables. Typically, this meant 60 to 90 grams of protein from all sources combined, one portion of fruit, and two portions of non-starchy vegetables.

At the end of 24 weeks, 54% of the intervention group entered remission of type 2 diabetes, compared with only 4% in the control group. Researchers defined remission as an HbA1c level below 6.5% without glucose-lowering medication for at least 12 weeks.

“This study proves what most physicians have advocated for quite some time. Cutler also added that this study provides a clear example of how difficult it can be to enter diabetes remission with diet and exercise alone. He stated that the small number of participants in the study also makes him wonder if these findings can be applied to all populations.

Another limitation of this study, as Cutler pointed out, was that the strict dietary limits imposed on study participants would be very difficult for the average adult to adhere to.

The study also followed participants for only six months, so it’s unclear how many would remain in remission over the longer term.

“It is worth trying diet and exercise first because they can work to achieve meaningful weight loss and remission of diabetes,” he said.

“The most important thing for people with prediabetes to know is they should lose weight, reduce their carbs, exercise daily and, if that doesn’t work, then consider medication,” he said.

I’ve read to my children each night at bedtime for as long as I (and they) can remember. We started with the likes of “Goodnight Batcave” and the lesser-known-in-the-US but oh-so-wonderful collection of verse-driven classics from former UK Children’s Laureate Julia Donaldson.

We moved on to B.J. Novak’s anarchic love letter to the power of words (and to great typography), “The Book With No Pictures.”

We eventually worked our way up to Roald Dahl’s wickedly dark back catalog, discovering more complex characters, language and plotting as we went.

When my eldest, Amelia, hit 6, it was time to roll out the big guns.

Over the last 5 years (she’s now 11), I’ve read the seven Harry Potter books aloud twice, complete with all the voices: once to Amelia on her own, once to both her and her brother, Henry, two years her junior.

I had feared she wouldn’t be keen on another helping, but Amelia immersed herself once more in the world of Hogwarts with no hesitation and took immense joy in watching Henry discover for himself every twist and turn on Harry’s journey.

We have done the same thing with Tolkien’s “The Hobbit” and his fantasy masterpiece “The Lord of the Rings,” and are currently midway through “The Two Towers” on our second go around.

There’s a huge amount of reciprocal joy we take from this nightly ritual, beyond the practical benefits of providing a calming demarcation between the end of the day and the wind-down to sleep.

For me, it comes as either a break in my working day or a signal that it’s over, depending on how hectic things are.

For the kids, it’s an all-too-rare chance to get me all to themselves, no deadlines, screens, or other distractions to pull me away from being anything but 100% present.

For all three of us, it’s a literal (and literary!) warm blanket — a chance to escape under the covers to familiar worlds and familiar faces. It is, in short, incredibly good for our well-being.

And yet, in the world of health information — including on this site — thousands, if not millions, of reading hours are dedicated to the benefits of nutrition, sleep, and exercise as the building blocks of good health. Reading itself rarely makes that shortlist.

For me, it unequivocally deserves a place on it. Research seems to support that case. Even if we need more of it to really understand how and why.

A recent editorial in Psychological Medicine co-authored by Barbara Sahakian, Professor of Clinical Neuropsychology at Cambridge University, draws on a broad range of research linking reading for pleasure to better mental health and greater empathy.

The positive impact of reading on young people — just like Amelia and Henry — lso carries evidence.

Among more than 10,000 US adolescents, those who started reading for pleasure early had fewer mental health problems, spent less time on screens and slept more. Although, per an earlier column of mine, I can’t vouch for the sleep side of things when it comes to early bird Henry.

For many, reading can be a form of therapy that improves well-being. Bibliotherapy is a complementary therapeutic tool for emotional release and reduced stress and anxiety, with its roots traced back to ancient civilizations.

There’s even research suggesting a link between a love of reading and a lower risk of cognitive decline.

In a 2003 study of people aged 75 and over, reading was associated with a 35% lower risk of dementia over roughly five years.

A separate 14-year study from 2022 found that people who read at least weekly saw less cognitive decline, whatever their level of education.

A happy (and healthy) by-product of this love of being read to is that both my kids have now started reading to me at bedtime. Henry recently read “Jeffy the Burglar’s Cat” to us, a lesser-known but no less compelling chapter book by Ursula Moray Williams that was a staple of my own childhood. And we’ve enjoyed several books from Amelia, including the wistful tale of friendship and the circle of life, “Charlotte’s Web” by E.B. White – not a dry eye in the house for the final few chapters of that one.

They’ve also gone off and re-read the Harry Potter books and “The Hobbit” to themselves.

The research above links reading to many health benefits: better attention and memory, fewer mental health problems, more sleep, less time on screens, and greater empathy. I haven’t measured any of those impacts empirically in our house. Nor am I naive to the fact that as they get older, they’ll ultimately grow out of me reading to them, with school, extracurricular activities, their friends, and inevitably screens competing for more and more of their time.

Come what may, though, I know that a child who considers missing a reading session as the most cardinal of cardinal sins, and who scrambles for the chance to read aloud to their dad at bedtime is, at that moment, concentrating, empathizing, discovering and growing.

Researchers are clear that studies espousing the benefits of reading show association rather than proof, and it’s evident that more experimental work and deeper research are needed to ensure the complex cause and effect stands on firm scientific ground.

Regardless, it’s a healthy habit Amelia, Henry and I are very happy to keep, and with a bit of luck, I’ll get to pass it on to their kids one day.
