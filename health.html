LNGSHOT Talk Musical Attitude & Group’s Turning Point: ‘S.W.I.S.H.’

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Ahead of the EP's release, Billboard Korea sat down with LNGSHOT for an exclusive interview.

LNGSHOT return with their new EP S.W.I.S.H. The release continues the “hustle” the group has kept up since debuting in January this year with their first EP SHOT CALLERS. Since then, they have put out two mixtapes, the EP Training Day collecting their pre-debut work, and a string of OSTs and singles.

S.W.I.S.H. captures LNGSHOT’s style in a different way from SHOT CALLERS. Every track on SHOT CALLERS has its own distinct feel. On S.W.I.S.H., you can hear how the tracks connect to each other. Woojin added that it is “an album with a really fun musical attitude.”

S.W.I.S.H. marks both LNGSHOT’s growth and a turning point for the group. Ahead of its release, Billboard Korea sat down with LNGSHOT for an exclusive interview.

Since September, you’ve been special guests on Jay Park’s world tour SERENADES & BODY ROLLS. How did it feel to perform for overseas audiences, and are there any moments that stand out?

Woojin: I remember that a lot of Louis’s family came to our show in France. We were also lucky to tour with Jay Park for two months, and every show sold out except in one city. The crowds were so responsive, and that gave us a lot of strength. Early on after our debut there was so much to worry about. But the more we performed and the more the audience responded, the more relaxed we got, and we could just have fun. We really enjoyed it. We’re the first idol group Jay Park has created, so I think when we look back later, these two months on the road with him as special guests will feel like a one-of-a-kind moment.

Ryul: I think I learned how to enjoy myself on stage. He also passed on a lot of know-how, like fan service, how to treat fans, and how to handle a busy schedule. He taught us how to take care of our mental health and how to stay mentally strong. We were preparing our comeback while touring, so we matured in some ways, and we learned a lot about what we need to do on stage and going forward.

How did you balance the tour with preparing for your comeback?

Woojin: Even before the tour, we kept making songs whenever we had time. In July and August, before we left, we sorted out all the songs, re-recorded them, and revised every part. During the tour, we shot the music video overseas, changed the choreography, and did more recording. Funnily enough, “Bananas” is a demo we’d been considering since before our debut.

Tell us what makes this album S.W.I.S.H. and its title track “Bananas” different.

Woojin: Looking back to our debut, I think we got off to a good start. I think we had the energy to show people what makes us appealing. On this album, more and more of our own style is coming through, the same way the Memphis sound did on SHOT CALLERS.

“Bananas” is a song with a really fun musical attitude. The hook makes you feel like things are about to get hyped and you’re about to turn up, but then there’s a twist where it dies down. That’s where it hooks you even more. That attitude is what makes it fun.

Ryul: The more you listen to S.W.I.S.H., the more strangely addictive it gets. The tracks are connected, so it’s even better when you listen all the way through.

Ohyul: Every track is good. We started with SHOT CALLERS by making what we love, and that start went well, so I believe people will connect with this too. We made this album in our own style as much as we could.

What time of day would you recommend listening to S.W.I.S.H.? Tell us what feels right to each of you.

Ohyul: No matter what time it is, if our music makes you turn up, I think that makes it a good song. So I hope people listen anytime and turn up. (laughs)

Ryul: All of us, me included, keep a close eye on fan reactions through our 4shoboiz account (LNGSHOT’s second Instagram account). The Instagram algorithm showed me a post from someone who said listening to “Never Let Go” (track 5 on SHOT CALLERS) before bed helps them fall asleep. I hope the songs on S.W.I.S.H. also stay with listeners, whatever time of day they listen.

How was “Bananas” chosen as the title track?

Woojin: Before our debut, the production team sent us links to demos. There were quite a few songs, and the one we picked was “Bananas.” When I heard the hook, the shock was like getting CPR. It felt kind of like an electric shock, too.

Before it became the title track, there were five versions of “Bananas,” with names like “Bananas A” and “Bananas D-1.” There was even a version with a band feel. While refining the song, we also tried putting the parts in all kinds of different places.

Ryul: Everyone liked “Bananas.” I was really surprised when I first heard it. The version on the album was reshaped into LNGSHOT style as we wrote some of the parts ourselves.

Ohyul: Right. The demo of “Bananas” was shocking. Before our debut, we recorded it in our practice room, sent it to Jay Park, and played it for him too. So it’s amazing to see that song make it all the way to the title track.

We heard “Bananas” honestly captures the complicated emotions you felt as everything around you changed so quickly after your debut. What were those emotions?

Woojin: For me, it felt like reaching a big turning point. I’d been a trainee since elementary school, and I debuted at 19, right before becoming an adult. Preparing for stages and working on music while going to school felt surreal. Going to school and then catching a flight at the airport, actually being with an artist like Jay Park, all of it. Music was a big part of why I became a fan of Jay Park in the first place. Meeting rappers and beatmakers was amazing too.

There was pressure, too. I was attached to SHOT CALLERS and the response was good, so the pressure was huge. When we came back with the next EP, we had to show something even better. I think that feeling comes through in “Bananas.” I see “Bananas” as a song with two sides. Both the lyrics and the beat have that duality, and I really relate to it.

Ohyul: I think this album holds more emotions than SHOT CALLERS. A lot of fun moments and a lot of shocking moments went into it. When the beat in “Bananas” switches, I get that dual feeling Woojin talked about, and I like that. Listening to it feels like being transported to another place.

Woojin: It’s almost like an out-of-body experience.

Did you play the new songs for any peers or friends? How did they react?

Ohyul: Early in recording, I played it for Woonhak from BOYNEXTDOOR, and he really loved it. We play each other a lot of our music, not just EPs but even practice tracks.

While working on this album or on tour, what feedback from Jay Park stuck with you?

Woojin: What he kept saying was “This is really sophisticated.” He said he was moved that, only nine months after debut, our music already sounded mature and even polished.

Ryul: He said we feel more grown up. We also got closer to him during this tour. We were already close, but it feels like we’ve gotten even closer.

S.W.I.S.H. stands for “Swag With Integrity Shapes History,” and we heard it reflects LNGSHOT’s direction of starting a new wave with a swag that is genuinely your own. What does “swag with integrity” mean to you?

Ryul: To me, “Kim Ryul’s swag” is “swag I try to hold on to.” Staying the same, always. I try to be the same person whether I’m working or not. I think that’s more human and cooler. Jay Park is always the same too, with no line between work and his personal life, and I’ve learned a lot from seeing that side of him.

What does S.W.I.S.H. mean to each of you?

Ohyul: It’s like the starting line of a marathon. This is LNGSHOT’s first comeback, so it feels like we’re setting off again, in a different way from our debut. I want to look at LNGSHOT’s journey over the long run. You don’t finish a marathon in one go. You run it while managing your pace. Like a marathon, S.W.I.S.H. feels like an album where we’re just starting to run.

Ryul: I think it’s an album that marks a turning point. We’re releasing it at an important moment, not just for me but for LNGSHOT.

Woojin: To me, S.W.I.S.H. feels like the end of my teens. It’s like a year-end party of an album. Maybe because I’m still in school, it still hasn’t really sunk in that I’ve debuted. But I think S.W.I.S.H. might be the album that lets me say goodbye to my trainee days. It’s sad, but I’m grateful too.

It’s year-end awards season. It must be hard not to think about Rookie of the Year.

Louis: We’ve given it our all and worked hard the whole way, so whether we win or not, I don’t think we’ll have any regrets. If we don’t win, that’ll become motivation in its own way.

Ryul: If we win, I plan to send a thank-you message to our SHOTTIES and give a better reaction than anyone. (laughs)

Ohyul: Of course it would be great to win. But I realized that if you keep thinking about just one thing, beyond the award itself, you end up chasing only that and might feel empty later. So I decided to focus on what I can do right now.

What kind of year was this for each of you, and what do you hope next year will be like? What do you want to achieve next year? Even small things are fine.

Woojin: Something I realized on tour is that next year I want to put on a show just for SHOTTIES (our fandom name). Festivals and events are great too, but I want to do a show that’s LNGSHOT’s own. For the rest, I’ll go with the flow. (laughs)

Ryul: I have no plans. This year we did a lot of overseas schedules, so next year I want to see our fans in Korea up close more often.

Louis: I don’t have a specific goal. It’ll keep being tough, but I want to keep having fun like we have so far.

Ohyul: For me too, it’s starting our solo concerts. Just being able to kick off with a solo show in Seoul would mean so much.

Most traversal Phantom Edges in Phantom Blade Zero appear as you play through the main story, but You Loong's Deluge is different. It's not far off the beaten path, and you need it to complete one of the side quests, but it's still easy to miss if you don't explore every corner.

It's one of the more useful Phantom Edges in the game because it lets you reach areas you can't access without it. You can also use it effectively in combat, making it a great asset in Soul's arsenal. Here's how to get your hands on You Loong's Deluge.

You Loong's Deluge can be found in Pangzhen, northwest of the Green Serpent House and southwest of the Lion Dance Hall, with the latter being the closest bell. Unfortunately, it can be found in the possession of You Loong, Leader of the Blackwater Crew.

If you're coming from the Green Serpent House, you can easily walk right past You Loong, because he actually isn't visible from the main path. You have to hang a bit to the left of the main path and explore a bit before you find the wide-open space that's always indicative of a fight.

You can identify this wide-open space by a large body of water filled with candle-lit lanterns floating on the surface. As you approach, You Loong will burst from the water and initiate the fight that earns you You Loong's Deluge.

Surprisingly, You Loong isn't an exceptionally challenging fight, though that depends on your build at the time. He's not overly massive, not exceptionally fast and doesn't hit hard enough to drain your health in one strike. Most of his attacks can be parried or blocked easily, and he doesn't have many red-glint attacks you have to dodge.

One main thing to keep in mind is to take advantage of him when he dives below the water. While he's under, you can not damage each other, so it's a great opportunity to heal with medicines that replenish HP over time. After a while, he'll burst from the water and use small ranged attacks, but you can easily parry them.

If you want to attack him before he comes out of the water on his own, you can walk directly to the section of bubbles that indicates where he is, and he'll burst out regardless of where he is, using the same ranged attack as he would otherwise. It's a good way to corner him or get the jump on your next flurry of attacks.

Keep following this pattern until you've whittled down his health, staying patient and using the periods when he stays underwater. After enough cycles, you'll claim You Loong's Deluge.

You Loong's Deluge is one of the Phantom Edges in the game that's really the most beneficial depending on what build you're using. It creates a constant stream of water you can use to push enemies away while also applying the wet condition.

This means that after you use it on an enemy, they become more susceptible to both lightning and ice damage, so if you're using weapons that apply those conditions, pairing them with You Loong's Deluge will be exceptionally useful.

It's also useful against enemies that use fire weapons. After using the Deluge on one of these enemies, you will do higher damage to them, regardless of what weapons you're using.

You can also use it as a traversal item because it lets you extinguish the large, burning branches that block some pathways throughout the world, regardless of whether you have it equipped. This opens up more doors to collect some of Quick Hand Zhang's treasures and extra resources.

High Fat Diet Linked to Higher Colon Cancer Risk, Early Research Shows

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

In a new study, researchers report that a high fat diet may potentially increase the risk of colon cancer.

The researchers from Arizona State University said a diet high in certain fats can cause lasting changes in intestinal stem cells that lead to the development of tumors and the possibility of colon cancer.

The study was conducted in mice, but the researchers noted changes in the DNA of intestinal stem cells that may explain their growth and survival.

The researchers published their findings on November 12 in the journal Stem Cell Reports.

The researchers said the study is important because 1 in 25 people develop colon cancer in their lifetime. In the United States, that translates into about 108,000 new cases of colon cancer every year. About 55,000 males and 55,000 females are expected to die from the disease in 2026.

Experts not involved in the study say the research provides valuable information, but they have reservations about the results, as the findings do not yet apply to humans.

“The findings provide a potential mechanism linking diet to intestinal cancer development, but we need to be cautious about translating these results to humans,” said Dalia Perelman, MS, CDE, a research dietitian at Stanford University in California.

“We already have evidence from human studies that obesity and certain dietary patterns, particularly those high in processed meats and low in fiber-rich foods, are associated with increased colorectal cancer risk,” Perelman told Healthline. “This study adds a potentially important mechanism to investigate, but it does not establish that dietary fat itself increases colorectal cancer risk in humans.”

Ketan Thanki, MD, a colorectal surgeon specializing in benign and malignant diseases of the colon, rectum, and anus at the MemorialCare Todd Cancer Institute at Long Beach Medical Center in California, shared similar views.

“This study provides a better understanding at the cellular level of how a high fat diet may contribute to colorectal cancer development,” Thanki told Healthline.

“Animal studies are an important starting point for understanding biological processes, but we must be cautious about directly applying their findings to humans,” he added. “Mice and humans share many cellular and biological mechanisms, which makes these studies valuable in identifying potential pathways involved in disease development. However, human diets, genetics, metabolism, and environmental exposures are more complex.”

To reach their findings, the Arizona State researchers fed mice what the scientists described as a Western-style high fat diet for 6 months.

They then compared those results with those from mice given a low fat diet.

The researchers said the mice on the high fat diet gained more weight than the mice on the low-fat diet. They also developed more intestinal stem cells, and those cells grew at a more rapid rate.

They added that mice genetically predisposed to intestinal cancer who were fed a high-fat diet developed more and larger tumors.

The researchers noted changes in the DNA of intestinal stem cells that may help explain their increased growth and survival.

They also pointed out that stem cell behavior returned to normal after high fat diet mice were switched to a low fat diet, although some DNA changes persisted. After four weeks, approximately 18% of these changes remained.

When mice were returned to a high fat diet, the researchers said their intestinal stem cells responded more quickly and strongly than those of mice exposed to the diet for the first time.

They concluded that the study suggests that maintaining a healthy, low fat diet may help regulate intestinal stem cell growth and reduce colon cancer risk.

Kristin Kirkpatrick, RD, president of KAK Consulting and a dietitian at the Cleveland Clinic Department of Wellness & Preventive Medicine in Ohio, wasn’t involved in the study but highlighted a particular aspect of the findings.

“What I found particularly interesting was that even after the mice returned to a low fat diet, some of the molecular changes persisted,” she told Healthline. “This raises questions about how our dietary patterns over time may influence long-term health.”

Experts said these new findings are interesting but noted numerous caveats.

“One important takeaway is that dietary habits over time matter,” said Thanki. “Even if someone has consumed a high fat diet for years, making changes now may still provide meaningful health benefits.”

“I also think these findings are particularly important for children and adolescents, as establishing healthier dietary habits early in life may provide long-term benefits,” he added.

However, Thanki noted that not all fats are the same. “The health risks depend partly on the type of fat consumed, how much is consumed, and what foods make up the overall diet,” he said.

“Diets consistently high in saturated fats, particularly those containing large amounts of processed meats, fried foods, and highly processed products, can contribute to obesity, elevated cholesterol, cardiovascular disease, and metabolic dysfunction. These factors can also contribute to chronic inflammation and may increase the risk of certain cancers, including colorectal cancer,” Thanki continued.

Perelman said the diet used in the study was extremely high in fat, providing 60% of calories from fat, primarily from lard.

“We know that the health effects of dietary fats differ depending on their sources and fatty acid composition, and we cannot assume that a diet rich in unsaturated fats from foods such as olive oil, nuts, and seeds would produce the same effects,” she noted.

Perelman added that the researchers primarily studied stem cells from the small intestine rather than the colon.

“Although these tissues share some biological characteristics, they have important differences, and we cannot assume that the same changes would occur in the colon,” she explained.

Finally, Perelman noted that “the mice consuming the high fat diet also gained weight, making it difficult to separate the effects of dietary fat itself from those associated with increased body fat and other metabolic changes.”

“The goal shouldn’t necessarily be to eliminate fat because our bodies need healthy fats for normal functioning,” said Thanki. “Instead, people should focus on reducing saturated and trans fats while incorporating healthier unsaturated fats in appropriate amounts.”

Thanki had several dietary suggestions. Among them:

“Consistency matters more than perfection. Even modest dietary changes, maintained over time, can have meaningful health benefits,” Thanki said.

Perelman agreed. “The overall quality of the diet matters. Diets that are very high in fat may displace other foods and nutrients that have important health benefits,” she said.

“For colorectal cancer specifically, higher intakes of dietary fiber and whole grains have consistently been associated with lower risk,” Perelman noted.

“Therefore, rather than focusing exclusively on reducing fat, it is important to consider the overall dietary pattern and whether it provides adequate amounts of fiber-rich plant foods and other beneficial nutrients.”

Kirkpatrick agreed that the type of fat is what is important. “A Mediterranean-style diet that includes olive oil, nuts, seeds, and fatty fish can be relatively high in fat but is consistently associated with positive health outcomes,” she said.

“On the other hand, a diet high in saturated fat, processed meats, and ultra-processed foods may increase the risk of certain chronic diseases, particularly when it’s also low in fiber and other protective nutrients.”

Kirkpatrick also noted that many lifestyle habits contribute to a person’s overall health.

“Our health is influenced by so many factors beyond diet, including how we manage stress, our sleep habits, physical activity, genetics, and overall lifestyle,” she said.

Trump admin loses fight to spy on random journalists’ LinkedIn DMs

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Confidential sources “lifeblood” of free press, court rules in LinkedIn fight.

The Trump administration’s fight to stop suspected leaks of top-secret military information on LinkedIn can’t sweep in “a vast number of confidential sources” of random journalists “who have no conceivable relationship” to the Department of Justice’s investigation, an appeals court ruled Tuesday.

The order came after the DOJ asked LinkedIn to turn over 18 months of communications from targeted journalists who collectively published more than 1,000 articles on many different topics over that time period. The request was not limited to journalists involved in a controversial report from a news outlet that Trump said exposed confidential military information in a foreign country. (The outlet is not being named to protect national security secrets.) Instead, the DOJ cast a wide net, requesting extensive LinkedIn data on journalists who had any possible link to the journalists around the time the report was drafted.

The majority of journalists have LinkedIn accounts and publish their contact information to encourage sources to connect on the platform, LinkedIn argued. So allowing that government overreach went beyond harming targeted journalists and threatened to undermine a critical platform where journalists frequently connect with confidential sources, judges ruled. Even more concerning, the Trump administration secured a secrecy order barring LinkedIn from telling anyone about the sweeping request for a year. That left journalists unaware for far too long that their First Amendment rights had been compromised, judges ruled.

Penning the unanimous opinion from a three-judge panel, Ronald Reagan-nominated judge J. Harvie Wilkinson III rejected LinkedIn’s request to quash the order entirely and ordered the US district court to substantially narrow the order to avoid posing a grave threat to press freedoms.

Without such an intervention, the US risked silencing dissidents who raise alarms about “events of large importance and governmental behavior of dubious legality and integrity,” Wilkinson wrote. “A request by the government to scoop up reporters’ records wholesale and ascertain the identity of their sources would tip the balance against informed discourse in a manner inimical to democratic health.”

“Many of the users identified have no conceivable relationship to the leak investigation,” Wilkinson wrote. “Instead, they are affiliated with media outlets, universities, and law firms which may have been relevant to the host of other stories the targeted journalists were investigating during the same considerable time period.”

As Wilkinson explained, the district court failed to consider LinkedIn’s claims that the request imposed undue burdens and threatened the First Amendment rights of users who would have no way of knowing that their communications were being reviewed by the government. The lower court reasoned that the request imposed no burden on LinkedIn because the company could feasibly respond to it, then ruled that, regardless of how these matters were weighed, the balance would tip toward the government’s interest in obtaining the LinkedIn data.

This was an error that must be corrected, the appeals court ruled.

“It is not appropriate for district courts to approve requests purely because the records requested are logistically feasible to compile,” Wilkinson wrote. He further emphasized that “serious security and speech claims are in tension in this case. There is space under law for both.”

The appeals court has ordered the district court to go back and assess how the disclosure order can be narrowed. That includes entertaining a very limited alternative to the government’s secrecy order that LinkedIn had proposed—which would have notified a single attorney of the news outlet under investigation about the government’s request.

The Trump administration fought that limited request, arguing that the news outlet’s attorney may not be trustworthy or could possibly tip others off to the LinkedIn probe, but Wilkinson rejected those arguments. Noting that the justice system often deals with “imperfectly informed litigants,” Wilkinson said that “the government should be given an opportunity to explain why LinkedIn’s narrow disclosure would somehow compromise its investigation.”

“Unless the government’s response to LinkedIn’s proposal is more persuasive than anything the government has presented thus far, LinkedIn’s narrow exception to the breadth of the Secrecy Order must be adopted,” Wilkinson wrote.

LinkedIn, which is owned by Microsoft, does not seem to expect the government to be able to defend the sweeping request on remand. While a narrowed order may not protect all irrelevant users from being caught in the government’s hunt for the military leaker, it would stop Trump from accessing a broad range of journalists’ interactions with confidential sources. In a statement, a Microsoft spokesperson told Ars that “this is an important ruling for the right to privacy and press freedom.”

As high food costs and cuts to food assistance squeeze families’ budgets, concerns are growing about whether low-income parents will be able to keep putting food on the table.

But these financial pressures threaten to do more than increase hunger. They also risk harming children’s diets and health in the long run — by making nutritious foods not only harder to afford, but also increasingly risky for parents to buy.

To understand why, consider the most universal of parenting frustrations: picky eating. Most parents experience it, but the stakes vary widely. For well-off parents, watching a child push away a helping of vegetables is annoying but manageable. A carrot left untouched one day can simply mean trying again the next.

But for parents living close to the bone, that same rejected carrot is more than frustrating. It represents money that could have been spent on something their child would have actually eaten.

Simply put: Children’s food refusals come at a cost — one that few cash-strapped families can afford.

Such financial risks profoundly shape how low-income parents feed their kids, as sociologists have documented for years. A decade ago, Caitlin Daniel first showed how the risk of wasting money on rejected nutritious foods led low-income parents to prioritize buying less healthy items they knew their kids would eat. Years later, one of us (Priya) found that catering to kids’ preferences was also driven in part by parents’ earlier bouts of food insecurity: Determined to spare their children those experiences, caregivers often relied on familiar, comforting foods they knew would fill their kids’ stomachs.

Our newest study adds another dimension to this picture. Low-income parents across three states described going to considerable lengths to prioritize children’s nutrition, often at the expense of their own diets.

At the same time, to conserve resources and keep dinnertime from turning into a battle, they paid close attention to their kids’ likes and prioritized buying what they knew their children would eat. As one mother put it, she’d rather her daughter be “happy and fed than sad and hungry.”

Within such severe financial constraints, catering to children’s preferences is strategic. But it can also undermine children’s diets and health over time. That’s because raising kids to be healthy eaters is a long game: Children’s tastes develop gradually as they encounter and become familiar with different foods, and young children often need to try unfamiliar, healthier ones — like bitter vegetables — multiple times before accepting them.

That’s why pediatricians and nutrition experts routinely encourage parents to keep offering fruits and vegetables, even when kids turn them down. It’s smart advice, but it assumes parents can shoulder the costs of following it.

For families with little money to spare, doing so has always been a gamble. And today, it’s becoming an even bigger one. Groceries now cost nearly a third more in dollar terms than they did in 2020. Meanwhile, participation in SNAP — the nation’s largest food assistance program — has fallen by over 5 million people, including an estimated 2 million children, since summer 2025.

These economic pressures are now showing up in parents’ food decisions. A national survey found that more than half of low-income parents reported cutting back on groceries, while over two-thirds worried about having to choose between paying bills and buying healthy food for their children.

Forcing parents into such trade-offs doesn’t just threaten their ability to keep children fed. It also undermines their efforts to help children eat — and learn to enjoy — nutritious foods.

Fortunately, there are ways to address both problems.

First and foremost, we need to ensure children have enough to eat. That means restoring access to food assistance. The next Congress should reverse the cuts to SNAP enacted through H.R. 1 and stop SNAP benefit costs from being shifted to states — a shift that could jeopardize continued access to a program millions of families depend on to put food on the table. To make a bill reversing the cuts veto-proof, it should be a bipartisan effort.

But we also need to invest in programs that support parents’ efforts to feed their children nutritious foods, including incentives that make those purchases more affordable and less financially risky. Protecting and strengthening WIC’s fruit and vegetable benefit and expanding produce incentives like Double Up Food Bucks for SNAP recipients can help families stretch their grocery dollars further, giving them more room to buy nutritious foods their children might not eat the first time around.

Schools and childcare programs must also be critical partners in this work. Beyond providing nutritious meals, they can give kids regular opportunities to encounter and try healthier foods without leaving parents to shoulder the full financial burden. Expanding initiatives like the Department of Agriculture’s Fresh Fruit and Vegetable Program and Farm to School programs, which help introduce children to new foods through fresh produce, taste tests, gardens, and other activities, can help.

Nutrition experts are right to encourage parents to keep introducing healthy foods, even when their children refuse them. But advice that families can’t afford to follow simply won’t cut it. We need food policies that not only ensure children have enough to eat, but also give parents the resources and support to help them develop healthy eating habits that last.

In fact, having a history of reacting to peanuts and positive allergy testing on their own may not be sufficient to diagnose a peanut allergy for many infants.

The study’s authors suggest that a threshold oral food challenge — in which the child is given increasing amounts of the suspected food allergen under medical supervision to look for an allergic reaction — should be routinely considered to help confirm peanut allergy.

This additional testing could safely prevent children who are not allergic to peanuts from undergoing unnecessary treatment with oral immunotherapy.

It could also spare families from burdensome food restrictions and allergy precautions.

The researchers’ goal was to evaluate the accuracy and safety of the Allergy Development to an Accelerated Path to Tolerance (ADAPT) peanut oral immunotherapy program, which was established in 2024 at 10 public children’s hospitals across Australia.

This program provides a standardized approach to evaluating and treating infants with suspected peanut allergies.

The study included infants who had experienced a mild to moderate reaction to peanut before 12 months of age and had evidence of peanut sensitization through allergy testing.

Researchers reviewed medical records to examine how accurately the program’s approach identified peanut allergies and whether it could safely guide treatment.

A key part of the evaluation was a threshold oral food challenge. The challenge involved up to seven doses, ranging from 15 to 1,300 milligrams of peanut protein.

Medical teams monitored the infants for reactions and provided treatment when necessary.

Researchers reviewed records from April 2024 through October 2025. During that period, 1,275 infants were assessed through the program, and 778 underwent an oral food challenge.

The researchers also examined allergy test results and whether infants who reacted during the challenges could begin oral immunotherapy at an appropriate starting dose.

At the time of the oral food challenge, the infants had a median age of 13 months.

Of the 778 infants who underwent an oral food challenge, 507 (65.2%) had an allergic reaction.

However, 271 (34.8%) did not react to peanut during the supervised test, despite having a history of a previous reaction and positive allergy test results.

These findings suggest that relying on medical history and allergy tests alone may lead some infants to be labeled with a peanut allergy when they can actually tolerate the food.

Among the infants who reacted during the challenges, 61 experienced anaphylaxis, a severe allergic reaction that can affect breathing and blood pressure. This represented 12% of infants with a positive challenge and 7.8% of all infants who underwent testing. Medical teams safely managed these severe allergic reactions.

Researchers also found that infants who reacted to peanut generally had higher skin prick test results and Ara h 2-specific IgE levels than those who did not react.

However, these test results did not reliably indicate how much peanut would trigger a reaction or how severe that reaction would be.

Of the 394 infants who began peanut oral immunotherapy, 384 (97.5%) successfully started treatment at a dose below the amount that had triggered a reaction during their food challenge.

The researchers say these findings support considering supervised oral food challenges to confirm suspected peanut allergies before starting treatment or recommending long-term avoidance.

Erin Banta, MD, a board certified allergist and immunologist who treats both children and adults at Latitude Food Allergy Care in Great Neck, New York, said these findings are significant because they provide evidence for early use of oral food challenges in infants with peanut reactions.

“In those with mild-moderate prior reactions to peanut, early oral food challenge may allow for either delabeling of food allergy (in 1/3 of the patients in this study) or diagnosis of food allergy,” she told Healthline.

Banta, who wasn’t involved in the study, added that early delabeling takes a significant burden off the patient and their family.

“On the other hand,” she explained, “early diagnosis allows for early initiation of treatment with oral immunotherapy at a time in the patient’s life associated with the greatest success rates.”

Banta further noted that the study offers different pathways for oral immunotherapy based on the patient’s reaction threshold. This means that some patients may be able to complete treatment more quickly due to their individual threshold dose.

According to Forstie, who was also not part of the study, the goal is to avoid unnecessary food restrictions while also keeping children safe.

“The key message is that not every positive allergy test means a child has a food allergy,” she told Healthline. “Accurate diagnosis helps protect children while allowing those who are not allergic to enjoy a more normal, less restrictive diet.”

I do know that what was taught, or not taught, to them before the assaults contributed to their behavior — for instance, when they ignored a person telling them “No,” either verbally or by their body language; when belonging to a group mattered more than doing what was right; when they disregarded a person’s boundary; when they did not stop when a person was impaired; or when they did not protect a person in danger, and all of this was seen as acceptable.

I couldn’t help but look back at my own children’s childhoods. Some of my proudest moments as a mother happened at parent-teacher conferences when I learned that my son had noticed a classmate sitting alone and invited him to join his lunch table. Another time, I learned that my daughter had stood up for a child who was being bullied, knowing it might cost her friendships.

We begin shaping our children the moment we hold them as infants and respond to their needs with love and compassion. Babies learn empathy by receiving it when we respond to their cries and name their feelings. They learn that their feelings matter, as do the feelings of others. How we treat our children and others becomes how they treat other people as well.

We teach toddlers about boundaries by teaching them that their bodies belong to them. That means not forcing them to hug relatives, and making sure that when a sibling says “stop,” they stop. And we teach them that behavior has consequences: When you hurt someone, you take responsibility for what you did and do what you can to make the situation better.

Children learn respect, compassion, and right from wrong through what their parents (or primary caregivers) say, how they behave, and what they overhear. If they hear the adults around them say a girl “asked for it,” that is where victim-blaming is learned. Instead, we can model kindness, empathy, respect for boundaries, respect for others, self-respect, respect for rules, and the critical thinking it takes to resist groupthink.

We can’t control every influence. Children learn from everything around them: school, friends, their friends’ parents, television, and social media. That is why parents’ influence is so important. Their words and actions matter. Parenting happens through both messaging and modeling. Children notice more than most parents realize: the subtle cues and the conversations they assume their children weren’t paying attention to. And when a parent does realize that they sent the wrong message through words or behavior, they can tell their children they made a mistake and explain what they want them to learn instead.

Through our own actions, we show children how to respect other people’s boundaries and needs. We teach them to read one another’s facial expressions and body language. We teach them that if someone has been drinking or using other substances and is not able to make decisions for themselves, they cannot take advantage of that person. We teach them that wrongdoing has consequences. And we teach them that if they are part of a group that is doing things they are uncomfortable with, or that they know are wrong, they need to separate from the group and even get help if needed. Being different from a group is hard, but doing what’s right matters more.

Teaching empathy, compassion, boundaries, respect, courage, ethics, consequences, and critical thinking is no guarantee against abuse, but it’s certainly a great start. And I hope that raising children with empathy means raising adults who believe survivors and provide support.

Reductions in hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) were observed with a novel small interfering RNA (siRNA) therapy in patients with chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, according to a phase II study.

Among patients randomized to monotherapy with one of four doses of the siRNA HRS-5635, all 32 on the highest dose (400 mg) had HBsAg levels less than 100 IU/mL after 48 weeks of treatment, with 88% still at that level 24 weeks after treatment ended.

Among this high-dose group, 16% achieved a functional cure, with an HBsAg clearance level of less than 0.05 IU/mL after 48 weeks -- a percentage that rose to 19% by 24 weeks post-treatment, reported Xieer Liang, PhD, of Southern Medical University in Guangzhou, China, at the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases annual meeting in Denver.

"A dose-dependent antiviral response was observed with HRS-5635," Liang said. "All HRS-5635 regimens were generally safe and well-tolerated."

An estimated 240 million people worldwide have chronic HBV infection, including about 640,000 U.S. adults. Chronic HBV can cause liver damage, cirrhosis, liver cancer, and death. Five antivirals and two immune modulators are approved in the U.S. to treat chronic HBV, with treatment regimens lasting from 6 months to over 1 year.

In a previous phase II trial, siRNA therapy with an immunomodulator reduced HBsAg in patients with chronic HBV infection. Last month, the FDA approved another nucleic acid-based therapeutic, the antisense oligonucleotide drug bepirovirsen (Hibsago), for patients with chronic HBV taking antiviral therapy based on results from the B-Well 1 and B-Well 2 studies. In those trials, 19% to 20% of patients with HBsAg levels between 100 and 3,000 IU/mL had a functional cure with bepirovirsen at week 72.

The current trial included patients with chronic HBV on stable nucleos(t)ide analogue therapy who had HBsAg levels over 100 and less than 3,000 IU/mL and HBV DNA levels less than 20 IU/mL. Patients could be hepatitis B envelope antigen (HBeAg) positive or negative, but they couldn't have cirrhosis. All patients received tenofovir amibufenamide during 48 weeks of HRS-5635 treatment.

Mean age ranged from 41.5 to 44.6 years in the four treatment groups, 59.4% to 76.7% were men, and mean HBsAg ranged from 1,058.2 IU/mL to 1,200.4 IU/mL. Most patients were HBeAg negative (65.6% to 73.3%).

The investigators defined functional cure as sustained HBV DNA suppression and HBsAg clearance of less than 0.05 IU/mL, with or without seroconversion, sustained for 24 weeks after treatment.

All four HRS-5635 doses achieved the study's primary endpoint of HBsAg reduction from baseline to week 12, with mean reductions of -1.26 log10 IU/mL in the 50-mg group, -1.90 log10 IU/mL in the 100-mg group, -1.99 log10 IU/mL in the 200-mg group, and -2.12 log10 IU/mL in the 400-mg group.

Among patients in the 400-mg group, 45% converted from HBeAg positive to HBeAg negative after 48 weeks of treatment, a percentage that slid to 27% at 24 weeks after treatment.

In patients receiving the 400-mg dose who were HBeAg negative and had HBsAg below 1,000 IU/mL at baseline, 33% reached the functional cure rate of HBsAg less than 0.05 IU/mL.

Across the four groups, 46.9% to 60% had a treatment-related adverse event. There were only two such events that were grade 3 or higher, and no serious adverse events related to treatment and no deaths occurred. Upper respiratory tract infections were the most common adverse event, occurring in 25% to 33.3% of patients in the four groups.

Alanine transaminase elevations occurred in up to 6.7% of patients during and after treatment, Liang said, but levels were generally stable during treatment, and no elevations reached a grade 3 level.

The follow-up period of 24 weeks after treatment ended was relatively short, Liang cautioned, but a longer-duration extension study is ongoing, along with a phase III trial.

Anxiety Is More Complex Than We Might Think

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Posted November 10, 2026 | Reviewed by Lybi Ma

Anxiety can be a necessary response to potential threats, but also a serious chronic condition that negatively affects our functioning and well-being. In either case, we feel wary and on edge, scared or irritable, with overactive thoughts. We are tense and easily startled into more powerful fear responses.

The key factor is whether our fear-system, our biological responses to threat, can ‘switch off’ once the threat has passed. If we hear a strange creak alone in bed at night, we might tense up, hold our breath and listen carefully; our heart beating faster. If we hear it again and recognise the hot water pipe noises, we can relax and quickly fall back to sleep.

However, if anxiety is a habitual state, we might still guess that it was the hot water pipes, but we can’t relax—we keep imagining an intruder breaking in—staying awake for hours in a state of fear.

What is anxiety? The biological response is at the core, what I call fear-alert, our instinctive reaction to danger sensed at a distance.[1] Our hearing, eyesight, and sense of smell become more acute to evaluate the threat, and our startle response is readied, and the nerve signal regulating our heart rate is withdrawn. The ‘brake’ that holds our fear responses in check is removed. We are now ready for extreme life-saving action.

This means our nervous system is highly volatile now; even minor or imagined threats can put us in a high-energy state of fear-arousal—fight, flight, or freeze. In this state, the heart's pumping capacity shifts up a gear, and a flood of hormones enriches the bloodstream with glucose and other nutrients, providing sustained energy.

Because fear-alert frequently switches into fear-arousal, our experience of ‘anxiety’ covers fear-arousal symptoms, such as a racing heart, dry mouth, sweaty palms, nausea or numbness, as well as the wariness, irritability and worrying thoughts of fear-alert.

None of this would be a problem if our fear responses deactivated once the threat had passed. However, this can only happen when we feel safe. If we are under threat and don’t feel safe for long periods, the biological processes that switch off our fear responses start to lose effectiveness, and we can get stuck in fear-mode, a chronic state of fear-alert that frequently tips us into fear-arousal or fear-collapse [2] [3]. This is another way of talking about complex trauma, which affects many areas of life, as we can see in the following example:

Adrian was a young man with a responsible managerial job who suffered from constant high levels of anxiety and was unable to make decisions about work and relationships, leading to constant self-criticism. He worked for an aggressive boss who treated him like a slave.

He found all social interactions stressful and exhausting; he felt the need to please everyone and keep them entertained. This burden led him to avoid intimate relationships, and the relationships he had didn’t last long.

He experienced all his pain and confusion as being "in his head," with his mind always overactive but his body “numb from the neck down.”

I worked with Adrian for nearly five years. He had previously tried CBT and found it unhelpful. I felt under huge pressure to alleviate his anxiety quickly, which triggered my own fear responses, but our work was painfully slow.

It emerged that he had suffered from anxiety since he was 3 years old. He had been highly disturbed by his mother’s acute distress at the death of her own mother. This loss of his caregiver was compounded by a sense that he had caused her collapse, and throughout his childhood he constantly tried to make her happy, becoming overburdened by responsibility.

Understanding the impact of his early history interested Adrian; however, it did not alleviate his anxiety, and realising that he didn’t have to take responsibility for the happiness of others also changed nothing. Being stuck for many years in a continuous state of fear activation had created a complex tangle of problems: nervous system volatility, chronic bodily tension, the numbing of sensation, deeply ingrained patterns of thought and behaviour towards others, habitual self-criticism and an inability to seek care.

We explored all these areas with little success at first. What started to shift things, however, was understanding and regulating my own fear-system. I stopped reacting with anxiety to Adrian’s pressing need for a cure and stopped overworking. I became a calmer presence, and he responded, eventually able to engage with ways of working that were not initially helpful. We worked on regulating breathing and using a focus-oriented approach that gradually helped him become reconnected with his bodily sensations. [4]

By the time Adrian finished work, he had become far less of a people-pleaser, had changed his boss and asserted himself more at work. He was in a relationship where he was gradually allowing himself to accept care as well as give it, and his generalised anxiety had greatly diminished.

Anxiety can be highly complex, and treating it at the level of a thought disorder that needs correction can lead to disappointment and frustration. Treatment may take time, and it may consider many aspects and use several different approaches. It also requires the therapist to understand and regulate their own anxiety in the work.

If part of the reason that you don’t want to give feedback to your therapist is because you are afraid that they will stop liking you, that is also really important information that you need to share with your therapist. There could be many reasons for you feeling this way, and they are all really important to identify and discuss in therapy.

It’s normal for you to want other people to like you. This is especially true of your therapist, with whom you have shared a lot of sensitive information about yourself. Therapists have an ethical obligation to do no harm, to try to benefit you with their care, to build trust, practice fairly and justly, and respect the dignity and privacy of all people. This means that the therapist needs to be making decisions based on what they feel is healthiest for you and the therapeutic relationship. Therefore, even if your feedback is somehow given in such a way that your therapist could feel negatively about it, that is not a reason that you would not share it.

A therapists job is to take in the information that you’re sharing and to consider how the therapy session could be augmented to better meet your needs. They will not stop liking you if you provide constructive feedback, and if you perceived that were somehow to be the case, that would be a really important area for discussion and ultimately may signal that you and your therapist are no longer a good fit.

The feedback you provide can help the therapist engage in shared decision-making with you to see if you need a new treatment approach, if they need to refer you to a colleague so you can meet with someone who specializes in a different areas (e.g., couples counseling or EMDR sessions). This could mean engaging with different clinicians for different treatments at the same time or pausing your current treatment while receiving this care.

As a therapist, avoiding feedback doesn't mean you can assume a client is completely satisfied with the therapy: it more likely means you won't have a good understanding of the reasons why clients discontinue their sessions with you. Asking for feedback routinely normalizes the practice, helps you provide better care, and helps you learn and grow as a clinician so you can build your skills and continue benefiting your clients.

Alzheimer’s may have early warning signs outside the brain

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Alzheimer's disease is best known for its effects on memory and thinking, but new research suggests that some symptoms may develop in a surprising place: outside the brain.

Scientists at the University of Central Florida (UCF) have discovered that genetic mutations associated with Alzheimer's can interfere with the nerves that control muscle movement, even without involvement from the brain or spinal cord. The findings could help explain why some people with Alzheimer's experience changes in walking, balance, or coordination long before noticeable memory problems emerge.

The discovery raises the possibility that movement difficulties could provide earlier warning signs of the disease and that treatments focused exclusively on the brain may overlook important problems elsewhere in the body.

The research was led by James Hickman, a professor at UCF's Nanoscience Technology Center, and Research Professor Xiufang "Nadine" Guo, working alongside scientists at the healthcare technology company Hesperos. Funded by the National Institutes of Health's National Institute on Aging, the study used laboratory systems built from human cells to investigate how inherited Alzheimer's mutations affect movement. The findings were published in Alzheimer's & Dementia: The Journal of the Alzheimer's Association.

"Motor deficits may be an earlier indication [of Alzheimer's]," Guo says. "If we can detect those changes and intervene earlier, that could help delay the onset of central nervous system symptoms."

Why Alzheimer's Symptoms May Appear Before Memory Loss

Alzheimer's is generally associated with progressive memory loss, confusion, and declining cognitive abilities. However, doctors have also observed that some patients develop physical symptoms, including changes in balance, coordination, and gait (manner of walking), years before clear cognitive difficulties become apparent.

These observations have raised an important question: Are such movement problems simply a consequence of damage occurring in the brain, or could Alzheimer's also interfere directly with nerves elsewhere in the body?

To investigate, the UCF team focused on familial Alzheimer's, a rare inherited form of the disease that typically develops earlier in life (from 40 to 65 years of age) than the more common form.

Their experiments revealed that motor neurons carrying mutations linked to familial Alzheimer's could disrupt the connections responsible for controlling muscles, even when the brain and spinal cord were completely absent from the experimental system.

Motor neurons are specialized nerve cells that transmit signals to muscles, telling them when to contract and produce movement. The new findings suggest that Alzheimer's mutations can interfere with this process directly, rather than affecting movement only through damage to the brain.

"This is the first time it's been demonstrated that deficits in the peripheral nervous system can arise directly from these mutations," Hickman says. "It means drugs that target the brain may not fix problems in the rest of the body."

The peripheral nervous system includes the nerves located outside the brain and spinal cord. These nerves carry signals between the central nervous system and the rest of the body, helping control movement, sensation, and other essential functions.

Guo also points out that preserving the ability to move could have benefits beyond physical health. Regular physical activity is associated with better cognitive well-being, so protecting motor function may also help support brain health.

How a Miniature Human Cell System Revealed the Discovery

To examine the relationship between Alzheimer's mutations and muscle movement, the scientists used an innovative laboratory platform known as "human-on-a-chip" technology.

Developed and manufactured through Hesperos, a company co-founded by Hickman, these miniature systems bring different types of human cells together under controlled conditions. They allow researchers to recreate specific biological functions and observe how diseases interfere with them.

Unlike conventional laboratory experiments that examine isolated cells, these systems can reproduce important interactions between different tissues. They also provide an alternative to animal models, which do not always accurately reflect how human diseases behave.

For this study, the team created a miniature model of the neuromuscular junction, the connection where a motor neuron communicates with a muscle cell.

The model was deliberately designed without a brain or spinal cord. This was a crucial part of the experiment because it allowed the researchers to investigate whether Alzheimer's-related changes could cause movement problems independently of the central nervous system.

The scientists combined healthy muscle cells with motor neurons derived from stem cells that carried genetic mutations associated with familial Alzheimer's disease. Stem cells are cells that can develop into specialized types of cells, making them valuable tools for studying human diseases in the laboratory.

The researchers then examined how effectively the altered nerve cells communicated with otherwise healthy muscle tissue.

The results showed that the Alzheimer's-associated mutations could interfere with normal nerve-to-muscle function. This suggests that at least some movement difficulties linked to the disease may arise directly in the peripheral nervous system rather than being caused entirely by degeneration in the brain.

Why Damaged Nerve Connections Can Affect Walking and Balance

Every voluntary movement depends on successful communication between the nervous system and the muscles.

At the neuromuscular junction, an electrical signal traveling along a motor neuron triggers chemical communication that causes a muscle to contract. This process makes everyday activities possible, from walking and maintaining posture to lifting objects and performing precise movements.

When the connection becomes impaired, muscles may respond less effectively to nerve signals. Depending on the severity and location of the problem, this can contribute to weakness, reduced coordination, and difficulty sustaining physical activity.

To evaluate these effects, the research team measured how consistently nerve signals produced muscle contractions and how long the muscles could continue contracting before becoming fatigued.

These measurements provided a direct assessment of how well the nerve and muscle cells worked together. They also resemble aspects of the functional testing physicians use when examining patients with movement disorders.

"You can't move unless the motor circuit works," Hickman says. "When a doctor taps your knee to check your reflex, they're testing that exact connection."

By demonstrating impaired nerve-to-muscle communication in a system containing no brain or spinal cord, the study provides evidence that mutations linked to familial Alzheimer's can produce effects beyond the central nervous system.

However, the experiments do not establish that these changes necessarily occur before memory loss in patients. Further research will be needed to determine how closely the laboratory findings reflect the progression of Alzheimer's in people, particularly those with the more common forms of the disease.

Could This Discovery Lead to Earlier Alzheimer's Detection?

The findings suggest a potentially important new direction for Alzheimer's research: looking beyond memory and other cognitive symptoms when investigating the disease.

If future studies confirm that changes in motor function are detectable early in certain patients, tests of walking, coordination, or nerve-to-muscle activity might eventually help identify disease-related changes before more obvious neurological symptoms develop.

The results also raise questions about treatment. Many Alzheimer's therapies are designed to address processes occurring in the brain, but medications that target the central nervous system may not necessarily correct abnormalities affecting peripheral nerves and muscles.

Understanding these separate effects could help researchers investigate treatments that protect physical function alongside cognitive health.

Human-on-a-Chip Technology Could Transform Disease Research

Beyond the implications for Alzheimer's, the researchers see considerable potential in using human-on-a-chip systems to investigate other diseases and evaluate possible treatments.

Because these platforms contain human cells and reproduce measurable biological activities, they can help scientists observe disease-related changes in conditions that more closely resemble human physiology than some conventional experimental models.

This approach may be particularly useful in drug development, where researchers need to understand not only how a potential treatment affects individual cells but also how it influences interactions between different tissues.

Such systems can also uncover biological effects that may be difficult to detect in animal experiments, potentially helping researchers identify promising therapies and recognize limitations earlier in development.

For Hickman, the progress represents the culmination of three decades of work devoted to understanding disease through more realistic laboratory models.

"These systems let us study disease in a way that's closer to what actually happens in the human body, and that's what we need to develop better treatments," he says.

Replacing dairy with plant-based alternatives (PBAs), even partially, could substantially lower iodine intake, especially if those alternatives aren’t fortified, according to a recent study published in the Lancet Planetary Health.

The United Kingdom-based researchers found that only 12% of UK plant-based milk, yogurt, and cheese products analyzed were fortified with iodine. This compares with 61% for calcium and 46% for vitamin B12.

In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) provides guidance on the nutritional content of PBAs, but fortification with iodine is voluntary.

The study found that replacing as little as 20% of dairy intake with plant-based alternatives could increase the risk of inadequate iodine intake, particularly among adolescent girls and women of reproductive age.

“People often worry about a decline in calcium intake when they replace some of their dairy with plant-based alternatives. As a result, iodine is frequently overlooked. This study serves as a valuable reminder of iodine’s significance as a nutrient,” said Victoria Finn, MD, board certified endocrinologist at Medical Offices of Manhattan, who wasn’t involved in the study.

The study included analysis of UK plant-based milk, cheese, and yogurt products, as well as dietary data from 3,500 people.

The researchers used statistical modeling to predict how swapping dairy for PBAs might affect iodine and other nutrients.

The people who are most vulnerable to iodine inadequacy are adolescent females and people at reproductive age, especially those who are pregnant or who plan to become pregnant.

When the researchers modeled the replacement of all dairy products with PBAs, the proportion of adolescent females with inadequate iodine intake rose to 80%. Among females ages 19 to 49, that proportion remained relatively stable as replacement rates increased.

It is important to keep in mind that the study used dietary modeling rather than direct measurement of iodine status.

Iodine is important for thyroid hormone production, metabolism, and both fetal and child brain development.

“Your thyroid gland requires iodine to produce thyroid hormones, which play a crucial role in regulating metabolism and supporting normal growth and development,” said Finn.

She added that if you consistently consume too little iodine over an extended period, the thyroid gland may enlarge and eventually struggle to produce enough hormones. This condition is known as hypothyroidism, which can lead to various symptoms such as lethargy, sleepiness, weight gain, dry skin, and more.

“Hypothyroidism can be particularly dangerous during pregnancy, as the baby’s brain, nervous system, and organs rely heavily on the mother’s thyroid hormone during the early stages of development,” Finn added.

In the United Kingdom, dairy is a major iodine source due to cattle feed supplementation, not because dairy is naturally high in iodine.

In the United States, dairy is also an important source of iodine for the same reasons.

However, the risk profile for iodine deficiency in the U.S. may differ from the U.K., as iodized salt is also a significant source of iodine and is widely available in the U.S., but not in the U.K.

According to the National Institutes of Health (NIH), the recommended daily intake of iodine depends on your age.

Plant-based or plant-forward diets have grown in popularity, in part due to the health benefits they offer. For example, consuming less meat can reduce your risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, obesity, and high cholesterol.

However, as this study suggests, it may be possible to reduce your iodine intake when transitioning to a plant-based diet. There are ways you can protect your iodine intake when choosing a more plant-forward diet, though.

Check the labels on plant-based dairy alternatives to find those fortified with iodine. You can also read the nutrition label. Iodine may be listed as potassium iodide or potassium iodate.

Seafood, eggs, and iodized salt are all good sources of iodine.

You can also discuss iodine supplements with your healthcare professional.

“Iodine supplements should be taken with caution, especially without checking your thyroid levels,” said Finn. “Additionally, be cautious with kelp supplements, as their iodine content can vary, and excessive iodine intake can disrupt thyroid function.”

Mammograms are widely used to help detect breast cancer, but they may also offer insight into heart health.

The routine X-ray screening may identify breast arterial calcification (BAC), which is the presence of calcium deposits within the walls of the small arteries in the breast.

On a mammogram, BAC is commonly identified as thin, white, parallel lines that give a “tram-track” appearance.

While BAC is harmless to the breasts, it is associated with a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular disease. A 2022 study found women with BAC were 51% more likely to develop heart disease or experience a stroke than those without it.

“The reasons for BAC resulting in higher risks for cardiovascular disease are not definitely determined; however, there are some potential mechanisms, one being that they share similar risk factors that result in vascular injury or metabolic stress that result in vascular calcification and atherosclerosis,” Mehta told Healthline.

Cheng-Han Chen, MD, interventional cardiologist and medical director of the Structural Heart Program at MemorialCare Saddleback Medical Center, added that because BAC and heart disease share many of the same risk factors, “calcium in breast arteries therefore suggests disease in the heart (coronary) and brain arteries as well,” he told Healthline.

While 1 in 39 females in the United States dies from breast cancer each year, cardiovascular disease causes one out of every three deaths, resulting in 1 death per minute.

“Cardiovascular disease is still the most common cause of death in premenopausal women,” Richard Reitherman, MD, PhD, a radiologist and medical director of breast imaging at MemorialCare Breast Center at Orange Coast Medical Center, told Healthline.

“The presentation of cardiovascular disease in premenopausal women is notoriously different than in postmenopausal women, primarily [because] there are no typical symptoms such as chest pain.”

Reitherman said that a prevalent and common health screening procedure such as a mammogram can lead to the early identification of women with silent cardiovascular disease.

“Cardiovascular outcome studies that have included breast arterial calcifications as a risk factor are increasingly reported in the medical literature,” he said.

“The hope is that identifying younger women with silent cardiovascular risks [like BAC along with] appropriate integrated risk identification, analysis, and interventions can significantly reduce mortality in this age group.”

Mehta said there are several reasons BAC has been overlooked as a risk factor for heart disease.

“Mammograms are completed for assessment of breast cancer, and BAC has been historically an ‘incidental’ finding, which is not clinically relevant from an oncologic standpoint,” she said.

Additionally, there is no standardization on quantification of BAC severity, nor is it a required component of mammogram reports, said Mehta.

However, routine reporting of BAC on mammograms is an urgent goal of the American College of Radiology, Society of Breast Imaging, and Medicare, said Reitherman.

In fact, it is gaining traction in the United States. A new Maryland law, passed on October 1, requires mammography centers to notify women when a BAC is detected on their mammogram.

“Automated identification and reporting of these calcifications detected on screening mammograms will become increasingly prevalent in the coming months [and] years,” said Reitherman.

The next step is to identify this group of women to primary care physicians so that appropriate risk evaluation can take place, Reitherman added.

In addition to doctors routinely screening for conditions related to heart disease, such as diabetes, high blood pressure, and high cholesterol, he hopes BAC will also be routinely considered.

“Under current development are protocols to identify patients needing a cardiologist’s training and expertise to appropriately stratify this group of patients,” said Reitherman.

“The common healthcare behavior [of] having regular mammograms not only reduces risk of morbidity and mortality from breast cancer but will hopefully contribute to the reduction of morbidity and mortality from cardiovascular disease in younger women.”

He said this type of integrated healthcare approach demonstrates cross-specialty integration and coordination among women, radiologists, and their healthcare providers to improve women’s lives.

“This is an amazing game-changing opportunity,” Reitherman said.

Mehta said it’s reasonable for women to ask if BAC is present on their mammogram report.

“But if it is not uniformly reported by the radiologist, the individual clinician may not have the answer,” she said.

More research is needed before BAC on mammograms can be used routinely to predict heart disease.

Finding out your BAC could provide additional information to help you and your doctors decide how aggressively to address your cardiovascular risk factors, Chen said.

Reitherman agreed. “The goal of reducing morbidity and mortality from any cause such as cardiovascular disease is based on recognition of risk factors, followed by appropriate lifestyle and pharmacologic interventions,” he said.

A client, whom I’ll call Jon, came to me for treatment for anxiety symptoms. Jon was a highly paid manager at a healthcare company. He reported feeling sick to his stomach on Sundays as he dreaded the deluge of demands on Monday. Jon had lost his appetite for pleasurable foods, as he complained of indigestion and chronic gut discomfort. “I don’t enjoy the weekends anymore. It’s not enough time to relax and feel refreshed. I’m constantly stressed out. My wife is sick of me snapping at her and the kids,” Jon said.

A recent survey by the American Psychological Association found that 75% of Americans report feeling physical and/or emotional symptoms related to stress. Another study reported in Forbes found that 66% of Americans feel burned out, with younger workers feeling it the most.

The reasons for all this stress and burnout are varied but can be summarized here:

It's hard to feel like you can't control the major factors that impact your quality of life. Feeling like you have no control can leave you feeling defeated, unmotivated, and down.

We can’t control broader labor and economic trends, but we can improve how we respond to them. Science-based strategies can help reduce stress and prevent burnout. Here are three you can try now:

Take a morning walk to get the positive effects of sunlight, which helps regulate your sleep/wake cycle. You want a morning cortisol boost to start your day. This helps lower stress by increasing focus early in the day and improving sleep at night. Plus, you reap the numerous health benefits of daily exercise (Huberman, 2026).

Refrain from starting and ending your day on the cellphone

Those who grab the cellphone in bed in the morning and evening report disrupted sleep and mood, contributing to burnout. Instead of cortisol peaking in the morning and lowering at night, cellphone use flips the script, leaving you feeling more anxious, sleep-deprived, and burnt out (Kheirinejad, et al, 2023).

Eat five to nine servings (a half-cup) of fruits and vegetables daily. In a study of healthcare workers, like Jon, those who ate a nutrient-rich diet had lower burnout scores. Only 8% of 5,000 healthcare workers surveyed reported getting the minimum daily requirement of five servings of fruits and vegetables (Utter, et al. 2023). Most nutritionists recommend the Mediterranean diet. That diet is high in vegetables, legumes, and olive oil, and low in sugar and animal fat, like butter. Another study found adherence to the Mediterranean diet reduced the risk of burnout (Amiri et al., 2025).

Jon was so eager to feel better that he tried all three strategies immediately. He got up a few minutes earlier to take an early morning walk before work. He talked to his wife about cutting out sugary sodas and sweets, and they agreed to move toward the Mediterranean diet. The hardest one for Jon was the cellphone. He had a habit of checking his phone and work emails immediately each morning, before he even got out of bed. I explained how this habit disrupted his cortisol regulation and contributed to his sleep issues. Because he couldn’t resist grabbing his phone first thing in the morning, I suggested he keep it out of the bedroom and check it only after his morning walk.

After three weeks implementing these new habits on most days of the week, Jon reported a meaningful improvement in mood. “My wife says I’m much nicer to be around. My gut isn’t waking me up at night anymore. I notice that I’m able to focus better at work too.”

If you’re feeling burned out, or like you’re headed in that direction, it’s always good to get a physical exam with bloodwork first, to determine whether there is a physical cause for the low mood and other symptoms. Sometimes a vitamin deficiency, a thyroid problem, or another medical condition can produce the same symptoms as burnout. If your overall health is good, try the three strategies above. Small changes over time can reap big benefits.

New research suggests that caffeinated energy drinks and stimulant supplements are associated with significantly higher risks of serious heart problems, including cardiac arrest and heart attack in young adults.

The findings, to be presented at the American Heart Association (AHA) Scientific Sessions 2026 November 6–9 in Chicago, are preliminary and can’t prove that energy drinks cause heart problems.

Still, the results highlight concerning cardiovascular effects of these popular products, particularly among adults under 35 who consume them regularly.

“These products are marketed as everyday pick-me-ups and sold next to soda and bottled water, so most people assume they carry no real risk,” study author Sai Samayamanthula, a fourth-year medical student at the University of Virginia School of Medicine in Charlottesville, VA, said in a news release.

“For many healthy people, if these products are consumed occasionally, the risk is probably small. However, individuals who consume energy drinks or other stimulant-containing supplements regularly can quickly exceed the recommended daily limit for caffeine, so it’s important for people to know that there may be a risk of cardiovascular harm with these products,” Samayamanthula continued.

Researchers from the University of Virginia School of Medicine analyzed 28,345 reports submitted to the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) between 2017 and 2025.

Of these, 854 involved caffeinated energy drinks or stimulant supplements, while the remaining reports involved non-stimulant products.

Compared with non-stimulant products, cardiac arrest was reported approximately 11 times more often with energy drinks and stimulant supplements. Heart attacks were reported 4.5 times more often, while ventricular arrhythmias and palpitations were reported about four times more frequently.

Nearly 45% of heart-related reports involved adults ages 18 to 34.

“Our findings support the case for more systematic safety monitoring of caffeine- and stimulant-containing products. In addition, healthcare professionals should remember to ask their younger patients who have palpitations, chest pain or fainting spells if they recently or regularly consume these products,” Samayamanthula said.

Gregory M. Marcus, MD, professor of medicine at the University of California, San Francisco School of Medicine, and chair of the 2026 AHA scientific statement on caffeine and cardiovascular disease, wasn’t involved in the study, but said the research “raises concerns that products with very high amounts of caffeine may be harmful to the cardiovascular system.”

“Although there are biological reasons why that connection could be possible, the study does not prove that caffeine products cause these events, because other factors may have influenced the results found in this analysis,” Marcus said in a news release.

“More research is needed to understand whether these stimulant products are truly responsible for the reported heart-related events.”

Cheng-Han Chen, MD, board certified interventional cardiologist and medical director of the Structural Heart Program at MemorialCare Saddleback Medical Center in Laguna Hills, CA, said the findings, although concerning, are not surprising. Chen wasn’t involved in the study.

“We know that energy drinks can have significant effects on the cardiovascular system, primarily because of their stimulant properties,” he told Healthline.

“Caffeine can increase blood pressure and heart rate, while other ingredients, such as guarana and taurine, may further contribute to cardiovascular stimulation and increased stress hormone levels. What is particularly concerning is the potential for these products to trigger serious complications, including abnormal heart rhythms, especially in people with underlying cardiovascular conditions.”

Chen agreed that occasional energy drink consumption is unlikely to have long-term consequences for heart health in most healthy individuals. However, he said that “frequent or excessive consumption can potentially increase the risk of hypertension and cardiac arrhythmias.”

An AHA scientific statement published in July 2026 notes that up to 400 milligrams of caffeine daily is generally considered safe for most healthy adults. That’s roughly equivalent to four or five 8-ounce cups of brewed coffee, depending on how it’s prepared.

However, some energy drinks contain up to three times as much caffeine per fluid ounce as brewed coffee.

Certain energy drinks may also contain stimulants, such as guarana, yerba mate, theobromine, synephrine, and yohimbine.

These ingredients may compound caffeine’s effects on the cardiovascular system.

“Caffeine stimulates the cardiovascular system, primarily by increasing sympathetic nervous system activity. This can cause the heart to beat faster, raise blood pressure, and increase the workload on the heart,” Chen said.

“Energy drinks may contain additional stimulants, such as guarana and taurine, which can further increase blood pressure and stress hormone levels. With excessive consumption, these effects can become more pronounced and potentially interfere with the heart’s normal electrical activity, increasing the risk of abnormal heart rhythms. People with underlying cardiovascular conditions, particularly hypertension or a predisposition to arrhythmias, may be especially vulnerable to these effects.”

Other research has echoed these concerns. A 2025 review suggests that high-volume energy drink consumption can prolong the heart’s QTc interval, increase blood pressure, and raise heart rate beyond the effects of caffeine alone.

“There is growing concern about the cardiovascular effects of energy drinks and stimulant-containing supplements, particularly among younger adults,” Chen said.

“One challenge is that younger individuals may not recognize that these products can have significant effects on their heart and blood pressure, especially when consumed frequently or in combination with other caffeinated products.”

Young adults accounted for a substantial portion of the FDA reports included in the study, highlighting potential cardiovascular concerns for this age group.

People with existing heart conditions, a history of irregular heart rhythms, or heightened caffeine sensitivity may face additional risks. Those with undiagnosed genetic heart conditions may also be vulnerable.

A 2024 study identified cases of sudden cardiac arrest occurring shortly after energy drink consumption, particularly among people with underlying inherited heart conditions.

“As a cardiologist, I am particularly concerned about people who may have underlying cardiovascular conditions that have not yet been diagnosed,” Chen said.

“Excessive stimulant intake can potentially trigger symptoms such as palpitations, chest discomfort, and lightheadedness. In susceptible individuals, it may also increase the risk of more serious complications, including abnormal heart rhythms.”

Additionally, combining energy drinks with strenuous exercise or alcohol may introduce additional cardiovascular risks.

The findings suggest an association between energy drinks, stimulant supplements, and reported cardiovascular events, but they are preliminary and do not establish cause and effect.

The FDA database also relied on voluntary adverse-event reports, meaning some events may never be reported, while others may receive disproportionate attention.

Researchers also do not yet know how many people consumed these products without experiencing any problems. Without this variable, the reports cannot establish actual risk.

Other factors, including underlying health conditions, medications, and lifestyle habits, may also influence the results. More systematic research and controlled clinical trials are needed.

For most healthy adults, consuming up to 400 milligrams of caffeine per day is generally considered safe.

However, Chen noted that individual tolerance for caffeine may vary, and people with underlying cardiovascular conditions, particularly high blood pressure or abnormal heart rhythms, may need to limit or avoid caffeine altogether.

“Energy drinks can vary considerably in their caffeine content and serving sizes,” Chen said.

“Depending on the product, a single can may contain anywhere from 80 to 300 milligrams of caffeine. This means that consuming even one or two energy drinks can put someone close to or above the recommended daily limit, particularly when combined with coffee, tea, or other caffeinated supplements.”

Seek emergency medical attention if you experience chest pain, particularly if it’s accompanied by shortness of breath, fainting, or other symptoms of a possible heart attack.

A simple swap for salt could spare millions of cardiovascular disease-related deaths, new research suggests.

In a study published in Nature Health on November 4, researchers at The George Institute for Global Health modeled the potential impact of 193 countries switching from regular table salt to potassium-enriched salt.

They found the switch could prevent nearly 3 million deaths and more than 10 million new cases of heart and kidney disease worldwide each year.

In the United States, this simple salt swap could prevent nearly 105,000 deaths and about 492,000 new cases a year, the researchers estimated.

About 130.6 million U.S. adults have cardiovascular disease, according to the American Heart Association (AHA).

Liping Huang, PhD, a senior data analyst in the Food Policy Division at The George Institute for Global Health and co-lead author of the study, told Healthline the team started with current blood pressure levels by sex, age, and country.

Posted November 3, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

A friend tells you she's lost 20 pounds. A coworker shows up looking noticeably smaller. Your sister mentions that she's started taking a GLP-1 medication for weight loss. You recognize that what they do with their bodies is their decision, but before long, you're thinking about your own appearance. You start wondering whether you've gained weight, studying yourself in the mirror, or questioning what you ate that day. Nothing about your body has changed since yesterday, so why are you suddenly feeling dissatisfied with it?

Weight loss is so widely celebrated that congratulating someone for becoming thinner has become a normal and socially acceptable response. Yet weight loss can happen for many reasons, including illness, depression, stress, inadequate nourishment, or an eating disorder. Even when someone has intentionally lost weight and feels good about it, praising the change reinforces the idea that a smaller body is a better body.

We rarely consider what these compliments might mean to someone who later regains the weight. After hearing how wonderful they looked when they were thinner, they may worry that others now find them less attractive or assume they've let themselves go. Nobody has to make a negative comment about their weight gain for them to feel ashamed. The earlier praise has already communicated which version of their body was considered more desirable.

These messages also reinforce the stigma people with larger bodies face, whose appearance is often used to make assumptions about their health, behaviors, and self-discipline. We have become so accustomed to associating thinness with health and attractiveness, and have been told that everyone can and should control their weight and shape. These beliefs end up pathologizing the natural diversity of human bodies.

I think there's more to these reactions than jealousy, particularly for people who have struggled with eating disorders or chronic dieting. Seeing someone else lose weight can bring back memories of when changing your body felt like the solution to so many things you were unhappy about.

Think about all the things you may have missed out on because you felt bad about your body. Perhaps you avoided swimming with friends because you didn't want to be seen in a bathing suit, made excuses not to go to parties because you felt uncomfortable with how you looked, or refused to buy clothes you liked until you went down two sizes. You kept promising yourself that you'd do these things once you were thinner. Looking back, there's sadness in recognizing how many experiences you missed while waiting to feel comfortable in your body.

For someone recovering from an eating disorder, watching another person receive praise for losing weight can bring back memories of the approval they once received. You may remember feeling accomplished when the number on the scale went down, even though maintaining that weight required behaviors that were harming you. It can be upsetting to miss those feelings, especially when you remember how miserable you were at the time.

Eating disorder recovery involves questioning beliefs about food, weight, and self-worth that are deeply embedded in our culture. Sometimes those old thoughts resurface, particularly when the people around you celebrate the very thing you've worked so hard to stop pursuing.

Talk about weight loss has always been difficult to avoid, but the widespread use of GLP-1 medications has brought even more of these conversations into everyday life. People share stories about medications, compare how much weight they've lost, and discuss changes in their appetites. Even a simple dinner can turn into a conversation about portion sizes or how little someone is eating.

In my work as a therapist specializing in eating disorders and body image distress, I hear how frustrating this has become for people who have spent years trying to improve their relationship with food. After working to become less preoccupied with eating and weight, they're surrounded by people discussing medications and the pursuit of smaller bodies. Some describe feeling pulled back into a way of thinking they've worked hard to leave behind.

GLP-1 medications have also changed how people think about losing weight. With weight loss increasingly presented as something that can be achieved through medication, those who have stopped pursuing it may begin questioning their decision. There's a growing sense that if changing your body is possible, you should at least consider it. For someone who has worked to accept their body, that pressure can be difficult to resist.

If hearing about someone else's weight loss makes you want to start dieting again, think about what your previous attempts to lose weight were actually like. Maybe you lost weight and felt good about yourself for a while, only to regain the weight, sometimes more than you initially lost. Each time, you blamed yourself for not having enough willpower or discipline and promised you'd do better with the next diet.

Weight regain is common, in part because our bodies respond to weight loss by increasing hunger and conserving energy. Yet when a diet fails to produce lasting results, people tend to blame themselves rather than question the diet. After years of losing and regaining weight, that repeated sense of failure can seriously affect how someone feels about themselves.

It's worth remembering what those years of dieting actually involved. Perhaps you constantly thought about food, felt guilty after eating, or judged yourself based on what the scale said that morning. If you've decided to stop dieting or are recovering from an eating disorder, you likely had good reasons for wanting something different for yourself. Another person's weight loss doesn't change those experiences.

It's understandable to feel uncomfortable when a friend talks about losing weight, especially if you find yourself comparing your appearance to hers. You might even feel envious of the attention she's receiving and then guilty for having those thoughts. Given how much importance our culture places on thinness, these reactions aren't surprising.

Sometimes the way to cope with those feelings and protect your eating disorder recovery is to first pay attention to what happens afterward. Are you checking your body more frequently, reconsidering foods you've been comfortable eating, or thinking about restarting a diet? Recognizing these changes early can help you avoid slipping back into behaviors you've worked to move away from.

It's also reasonable to limit conversations about dieting and weight loss. You can change the subject or tell someone, "I'm trying to spend less time thinking and talking about weight, so I'd rather hear about what else is going on in your life." You don't owe anyone a detailed explanation of your relationship with food or your body.

We can't prevent other people from pursuing weight loss or control how much attention their changing bodies receive. But we can question why someone else's weight loss has become a reason to criticize our own appearance. You don't have to return to dieting, change how you eat, or become smaller simply because someone else has. Their body is theirs, and yours doesn't need to change in response.

Afterwords – Marvel’s Wolverine

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

We interviewed game director Mike Daly after the launch of Insomniac’s recent Wolverine game to get his take on what makes Wolverine and Spidey different to work on, his thoughts on breadcrumb trails, and why Logan can’t ever seem to get the girl.

Insomniac had extensive experience working on well-known superhero characters before starting on Wolverine. Early on, what traits or qualities of Logan as a story character and playable avatar did you know you wanted to focus on, distinct from the Spider-Man games and character?

From a story perspective, we wanted Logan to be a man with centuries of experiences that he can’t remember, creating a mix of being worn, wizened, and cynical. His resilience makes him fearless, which comes through in his candid manner, and he can’t ignore his sense of justice, as much as he would like to.

For gameplay, it was important that players felt the difference made by his healing factor, claws, berserker rage, animalistic strength, enhanced senses, his adamantium skeleton, and corresponding weight.

In both story and gameplay, emotionally and physically, Wolverine is a hero who gets hurt and feels that pain deeply but always gets back up again and presses on. This dogged resilience was really important to us and a central theme of story and gameplay.

Wolverine is more of a linear adventure than the open-world exploration of a big city seen in Spidey. In my playthrough, that added to the sense of relentless pacing that characterizes Wolverine as a fictional lead. Was that part of the thinking behind why the game was built that way, or was the more linear path there for other reasons?

Ultimately, we made a game suited for a Wolverine story that had comic book pacing – action and meaningful story development on every page.

We experimented with different structures and stories, evaluating from the perspective of: “Is this the best way to do Wolverine justice?” As the various things we tried didn’t feel right, we had to challenge our assumptions and look to our favorite Wolverine stories for inspiration, which led us to where we ended up – a story with momentum in which every beat felt authentic to the character.

Scenes and gameplay sequences that might have been fine in other games but didn’t strongly evoke Wolverine undermined the consistency of the fantasy we were trying to create, so we tried to keep things very focused.

After the game’s release, there was an active online conversation about some of the breadcrumb-trail guidance the game offers to lead you to pickups, or to mark the continued golden path of progression. What was the thinking internally about that design decision, and your take on the debate after the game launched?

Our thinking was that it was essential to make the player feel like they were empowered by Logan’s enhanced senses, a canonical mutant ability, enabling players to do things they could not with other characters. We wanted to weave that power into as many parts of the game as we could, so that if Logan could smell or hear something relevant, the player could see it too.

For collectibles, we wanted players to enjoy seeking out and spotting senses cues, enabling you to find things that would have been obscure to other characters. Using the cues, players can cover a lot of ground without feeling the need to scour, maintaining story and action momentum.

After the game launched, we saw feedback from players that interpreted senses as abstract designer guidance, along with a desire for options in how they approached exploration and discovery. Fortunately, we have been able to incorporate this feedback from players in our post-launch patches. One aspect we’ve improved is the scent trail’s organic presentation, so they give a stronger impression that they belong in the world rather than being an abstraction. For players that want to do more manual exploration, we’ve added options to remove the sense cues for optional collectibles, and one to make scent trails dimmer until the player requests a hint.

Ultimately, enhanced senses are still essential to creating an authentic Wolverine, and that’s still reflected in the cinematic and environmental storytelling of the game. I think it’s great that on top of that, players now have more flexibility in how they experience them.

As a longtime comics fan, I found Logan’s combat system especially exciting, particularly for the relentless forward momentum it provided. What do you think were some of the keys to nailing that dynamic, as distinct from other high-quality third-person action games?

There were a few key elements we wanted to get right - Logan’s resilience, rage, claw feel, and overall lethality.

For resilience, Logan’s healing factor needed to feel important for survival - a prominent element of the system. A natural starting point there was to try continual regeneration so you could outlast enemies, but that quickly fell apart as it incentivized the player to slow the pace of combat or even retreat for maximum advantage. Even though it was incredibly effective at enabling survival, it was also hard to notice by the player so it didn’t feel prominent.

The key to solving this problem was making a connection between rage and healing factor. The Last Stand mechanic is a healing surge that can happen when you lose all your health, themed as using adrenaline to keep Logan’s heart beating. This incentivized aggression and made a space for an up-close look at visceral healing.

Speaking of rage, it’s the backbone of the combat system where being an indicator of momentum is key. At the low end of rage, you don’t have the safety net of Last Stand, so your decision-making naturally biases towards survival; ensuring you prioritize threat assessment and defense. As you climb into the middle tier, you have the leeway to push your luck for longer combos or barrages that let you build up rage and get kills faster. Finally, you get to rage tier 3, where the quick decay of rage makes your rate of kills the top priority, so you might prefer to attack groups over ranged stragglers just for the improved kill density. Beyond the mechanics, we change the presentation of combat, especially through Logan’s animations and exertion to reflect his state of mind. The presentation and systemic incentives drive the player into a frenzy, just like Wolverine.

We invested a lot in the details of claw feel to make sure it was distinct from general game attacks. Claws shred, so we added locational body damage to our enemies to reflect that, along with a dynamic blood system where it globs together as it is spilled, splatters on contact with characters or the environment, drips down the walls, diffuses in the water, and other details. Many kills throughout combat are punctuated with custom animations with cinematic camera work that pushes in and frames the puncturing of claws into the body for a strong impression of the connection and weight of the puncture. Severing limbs on these attacks is an occasional reminder of how sharp Logan’s adamantium claws are. Reinforcing this is top-notch sound and haptics design, so each hit registers.

The last aspect I’ll touch on is lethality, the rate at which Wolverine is able to dispatch enemies and vice versa. We found that fights where enemies were more technical and could survive a lot undermined what players expected to experience as Wolverine, which was aggressively and lethally tearing through a lot of enemies. On the other hand, combat was less exciting if the enemies didn’t feel like a serious and deadly threat, and you didn’t feel like Wolverine if a little bit of caution could enable you to defend perfectly and avoid all damage.

These aspects led us towards encounters where you confronted large groups of enemies that were highly aggressive, to the point of feeling unfair. Enemies can’t believably go toe-to-toe with Wolverine alone, even world-class mercenaries, so their strategy is in group coordination, which creates a counterweight to Wolverine’s own rage-based momentum.

Marvel’s Wolverine draws in a number of characters from across the X-Men comic mythology, but notably makes no mention of some foundational figures of that fiction – Professor Xavier and Magneto. That, in turn, leads to dramatically different lives for characters like Jean Grey or Logan. Is the narrative you crafted an intentional exploration of a world where those figures weren’t present in the early days of mutant emergence?

I would say it was more of an exploration of the character than the world. We wanted a story where Logan is pulled into conflict not because he’s a crusader, but because there’s no other option. There are no X-Men to help. His growth as a person should be a challenging journey, which could go unearned if he was surrounded by wise, supportive, heroic characters pushing him in the right direction. In contrast, his dynamic with the members of Team X evokes his struggle. The adversity to personal growth is what makes it interesting.

Jean does end up being the person that pushes him in the right direction, and that works because they can relate to each other, having had similar struggles.

In several versions of Marvel storytelling, Jean and Logan share a special bond, but Jean ultimately chooses Scott Summers as a partner. But even without that barrier in your story, Logan still hits some roadblocks to love. Is Wolverine fundamentally a better character when he just can’t get the girl?

The way I would put it is that a Wolverine story is fundamentally better when you empathize with Logan’s pain and admire him for pressing on despite it. That goes for his emotional pain as much as physical. He’s a tragic character in this regard – all he wants is to be at peace but can never find it for long.

Wolverine gets to take on a lot of the “heavy hitters” of his comic mythology in different boss fights throughout the game. When you began development, do you recall who the very first character was that you decided he simply had to fight in your game?

Sabretooth. No character is a better foil for Logan. His inner turmoil and chaotic behavior helps illustrate Logan’s own inner beast and his struggle to resist it. Sabretooth’s strength and mutant powers also provide a mirror to Logan in combat, and his savagery creates shocking moments that leave an impression. We knew an epic confrontation with Sabretooth was going to be a thing from the start.

Early onset type 2 diabetes is when the condition is diagnosed before age 45.

Those with early onset type 2 diabetes may be at a higher risk of complications like long-term damage to the capillaries (microvascular complications), adverse cardiovascular outcomes, and earlier death.

Typical treatment for early onset type 2 diabetes is often a combination of lifestyle changes, like diet and exercise, and medications.

However, this new study suggests that a supervised low calorie diet and exercise program may help some people with early onset type 2 diabetes enter remission.

The RESET for Remission trial included 96 adults from England and Canada who were between the ages of 18 and 45, had early onset type 2 diabetes, obesity, and were not on insulin treatment.

The participants had an average age of 38 and an average body mass index (BMI) of 35.2 kg/m2. They were randomized into a control group that received usual care alone and the intervention group, which ate a low calorie diet and participated in supervised exercise programs.

During the first 12 weeks, participants in the intervention group followed an 800–900-calorie-per-day diet and completed twice-weekly supervised aerobic and resistance exercise sessions, along with one independent exercise session per week.

For weeks 13–24, they transitioned to a weight-maintenance diet and independent exercise.

Both groups were supervised by physicians, and the intervention group was also closely supervised by licensed dietitians.

The low calorie diet consisted of 30% protein, 50% carbohydrate, and 20% fat. Participants were also encouraged to drink at least 2 liters of calorie-free fluids per day and given access to a fiber-based laxative as needed.

In the first two weeks, all meals were replaced with specially formulated nutritional products (Optifast). Then, in weeks 3–12, the participants maintained the same calorie intake but had more flexibility in their food choices.

This second phase of the diet included one conventional meal per day, with daily intake targets that included protein, fruit, and vegetables. Typically, this meant 60 to 90 grams of protein from all sources combined, one portion of fruit, and two portions of non-starchy vegetables.

Cutler also added that this study provides a clear example of how difficult it can be to enter diabetes remission with diet and exercise alone. He stated that the small number of participants in the study also makes him wonder if these findings can be applied to all populations.

Another limitation of this study, as Cutler pointed out, was that the strict dietary limits imposed on study participants would be very difficult for the average adult to adhere to.

The study also followed participants for only six months, so it’s unclear how many would remain in remission over the longer term.

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

GLP-1 drugs are more effective in helping prevent type 2 diabetes than the commonly prescribed medication metformin, according to a new study.

Researchers said GLP-1s such as Ozempic and Mounjaro were more likely to reduce blood sugar to healthy levels in people with prediabetes and obesity.

They added that people prescribed GLP-1 medications had a lower risk of progressing from prediabetes to type 2 diabetes.

The researchers noted that GLP-1 users also had a lower risk of death from any cause as well as fewer heart attacks, strokes, and heart failure events compared with metformin users.

“The great news with prediabetes is that it’s still possible to reverse the progression of the disease,” said Vinicius de Sousa Barzon Serra, MD, a study author and research fellow in cardiovascular medicine at the Mayo Clinic in Scottsdale, Arizona, in a press release. “This research may add to the case for considering GLP-1 medications earlier for certain patients, rather than waiting until they develop type 2 diabetes.”

Serra noted that metformin still remains an effective treatment for people with prediabetes and type 2 diabetes.

“This is less about replacing one medication with another and more about discussing additional options with specific patients,” he said.

The researchers presented their findings at the American Heart Association Scientific Sessions 2026 in Chicago. The research has not been published yet in a peer-reviewed journal.

David Cutler, MD, a family medicine physician at Providence Saint John’s Health Center in Santa Monica, CA, wasn’t involved in the study, but said the findings were “quite impressive.”

“It elevates the comfort of prescribing GLP-1 drugs as a front-line treatment,” he told Healthline.

Robert Eckel, MD, a former president of the American Heart Association who was likewise not involved in the study, agrees the research provides more options for patients and prescribers.

“GLP-1s provide another option for prevention, perhaps as an add-on therapy. However, costs and access to these medications are important factors,” said Eckel, an endocrinologist at the University of Colorado School of Medicine, in the press release.

For their study, the researchers analyzed the medical records of 256,000 adults in the United States with prediabetes and obesity. About 76% of the subjects were women, and the average age of the participants was 52.

About half of those studied were taking GLP-1 drugs with the active ingredient semaglutide, tirzepatide, liraglutide, or dulaglutide.

The other half had been prescribed metformin, a medication used to help manage blood sugar levels in adults and some children with type 2 diabetes by decreasing glucose production in the liver, reducing glucose absorption from food, and improving the body’s insulin sensitivity.

The researchers reported that the people in the study who were taking GLP-1 drugs had a 1.5 times higher chance of reversing high blood sugar to healthy levels compared to people who were using metformin.

The researchers also noted that GLP-1 medication users had a 31% lower risk of developing type 2 diabetes and a 41% lower risk of death from any cause.

They added that GLP-1 medications were associated with 15% fewer heart attacks, 20% fewer strokes, 25% fewer cases of heart failure, and 8% fewer cases of atrial fibrillation (AFib), a common type of irregular heartbeat.

The researchers cautioned that their study is observational and, therefore, cannot prove a cause-and-effect relationship. They also noted they relied on prescription records, so the frequency and dosage at which patients took their medication was not reflected.

In addition, the GLP-1 drugs were grouped together, so individual differences between medications were not considered.

Eckel said in the news release that the benefits for people taking GLP-1 drugs were probably mainly due to weight loss. Eckel noted that the research results open up a wide range of possibilities.

“This is an important finding because the GLP-1 receptor agonists are commonly used today and this is a population that in the transition from prediabetes to diabetes are at increasing risk for cardiovascular disease,” Eckel said in a taped interview alongside the study findings.

Cutler pointed out that several GLP-1 drugs also target blood sugar levels and insulin production. They could be effective in people with prediabetes who don’t have weight management issues.

Cutler noted that these people, however, might be reluctant to take GLP-1 drugs because they may not be experiencing symptoms of their prediabetes but could develop side effects with the medications.

“They would not be getting any immediate benefits,” Cutler said. He added that metformin, which was first used clinically in 1958, remains an effective alternative. “It’s been proven safe, effective, and it’s very cheap,” Cutler said.

It’s estimated that 40 million people in the United States have diabetes, with more than 90% of those cases being type 2 diabetes, a chronic condition characterized by high blood glucose levels that results from the body’s ineffective response to insulin or insufficient insulin production.

It’s also estimated that 115 million adults in the United States have prediabetes, a condition in which a person has higher-than-normal blood sugar levels but not high enough to be diagnosed as type 2 diabetes.

Around 5–10% of people with prediabetes develop diabetes every year. More broadly, up to 70% of people with prediabetes eventually develop diabetes.

Medication is sometimes prescribed for people with type 2 diabetes and prediabetes. Lifestyle changes can help reverse type 2 diabetes and prediabetes, as well as prevent the progression from prediabetes to type 2 diabetes.

Cutler said the lifestyle changes needed to prevent type 2 diabetes are pretty clear.

“The most important thing for people with prediabetes to know is they should lose weight, reduce their carbs, exercise daily and, if that doesn’t work, then consider medication,” he said.

Leading Medical Groups Team Up to Redefine Female Heart Health

📅 Nov 01, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

A quartet of medical organizations has published a comprehensive set of reports calling for a coordinated plan of action to improve heart healthcare for women throughout their lifetimes.

The reports detail how cardiovascular health issues can be prevented, monitored, and treated from the early years of menstruation to peak fertility age to pregnancy to perimenopause to postmenopause stages.

The report authors call for healthcare professionals to identify heart health risks early in girls and women and to coordinate care across medical specialties.

They stress that females have specific biological factors that increase their risks of cardiovascular disease. Those components include hormonal and emotional factors.

The authors note that heart disease is the number one cause of death for females in the United States, as well as the number one cause of death during pregnancy. Yet, only 44% of females are fully aware of the dangers they face from heart disease.

The organizations issuing the reports are the American Heart Association (AHA), the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP), the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), and the Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine.

The groups presented their findings in a trio of reports published in the AHA’s flagship journal, Circulation on October 26.

“Women experience their healthcare across clinical specialties, but it often is fragmented or disconnected,” said Sadiya Khan, MD, the chair of the collaborative writing group for the reports and a professor of cardiovascular epidemiology at Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine in Chicago, in a news release.

“Specifically, these reports connect the dots for clinicians, for patients, and across health domains so that care is holistic and begins in adolescence, recognizing that women’s heart health is uniquely shaped by biological transitions not experienced by men — periods, pregnancy, perimenopause, and menopause.”

Experts not involved in the reports told Healthline the updates were a long time coming.

Nicole Weinberg, MD, a cardiologist at Pacific Heart Institute’s Women’s Heart Center in Santa Monica, California, said the reports present a positive framework for women’s health.

“Women have unique cardiovascular risk factors related to pregnancy and menopause, and certain traditional risk factors, including diabetes, may increase their risk of heart disease more significantly than they do in men,” noted Nissi Suppogu, MD, a cardiologist and medical director of the Women’s Heart Center at MemorialCare Heart & Vascular Institute at Long Beach Medical Center in California.

“Recognizing these differences is essential to identifying women at risk earlier, improving prevention strategies and ensuring they receive appropriate treatment,” she said.

“I am incredibly excited to see these new reports on women’s heart health,” added Svati Shah, MD, the president-elect of the AHA, as well as the associate dean of Translational Research and the director of Precision Genomics Collaboratory in the Duke School of Medicine in North Carolina.

“They are a call to action for increasing education, research, and coordinated clinical care for women’s heart health,” Shah said.

The organizations spelled out their recommendations in three reports.

The first report is a presidential advisory that lays out a “female-specific life course approach to the prevention and management of cardiovascular disease.”

In it, the authors state that “girls and women uniquely experience fragmented care” across medical fields, including pediatrics, obstetrics, and gynecology.

The authors call for a “unified life course approach that addresses the female-specific interconnectedness of hormonal, psychosocial, and cardiovascular health.”

This report breaks down the life cycle for girls and women into five stages.

“Emerging data suggest that unique biologic transitions or life stages experienced by girls and women are linked with changes in [cardiovascular disease] risk, which may be due in part to physiologic changes in sex hormones,” the authors wrote.

Weinberg said this life-stage breakdown is important, especially for risk reduction.

She said issues with menstruation during the teen years may be a harbinger of heart health problems later in life. The same is true for health issues during pregnancy, especially high blood pressure or high blood sugar levels.

“We need to be aware of these heart disease risks so we’re not caught by surprise 10 to 20 years down the road,” Weinberg said.

Suppogu agreed. “Medical professionals should emphasize that heart health is a lifelong priority and prevention should begin early. Encourage girls and women to establish healthy habits, understand their cardiovascular risk factors, and maintain regular checkups, even when they feel healthy.”

“Women’s heart health has a unique constellation of risk factors and manifestations that are shaped by biological transitions that vary across a woman’s lifetime,” said Shah.

The second report is a science advisory that recommends a comprehensive plan for women’s cardiovascular care.

In it, the authors call for a system that “integrates assessment of hormonal, psychosocial, and cardiovascular health across life stages.”

They propose a “national infrastructure” of centers that would coordinate clinical care, education, training, research, and community engagement around women’s heart health.

“These centers can reduce fragmentation, enhance earlier risk identification, and expand opportunities to increase representation of women in clinical research,” the authors wrote. “In doing so, they offer a bold opportunity for a scalable and equitable model to improve cardiovascular health for all women.”

To illustrate their point, the authors noted that only about 60% of women with hypertensive disorders during pregnancy in the United States have a follow-up visit with a healthcare professional about their condition within the first 6 months after giving birth.

“Given that pregnancy is a cardiovascular stress test and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy are risk-enhancing factors for long-term [cardiovascular disease] risk, this represents a major missed opportunity to address cardiovascular risk,” the authors wrote.

“Pregnancy and the postpartum period are important windows into cardiovascular health throughout the life course,” said William Grobman, MD, the vice-chair of the writing group for these reports and a professor of obstetrics and gynecology at The Warren Alpert Medical School of Brown University in Providence, Rhode Island, in the press release.

“An adverse pregnancy outcome can unmask increased cardiovascular risk, and that information should guide care well beyond pregnancy,” he continued.

The third report is a scientific statement that suggests a “consensus guideline for the female-specific prevention and management of cardiovascular disease.”

In it, the authors state that there is “growing recognition that the optimal prevention and management of cardiovascular disease in girls and women requires attention to sex-specific considerations relevant to the interconnected domains of hormonal, psychosocial, and cardiovascular health across the life course.”

The authors note several age-related warning signs of heart issues. These are spelled out in the AHA press release.

Females comprise around half the population, and their cardiovascular health “must be a part of everyday care across medicine,” said Mariell Jessup, MD, the chief science and medical officer of the American Heart Association, in the press release. “These publications give clinicians and health systems a shared foundation for recognizing risk throughout a woman’s life.”

In a scientific statement published in February 2026, the AHA projected that 60% of females in the United States will have at least one type of cardiovascular disease by 2050.

The organization also predicted that 25% of U.S. women will have diabetes and 60% will have obesity by that year if current trends continue.

Coffee consumption among U.S. adults remained relatively stable between 2003 and 2018, but the average number of calories consumed from coffee beverages increased by nearly 40%, according to a study published in the Journal of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics.

Letter From the Editor-in-Chief: What a Decade of Bedtime Stories Taught Me About Health

📅 Oct 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

I’ve read to my children each night at bedtime for as long as I (and they) can remember. We started with the likes of “Goodnight Batcave” and the lesser-known-in-the-US but oh-so-wonderful collection of verse-driven classics from former UK Children’s Laureate Julia Donaldson.

We moved on to B.J. Novak’s anarchic love letter to the power of words (and to great typography), “The Book With No Pictures.”

We eventually worked our way up to Roald Dahl’s wickedly dark back catalog, discovering more complex characters, language and plotting as we went.

When my eldest, Amelia, hit 6, it was time to roll out the big guns.

Over the last 5 years (she’s now 11), I’ve read the seven Harry Potter books aloud twice, complete with all the voices: once to Amelia on her own, once to both her and her brother, Henry, two years her junior.

I had feared she wouldn’t be keen on another helping, but Amelia immersed herself once more in the world of Hogwarts with no hesitation and took immense joy in watching Henry discover for himself every twist and turn on Harry’s journey.

We have done the same thing with Tolkien’s “The Hobbit” and his fantasy masterpiece “The Lord of the Rings,” and are currently midway through “The Two Towers” on our second go around.

There’s a huge amount of reciprocal joy we take from this nightly ritual, beyond the practical benefits of providing a calming demarcation between the end of the day and the wind-down to sleep.

For me, it comes as either a break in my working day or a signal that it’s over, depending on how hectic things are.

For the kids, it’s an all-too-rare chance to get me all to themselves, no deadlines, screens, or other distractions to pull me away from being anything but 100% present.

For all three of us, it’s a literal (and literary!) warm blanket — a chance to escape under the covers to familiar worlds and familiar faces. It is, in short, incredibly good for our well-being.

And yet, in the world of health information — including on this site — thousands, if not millions, of reading hours are dedicated to the benefits of nutrition, sleep, and exercise as the building blocks of good health. Reading itself rarely makes that shortlist.

For me, it unequivocally deserves a place on it. Research seems to support that case. Even if we need more of it to really understand how and why.

A recent editorial in Psychological Medicine co-authored by Barbara Sahakian, Professor of Clinical Neuropsychology at Cambridge University, draws on a broad range of research linking reading for pleasure to better mental health and greater empathy.

The positive impact of reading on young people — just like Amelia and Henry — lso carries evidence.

Among more than 10,000 US adolescents, those who started reading for pleasure early had fewer mental health problems, spent less time on screens and slept more. Although, per an earlier column of mine, I can’t vouch for the sleep side of things when it comes to early bird Henry.

For many, reading can be a form of therapy that improves well-being. Bibliotherapy is a complementary therapeutic tool for emotional release and reduced stress and anxiety, with its roots traced back to ancient civilizations.

There’s even research suggesting a link between a love of reading and a lower risk of cognitive decline.

In a 2003 study of people aged 75 and over, reading was associated with a 35% lower risk of dementia over roughly five years.

A separate 14-year study from 2022 found that people who read at least weekly saw less cognitive decline, whatever their level of education.

A happy (and healthy) by-product of this love of being read to is that both my kids have now started reading to me at bedtime. Henry recently read “Jeffy the Burglar’s Cat” to us, a lesser-known but no less compelling chapter book by Ursula Moray Williams that was a staple of my own childhood. And we’ve enjoyed several books from Amelia, including the wistful tale of friendship and the circle of life, “Charlotte’s Web” by E.B. White – not a dry eye in the house for the final few chapters of that one.

They’ve also gone off and re-read the Harry Potter books and “The Hobbit” to themselves.

The research above links reading to many health benefits: better attention and memory, fewer mental health problems, more sleep, less time on screens, and greater empathy. I haven’t measured any of those impacts empirically in our house. Nor am I naive to the fact that as they get older, they’ll ultimately grow out of me reading to them, with school, extracurricular activities, their friends, and inevitably screens competing for more and more of their time.

Come what may, though, I know that a child who considers missing a reading session as the most cardinal of cardinal sins, and who scrambles for the chance to read aloud to their dad at bedtime is, at that moment, concentrating, empathizing, discovering and growing.

Researchers are clear that studies espousing the benefits of reading show association rather than proof, and it’s evident that more experimental work and deeper research are needed to ensure the complex cause and effect stands on firm scientific ground.

Regardless, it’s a healthy habit Amelia, Henry and I are very happy to keep, and with a bit of luck, I’ll get to pass it on to their kids one day.

You saw a plant you loved in a shop or nursery. You brought it home, and for months it warmed your heart. But then it started to decline. What are you doing wrong? Is it dying? Can you save it?

For answers, we talked with the plant doctor: Christopher Satch, who has a master’s degree in botany from Rutgers University, teaches plant science at the New York Botanic Garden, and is involved with the Manhattan Orchid Society and the American Orchid Society. Oh, and he’s also the plant scientist for The Sill, which has two shops in Manhattan and ships houseplants nationwide.

As the plant doctor, Chris fields calls and email from people seeking advice about ailing houseplants; he also dispenses knowledge during weekly drop-in clinics at The Sill’s Upper West Side shop. The best way to keep a plant happy, he says, is to learn what it wants before you buy it—and only buy plants that suit the conditions you can offer. If you live in a ground-floor apartment with little light, say, don’t get a plant that will only thrive with tons of direct sunshine.

How can you diagnose a dying plant’s problem in time to cure it? “A lot of symptoms overlap with many causes,” says Chris. “You have to piece together the puzzle to diagnose the problem.” Here’s how to cure your plant’s ailment.

Symptoms: “If the leaves are turning yellow, the soil is moist, and you can see fungus growing at the base, you’re overwatering,” says Chris. Customers are sternly warned about how often their new plants will need water.

Solution: “Keep cacti and succulents in the sun, which cooks them dry very fast—that’s what they like,” Chris says. “Give these plants a dry rest, maybe a week, and then water them. Shade plants like ferns want their soil to be moist for a little while. Let them approach dryness—but then hit them with water right away.”

Prevention: No plant likes its roots sitting in water, which is why the pot needs good drainage—either a hole in the bottom or a layer of lava rocks or recycled terracotta shards (they’re porous, so they absorb excess water and slowly release it).

Symptoms: If leaves look droopy and are falling off, it’s a good sign that the plant’s not getting enough water.

Solution: “The goal is to saturate the soil so it’s evenly moist, and then let it dry out before watering again,” says Chris. “Most indoor plants are tropical, and they like warm water, not hot or cold.”

Chris outlines two basic watering techniques. The first: “Pour a little water into the center of the pot, let it sink in, pour a little more, and keep doing that until the soil is saturated.” How much water in all? A good rule of thumb is about a quarter to a third of the pot’s volume.

The second technique: soaking. “Put the potted plant in a sink or bowl and pour water slowly onto the top of the soil. Keep going until about a half inch of water has gone through the pot and collected in the sink or bowl. Then let the plant soak—maybe even a full day. Take it out and let it drip dry before putting it back in its planter or plate.” (This method only works when the pot has a drainage hole in the bottom—it can even be plastic.)

Prevention: “Sometimes people just dump water on the plant and it immediately flows through and comes out the bottom,” says Chris. “Your plant has not been watered—if you stick your finger into the soil you’ll see that the center of the root ball is still bone dry. That’s why soaking is important.”

Tip: That trick about putting ice cubes in the pot so the water is released slowly? Forget it.

Symptoms: You can blast most indoor plants with light and they’ll be fine, because the sun is much weaker when it comes through a window. But plants that prefer low light, like ferns and calathea, will be burned by too much sun—blanched leaves are a sign. On the other hand, plants that are not getting enough light may get spindly and stretched out or start dropping leaves, says Chris.

Solution: Most houseplants prefer medium to bright light. “Think of the fiddle-leaf fig, which people plant as outdoor hedges in Florida. They love the sun, and they’ll drop leaves in response to lower light.”

Prevention: Artificial light may help. If the natural light in your home is really low, artificial light can make plants happier. No bulb offers the full spectrum of light that plants need—not even the ones designed as grow lights—but using a more intense bulb will increase the effect. Chris recommends an LED or CFL (compact fluorescent) bulb (screw-in, not tubes), as long as it’s 1,000 lumens or more; either warm or cool is fine.

Once the nutrients have been exhausted, the plant says ‘Uh-oh, what am I going to do?’

Solution: “You have to be their savior, and add fertilizer to provide nutrients—it’s literally a multivitamin for the plant,” says Chris. Follow the instructions on the label.

Prevention: Use crystals, liquid, or slow-release pellets as recommended.

Solution: A rule of thumb is that the volume of the plant should be two-thirds above ground and one-third below ground. The soil level should be within an inch or two of the rim, depending on the pot size, so when you pour in water it can pool before seeping in.

Prevention: When you buy a plant that comes in a plastic pot, repot it right away. “Plants are sold overgrown; they’re not meant to live in those pots. They need room to grow,” says Chris.

Tip: Don’t let your soil get too old. “Potting mix does decay and get mucky over time,” says Chris. Even if you’re fertilizing regularly, swap out some of the soil every year or two. It’s also a good opportunity to see if your plant needs a larger pot.

Symptoms: Dry air is very bad, causing plants to crisp their leaves, says Chris.

Solution:  Keep plants well away from vents, heaters, radiators, and air conditioners. To combat low humidity, he offers three pieces of advice: “Humidifier, humidifier, humidifier.” Get the largest one you can—it’s great for human health as well. If you only have a dinky one, put it right next to the plants.

Prevention: Misting can help if it’s done regularly, but as soon as the mist dries, the effect is gone. You can also group plants with similar needs to create a microclimate—but note that if you get pests, every plant will be infested.

Symptoms: Mealy bugs look like tiny white cotton balls. Other bugs look like, well, bugs.

Solution: “Using an insecticide like Raid is like using an atom bomb to knock down a house,” says Chris. “And those chemicals can hurt the plant.” He recommends washing the plant with warm water, then wiping it down to remove as many bugs as possible. Follow up with a spray of horticultural oil or insecticidal soap, being careful to reach the crevices and the undersides of the leaves.

Prevention: Keep your plant clean (dust or wipe its leaves gently) and check it regularly for bugs.

Otherwise you can always contact Chris at The Sill, search online, or send your question (with a photo) to your local botanic garden.

“If it’s brown or looks dead, it probably is dead,” says Chris. But some woody plants, like the rubber tree and the fiddle-leaf fig, can recover even if they’ve died back to the stalk. “Blast it with light, give it a little fertilizer, water it when it’s dry, and it’ll say ‘Life is worth living! Let’s regrow!’ You could have the same luck with plants like pothos and monstera, as long as there’s a little vine or stem left.”

On the other hand, if a plant’s too far gone—and definitely not beautifying your home—it can be wise to cut your losses and start again with something fresh.

“Most indoor plants will do absolutely fine on a steady diet of dappled sun and water when they’re dry,” Chris says reassuringly. “Cacti and succulents need more bright light and dry soil; ferns need wetter conditions. And then there’s Sansevieria, which deserves all the respect—whether it’s under artificial light or blasted with direct sun, it’ll just keep growing.”
