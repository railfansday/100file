Kimchi Bolognese

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

I’ve always loved Italian American food. Maybe because Italian and Korean cooking have traits in common: Both are homey and resourceful, and grandmothers are revered for their signature dishes made without formal recipes. This recipe was one of the first Korean Italian fusion recipes I developed, and it makes use of my two favorite secret weapons: kimchi and fish sauce. The result is the Bolognese you know and love, but with a deep, tangy twist. Fish sauce in Italian cooking is not a new idea, actually—Romans have been using salted anchovies and garum, an ancient kind of fish sauce, for centuries. For an extra-special finish, I love garnishing this with baek (white) kimchi for acidity. But it’s optional, and regular kimchi (or no kimchi) works just fine, too.

Start the Bolognese sauce: Heat 2 Tbsp. extra-virgin olive oil in a large Dutch oven or pot over medium-high heat. Stir in 1 medium-yellow onion, finely diced (about 1½ cups), 2 carrots, peeled and finely diced, and 3 stalks celery, finely diced, and cook, stirring occasionally, until the onions have softened and the edges start to brown, about 4 minutes. Stir in 4 cloves garlic, minced, and a pinch of kosher salt and cook for 30 seconds, then stir in 1 cup chopped kimchi and 1 Tbsp. tomato paste and cook until the kimchi pieces start to soften, about 3 minutes. Pour in ½ cup red wine, increase the heat, and simmer, stirring with a wooden spoon to dislodge any bits that have stuck to the bottom, until the wine has nearly disappeared, about 2 minutes.

Add the meat: Stir in 1 lb. ground beef, season with generous pinches of salt (at least ½ tsp.), and cook, breaking up the beef into small pieces with a wooden spoon, until cooked through, 6 to 8 minutes.

Add the tomatoes: Pour one 28-oz. can whole San Marzano tomatoes in a bowl. Using clean hands, squish the tomatoes into small pieces. Pour the tomatoes into the pot and add 1 cup beef broth or water, ½ tsp. dried Italian seasoning or dried oregano, a pinch of salt, and a few grinds of freshly ground black pepper. Bring to a simmer and lower the heat to medium-low. Gently cook, stirring occasionally, until the sauce is just thick enough to stand up on a spoon, about 20 minutes. Stir in 2 Tbsp. fish sauce and taste, then add more salt or fish sauce if needed.

Cook the noodles and serve: In a pot of boiling salted water, cook 1 lb. spaghetti according to package instructions, stirring occasionally to prevent sticking, until al dente. Drain the noodles in a colander, then give the colander a shake to get rid of extra water. Divide the noodles among serving plates and spoon the Bolognese on top. Garnish with 1 cup chopped kimchi (white or regular; if using) and 2 Tbsp. chopped flat-leaf parsley and serve.

Do Ahead: Leftover Bolognese sauce (without the noodles) keeps in an airtight container in the refrigerator for up to 5 days, or in the freezer for up to 2 months.

Make Your Next Breakfast Sandwich Korean-Style For A Major Fiber & Flavor Boost

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Think of Korean cuisine, and a sandwich likely isn't the first thing that comes to mind. However, bread-based dishes are widely enjoyed in the East Asian country, both in cafes and as street food. An especially popular breakfast rendition is gilgeori toast, a comforting breakfast sandwich stuffed with eggs and shredded vegetables.

Easy-to-make, packed with fiber, and delicious, the dish is a great candidate for your breakfast rotation. Traditionally, this sandwich calls for a fluffy loaf of milk or brioche bread, the buttery soft texture turning crisp on the outside once toasted. Meanwhile, the filling comprises a variable shredded vegetable medley: Cabbage traditionally forms the centerpiece, joined by carrots, onions, scallions, and often garlic chives. Mixed with egg and then pan-fried into an aesthetic golden-brown patty, the filling delectably complements the soft bread. Gilgeori is usually rounded out with a sprinkle of sugar and a drizzle of ketchup and mustard.

It's an assembly that easily comes together during bleary morning hours, yet nevertheless offers a nutritious start to the day. One sandwich can pack in around six ounces of shredded cabbage, which amounts to over 4 grams of fiber. Add up the fiber from a tablespoon of carrot, two of sliced onion, and two whole green onions, and you're including another 1.5 grams. Then, add in two slices of thick-cut brioche sandwich bread, and you've got an extra 2. That's about 7.5 grams, which is around a quarter of what you need a day, already knocked out by breakfast. Mixed with the roughly 6.5 grams of protein from the egg alongside filling bread, the sandwich serves as a terrific way to start the day.

The morning isn't always the best time to precisely reconstruct a recipe. Thankfully, gilgeori toast welcomes a wide array of reinterpretations and ingredients. Starting with the bread, feel free to swap the milk bread for sourdough, whole wheat, or even a convenient store-bought whole grain like Ezekiel or Dave's Killer. You'll lose out on some of the dish's airiness, but potentially gain extra fiber content in the process. Two slices of Dave's Killer 21 Whole Grains and Seeds contain some 8 grams of fiber, more than doubling the total of the sandwich.

Up the satiation further by adding on a slice of seared ham, a filling also enjoyed in Korea. And cheese is always welcome, whether American, cheddar, or fontina – Martha Stewart's go-to for breakfast sandwiches. Instead of the sugar sprinkle, lend the dish a sweet note by using jam: Raspberry or cherry jam makes a great fit. Not to mention, you can replace the ketchup and mustard with a condiment of your preference; you can buy a bottle of Gochujang Sriracha Hot Sauce online, for instance. The template of bread and shredded vegetables works with many flavors, perfect for morning enjoyment.

Te Araroa: more muddy boogeymen

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

In order to walk Te Araroa you must accept mud into your heart (and shoes and socks and blisters). You also must never trust roots for they are deceivers and false friends (slippery, so slippery). The dome is no exception to these rules. You will never escape the mud until you have completed the trail.

Dome day dawned and I was NERVOUS. I had been thinking about a zero for about a day and a half before that, but had kept pushing on because I wanted to stay with my group. I also made the biggest mistake of my life, not having a coffee.

The dome began with fields of mud, and our group split up pretty early into the day. It was the kind of terrain that we all prefer to navigate solo, it’s no fun when people are watching you slip and slide up and down a muddy hill, or you feel like you’re holding others up. I was feeling so rough mentally, and my hiker hunger had really started to kick in the night before, so I spent the morning mentally cataloguing all the food I had left and whether it would be enough to get me to the dairy in Puhoi. I was exhausted physically and mentally before the day even really began. But I kept going.

When I told people about walking Te Araroa I talked a lot about wanting to build resilience and the ability to keep going even when I really didn’t want to. Some part of me knew this was the first moment where I could start to learn that. So singing broken clips of songs to myself and sometimes even counting my steps, I trudged on.

I caught up with Sinèad at the end of the first big hill and descent section, and we walked the road section together. I fantasized about going to one of the open houses along the road and asking them for a coffee. I didn’t think they’d let my muddy hiker self in so I kept moving.

When we approached the next big, forested hill section of the day we found a fellow hiker, Xavier, eating half his lunch. I immediately sat down and ate a handful of every one of my accessible snacks. The others took off and I spent a further ten minutes talking myself into continuing. I knew that on the other side of this hill was a campsite where, if I really wanted to, I could stop for the night. I also knew that in less than a kilometre I’d hit the 500km mark.

I felt so sleepy, not just tired but burny-eyes-sleepy. I thought desperately about the single sachet of instant coffee deep in my food bag and sang “just keep swimming” to myself. By the time I was about ¾ through this hill I was once again mentally inventorying my meals, trying to decide if maybe I could break this section into two days.

Then I heard some girls ahead of me and like a puppy seeing its people again after a long work day, I perked up, thinking it was my friends. Unfortunately it was just two very clean-looking, white-sneakered day hikers.

Though they weren’t who I was hoping to see, the whiteness of their sneakers told me that from here, the trail would be more chill. I pushed on and eventually I could hear Sinèad’s accent floating through the trees. And there was our friend, Zoe, getting water from the stream! I crossed the stream and climbed up to the campground where I was delighted to find there was a picnic table so I wouldn’t have to sit on the ground.

I laid out my very wet tent to dry (still thinking about staying there), drank the last of my water, and filtered some into my mayo-turned cold soak jar to shake up my last instant coffee sachet. Sinèad said she watched me essentially reboot as soon as I started drinking the coffee.

It was 1:30 at this point, we’d been walking for six and a half hours, and I didn’t really have a sense of how much further there was to go. I decided to take a long lunch and to eat two wraps, food inventory be damned!

And then my saviours began to arrive. First was another set of day hikers, the older of whom helpfully told me I seemed very tired (I did not hold back when I agreed with him). And then Annabel, who I had been worried about because she normally walks at a similar pace to me and I hadn’t seen her all day.

After some lecturing about my choice to not wear boots, the day hikers gave me a bag of pumpkin seeds, dates and ANZAC biscuits. The snacks were enough to convince me to put my wet, muddy socks back on and walk the final five or six kilometres to the dome cafe.

Annabel and I climbed the third and final hill of the day which was actually suddenly easy terrain, and then descended a really well-graded, touristy track. I honestly felt like I was flying on that terrain and having Annabel around to chat to made it go really quickly. After eleven hours of walking (including the two hour lunch I took) we made it to camp. The dome cafe was an oasis, it had a bathtub and yes I did sit down in it. I pitched my tent and we all took turns having a hot shower and I think that place saved my life. My mashed potatoes have never tasted better. This was a big day of learning for me. Mostly that I can do it even when it’s a tough day.

I hope my work colleagues enjoy seeing my muddy sock water

Anchor me by The Mutton Birds. It should be noted that I did not choose this. It was unceremoniously bestowed upon me by the “here’s a song you didn’t want stuck in your head” gods. But it would be disingenuous to choose anything else because it is what was mostly going through my mind when I wasn’t inventorying my food or dreaming of coffee.

The Tucson Restaurant Turning Cactus Into Fine Dining

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Traipsing through Tucson Mountain Park, a Midwestern blow-in might not identify much to eat. Plants armed with burs, barbs, and spines all but scream “do not touch.” For chef Aaron Lopez and June Chee, however, this prickly, dusty landscape stocks a pantry of possibility. The married co-owners of Ursa see the Arizona desert as a rich ground for foraging.

Gray, tangled brittlebush, which the average Tucsonan walks or drives by almost daily, can be pressure-cooked, caramelized until black, and baked into cake. Dusty, sage-colored salt bush, when hung to dry, takes on an intensely umami, aquatic saltiness like seaweed. The sweet buds of the Seussian-green palo verde tree become briny, like olives, when suffused with lye.

Don’t even get Lopez started on the dozens of cacti varieties that thrive here, which the restaurant sources from local grower Toadlet Farm and which Lopez harvests throughout the year. The restaurant processes the young and mature paddles, fruit and their seeds, buds, and flowers, which are then grilled, poached, cold-smoked, juiced, dehydrated, nixtamalized, or preserved into jam, wine, and vinegar.

There are hundreds of edible plants in the Sonoran Desert. Ursa strives to showcase their potential, one 10-course meal at a time.

“We treat Ursa as an art showroom, demonstrating to people what these plants can do, what sustainable means, and what was here before it got easy,” says Lopez. The idea was inspired in part by his Sonoran grandfather, Hector Lopez, who died in the spring. “[He] was the first person I ever heard say they’re proud to be from the desert,” the chef said.

Ursa began as a pop-up in El Centro, California, in 2023. Lopez and Chee moved to a historic brick building in downtown Tucson, re-opening their doors in October 2025. It’s a homecoming of sorts for the couple, who are both from the Sonoran Desert borderlands. They met in Hawaii, where Chee was working in clean energy and transportation. Lopez, meanwhile, spent roughly 18 years cooking in fine dining and modern progressive restaurants like Redbird and Orsa & Winston in LA, and Pai Honolulu. But he was longing for the desert.

After helping a friend open a restaurant and hosting a few popups in Austin, Lopez and Chee bought a desert foraging guide, packed up their two dogs and began driving around the Southwest. It made sense to settle in Tucson, which was recognized by UNESCO in 2015 as a Creative City of Gastronomy for its continuous 4,000-year agricultural history and deeply rooted Sonoran Desert food traditions.

“That’s a unique thing about being here, the connection from seed to plant to restaurant that we hadn’t been able to grasp before,” Chee says.

Consider a recent course that Ursa built around tepary beans, in which the ancient, nutty bean is refried and topped with h:al squash that’s been lactofermented. The squash’s leaves are fermented to make Japanese-style kosho and its flowers are pickled, dried, crushed with dried saltbush, then tossed with barrel cactus seeds for a desert furikake. It’s served with grilled coins of chorizo made from Lopez’s great-grandmother’s recipe and housemade flour tortillas, to all be washed down with an amazake drink made from fermented wheat and goat fat. Don’t get too attached, however. Riffs owing to finicky ingredient availability occur here almost daily.

Running a restaurant using only ingredients foraged and farmed from within about eight to 10 miles means abiding by pretty intense guardrails. The only wheat used is pima club, an ancient soft white variety cultivated for centuries by the Akimel O’odham (Pima) people. Ursa does not use cucumbers, radishes or citrus.

“We’re not trying to substitute anything, like, ‘How do we achieve cucumber? Let’s grab a cactus,’” Lopez says. “We have to throw everything out, really strip it down. Sometimes we have to let the ingredients be bitter or spicy. We have to put our egos aside and let the ingredient be the star.”

For staff who didn’t grow up or learn to cook here, the learning curve at Ursa can feel a little like alighting on a new planet, even if certain flora happen to resemble prickly weeds in their own backyards.

“We have these really cool moments in the restaurant where people are like, Oh! I have lamb’s quarter just growing in my yard!” Chee says of the spinach-like desert green that appears all over Ursa’s menu.

A young Aaron Lopez with his grandfather Hector Lopez.

Ursa’s approach isn’t always easy for customers to understand, so the couple came up with a more down-to-earth option. This fall, the restaurant debuted a four-course, family-style Lineage menu for $75, alongside the existing 10-course Harvest menu ($150). There’s now a pozole course inspired by the family’s annual springtime tradition of roasting goat in agave leaves, barbacoa-style, and shredding it for soup, and a red masa tamal with glazed quail and yucca blossoms in mole made with burnt mesquite bread, a technique Lopez remembers from childhood.

The idea took shape during Ursa’s extended summer break, as Lopez recovered from foot surgery and reflected on the loss of his late grandfather, the tenacious keeper of family traditions.

“I feel so much closer to my ancestors and the land; we want Ursa to be less esoteric and more soulful,” he says. “The story of Ursa is partly of the desert, partly my family. Now we’ve advanced to the point where they’re symbiotic, and I can't tell them apart.”

Fall Color in Nancy’s Massachusetts Garden

Hi GPOD! Today we’re heading to Hingham, Massachusetts, for some beautiful fall color from Nancy. Nancy sent in photos of the trees and shrubs winding down the season in her garden, along with one of the last flowers still hanging on.

This is our sugar maple (Acer saccharum, Zones 3–8) showing off its fall foliage before it drops its leaves to keep my gardens warm this winter.

My ‘Forest Pansy’ eastern redbud (Cercis canadensis ‘Forest Pansy’, Zones 5–9) is maroon most of the year until fall, when it turns a lovely yellow.

My paperbark maple (Acer griseum, Zones 4–8) has had a hard time coping with our summer droughts and water restrictions, but it looked a lovely red this fall.

This is another native tree that many people are not familiar with, fringe tree (Chionanthus virginicus, Zones 4–9), which has fragrant white flowers hanging from its branches in late spring to early summer. As you can see, it also looks beautiful in its yellow fall foliage.

In the front of this picture is a young sweetgum (Liquidambar styraciflua, Zones 5–9), which has spectacular red color in the fall and develops spiky seed balls as it matures. In the distance is a blackhaw viburnum (Viburnum prunifolium, Zones 3–9) showing its orange fall color.

‘Mount Airy’ fothergilla (Fothergilla ‘Mount Airy’, Zones 5–8) glows red and orange in front of fragrant abelia (Abelia mosanensis, Zones 4–8), whose fall color is beginning to fade. The abelia will charm us all again in spring with its fragrant blooms.

Sweetshrub (Calycanthus floridus, Zones 5–9) on the right has turned a lovely yellow. Amazingly, all three sweetshrubs shown here had the bark eaten off around every trunk by rabbits last winter, killing every one. They all grew back from the roots, showing just how resilient they are. On the left, ‘Mount Airy’ fothergilla is showing off its red fall foliage, and in back is ‘Alice’ oakleaf hydrangea (Hydrangea quercifolia ‘Alice’, Zones 5–9) with maroon fall foliage.

One of the only flowers left blooming is ‘Sheffield Pink’ chrysanthemum (Chrysanthemum ‘Sheffield Pink’, Zones 5–9), which complements the dark foliage of Diabolo ninebark (Physocarpus opulifolius ‘Monlo’, Zones 3–7) behind it.

Thanks for sharing your fall garden with us, Nancy! Nancy says she’s ready to wander the garden, make her list of what needs dividing in spring, and figure out what new plants need to be bought. Sounds like a good way to spend the off-season to me!

Not Ribeye: Bobby Flay Recommends This Steak Cut For The Best Tacos

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

When taco night rolls around, you'll want to head to the butcher and grab some good meat. If you're making carne asada, your instinct might be to pick the ribeye. While it might be the gold standard of steak, tacos and ribeye are an inherent mismatch. Tacos are one of the most democratic foods out there, while ribeye is made for deep pockets and expensive tastes. Even Food Network's Bobby Flay recommends skipping it. Instead, he swaps the pricey cut for the seriously underrated skirt steak.

Ribeye is America's favorite steak thanks to its rich marbling and tender texture, but you'll rarely find it in taquerias. Beyond being pricey, ribeye's assets are its downfall in tacos. In an interview with Food and Wine, Flay revealed the reason why he often chooses to skip ribeye altogether: "[Ribeye] can be inconsistent because the fat doesn't always melt even when cooked properly." This is a real problem in tacos. Encased in a taco, you don't have the option to trim around a rubbery pocket of fat or leave it on the plate. Even if you trim the fat before adding the steak to your tacos, it's so unevenly distributed that you risk ending up with wildly inconsistent slices.

Unlike ribeye, skirt is cut from the plate primal, a hard-working area near the diaphragm. Rather than being streaked with generous ribbons of fat, it's comprised of long, coarse muscle fibers, which give it a chewier, more fibrous bite along with a bold, beef-forward flavor. Its thin profile may not win it fans at white tablecloth restaurants, but it's exactly why taquerias can't get enough of it. All that surface area compared to its thickness means skirt steak cooks quickly, soaks up marinades fast, and slices evenly, which is everything you want from taco meat.

There's no cut of beef more well-suited to carne asada than skirt. Literally translating to "grilled meat," the beef is usually marinated in a citrus and garlic blend, with the acidic ingredients helping to tenderize the surface of the meat. After being left to soak up the flavor for a couple of hours, it's then seared or grilled over an open flame or charcoal to impart a smoky, charred flavor (an air fryer also works if you can't grill). Because skirt is such a thin cut, it doesn't enjoy spending too much time over high heat; it only needs around two to four minutes per side. Skirt can be prone to drying out, so it would be wise to avoid cooking past a medium doneness.

How you cut skirt is just as important as how you cook it. Compared to other cuts, skirt has particularly long, dense muscle fibers, and if you slice them with the grain, you'll leave them intact, resulting in chewy, rubbery bites. Slicing against the grain shortens these fibers, turning them into more satisfying, bite-sized strands. To identify the grain, look closely at the surface of the meat to spot the direction of the muscle fibers running through the beef.

Although skirt is beloved by taquerias, that doesn't make it suited to all beef tacos. One popular style where we would skip skirt is birria, the slow-cooked, tender beef marinated in a chili-and-spice adobo served with a flavorful broth for dipping. The whole concept of birria revolves around slow-braising, requiring tough, collagen-rich cuts like chuck or beef cheek, which break down into tender, shreddable meat over several hours. While skirt does have some connective tissue, it's simply too thin and lean to survive hours of prolonged heat.

This 30-Mile-Long Canyonlands Art Gallery Might Be Utah’s Best Backpacking Trip

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

When you think of ancient rock art, what comes to mind? Handprints? Stick figures? Maybe a long-limbed man holding something? For the most part, you’d be right. The petroglyphs and pictographs of the southwest are beautiful, but they aren’t known for their intricate details.

But there’s one canyon in Utah that blows those expectations out of the water.

Hidden deep inside Canyonlands National Park, Salt Creek Canyon holds some of the most impressive pictographs I’ve ever seen. Enormous human figures, elaborate patterns, and colorful paintings that have survived for hundreds of years. It’s an open-air art museum, and one that’s slowly weathering away.

And the museum doesn’t stop at rock art. There are Ancestral Puebloan granaries, settlers’ abandoned ranch buildings, and reminders of the conservationists who helped protect this landscape.

A walk through Salt Creek Canyon is a walk through history. Over two days and more than 30 miles, it became one of my biggest backpacking surprises of the year.

Trail: Salt Creek Trail + Angel Arch Detour Start: Cathedral Butte / Salt Creek Canyon Trailhead Finish: Cave Spring / Salt Creek Gate Distance: 30.78 miles (my GPS; mapped distance may differ) Elevation gain: 1,054 feet (my GPS) Type: Point-to-point, vehicle shuttle required Permits: Required for overnight trips Highlight: The All American Man pictograph

The trail begins high in the contested Bears Ears National Monument, in the shadow of Cathedral Butte. Typically, this is the sort of view you’d have to earn after hours of climbing. Instead, you start at the highest point, looking down into a canyon that stretches toward the horizon.

It’s mostly downhill from here, but don’t mistake that for easy. Thirty miles with a loaded backpack is still 30 miles, especially when much of it is through deep sand.

About two miles in, we crossed into Canyonlands National Park. My Gaia GPS map was littered with waypoints and “Don’t miss!” markers, the first of which were Kirk Spring and Kirk’s Cabin.

In an otherwise dusty landscape of sagebrush and juniper, a little oasis appeared. Reeds, cottonwoods, and bright green vegetation surrounded a small spring. We filtered some water and continued toward the cabin, an 1890s-era log home with a fieldstone chimney.

Outside the weathered log structure sat the wheels and axles of an old wagon. Inside, bear traps and antlers hung on the walls. It looked like something out of a history museum, only sandier, dustier, and considerably more authentic.

This was our first introduction to one of Salt Creek’s defining qualities: Layers of human history occupy nearly every corner of the canyon.

Long before ranchers arrived, Indigenous people—Ancestral Puebloans and Fremont, then Ute, Paiute, and Diné—lived, hunted, and farmed here. Later came European explorers, conservationists, and eventually backpackers like us. Different people, different centuries, but all drawn to the same remarkable place.

One of the biggest reasons was water. Unlike much of Canyonlands, Salt Creek supports a surprisingly lush corridor of vegetation, even in a dry year. We encountered several places to filter water, although sources became scarcer farther down the canyon. (Water here is seasonal and never guaranteed, so come prepared to carry plenty.)

Around mile six, a small spur trail led us beneath a sandstone overhang. Tucked into the alcove were several stone structures, likely granaries used by Ancestral Puebloan people to store their harvests. Just below them, dark-green vines spilled across the sand. They looked like squash plants, still thriving in the shadow of structures built centuries ago.

I’d come to Salt Creek expecting incredible rock art, but nothing prepared me for what we found around mile eight: The All American Man.

High inside a sandstone alcove stands a human-like figure, nearly life-size, painted in striking blue, white, and red. He appears to wear white boots, an elaborate headdress, and something resembling an American flag across his body.

For decades, the figure’s unusual appearance led some to question whether it was even ancient. Radiocarbon testing eventually confirmed its authenticity, suggesting it dates to around the 14th century. Standing below it, I found the whole thing almost impossible to believe. (Do yourself a favor: If you plan on hiking Salt Creek, don’t look up pictures of the All American Man beforehand. Let it surprise you.)

Not far beyond, we encountered another extraordinary panel near Four Faces Spring. Four enormous, intricately patterned faces stared out from beneath a sandstone alcove. Remnants of stone structures sat beneath them.

Who were these figures? Were they people, spirits, or something else entirely?

Their exact meaning remains uncertain, but the artwork survives, part of a cultural history that remains important to Indigenous communities today.

The farther we walked, the more the canyon changed.

Towering sandstone walls closed in around us. Willows, reeds, and cottonwoods transformed the dusty trail into something resembling a jungle. Small streams appeared beneath the vegetation.

Unfortunately, so did the mosquitoes. I’ve spent a lot of time hiking through the desert, but I’ve never encountered so many of the little suckers.

After miles of hiking, a soft rain finally brought some relief. A rainbow appeared against the red canyon walls just as we approached SC4, our reserved campsite for the evening.

At roughly mile 12, SC4 is the last of the four designated Salt Creek campsites when traveling north. It wasn’t quite halfway through our route, which meant we had an even longer second day ahead.

The next morning, we packed up and headed toward another of the canyon’s great attractions. Several miles off the main trail, at the end of a remote side canyon, stood Angel Arch.

Arches National Park may be famous for its sandstone formations, but Angel Arch can hold its own against just about anything you’ll find there.

Its massive sandstone opening rises roughly 135 feet high, with an unusual formation beside it resembling an angel leaning forward with folded wings. It’s an incredible sight, made even more impressive by the effort required to reach it. We were the only people there. In fact, we were the only hikers on the entire trail: We hadn’t seen a single other person or group. The detour added several miles to an already long day, but I’d do it again without hesitation.

Back on the main route, one more archaeological treasure awaited us: the Flying Carpet Panel. Like the All American Man and Four Faces, the artwork was enormous, with figures and intricate designs spread across the sandstone.

Then came the final miles. The beautiful canyon trail eventually gave way to a sandy four-wheel-drive road, where every step seemed to require twice the effort. After nearly 30 miles, walking through deep sand was just about the last thing my legs wanted to do.

Eventually, we reached a gate, a paved road, and the end of our journey.

As spectacular as the scenery was, what I’ll remember most about Salt Creek isn’t the canyon itself. It’s the people who called it home.

For centuries, Ancestral Puebloan communities farmed along the creek, stored food in cliffside granaries, and created artwork that still watches over the canyon today. Walking past these places, I couldn’t help thinking about how many people had traveled this same canyon before us. How many had looked up at those same cliffs, collected water from those same springs, or sheltered beneath those sandstone overhangs?

We spent two days here, carrying everything we needed on our backs. For others, this canyon was home. For a brief moment, we got to be part of its story too.

Children’s brain responses to food may help predict later fat gain

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Children who ate more ultra-processed foods showed stronger brain responses to food pictures, which were linked to greater body fat gain later.

Breakfast cereal, packaged bread and instant soup get blamed for childhood weight gain because of what’s in them.

A new study links children’s ultra-processed food intake to how their brains respond to pictures of food.

A year later, the children with stronger reactions had gained more body fat. Every child was a healthy weight when the study began, and this kind of study can show a link but not prove a cause.

Dr. Kathleen Page, a physician and professor of medicine and pediatrics at the Keck School of Medicine of USC, is the study’s senior author, and Jasmin Alves is the first author.

“Children’s brains are still developing, habits are still forming and food preferences are still being made. It’s an important period where the brain is very sensitive to food and the food environment,” Dr. Page said.

The 46 children were 7 to 10 years old and part of the BrainChild study.

That long-running project follows children whose mothers did or did not have diabetes during pregnancy, and it recruits them through Kaiser Permanente Southern California.

Each child had a BMI below the 85th percentile for their age and sex.

On two separate days, each child and a parent walked trained staff through everything the child had eaten in the previous 24 hours.

The researchers then sorted every food by how processed it was. On average, ultra-processed foods supplied about 68% of the children’s daily calories, close to the 67% a 2021 analysis found for American kids as a whole.

About a month later, each child lay in an MRI scanner after a 12-hour fast and then a glucose drink.

The scanner recorded how active two deep brain areas were during the food photos.

Both areas are part of a structure called the striatum, which handles motivation, learning and reward.

The ventral striatum is involved in anticipating and experiencing rewards. The dorsal striatum helps link cues with learned actions and habits.

Children who got more of their calories from ultra-processed foods had stronger dorsal striatum responses to the food photos.

The ventral striatum showed no clear pattern. The association with dorsal striatum responses remained after accounting for each child’s age, sex, body size and whether their mother had diabetes during pregnancy.

In an interview with Earth.com, Dr. Page emphasized that the association held even after the researchers accounted for total calorie intake, suggesting that how much children ate did not fully explain the pattern.

The children also consumed a standardized drink before their scans, “so the stronger response to food cues wasn’t simply due to hunger,” she said.

Rodent studies had already shown that a steady diet of sugar-rich food changes how the dorsal striatum works.

No earlier study had looked directly at how much ultra-processed food people habitually eat and how their striatum responds, the authors wrote.

The children came back for measurements roughly a year later, with an average gap of about 15 months.

Their BMI had risen by one point on average, their body fat by close to one percentage point, and their waists by about 1.5 inches (3.9 centimeters).

Children whose dorsal striatum had reacted more strongly to the food photos gained more in BMI and body fat. Eating more ultra-processed food was linked to a bigger waist a year later.

Ultra-processed food intake’s links to BMI and body fat were weak enough that chance could explain them.

The team then asked how much of the link between diet and fat gain involved the brain.

Once the dorsal striatum response was in the model, the association between ultra-processed food and body fat gain shrank by 44%, and the one with BMI by 31%.

Dr. Page said the result does more than repeat that these foods are tied to weight gain, because it points to a pathway in the brain.

“It actually gives us insight into the neurobiological underpinnings by which these foods may be linked to body composition over time.”

What stood out most to Dr. Page was that every child was a healthy weight when the study began.

“This suggests that the link between ultra-processed foods and the brain’s response to food cues may be detectable before obesity develops, rather than simply resulting from excess weight,” she told Earth.com.

Added sugar is in most ultra-processed foods, so the team checked whether sugar was driving the result.

Children who ate more added sugar tended to have stronger striatum responses too, but the pattern wasn’t clear enough to rule out chance.

When the team accounted for added sugar, the link between ultra-processed food and the dorsal striatum weakened and was no longer statistically clear.

Still, the authors wrote that sugar is unlikely to be the whole story.

Forty-six children is a small group, and the authors wrote that this limited their power to detect modest effects. Brain scans of this kind are also hard to reproduce in small groups.

Each child was scanned only once, so no one knows yet whether the stronger responses persist or fade as children get older.

An observational study like this one can’t say which came first. Children whose reward circuits already react more strongly to food may be the ones who reach for ultra-processed foods, rather than the other way around.

And because every child started at a healthy weight, the pattern may look different in children who already carry extra weight.

The research team said the burden shouldn’t fall only on families and children, because these foods are often the most accessible, affordable and convenient ones available.

“Changing the food environment to help people access healthier, more minimally processed foods is an important part of the solution,” Dr. Page noted.

For families in the meantime, Dr. Page said the practical goal is to shift the overall diet toward more minimally processed foods. “Parents should not feel that they have to eliminate every packaged food.”

Her examples were water instead of soda and fruit instead of fruit juice or fruit snacks. The aim is more vegetables, beans, nuts, eggs and other foods closer to their original form.

One way to test whether the diet is driving the brain response is to change the diet.

“A future intervention study could test whether replacing ultra-processed foods with mostly minimally processed foods changes how children’s brains respond to food cues and helps prevent excess body-fat gain,” Dr. Page said.

Meanwhile, the BrainChild team will keep following these children through puberty and into young adulthood.

Dr. Page said the next step is to see how their diets relate to changes in the developing brain. The team will also look at their risk for obesity over time.

The full study was published in the journal JAMA Network Open.

An excavator found figurine molds at a Sudan temple in 1912 that was once a faience workshop

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Digs at Sanam Temple in Sudan from 2018 to 2020 found a workshop from the mid-600s BC that made green figurines for Kush's royal tombs.

In 1912 the Egyptologist Francis Llewellyn Griffith dug at Sanam Temple, on the Nile in northern Sudan. His single season there lasted three months. He removed the fill on a light narrow-gauge Decauville railway.

Griffith also found clay molds for shabtis, the little figurines put in tombs. In 1922, however, he wrote only that they revealed one of the industries pursued in the temple.

He did not go looking for the place where that industry worked. Griffith did not systematically explore outside the walls, and he dumped his spoil heaps right up against them.

Excavations from 2018 to 2020 have now uncovered the faience workshop, behind the temple’s rear wall. It had three firing pits, two mudbrick basins and hundreds of beads, and it dates to the mid-seventh century BC. Some of its raw materials are still not identified with certainty.

King Taharqo ruled Kush and Egypt from about 690 to 664 BC. He founded the temple around 675 BC at Napata, his capital near the Fourth Cataract. He dedicated it to Amun-Bull-of-Nubia, a local form of the god.

A building known as the royal treasury stood nearby and held imported storage jars, finished goods and elephant tusks. The town grew up near Jebel Barkal, on land where shifts in the Nile created stable ground for a city.

Taharqo built all the way from the Nile delta to Napata. Much of his effort went to Karnak, where the oldest part of the temple sat on high ground among Nile channels. On the walls at Sanam is the Sanam Historical Inscription, the longest Kushite royal inscription that survives.

The new project’s first season lasted three weeks in January 2018. Kathryn Howley of New York University’s Institute of Fine Arts wrote it up in the first field report.

It also lists two rifle cartridges, probably from the late 19th century, by a wall the team suspects was Kitchener’s Fort, a colonial building Griffith took down.

Trench 3 was opened to the rear of the temple as a square of about 8 feet (2.5 meters). Below a lot of charcoal came faience beads, one intricately molded piece and wasters, lumps that went wrong in the firing.

Small shells and grinding stones lay with them. Workmen also picked up many beads on the surface around Griffith’s old spoil heaps.

In 2019 and 2020, workers from Karima and Merawi helped widen the trench to about 16 feet (5 meters) a side. Under the pits, the team then found a floor of broken storage jars. Someone had laid them flat to give a solid surface to work on.

The ash around the pits held hundreds of disk beads in blue, green and white, plus bits of faience vessels. Faience is mostly silica, with some alkali and lime. Simple ovens can reach the roughly 1,650 degrees Fahrenheit (900 degrees Celsius) needed to fire it.

One fragment, a broken green shabti, had no inscription on it. A new clay mold found inside the temple matches it exactly. Griffith’s older molds match shabtis George Reisner dug up at Nuri, the royal cemetery.

In Napatan Nubia only royalty used shabtis, while in Egypt people of many ranks had them. Egyptian cliff tombs still turn up burial goods, such as the pottery vessels and mirrors in rock-cut tombs of the south.

The paper calls Sanam “the first workshop securely identified for Kushite Nubia.” Egyptian marl jars with folded rims under the floor point to the eighth and seventh centuries BC. Red-slipped beakers fit the same range.

Faience paste needs lime. In Egypt it probably came from limestone, but most of Sudanese Nubia sits on the Nubian sandstone formation and has very little of it.

In 2018 Howley took the shells for Nile snails, which would have been easy to collect. She also noted that they were too small for anyone to gather just for food.

The new paper is by Rennan Lemos of Durham University’s Department of Archaeology, Howley and independent scholar Siobhan Shinn. Shadia Abdu Rabo of Sudan’s National Corporation for Antiquities and Museums co-wrote it.

The shells came out of the ash by the thousands. One bivalve is most likely Transkeia globosa, a Red Sea clam once called Diplodonta globosa.

Some snail shells still show traces of brown, and their shape fits both Nile snails of the family Hydrobiidae and marine Phasianellidae.

Workers broke the shells in the mudbrick basins with grindstones, which still carry the marks. Then they mixed the powder into the paste before firing.

Earlier Nubian potters had also put shell into their clay. Hauling so much shell from the coast was no small task. The authors argued that it took either direct control of distant communities or deals with desert groups who moved goods.

They described the shell-based recipe as a locally adapted version of an Egyptian craft. They also argued it served the production of objects for royal use.

Faience firing gives off smoke, and the smell depends on the fuel. In Egypt, workshops often made faience at the edge of a settlement, where the wind took the fumes off.

At Sanam, however, workers dug the pits immediately outside the temple’s sandstone wall. The authors read that placement as a sign that the temple controlled production, with some items meant only for royal use.

The 2020 season also turned up dark resin nuggets wedged between flagstones in the rear rooms. The team burned one lump as an experiment. It gave off a heavy, sweet smell like the bakhuur incense used in Sudan today, Howley wrote in her 2019-2020 report.

Nobody knows yet if real kilns stood over the pits. Collapsed mudbrick between pits 1 and 2 might be kiln walls. Then again, the workers might have used a cover they could lift off. The team dated the workshop by its pottery, and the paper gives no radiocarbon dates.

Scientists Just Challenged a 50-Year-Old Theory About How Evolution Works

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

In bacteria and yeast, a gene’s importance did not predict its mutation rate, challenging the idea that evolution gives vital DNA preferential treatment.

Not all genes are equally important. Some are essential for survival, controlling basic functions such as copying DNA and keeping cells alive. Others can be lost with little or no effect. But does that mean evolution has found a way to shield its most vital genes from potentially harmful mutations?

For decades, scientists have debated whether essential genes are naturally less likely to mutate. Now, researchers at the University of Bath have put that idea to the test, with results that challenge a long-standing evolutionary hypothesis.

Dr. Sofia Radrizzani and Professor Laurence D. Hurst, from the university’s Centre for Evolution, examined spontaneous mutations in the bacterium Escherichia coli (E. coli) and baker’s yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae). Their study, published November 12 in PLOS Biology, found no evidence that essential genes receive extra protection and offers a new explanation for why earlier research may have suggested otherwise.

“We’d expect natural selection to rapidly remove mutations when they occur in more important genes, because these mutations are more likely to be harmful to the organism. This can create the illusion that important genes have fewer mutations when, in reality, some mutations have simply disappeared before we have a chance to analyze them – what you can think of as ‘ghost mutations’,” Hurst said.

To reduce that problem, the team used data from “mutation accumulation” experiments designed to minimize the effects of natural selection. Three separate tests found no evidence that selection had substantially skewed the results.

“This looks like the largest and cleanest sample of mutations assembled to date,” Radrizzani said.

The researchers compared mutation rates with experimental evidence showing what happens when individual genes are removed. Deleting an essential gene kills the cell, while losing other genes has little or no effect. This gave the team a way to test whether genes a cell depends on most were less likely to mutate.

“Analyze the data any which way and we found nothing. In neither species did these key genes have a lower mutation rate,” Hurst said.

The team also assessed importance through the amount of a gene’s protein the cell produces and the strength of natural selection acting on the gene over evolutionary time.

“We found that genes producing more abundant proteins don’t have lower mutation rates. Nor does the way genes evolve over thousands of years predict how frequently they mutate,” Radrizzani said.

These findings address a debate that began in 1972 and resurfaced as recently as 2023. At stake is the relationship between the two steps in the conventional account of evolution. Mutations first generate genetic variation. Natural selection and other forces then act on that variation, favoring some traits over others.

Darwin’s finches, which Charles Darwin encountered on the Galápagos Islands in 1835, offer a familiar example. Random mutations contribute to differences in beak size, allowing birds to use different food sources. When conditions favor particular foods, birds with beaks better suited to eating them are more likely to survive and reproduce. Their traits consequently become more common over generations.

The disputed proposal suggests that a gene’s importance also influences how often mutations arise in it. Vital genes would receive greater protection, while genes where change could help adaptation would mutate more frequently. Examples of the latter include genes in which mutations give bacteria antibiotic resistance or alter birds’ beak dimensions.

There is a theoretical difficulty with that proposal. Gene mutation rates are already so low that reducing them further for individual genes would offer very little benefit, making it difficult for natural selection to favor such a protective system.

“The claim that mutation is slower for important genes is a triumph of hype over substance,” said Radrizzani, who co-wrote the paper as part of her PhD. “It’s a really old suggestion and is reinvented in various forms routinely.”

Genes copied later during DNA replication did tend to mutate more frequently in the team’s analysis, showing that some features of the genome can help explain differences in mutation rates.

“While we see known causes of variation in mutation rates, we found no evidence for the effects that have been proposed as a challenge to the conventional model. None predicted which genes would have high or low mutation rates, and there are certainly no grounds for a shift in paradigm,” Hurst said.

Gut bacteria cannot see the sun so they use your meal times to control how your body absorbs nutrients

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Gut bacteria in mice read mealtimes, not daylight, and tell the intestine when to absorb, helping underfed mice grow and overfed ones get fat.

The body clock is supposed to run on light. Sunrise wakes the brain, and the rest of the body falls in line. But the bacteria in the gut live in the dark.

In mice, a common microbe keeps time another way: it reads when food arrives and tells the cells of the intestine to start absorbing.

The signal is a small molecule the bacterium makes while it digests phytate, a compound in grains, nuts and seeds. The molecule switches on genes that pull amino acids and fats out of a meal.

Give it to undernourished young mice and they grow. Give it to mice on a high-fat diet and they get fatter.

Zheng Kuang, an associate professor of biological sciences at Carnegie Mellon University, led the work from his lab. Colleagues at the University of Pittsburgh, UT Southwestern Medical Center and the Quadram Institute in England took part.

All of it was done in mice and in human gut cells grown in a dish.

In an interview with Earth.com, Kuang said what struck him most was that the bacteria convert the timing of a meal into chemistry.

“Their absolute numbers did not need to change substantially across the day – their metabolic activity changed,” he said.

The bacterium is Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, one of the most common residents of the human gut.

Kuang’s team raised mice with no gut microbes at all, then gave some of them this one species and nothing else. They sampled the lining of the small intestine around the clock.

Mice eat at night. In the hours after dark, the colonized mice carried more of the bacterial signal molecule in their gut lining than they did in the morning.

Germ-free mice showed no such rise, and neither did mice given a mutant strain missing the enzyme that breaks down phytate.

The bacteria themselves were no more numerous at night than in the morning, Kuang’s team found. What rose was the activity of that one enzyme.

“We are not claiming these bacteria have their own internal clock,” Kuang told Earth.com. “Rather, they respond to feeding cues and produce molecules called inositol phosphates, which activate a protein called HDAC3 in intestinal cells.”

That protein, HDAC3, works inside the cells lining the intestine and helps decide which genes are on.

The bacterial molecule makes it grip a partner protein more tightly, and the pair then turns on a set of transporter genes that carry nutrients across the gut wall.

In colonized mice, 14 amino acids and 14 fatty acids rose in the blood during the feeding hours, while in germ-free mice they stayed low.

Of 248 transporter genes the team tracked, 122 cycled more strongly with the bacterium present, and most of those handle amino acids, sugars or fats.

The body’s master clock genes kept their normal daily rhythm either way. The bacterium didn’t reset the clock; it strengthened the daily rise and fall of the metabolic genes that follow it.

The team then fed mice only during the day, which is their normal sleeping time. The signal molecule fell and the gut’s daily rhythm in gene activity flattened, though the animals ate no less than before.

Mice allowed to eat only at night looked like mice allowed to eat whenever they liked.

The feeding experiment is the result Kuang pointed to when asked what people should take from the work. “The key message is to align your feeding schedule with the natural day-night cycle – eating during your active, daytime hours,” he said.

Artificial light has pushed human eating into the night, he added, and the study suggests that misalignment disrupts the bacterial signals.

Kuang said there was likely a metabolic cost to that disruption. The feeding experiment itself tracked the signal and the gene rhythms, not the mice’s weight.

Young mice were weaned onto a diet with the usual calories but little protein or fat, a model for childhood malnutrition.

Those given the bacterium grew longer bodies and longer thigh bones than those given the mutant strain. They had heavier organs, more amino acids in their blood and better blood sugar control, and they ate the same amount.

The signal molecule on its own did the same, in mice with and without a normal gut community. In mice bred to lack HDAC3 in their gut cells, neither the bacterium nor the molecule helped, and a drug that blocks HDAC3 blunted the effect too.

In a public dataset of children’s stool samples, the bacterium was among 19 species more common in healthy children than in undernourished ones.

Adult mice fed a high-fat, high-sugar diet for 12 weeks gained more weight with the bacterium than with the mutant strain.

They carried more belly fat and liver fat and had higher blood fats and worse blood sugar handling. They absorbed more fat from food and passed less of it in their stool.

Again, the mice lacking HDAC3 in their gut cells showed none of this, and food intake didn’t differ.

Kuang said that double edge is the finding he most wants understood. “This illustrates why it is too simple to label a bacterium as inherently ‘good’ or ‘bad’ – its effects depend on diet and the metabolic state of the host,” he explained.

The team’s reading is evolutionary. A pathway that extracts the most from every meal was an advantage when food was scarce, and the same pathway can work against a body surrounded by high-fat diets.

Whether this bacterium even lives and works in the human small intestine is an open question. The team also could not prove that the signal molecule in the gut cells came from the bacteria rather than from the cells themselves, which make related molecules.

And phytate intake varies from one diet to another, so the signal’s strength probably does too, the authors wrote.

Whether the same signal shifts in people who eat on a jet-lagged or night-shift schedule is a question the study does not reach.

Asked how soon the bacteria could be tested against malnutrition, Kuang said it is too early to give a reliable timeline for human testing.

The next step, he said, is to look for the pathway in human intestinal samples and in children with undernutrition. Until then, the case that mealtimes talk to the gut through its bacteria rests on mice.

The full study was published online in a publication of the Carnegie Mellon University.

Simple fungi can recognize shapes, like circles and crosses, when looking for wood to eat

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Daily Wonder, Pink Oyster Mushroom A fungus has no brain, no eyes, and no nerves. Yet a wood-rotting fungus, given a cross of wood blocks to grow on,…

A fungus has no brain, no eyes, and no nerves. Yet a wood-rotting fungus, given a cross of wood blocks to grow on, attached more of its threads to the outer blocks than to the inner ones.

Given a circle to grow on, it showed no such pattern and left the center bare. The researchers suggested the difference may be a form of pattern recognition.

Yu Fukasawa of Tohoku University in Japan led the 2024 study with colleagues at Tohoku and at Nagaoka College.

The fungus they used lives on forest floors and builds its networks by sending thick strands of thread, called cords, from one piece of dead wood to the next.

Its name is Phanerochaete velutina, and Fukasawa’s team had earlier found that it remembers the direction to a piece of wood.

“You’d be surprised at just how much fungi are capable of,” Fukasawa said. “They have memories, they learn, and they can make decisions. Quite frankly, the differences in how they solve problems compared to humans is mind-blowing.”

Even so, the team phrased its own conclusion as a question, not a verdict.

The mushroom in the video above is a pink oyster, not the fungus in this study. Pink oysters, named for their salmon-pink caps, grow on dead hardwood and are farmed on straw as food.

The study’s fungus grows as a thin crust on wood, with no cap or stem. Both break down dead wood with a network of threads.

Fukasawa’s team cut beech wood into cubes less than half an inch (1 cm) on a side, weighed them, and let the fungus grow into them for six weeks.

The team then set nine cubes on each tray of damp soil, in a circle or in a cross, with 2 inches (5 cm) between neighboring blocks. Each tray was about 9.5 inches (24 cm) square, and there were ten of each shape.

Everything but the shape was the same from tray to tray. The trays stayed in the dark at 68°F (20°C) for 116 days, nearly four months.

Once a week the team misted them with sterile water and moved them around the incubator. A camera 18 inches (46 cm) above the trays photographed each one monthly.

At the end, the researchers counted the cords attached to each block, then dried and weighed the blocks again to see how much wood the fungus had broken down.

After 116 days, cords ran from block to block across every tray, though a few blocks ended up with no cord attached at all.

In the cross, the four blocks at the ends of the arms had more cords than the four middle blocks and the one at the center. The count ran from zero cords on some blocks to nine on others.

In the circle, no position in the ring had reliably more cords than another. The cross result was the opposite of what the team had predicted.

They had expected the center block, the one with four neighbors, to be the most connected, and instead the blocks at the ends of the arms were.

The team suggested the outer blocks may have worked as outposts, where the fungus absorbed water and nutrients from the soil. The inner blocks, with fewer cords, may have become little more than a path for cords passing through.

The more cords a block had, the more of its wood the fungus had broken down. Blocks in the circle lost between 18% and 65.5% of their dry weight, and blocks in the cross lost between 16.7% and 62.7%. Overall, the cross blocks lost a little less than the circle blocks did.

The researchers also reran the analysis without the extreme blocks, the ones with no cords or with nine. Without them, the difference between the two shapes faded, while the link between cords and decay remained.

The fungus also spread roughly the same amount of thread over the soil in both shapes. So the cross blocks didn’t decay less because the fungus grew less. The network was built differently, and the wood decayed differently.

The recognition argument rests on that difference. In a human brain, different images activate different groups of neurons, and that contrast in activity is what lets the brain recognize an image.

A network of fungal threads that builds itself one way for a cross and another for a circle may be doing something similar, Fukasawa’s team wrote.

Every block started the same way, with threads growing outward in all directions. Then the fronts from neighboring blocks met and fused into one body.

In the circle trays, the merged fungus kept growing outward, and by day 34 a large bare patch had formed at the center.

Fukasawa’s team compared this to fairy rings, the circles of mushrooms that widen year after year because the fungus beneath them grows outward from its edge. Why the fused network left the center empty is something the team can’t yet explain.

One possibility they raised is crowding: fungal growth is known to stop once the threads get dense enough, and the threads inside the ring may have reached that point.

Whether the fungus is conscious is irrelevant, the team wrote, because cognition can run without consciousness even in a brain.

Slime molds, which also have no brain, have already solved a maze and linked food sources with networks about as efficient as a real railway.

The team raised one possible explanation, and it involves rhythm. The amount of calcium at each growing tip rises and falls in a cycle, and so do the flow of material and the electrical activity through the fungus.

When separate colonies fuse, those rhythms tend to synchronize, and synchronized rhythms may be how the network carries information about where things are.

The rhythm idea is a hypothesis, and this experiment didn’t measure it. The team also can’t yet say whether the tray lids affected the empty center of the ring.

The lids were loose and came off every week, so the researchers doubt the middle ran short of oxygen. The way to settle it is a tray with no lid, and that’s the experiment they said would be worth running.

The full study was published in the journal Fungal Ecology.
