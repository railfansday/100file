New hypothesis explains why the right side of the human brain controls the left side of the body

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

The brain's crossed wiring runs through every vertebrate group, and two neuroscientists trace it to the eyes of the first fishes.

A stroke on the right side of the brain weakens the left arm and leg. The brain is cross-wired that way, and for more than a century scientists have assumed there was an evolutionary reason for it.

Two neuroscientists have traced the crossing through every major group of living vertebrates. They now propose that evolution may never have selected it for the jobs it does.

On their reading, the crossed wiring may simply be inherited from the way the first fishes’ eyes were wired to their brains, some 500 million years ago.

Martin Wessendorf, an associate professor emeritus of neuroscience at the University of Minnesota, and Daniel Wang, a researcher in the University’s Department of Neuroscience, wrote the review.

It is a hypothesis built from more than a century of published anatomy rather than a new experiment. However, the authors wrote that it may be testable in fossils.

In an interview with Earth.com, Wang said the question reaches beyond his own field.

“This’ll be relevant to anyone who wants to know more about why brain injury on one side of the head often leads to loss of function on the opposite side of the body,” he said.

The crossing begins at the eye. The lens flips the image, so whatever sits to your right lands on the left side of each retina. The nerve fibers carrying the right half of the view then sort themselves at a junction behind the eyes and run to the left side of the brain.

One place that information goes is a structure in the midbrain called the optic tectum. It holds a map of the opposite half of visual space, and it fires fast turning and escape movements.

Every vertebrate examined appears to have one, lampreys and hagfish included, along with the crossing junction behind the eyes.

Living jawless fish carry both, so the authors conclude that the last common ancestor of all vertebrates probably did too. That puts the first crossed wiring in the Cambrian period, among jawless fishes that lived roughly 538 to 486 million years ago.

Anatomists call crossed wiring contralateral organization, and its age is what struck Wang.

“What surprised me the most was likely just how old the contralateral organization of the optic tectum is for vertebrates – spanning even to the Cambrian,” he told Earth.com.

Body senses crossed next. Two pathways carry touch, pain, temperature and body position from the spinal cord up to the brain, and both cross the midline in amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.

The one for light touch and body position is missing in fishes, so the authors trace it to the last common ancestor of the four-limbed animals.

Movement pathways cross too. One runs from the optic tectum down to the spinal cord and turns the body toward whatever just appeared at the edge of vision.

Lampreys and bony fish appear to have no such pathway. Sharks have one that mostly stays on its own side, and rays and lungfish have a crossed one.

Reptiles appear to have lost that pathway. A second crossed pathway, from a cluster of midbrain cells called the red nucleus, is thought to help control limbs.

True mammals appeared about 165 million years ago. By then, those crossed senses and movement pathways had already existed for almost 200 million years.

The pathway that lets you move one finger at a time is the special case. It runs from the cerebral cortex, the six-layered outer sheet found only in mammals, down to the spinal cord, and it crosses in the lower brainstem.

In most mammals, it ends where touch signals enter the cord and adjusts those signals. Few mammals besides primates, raccoons and kinkajous have a version that reaches the nerve cells that drive muscles.

So the circuit that gives humans fine control of their hands appears to be a sensory circuit put to a new use. Wang said that was the finding that surprised his co-author most.

“This demonstrates that circuitry that’s involved in one function can, with just a change or two, become a function that’s entirely different.”

The crossing isn’t universal in this pathway. In goats and elephants, it runs down both sides of the spinal cord, and in hedgehogs and moles it mostly stays on its own side. Even in humans, an estimated 10 to 25 percent of its fibers don’t cross.

Wang pointed to the goats and elephants, “so there’s a decent amount of diversity there,” he said. The authors read that variety as a sign that natural selection wasn’t strongly favoring the crossing in this pathway.

Isaac Newton proposed in 1704 that the junction behind the eyes sorts nerve fibers by visual field.

Ramón y Cajal, who later won a Nobel Prize for his work on the structure of the nervous system, went further in an 1898 paper. Once the visual map crossed, he proposed, it would pay for the maps of hearing, touch and movement to match it.

In computer models published in 2014, an animal controlled by the opposite side of its brain was likelier to survive a blow to one side of the head or body.

At least two other research groups have blamed a twist in the body plan, dating to the split between vertebrates and their invertebrate ancestors. That argument goes back to an 1822 dissection of a crayfish.

The authors see a problem with the twist. The visual system was probably crossed in the first vertebrates, but the pathways for touch, limb control and finger control didn’t exist yet.

So the authors doubt that a single ancient twist can explain them.

The authors’ own answer starts with the optic tectum. Its map of the opposite half of visual space sits on top. Beneath it, in register, sit maps of touch and hearing from the same half, and at the bottom sit the cells that fire movements.

Wessendorf and Wang propose that this stack biased the sensory and motor pathways that evolved later toward crossing.

When the cortex evolved, most of the pathways carrying signals into it were already crossed. A cortex wired to its own side would have needed extra crossings to collect vision, touch and hearing from the far side, or a rewiring of every older circuit.

The authors argue that either change would have carried a “wiring cost,” leaving mammals that were less energy-efficient, less intelligent, or both.

Not everything crosses. Smell and taste mostly stay on the same side, and the authors note that neither sense maps space the way vision, hearing and touch do.

The cerebellum, which coordinates movement, works the same side of the body it sits on.

“We propose that contralateral organization didn’t necessarily evolve as an adaptation, but because evolution is an engineer that builds with the foundation and materials it has at hand,” Wang and Wessendorf wrote.

The authors list what would test the idea. Their first prediction is that the last common ancestor of vertebrates had both the crossing junction and an optic tectum.

Cambrian fossils of jawless fish exist, and a fossilized brain cast from one could show whether both were there.

Wang said the finding that would complicate the idea is an early fossil vertebrate whose optic tectum handled vision on the same side rather than the opposite one.

The authors also predict that crossed pathways from the forebrain to the spinal cord will prove widespread in birds. Unfortunately, relatively few bird species have been examined.

Crocodilians appear not to have been checked, and the authors predict they have one too.

Hearing is the one sense the authors treat with caution. It appears to have evolved partly on its own in different lines.

What remains unknown is whether any Cambrian fish skull preserves enough of its brain to show the crossing.

Until one does, the idea rests on living anatomy. The next checks are in the fossil record, and in the birds and crocodilians whose spinal pathways have yet to be mapped.

The full study was published in the journal Frontiers in Neuroanatomy.

The Ford Mustang GTD 1901 Takes Inspiration From a 125-Year-Old Race Car

The Blue Oval is celebrating a century and a quarter of racing history with a special-edition Mustang GTD.

Henry Ford was racing cars before the Mustang, GT40, or even the Ford Motor Company itself existed. Back in 1901, he won his first race behind the wheel of a two-cylinder race car nicknamed “Sweepstakes,” a car that produced just 26 horsepower. 125 years later, Ford has unveiled the 815-horsepower 2028 Mustang GTD 1901—a special edition inspired by the car that helped launch Henry Ford's automotive empire.

The Mustang GTD 1901 has a 789-horsepower advantage over the Sweepstakes car, but the design inspiration is clear. The Mustang GTD 1901 features a distinctive Royal Red exterior and a wide, off-center black racing stripe featuring a bright red centerline. The color combination reverses the original race car's black body and deep red chassis components. Additional diagonal graphics and exposed carbon-fiber elements, including the front splitter, rocker panels, rear diffuser, and wing, complete the exterior look.

Have a seat inside the Mustang GTD 1901, and you immediately realize this is not an ordinary GTD—that is, if there is even such a thing as an ordinary GTD. The first thing you notice are the Mahogany Red leather seats with Race Red stitching. Keep looking, and you’ll see there are also matching red accents on the seatbelts, while the steering wheel receives Mahogany Red faux suede.

The Performance Package is standard equipment on the Mustang GTD 1901. Options include the magnesium wheels, which reduce the unsprung weight by 50.3 pounds. If you want to spice up the interior, you can order the optional titanium trim package, which gets you redesigned titanium shifter paddles that have been lengthened for the 2028 model year. The titanium trim in the GTD, by the way? It comes from retired F-22 fighter jets.

Ford has not announced how many Mustang GTD 1901’s will be built, or how much they will cost. But we wouldn’t count on seeing too many at your local track day.

A lot has changed since Henry Ford won his first race; horsepower figures, aerodynamics, and suspension technology have come a long way. But the desire of the company that bears his name to prove itself on the racetrack apparently hasn't changed much at all, and that’s something we’re certainly thankful for.

Bentley’s First EV Is Already Sold Out, And Production Hasn’t Even Started

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

Browse this and many other websites without banner ads, personalized tracking and video ads.

Bentley’s first electric car, the Torcal SUV, looks like a hit already. Even though production hasn’t started yet, the $220,000+ EV is sold out for 2027, according to Frank-Steffen Wallier, the company’s head honcho, quoted by Automotive News.

The British luxury automaker said that all available production slots for next year have already been allocated and that the company is now taking orders that will be delivered in 2028. Bentley didn’t say how many EVs it will build in 2027, but the initial customer response has made the marque quite happy with the outcome.

“Needless to say, we’re very pleased with the response and position we’re in,” a company spokesperson told Automotive News.

That’s great news for Bentley, which isn’t doing great in terms of sales and profit. From January through September, it sold 5,970 cars globally, 17% fewer compared to the same period last year. Meanwhile, the profit dropped by more than half to $54.1 million through the first nine months of the year.

The Torcal, which is smaller than the Bentayga SUV, is the brand’s cheapest model, though it still costs a pretty penny. The base Torcal starts at $222,495, followed by the more powerful Torcal S at $262,695, and the Torcal First Edition at $300,995. All things considered, Walliser said that the smaller size and relatively lower price compared to the Bentayga have attracted new and younger customers to the brand.

By comparison, the Bentayga starts at $231,150, the Flying Spur sedan starts at $254,850, and the Continental GT two-door starts at $288,750.

So far, the Torcal S has proved to be the most popular version of the new EV, but when it comes to personalization, the sky’s the limit. “We see cars spec’d north of 300,000 pounds ($397,086),” said Walliser. “Individual content does not stop in case of an electric car. There have been some assumptions, and some people have been critical if people would be ready to do so.”

Based on the same underpinnings as the electric Porsche Cayenne, the Bentley Torcal is powered by a 113-kilowatt-hour battery that can be charged at up to 400 kilowatts. It can go from 10% to 80% in less than 20 minutes, and the estimated EPA range is expected to be over 300 miles.

Built on the Premium Platform Electric (PPE) architecture, it has two electric motors that make 810 horsepower and 881 pound-feet of torque in the standard Torcal and a whopping 876 hp and 996 lb-ft of torque in the Torcal S. When the model debuted in September, Bentley said it was the most powerful and quietest model to come out of the factory in Crewe.

To make the Torcal a reality, Bentley invested $463 million to upgrade its factory in England, plus at least the same amount for supply chain preparations and research and development, Walliser said.

Despite the electric SUV’s initial success, the British automaker is still heavily invested in gas and plug-in hybrid models, and the Bentayga isn’t going anywhere. “We have a very good combination with Torcal and Bentayga,” Walliser said. “The segment definitely has space for two cars.”

Ford Teases Reborn Cobra R That Picks Up Where The Shelby GT350 Left Off

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

Folks, the Cobra is back. In a 36-second teaser posted by Ford Racing on X, the everything app, the Blue Oval said an "all-motor heart born from racing" is on the way for the Mustang, and if you read between the lines, "all-motor" means naturally aspirated. The engine audio clip playing in the background certainly lends credibility to that idea. With a 2027 launch date, there aren't any technical details to speak of, but the name "Cobra" gives me a lot of confidence this bad boy is going to sit above the GT and possibly even the Dark Horse.

You'll want to hear this. For the drivers, with an all-motor heart born from racing.  Ford Mustang Cobra, a Legacy Reborn.  The story continues in 2027. pic.twitter.com/fogI982jfl

— Ford Racing (@FordRacing) November 10, 2026

Of course, nothing is official, but leaked dealer information lends some credence to how this new Cobra will be positioned within the Mustang lineup. You can think of it as the "spiritual successor to the Mustang Shelby GT350," according to Ford Authority, and it'll launch next year as a 2028. Get it while you can, though, because it's expected to have a relatively short production run through the 2029 model year. Under the hood will be a naturally aspirated V8, obviously, and it's either going to be 5.2 or 5.4 liters, depending on how much freedom Ford feels like dispersing. Don't worry, it's also expected to have a Tremec six-speed manual transmission.

There's no word on horsepower numbers just yet, but we can look at the data as a guiding light. Currently, the Dark Horse's 5.0-liter V8 pumps out 500 horsepower and 418 pound-feet of torque. The Dark Horse SC takes those numbers all the way up to 795 and 660, respectively. Looking back, the 5.2-liter Voodoo V8 in the GT350 produced 526 hp and 429 lb-ft. If I were a betting man, I'd put my money on the horsepower number being somewhere around the 550-575 mark.

It has been quite a while since the Cobra name appeared on a Mustang from Ford. The last time it popped up was in the early 2000s supercharged SN95 Mustang SVT Cobra, which was a beast for the time. Since then, there have been some Shelby-specific Cobras, but nothing from Ford itself.

You may be asking yourself, "Why did Ford revive the name Cobra rather than just call the Shelby GT350's successor a Shelby GT350?" Well, it's because of money. As we explained earlier this year, Ford has to pay to use the name Shelby on its cars, and it doesn't want to anymore. Shelby America owns the name. It's the same reason the Dark Horse SC has its name rather than Shelby GT500. It hasn't been cheap, either. Ford was paying about $800 in royalties per Shelby Mustang it sold, racking up over $30 million in licensing fees in the process.

At the end of the day, it doesn't really matter what this thing is going to be called. Of course, "Cobra" is sick as hell, but it could be called the Ford Mustang Beige Wallpaper for all I care. It's going to be sick as hell.

Tesla Is Killing The Cybertruck To Make Room For More Robotaxis

The controversial Tesla Cybertruck electric pickup will be retired next year, CEO Elon Musk confirmed on X. The angular truck went on sale at the end of 2023 and instantly became a social statement—for better or worse.

Production of the Cybertruck will start winding down next summer at the company’s Texas Gigafactory, and the extra floor space will be used to build more Cybercabs.

The unpainted truck had a short burst in popularity at the beginning of its production run, but interest fizzled, and sales never reached the potential that Musk and Tesla had envisioned back in 2019, when the vehicle was first presented as a concept.

In this year’s third quarter, the Cybertruck amassed 4,323 sales, according to data from Cox Automotive. From January through September, sales totaled 11,586 units. Tesla doesn’t break out individual sales figures for the Cybertruck, but industry estimates put the vehicle’s overall deliveries at roughly 70,000.

That’s a far cry from the hundreds of thousands of sales recorded every quarter by the Model 3 and Model Y. It’s also another reminder that electric pickups haven’t quite taken off as their manufacturers had hoped, and Tesla is now taking the fall, just like Ford did with the F-150 Lightning, which went the way of the dodo last year after lackluster sales and ballooning costs.

There’s no denying that you either love or hate the Cybertruck, just like there’s no denying that Tesla dared to do things like no other car manufacturer before it. Sure, some might argue that the angular EV looks like an unpolished fridge, but it brought steer-by-wire and a 48-volt low-voltage architecture to the masses. Whether or not they’ll be adopted by other companies is another story, though.

At roughly the same time as Elon Musk’s announcement that Cybertruck production would wind down in the middle of 2027, Tesla stopped taking orders for the entry-level Dual-Motor All-Wheel Drive version of the model, leaving just the Premium and Cyberbeast trims available to order on the automaker’s website.

Additionally, the price of the remaining models has been increased. The Premium AWD model now starts at $92,490, up from $89,990, while the top Cyberbeast now costs $102,490, up from $99,990. The most recent price bumps come after a similar move in August.

The Temerario might be the runt of the Lamborghini supercar litter, but it's still one raging bull. Taking the reins from its widely lauded Huracan predecessor, the Temerario is the least expensive way into Lambo's supercar lineup, but it doesn’t sacrifice much performance compared to the company's Revuelto flagship—in fact, it beats its brawnier older brother to 60 mph by a tenth of a second.

Under the Temerario's origami-folded sheetmetal lies a newly developed 4.0-liter twin-turbo V-8 nestled into the car's aluminum spaceframe just ahead of the rear axle. As if 789 horsepower from the engine alone wasn’t enough, a trio of electric motors—one mounted between the engine and transmission and one at each front wheel—ups its total output to 907 Italian stallions. If you somehow tire of the sound of the flat-plane-crank V-8's goosebump-raising 10,000-rpm wail, the electric motors also allow for short periods of silent, all-electric driving. With its mighty hybrid-assisted thrust, poster-worthy looks, and agile moves, the Temerario is every inch a purebred Lambo and well worthy of supercar status.

The Temerario's powertrain consists of a twin-turbo 4.0-liter V-8 with a flat-plane crankshaft, a trio of axial-flux electric motors, and an eight-speed dual-clutch transmission. The Temerario's V-8 alone is good for 789 horsepower; adding the output of the three electric motors brings the total combined output to 907 horsepower. That's just 94 ponies shy of the Lamborghini Revuelto, the brand's V-12 halo car. On the Temerario, one electric motor mounts to the V-8 in front of the transmission, while the remaining two live up front, one at each wheel. The Temerario's V-8 redlines at 10,000 rpm, and the engine's peak torque of 538 pound-feet is available from 4000 to 7000 rpm. While the flat-plane-crank V-8 sounds glorious when pushed to its limit, we wish that its exhaust packed a bit more fury earlier in the rev range.

With the electric motors in play, maximum torque output rises to 590 pound-feet. In addition to supplying enormous power, the hybrid configuration helps provide instantaneous torque and linear power delivery over a broad range of speeds and driving situations. Thirteen drive programs are available via a rotary button on the steering wheel, including launch and drift modes, the latter offering three levels of tail-out leeway. We drove the Temerario at the track and on the street and were enthralled with its lively powertrain and playful demeanor.

In Comparison: In our testing, the Temerario got to 60 mph in a ridiculously quick 2.1 seconds. It also ripped through the quarter-mile in 9.4 seconds at 150 miles per hour. Both figures beat its big sibling, the Revuelto, by 0.3 seconds and 2 mph, respectively.

Juice is stored in a 3.8-kWh lithium-ion battery pack. The battery resides in the center tunnel along with the transmission—the location is said to help optimize weight distribution and is protected from underneath by structural elements. Regenerative braking and power from the internal-combustion engine both replenish the battery's state of charge. Plug-in charging is also possible via a standard 7-kW onboard charger; with typical household current, it can recharge an empty battery to full capacity in a claimed 30 minutes. An EV-only mode provides around 4 miles of range.

The EPA estimates that the Temerario will deliver 14 mpg city, 19 mpg highway, and 16 mpg combined. First Row: Thanks to its all-aluminum chassis, the Temerario's interior offers more space than the Huracán it replaced. Headroom increased by 1.3 inches, legroom by 1.8 inches, and Lamborghini claims the interior is large enough to accommodate drivers up to six feet five wearing a helmet. The interior shares some styling cues from the Revuelto; four stitching patterns are available, and the 18-way adjustable driver's seat is heated and ventilated. The Temerario also borrows the Revuelto's steering wheel, which is too busy with controls for our liking.

Cargo Capacity: If you're planning on taking the Temerario on a weekend getaway, you'd better pack light; the front trunk only has a four-cubic-foot capacity.

Lamborghini says the Temerario offers the most advanced suite of infotainment and connectivity features of any model in its lineup. Three displays—a 12.3-inch instrument panel with three user-selectable views, an 8.4-inch infotainment screen, and a 9.1-inch passenger screen—occupy prime dashboard real estate. Wireless Apple CarPlay and Android Auto are standard, as is a Sonus Faber audio system.

The most notable feature, though, is the optional Lamborghini Vision Unit (LAVU) system. Essentially an on-board data acquisition system, the setup utilizes three cameras: one viewing the road, one focused on the passengers, and one looking forward over the driver's shoulder. Preloaded with data for 150 different racing circuits, the system can be used to evaluate and improve driver performance. It also enables a "memories recorder" function that allows for up to two minutes of off-track recording, and works as a dashcam that can capture one minute of incident history.

Compared to most other cars, the Temerario doesn't offer many driver-assistance features.

For information about the Temerario's crash-test results, visit the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) and Insurance Institute for Highway Safety (IIHS) websites.

Compared with most mainstream automakers, Lamborghini doesn't provide stellar warranty coverage. But its protection plans are competitive with those of high-end rivals such as Ferrari and can be enhanced by optional extended coverage. A five-year complimentary scheduled maintenance plan is now standard.
