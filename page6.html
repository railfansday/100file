‘I Started Strength Training at 74. At 78, I Can Deadlift 132 Lbs—Here’s How.’

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

“While I love lifting weights and hitting new PRs, the greatest reward is how much easier everyday life has become.”

If there’s one thing I’ve learned as I’ve gotten older, it’s that being active and being strong aren’t necessarily the same thing. For most of my life, I’d been recreationally active, and had loved kayaking, swimming, and taking my dog on long walks. I’d never done any kind of structured strength training, until right before my 75th birthday. One day, I was sitting on a curb outside my house, and when it was time to stand up, I couldn’t. I felt embarrassed, and the idea of falling and not being able to get up or being in a situation where I couldn’t take care of myself was scary.

I wanted everyday activities, like climbing stairs, carrying groceries, and getting up off the floor, to feel easier. I also wanted to trust my body more, and feel confident in my ability to handle whatever life threw my way. So, I joined Aspire Athletic Club, a gym near my home in Schenectady, New York, and began working out once a week with the owner, Chris Wertman. In the beginning, even simple movements were challenging, so I started with basic bodyweight exercises like the box squat, step-up, and bird dog. The focus was on learning proper form and getting comfortable with the movements.

After a few weeks, I gradually incorporated 5- to 8-lb dumbbells into the movements, and a month or two later, I noticed a real difference in my strength: I could do more repetitions, lift even heavier weights, and move with greater confidence. It was so encouraging to see the hard work pay off, and it motivated me to keep going. About a year into consistent training, I was feeling stronger and ready to take on more. I began training full-body twice a week and incorporated more challenging exercises like squat variations, the walking lunge, weighted glute bridge, and chest fly to build overall strength.

I’d never deadlifted before, so seeing someone my age lift with that kind of strength was incredibly inspiring. That decision marked the beginning of a new chapter in my strength training journey—I told my new trainer of about six months, Jack Tyree, that I wanted to lift heavier weights and try deadlifting. I even began training for my gym’s upcoming annual deadlift exhibition, which was in eight months.

Since I’d just been training with dumbbells, I tried working with a kettlebell while I got comfortable with the movement and proper technique. As I got stronger and more confident, I graduated to the barbell and continued adding more weight each week. Then, this past May, I deadlifted 132 lbs for a single rep at the exhibition, setting a personal record. I never imagined I’d be able to do something like that, but I was so proud of how strong I’d become—and I was excited to see how much further I could push myself.

Six months after the exhibition, I now strength train three days a week: one day focused on upper body, one on lower body, and one dedicated to mobility and balance. Jack still programs all my workouts, and every session includes six to eight exercises, with 3 or 4 sets of 6 to 8 reps each. I use a mix of free weights and weight machines, and some of my go-to moves include the farmer’s carry, curtsy lunge, lat pulldown, scapular pushup, squat, and bench press. Deadlifts are still my favorite; I can now do three reps at 132 lbs, which I’m really proud of.

Outside the gym, I love getting outside and staying active, so I walk a few miles with my dog every day for my cardiovascular health. In addition to mobility and balance work at the gym, I also try to stretch at home whenever I can to ease muscle soreness and improve my range of motion, especially in my shoulders and hips.

I know nutrition is an important part of recovery too. Although I don’t follow a strict nutrition plan or count macros, I try to eat protein at every meal to support muscle growth. Usually, it’s chicken, red meat, or a scoop of protein powder mixed into my morning oatmeal. I also prioritize whole foods and aim to incorporate a variety of fruits and vegetables throughout the day.

When I first started strength training, I didn’t know where to begin. So, having someone guide me through the movements, teach me proper form, and help me build strength safely made all the difference. My trainers have helped me recognize what I’m capable of, push beyond what I thought was possible, and progress at a pace that feels right. They also create workouts that challenge me while keeping my goals in mind. Having their support and encouragement has given me the confidence to try things I never imagined I could do. Whether it’s deadlifting heavy weights, bench pressing a barbell, or taking less rest between working sets, my trainers have taught me not to put a ceiling on what I think I can accomplish.

Before I strength trained, I felt weak and unsteady, but I didn’t give up and accept that I’d feel like that for the rest of my life. In some ways, I completely started over with exercise at 74 because I tried something brand new—and that’s affected my life outside of the gym.

Now, at 78, I feel stronger than ever; I can get up off the floor with ease, carry my own groceries into the house, shovel snow, garden, and play sports with my grandchildren. These are things I want to keep doing independently, and having the strength to do them confidently means the world to me. While I love lifting weights and hitting new PRs, the greatest reward is how much easier everyday life has become.

Learning to lift in my mid-70s wasn’t easy, and building that strength didn’t happen overnight, but I kept showing up, putting in the work, and gradually accomplishing what I once never thought was possible. Now, as I approach 80, I’m still getting stronger, and I want other women to know they’re never too old to get started, and they may be capable of far more than they realize. If I can go from struggling to get up from a curb to deadlifting 132 pounds, who knows what else is possible?

Swatara Treasure Hunt

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

I battle rocks and falling temperatures while searching for a lost item on trail.

My plans are rarely flexible, until they are. I checked in with Pebbles last night to see what she, Rebar, and Greasetrap had planned (Soaked completed his hike the previous day, huge congrats to him!). They planned to slack-packing 15 miles starting from Swatara Gap and heading south. I would be doing the same – but I had the section heading north scheduled first. Not a big deal, I could just swap them. However, my section south of Swatara Gap called for 24 miles of hiking. I would need to start early and hike flat out all day to make it before sunset. I knew that isn’t really their style, which is valid. I had made up my mind and was sticking to my plan. I was up early and decided to head towards town to buy a coffee. While enjoying my hot beverage in my car I decided to take one last look at both itineraries. I might not be able to hike with them, but simply flipping my current section might allow me to pass them early in the day. Eventually I have to earn my trail name, right? I set a course for Swatara Gap and was on trail close to 8:00AM.

I texted my change in plans to Pebbles as I made my way down the trail. No sooner had I switched my phone into airplane mode and put it back in my pack than 3 hikers appeared ahead. Ca-caw! The group chimed. Ca-caw! I called back. Soon we were catching up and discussing our future hiking plans and Rebars new kicks. His previous Altras had taken a beating on the trail, I recall him putting some kind of indestructible adhesive on them during a previous encounter. He now had a snazzy new pair of Altras and was clearly enjoying the upgrade. After chatting for a few minutes we agreed to keep in close contact since I’ll soon be skipping ahead of them to close out my hike. The trail crosses the Swatara Creek on the 19th century Waterville Bridge, an interesting piece of industrial era engineering.

As I approached the bridge, I felt my phone vibrate. I had left it on since I had parked my car in a lot that requires calling the state park if you intend to leave your vehicle there overnight. I thought I might get a call back since I left a message. Instead I saw that it was Pebbles calling me. “So, you know how you like treasure hunts? Well, Rebar left a dry bag at the last campsite…”. For the third time, I was going to help the crew out by retrieving an object they’d left behind. I think I could make a living doing this professionally given the dozens of items reported missing on FarOut. Drive to the nearest trailhead, hike out and retrieve the item, drive it up the trail or ship it back to its owner, profit. Of course, for friends I’ll stick with the rate of a few guava candies. A few miles later I was staring at a bag resting neatly by a fire pit, it was definitely Rebar’s, my easiest find yet!

The trail had leveled off onto another long ridge, but the rocks made sure things weren’t too easy for me. The rocks and cold became the theme of the day. I had endured many cold days but not one single time had I ended a day with my sleeping layers still on. Hiking always warmed me up a ton and often after 30-minutes of hiking I’d shed them and hike in my shorts and sun hoodie. The climb out of Swatara Gap warmed me up and had me considering stopping to make the adjustment. But I wanted to see how I felt once the climbing stopped and I was just moving along the mostly level ridge. My temperature returned to a comfortable level quickly, so I was content to keep moving and keep the layers on. There were a handful of views along the ridge, but otherwise minimal points of interest aside from some boulder fields.

Eventually I came to Route 501. I considered taking the path to the 501 Shelter, but the comments on FarOut said it was boarded up with no facilities for hikers. That didn’t sound worth documenting for the blog so I continued along the AT a few miles to the Showers Steps viewpoint. It was a gorgeous view, but unlike many of the viewpoints from the ridges I have been hiking along it faced the sunset. It was cloudy, but there were signs that some semblance of a sunset might be visible. A few breaks in the clouds, tunnels of light illuminating small areas of the valley below. I hadn’t planned to camp here, but a check of FarOut revealed that I had just enough time to hike to Route 183 and back if I really hauled. There were more viewpoints ahead so I decided to check those out and make a decision along the way.

The trail traded rocky sections with cruisy for the next few miles. Each viewpoint I passed faced too far toward the east to offer a view of the sunset. They probably would have been great for sunrise, but it was supposed to be cloudy. Sunrise would be a total question mark as to whether there would be much to see. Today’s sunset at least had a little promise. In fact, the beams of sun were occasionally shining on the trail, I felt external warmth for the first time all day. Was it a sign that I should camp at Showers Steps? I figured that was the best plan and hurried down the trail.

On my way back I stopped at the Hertlein Creek to collect water for the evening. I stopped at the first trickle of water, which meant I missed seeing the nearby campsites and swimming hole complete with rope swing. It probably wasn’t the right weather for the latter but it would have been cool to have a quick look-see.

I was back at the Showers Steps in time for sunset, but I could tell the whole way back that was not really going to be much to see. The clouds had rolled back in, some quite dark. The view was still great, just not quite what I’d hustled for. I pitched my tent near a makeshift shelter, noticing that, by some miracle, my tent was already mostly dry. The rain two nights ago had soaked it, I expected a long wait for it dry out in the 40 degree weather, but was pleasantly surprised that I could go right ahead and set up my bed for the night. Soon I was bracing for the night, which was going be perhaps the coldest night I’ve spent in my tent all year.

A homelabber detailed improvements to the second-gen Optane AI build in a follow-up post on Reddit, showing the addition of two RTX 3090s to run the 2.8-trillion-parameter Moonshot Kimi K3 model at 3.11 tokens per second. Transferring almost half the model onto a RAM drive added a significant 41% to generation speed and 26% to prompt evaluation. The extra fans are still needed, though, as the persistent Optane memory gets very hot.

That speed is not the most impressive, but it comes with 16.8 tokens per second for prompt evaluation, with 45 of 94 shards, or 414 GB out of 865 GB, living on a RAM drive. Such an arrangement is not new to enthusiasts who would set aside system memory for extra-fast performance in a pseudo-non-volatile manner — it’s still volatile, but appears like a disk — although not everyone has that much RAM sitting around free these days. With both GPUs but no RAM drive, the build ran at 2.20 tokens per second for generation and 13.3 for prompt evaluation.

Even a quick read of the details makes it clear that the GPUs aren’t holding the model: two RTX 3090s, at 24 GB apiece, only have a total of 48 GB of VRAM, which is about 5.5% of the 865 GB model file. That said, the model is a mixture-of-experts (MoE) type, where its weights are split into multiple “experts” with only a few run per token. Any given token will have “routed” experts selected for it, while “shared” experts run on every token.

For this model, with 896 experts, 16 are chosen per token, with two shared, with an active 104B parameters out of its 2.8T. All 2.8T parameters would take about 5.6 TB at 16-bit precision. Moonshot’s own release, which uses the 4-bit MXFP4 format, comes to about 1.56 TB, and the Q2_K file used here shrinks that to 865 GB, an average of under 3 bits per parameter. The 865 GB figure is likely in gibibytes (GiB), but for clarity we’re using the GB values as written by the homelabber.

The configuration here has the routed-expert tensors stay on the CPU side, originally in Optane, and now in Optane plus the RAM drive. The RTX 3090s handle attention, or how each token weighs the rest of the context, and the dense feed-forward network layer that isn’t split into experts, where every token passes through it. They also manage the shared experts, other active-dense components, the KV cache, and the stored context of the current conversation, with the GPU compute buffers. Basically, the parts on the GPUs are “used on every token,” and the improvements there come from the high level of GPU compute and memory bandwidth.

DRAM is a lot slower than VRAM, but it’s still capable of giving a decent speedup. The new build involves using Linux’s tmpfs, which, per the kernel documentation, “is a file system which keeps all of its files in virtual memory.” Those shards now live in memory, which admittedly is not ideal when the original build was designed to save money by using Optane instead. With those shards in DRAM, which the builder has said is faster than Optane, that part of the model no longer has to be read from the slower memory; the workload is decidedly distributed.

The speed that came with the GPUs is within the range of what the builder expected, guessing “somewhere between 2 and 4 tokens per second” in a prediction from the original build thread. The GPU install had to wait, in part, for modifications to the case, which required cutting out some of the metal and relocating the PSU to make room for the GPUs and their cooling. Cooling remains a huge issue, with “the improved throughput [not changing] the original thermal warning,” and the two 12W blower fans for the Optane DIMMs are kept in place. Add in dual 350W-class GPUs, and the changes are understandable.

Things won’t stop here, as more optimizations are possible, including “customized quantization,” which may be similar to the techniques used by lawyer Nick Mermiges. Quantization selected per tensor or layer, or by where the data lives, might reduce bandwidth and capacity pressures for the slower tiers; the setup “might be faster yet.” Speed, or tokens per second alone, is not enough, and output quality must be taken into consideration in any future plan.

A Space Probe Returns to Asteroids Where NASA Tested Planetary Defense Systems

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

It’s mostly been the subject of Hollywood movies, but one day, an asteroid or comet could collide with Earth and cause serious damage. We don't have to look all that far back for an example. In 1908, in an incident now known as the “Tunguska Event,” a space rock, likely a few dozen meters wide, exploded over Siberia. Though scientists weren’t able to visit the site for another two decades, the landscape was still scarred from the explosion.

Astronomers predict that in mid-April 2029, the asteroid Apophis will fly by Earth. The 450-meter-wide body will miss us at a distance of only 19,000 miles. That’s about 12 times closer to us than the moon and much closer than many satellites. It will even be visible to the naked eye.

In the past few decades, there’s been enormous progress in making these kinds of predictions. As space probes deepen our knowledge of small bodies in the solar system, astronomers have increasingly reliable and detailed data for finding potentially hazardous asteroids. Our ability to respond to such a threat, however, is another story. This is the missing and extremely important link—planetary defense technology that could allow us to deflect a dangerous object in time.

In a sci-fi flick, such situations are often solved with a nuclear bomb. But in the real world, blowing up a big space rock into tiny bits is not ideal. Instead, it’s better to alter the asteroid's orbit. Everything in the vacuum of space is in motion. Our own planet travels about 20 miles every second. If the object hurtling towards us can be slowed down and thus reach the meeting point a little later, it will simply miss the Earth.

Four years ago, this was essentially what NASA tested on a pair of celestial bodies known as Didymos and Dimorphos. Astronomers at the Kitt Peak Observatory discovered Didymos, an asteroid measuring 780 meters across, 30 years ago. A few years later, Czech astronomer Petr Pravec observed that this asteroid had a “moonlet” orbiting it about every 12 hours. This smaller body, measuring about 151 meters in diameter, was then named Dimorphos.

The paired objects were a perfect testing ground for the kind of deflection a planetary defense system would involve. Locked in on these targets, NASA launched the Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) in late 2021. Nearly a year later, the DART probe crashed into Dimorphos.

Scientists had hoped that the impact of the probe—which was barreling toward the asteroids at over 13,000 miles per hour—could shorten the period of the mini-moon's orbit by a minute or more. In the end, it was slowed down by 33 minutes. This major deviation was caused by the ejection of debris, leading to a much greater recoil than what was produced by the impact itself. In addition, the orbital speed of both asteroids around the sun also slowed. And in a sense, Dimorphos briefly became a comet—the debris released by the impact creating a long tail behind it.

But questions remain about the longer-term impacts of this test. In October 2024, the European Space Agency (ESA) sent off the space probe Hera to rendezvous with both asteroids. After a flyby of Mars, the probe began its arrival phase this past October. It took its first image about 800 miles away, and the first detailed image of Didymos was published in early November.

Now in their vicinity, Hera will study both bodies in depth. Though there’s much we can learn about asteroids by observing them from our planet, nothing can replace close-up exploration, and this will be the first time a binary asteroid system will be explored in detail. One of the mission's goals is to find out how such asteroid pairs form. Didymos is also interesting for its rapid rotation, turning once every 2.3 hours—placing it on the very edge of stability.

But of course, beyond this traditional astronomy research, which could also take place at another set of binary asteroids, Hera will investigate the consequences of the DART mission. This will include studying the impact site. Because there are so many knowns about the impact itself, scientists can also use this opportunity to understand craters more broadly, answering questions such as whether the strength of the material or gravity plays a more decisive role in crater formation.

Hera will also determine the exact mass of Dimorphos, an important detail in assessing the effectiveness of the DART mission as a test of planetary defense technology. The probe also contains a lidar instrument for mapping the asteroid's topography and instruments for investigating the chemical composition of the surface. Additionally, we can expect to see detailed images of the asteroids taken with a resolution of 1 meter per pixel.

The probe is also carrying two miniature, cube-shaped satellites, called “CubeSats” for short, which it will deploy in January 2027. In Earth’s orbit, CubeSats are often used for simple experiments, including student projects, and they can even be stacked together to create larger satellites.

Hera’s CubeSats, named Juventas and Milani, will attempt to land on the surface. Using radar capable of probing up to a depth of 100 meters, Juventas will focus on geophysical exploration of the asteroid Dimorphos. Additionally, a gravimeter on board will measure how the gravity on Dimorphos changes during its orbit due to the parent asteroid, Didymos. Meanwhile, Milani will carry out a detailed investigation of the asteroids’ surface composition and will search for traces of dust and the volatile substances contained within many space rocks.

According to current plans, this main phase of the Hera probe's mission is expected to last until the summer of next year, and findings from this probe will likely only become more relevant, not just as Apophis approaches, but even after it slings back into space. In the year 2000, we knew of only 300 near-Earth asteroids—those that approach Earth or directly cross its orbit. Today, we know of over 43,000.

Torie Bosch is the First Opinion editor at STAT.

To encourage robust, good-faith discussion about issues raised in First Opinion essays, STAT publishes selected Letters to the Editor received in response to them. You can submit a Letter to the Editor here, or find the submission form at the end of any First Opinion essay.

The authors’ proposal hinges on the premise that learning the foundational sciences and learning to become a physician can be separated without compromising either. My first year of medical school was peppered with clinical exposure and taught me otherwise. I floundered with a blood pressure cuff during a wellness visit, fumbled for words as I interviewed a man waiting for a heart transplant, and listened to a patient recount her immense sorrow after multiple miscarriages. These encounters remain among my most formative.

I agree with the authors that students are often preoccupied with medical licensure exams, and that early clinical exposure might not cure this. In my experience, however, it is the strongest counterweight. After meeting those patients, I was learning for more than an exam. The proposed model would abandon that counterweight. Students would still encounter patients eventually, but we would have forfeited valuable time. Becoming a compassionate, humanistic physician is slow work, and it deserves all the time we can offer. A flashcard can teach a student what causes a miscarriage. It takes a patient to teach what it means to grieve one. And that lesson should not wait until after Step 1.

— Surya Pulukuri, Harvard Medical School student

Markovich is correct: The U.S. health care “non-system” is broken. There are many culprits, including hospitals, whose prices are set more by market dominance than by quality. He should also look at the administrative complexity driven largely by our myriad of private insurance companies. Some 25% of U.S. “health care” spending goes to administrative costs. The insurers need to agree on a single set of billing codes and procedures to cut doctors’ and hospitals’ cost of billing. They need to sit down with doctors and come up with a uniform set of procedures needing pre-authorization and a single set of criteria. They need to agree on a small number of policy options that are understandable to the public. Finger-pointing is easy, but should start at home.

— Edward Hoffer, M.D., Harvard Medical School

A chatbot can handle every conversation responsibly and still, over time, take the place of someone a person used to confide in. In their First Opinion essay, Qian Yang, Ned Cooper, and Ibrahim Emara show how easily a gradual loss of human connection can escape safeguards designed for individual conversations. They make a persuasive case for giving independent researchers access to platform data and developing tools to distinguish the effects of chatbot use from other causes of isolation. Those tools should ask whether people could reach someone they trusted when they needed help.

That question is especially worth asking adolescents, who may have fewer ways to seek help privately. Imagine two teenagers who each spend an hour every night talking to a chatbot. One used to call a friend during difficult times, but he called less often as talking to the chatbot became a habit. The other still talks to friends every day. When she wanted to discuss something she feared would get back to her parents, she asked to see a school counselor. While she waited for an appointment, she took the problem to a chatbot. Their chat logs might look very similar. The authors suggest measuring school counselor time as one way to estimate what isolation costs schools. That measure captures time counselors spend helping students. Hours, days, or weeks spent waiting leave no trace.

Researchers could ask teens to think about the last time something was troubling them. Did they talk to someone about it, try to reach someone, or decide against reaching out? If they tried, did they get to talk to someone? Did they talk to a chatbot about it?

Asking these questions regularly could help researchers distinguish a teen who reached out to people less often as their chatbot usage grew from one who turned to a chatbot only after trying to get help. The distinction matters for policymaking. When chatbot use displaces human contact, usage limits may help. When it fills a gap in support, the priority should be making help easier to reach. A usage limit might prompt one teenager to call a friend. Another may still be waiting for someone to call back.

— Brian J. Carter, J.D., Northwestern University Feinberg School of Medicine

In her essay “How bad is healthy supposed to feel?” Kirsten Balin properly notes that medicine has a regrettable history of dismissing symptoms, particularly in women. However, she also says that health professionals tend to give an illness label to the aches, pains, and anxieties of everyday living when the symptoms have no underlying biological explanation. As examples, she lists chronic fatigue syndrome and autonomic dysfunction. In fact, there are tens of thousands of peer-reviewed publications in top scientific journals that document the biological abnormalities in these two illnesses. The same is true with several other similar illnesses such as fibromyalgia and long Covid. It now is clear that they all are “real” illnesses.

— Anthony L. Komaroff, M.D., Harvard Medical School

The authors of the First Opinion commentary on pediatric emergencies from Oct. 27 identify a growing but hidden problem: stark gaps in delivery of pediatric emergency care across the U.S. The solutions they propose — payment reform, expanding regional care coordination hubs, and incentivizing large pediatric medical centers to support smaller hospitals — all merit serious consideration.

However, such approaches risk overlooking existing strengths that we can build upon now to support emergency departments and prehospital agencies aiming to develop their own internal capacity. Pediatric readiness refers to the infrastructure and resources EDs and prehospital agencies need to provide high-quality care for children. In our roles as researchers and physicians, we collaborate in community-engaged research to identify strategies to support EDs and EMS agencies in their readiness.

This is vital: EDs with high pediatric readiness have up to 76% lower mortality for critically ill children. It would cost approximately $200 million to make all EDs nationwide pediatric ready, a tiny fraction of annual U.S. health care expenditures. Many EDs lack designated pediatric champions, knowns as pediatric emergency care coordinators (PECCs), whose presence has been shown to be closely associated with readiness. As the authors rightly point out, larger pediatric medical centers can support smaller hospitals and PECCs.

That’s why we collaborate with local, national, and international partners in readiness improvement strategies such as telehealth support to smaller hospitals, simulation-based systems-testing and medical education, developing PECC networking and mentorship models, and more. This research stems from our experiences in rural, indigenous, and urban hospital systems. This has helped us understand the importance of foregrounding community members’ concerns and supporting their local expertise and leadership in our research efforts.

The authors grounded their commentary in two stories of what pediatricians are seeing nationwide. We agree that stories from families and local providers should drive our research. For us, that means collecting stories about what pediatric emergency readiness should look like, collaborating with community hospitals and families to identify research questions and select appropriate methodologies, and finding sustainable dissemination and implementation strategies.

EDs can start their own readiness journey by participating in the National Pediatric Readiness Project’s national assessment to identify current strengths and opportunities for improvement. As we work toward a future where any child can receive high-quality emergency care when and where they need it, we recommend starting by identifying and building upon strengths already in place.

— Elizabeth Sanseau, M.D., Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP) PolicyLab, CHOP Indigenous Health Unit, and the University of Pennsylvania’s Perelman School of Medicine; and Jeremy Jones, M.D., CHOP PolicyLab

As a pediatric emergency physician and clinical ethicist, I have experienced and witnessed aggression at work many times. The authors’ concern comes from a good place, but it misses the realities of the clinical setting. They write that ineffective signs would be “pointless, but not harmful.” I disagree, for three reasons.

First, the essay overstates the evidence against signage. Its claim rests on two sources: a 2006 survey of Australian nurses and a 2008 commentary on the U.K.’s zero tolerance campaign. Neither evaluates whether signs change behavior. Nor has anyone tested the claim that signs erode patient trust. Research on violence prevention is methodologically weak, though multicomponent programs show mixed but encouraging results.

Second, deterrence is not the only purpose of a sign. Even if the signs prevented nothing, they would matter. The authors acknowledge that violence against health care workers has risen. In emergency departments specifically, it has introduced a mental and physical burden that fundamentally changes what it means to do this work. Signage stating an obvious standard of behavior may seem trivial, but it can have meaning as a piece of a broader institutional commitment to health care worker well-being. It tells staff that their hospital understands the realities of their work and supports them.

Third, signs remind clinicians where their obligations end. Many clinicians, especially trainees, are unsure what they owe a patient or family who is abusing them. A hospital must screen and stabilize anyone who comes to the emergency department, whatever their behavior. Beyond that, no patient or family is entitled to further care from a clinician they are making unsafe. This is easy to forget amid the pressures of clinical care, and a visible reminder helps.

The authors worry about the message these signs send to patients and families. That concern is thoughtful, but it is aimed at the wrong signs. The signs I see in emergency departments name physical assault, threats, and harassment. A sign that polices tone or discourages complaints could deserve criticism, but those signs are the exception and should not set the terms of this debate. Even the authors want their alternative signs to make clear that violence will not be tolerated. They close by asking that the words on hospital walls communicate medicine’s values. Protecting the people who care for patients is one of them.

— Joseph Shapiro, M.D., Children’s National Hospital

ChatGPT shrunk down to run on a Super Nintendo

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Just don't expect complex, coherent, or always correct answers

If you’ve ever wondered how small an instance of ChatGPT could get, wonder no longer. Someone has actually rigged up a tiny version of the chatbot to run on a Super Nintendo cartridge.

Tech journalist and retro tech enthusiast Benj Edwards is responsible for ChatGPT for SNES. He's published it online running in an emulator, complete with SNES-era music and sound effects, and for download as an SNES ROM, too.

It’s not a big model, mind you, and performance is thus pretty limited: According to the website, it’s a 2.2 million-parameter offshoot of OpenAI’s GPT-OSS-120b, which was used to develop its training conversations – so in that sense it truly is a ChatGPT that runs on an SNES cartridge. Edwards described it to The Register as a distilled version of the aforementioned open-weight model.

There are two options built into ChatGPT for SNES: One that’s purely the model, with every word generated coming right from its tiny little silicon brain, and another that includes a fact book culled from the opening sentences of around 150,000 simple English Wikipedia articles. If you opt to run both versions and ask it the same questions, you’ll notice a massive difference.

Given some of the other responses we got while testing, there’s really no good reason to run the base model – memory isn’t really an issue given the basic build is about 4MB and the factbook build is just 19MB.

Edwards admits that it’s not the most helpful AI model, either – this is just an experiment after all.

“It is not a useful assistant: the model is millions of times smaller than the models behind ChatGPT, knows about 8,000 words, and often gets things wrong, sometimes confidently,” Edwards noted in his writeup. In that sense, it really is vintage AI on vintage hardware. “The fact book can carry mistakes too.”

If you have an SNES lying around (lucky you), you can download the ROM from this link. If you want to get it running on an SNES you’ll need a cartridge that allows running of custom ROMs, and that supports Nintendo’s Super FX graphics coprocessor. Cartridges like the FXPak Pro and SD2SNES support it. The factbook version also requires support for the aftermarket Media Streaming Unit 1 (MSU-1), which adds CD-ROM like features to the SNES. MSU-1 is available in both of the flash carts mentioned above.

If you have some real hardware skills, however, you could technically make your own ChatGPT cartridge.

“The ‘pure’ version, which generates all responses from the AI language model, could run from a real custom SNES cart,” Edwards told us – it doesn’t even actually need the Super FX chip. “I do have a version of ChatGPT for SNES that can run without the Super FX chip, but without the extra processing power the answers are not as good.”

That said, Edwards believes its absolutely crazy and nonsensical answers are part of the fun.

“ChatGPT for SNES can definitely make mistakes too, but it's almost cute when it does it because of its limited knowledge and its playful interface,” Edwards told us.

While a human may have designed the experiment, the coding work was done by AI, Edwards told us – he’s been interested in it since the 1990s and is pursuing more experiments with tiny AI models using the help of the bigger ones.

“I've been theorizing about what I call ‘tiny talking machines’ … over the past year,” Edwards told us, and he’s been able to use coding agents to turn those ideas into actual software. ChatGPT for SNES is one example, and he also created an NES ROM called Séance that uses an even smaller AI model able to run on an original Nintendo Entertainment System that simulates talking to a ghost in a haunted game cartridge.

“I designed the scope of what the software should be, how it should work broadly, what chip it should use, how the interface should look and work, and the sounds it makes when it types responses,” Edwards told us. He added that he sees the project as an AI/human collaboration, which is where he believes AI works best.

“The AI agents who did the coding for this project currently advise that we've hit the limit of how good the AI model can be on the limited hardware,” Edwards said - he’s not taking their word for it, though. “It's up to human creativity to transcend those assumptions. I have already come up with new ideas that I believe can make a tiny model like ChatGPT for SNES even more coherent, and I'm working on them now.” ®

The specialty insurer and holding company operates a model that is not unlike Berkshire Hathaway (BRKB -0.36%). In fact, some have referred to it as a baby Berkshire Hathaway. Like Berkshire, Markel is mainly an insurance company that uses its float — the premiums it receives before paying out claims — to invest in a portfolio of stocks and privately owned businesses.

Markel has also not been as successful as Berkshire Hathaway, at least not in recent years. It has averaged a 9.2% annualized return during the past five years compared to Berkshire's 15.7% average return. During the past 10 years, Markel stock has posted an average return of 8.5% to 14.1% for Berkshire.

But like Berkshire, there is a lot to like about Markel.

You can't gauge Markel's quarter by looking at traditional earnings based on generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP) because the unrealized investment gains and losses from its portfolio are included. Those unrealized gains or losses distort the actual performance of the company.

But if you look at its adjusted operating income, that shows how much the company made on the businesses it owns. In Q3, its adjusted operating income rose 14% from a year ago to $707 million, or $37.51 per share. That soundly beat analysts' estimates of $27.76 per share. Its revenue jumped 6% year-over-year to $4.16 billion, which easily beat estimates of $3.78 billion.

Within insurance, Markel saw its adjusted underwriting gross premium volume increase by 9% for the quarter. This excludes its global reinsurance division, which it exited in the past year, and its specialty car insurance business Hagerty, which transitioned to a fronting arrangement. Also, its net investment income increased 5% to $233 million.

The key result in insurance is the combined ratio, which measures how much the company makes in profits from premiums after claims are paid out and operating expenses are subtracted. Markel had a combined ratio of 92%, down from 93% a year ago.

For the full year, the combined ratio is 92%, down from 95% in 2026. This is a good thing because a combined ratio of more than 100 means you are paying out more than you are taking in. A ratio below 100 means you are making more than you are paying out. So, a combined ratio of 92% means Markel is making more profit per dollar of premiums than it was last year.

Markel's businesses, known as Markel Ventures, also performed well. Its industrial holdings saw operating income rise 29% year-over-year to $131 million, while its financial segment increased 30% to $80 million and its consumer properties rose 80% to $31 million.

Markel doesn't provide short-term projections, but its trends within insurance and its holdings are promising. The company is built to perform well in negative markets, which is a big reason the shares surged in recent weeks.

When investors make a flight to safety, they head for companies like Markel, which is grounded by its insurance holdings and conservative underwriting and its durable, value-oriented investments with low debt and stable earnings.

The stock is also fairly cheap right now, trading at less than 14 times earnings. I like it as a long-term buy, and it's particularly valuable now during bouts of market volatility.

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google has handed school districts what many have called an ultimatum: Allow students to see AI summaries in their Google searches, or give up Google Search on school accounts altogether.

In August, Google switched on a Gemini tab in Google Classroom by default for K-12 students whose schools had already allowed its AI chatbot, Gemini. On Nov. 3, it reversed course, announcing that its AI tools will be off for K-12 student accounts unless school administrators turn them on, starting early next year. But Google also made clear that schools that want students to have Google Search will have to accept AI Overviews, the AI-generated summaries that have become a familiar sight at the top of search results.

“Search and AI are deeply integrated,” Chris Phillips, vice president for education at Google, told The New York Times, adding that students at schools that don’t allow AI “will not have access to search, and they’ll see a message from their school that indicates it’s not available.”

Many school districts have restricted or banned student AI use, citing safety and privacy concerns. They may not have expected those restrictions to cost them the world’s most popular search engine, a trade-off that has left edtech experts questioning Google’s rationale.

Google’s decision comes as tech companies face mounting legal pressure over harm to children. In March, a Los Angeles jury found Meta and Google’s YouTube negligent and determined they failed to warn users about the dangers of their platforms. In January, Google and Character.AI, a chatbot company with ties to Google, agreed to settle lawsuits from families who said their children were harmed by its chatbots.

And earlier this month, a family sued Google, alleging their 13-year-old daughter used Gemini on her school-issued Chromebook to plan her suicide and that Google pushed its AI products on students without warning parents or schools.

“We hear parents and teachers’ concerns about students using AI to shortcut learning, and we share the same goal: help students learn,” a Google spokesperson said, adding that the company is piloting new Gemini features to support “productive struggle” by helping students “work through problems without skipping straight to answers.”

Whether that’s enough to win over districts that have restricted AI is another question. The country’s four largest school districts have landed in different places, both on AI and on Google’s decision to tie it to Search.

Alphabet, Google’s parent company, did not respond to Fortune‘s request for comment.

Chicago Public Schools has pushed back the hardest. On Dec. 22, 2025, the district began blocking students from AI tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and other third-party AI products. Students may use only AI-enhanced edtech tools vetted through the district’s compliance process under SOPPA, Illinois’ student data privacy law.

On Nov. 11, about 30 protesters gathered outside Google’s West Loop office, chanting, “Hey Google, nice try. We don’t want your Gemini.”

In a statement to the Chicago Sun-Times, a CPS spokesperson said the district is “actively exploring all options to independently suppress” the AI Overviews in Google searches.

“The District would like to continue to provide students with access to the Google search engine without the AI component. If such an arrangement can’t be reached, the District will look to other search solutions,” the spokesperson said.

New York City Public Schools, the nation’s largest district, appears headed in a similar direction, though it has yet to decide. On Sept. 2, Mayor Zohran Mamdani and Schools Chancellor Kamar Samuels announced a one-year moratorium on student-facing AI for 2-K through eighth grade. They also banned companion chatbots in all grades.

After Google’s announcement, Mamdani posted on X that “Google is trying to use its monopoly power to force AI into our public schools. No corporation should have the power to dictate how we teach our children.”

City Council Speaker Julie Menin and Council Member Shekar Krishnan sent a letter to Google CEO Sundar Pichai asking whether Google would let students keep using Google Search without AI Overviews and, if not, why not, given Google’s record of restricting product features for young users, as it does with YouTube Kids. The stakes are large: The district has about 1.3 million Chromebooks, including 500,000 assigned to students, according to the letter.

District spokesperson Nicole Brownstein told Gothamist that officials are “reaching out to Google to discuss this update further,” and that the district had already disabled the Gemini chatbot and AI Mode on student Chromebooks. If Google doesn’t budge, the nation’s largest school district will have to choose between softening its stance and finding another search engine.

Los Angeles Unified, the nation’s second-largest district, announced on Sept. 2 that it would block generative AI for students in all grades on district-issued devices for the 2026-27 school year, a move affecting an estimated 378,000 students.

During public comment at a district Generative AI Ad Hoc Committee meeting after Google’s announcement, Hillary Power, a parent and member of Schools Beyond Screens, called for Los Angeles Unified to continue “formalizing, extending and expanding the current T-K to 12 moratorium of student use of generative AI.”

“Google has been particularly clear on their priorities when several days ago they issued a monopolistic and, frankly, pathetic ultimatum to schools to use their AI search products or be cut off,” she continued.

The district has asked Google for a way to turn off the AI summaries, according to LAist, and said it is reviewing Google’s announcement and its “potential implications for schools, educators and students.”

Miami-Dade County Public Schools, the nation’s third-largest district, may be headed in a different direction.

In 2023, the district blocked AI chatbots such as ChatGPT over concerns about cheating and misinformation, according to The New York Times. Two years later, it reversed course, offering Gemini for Education to about 100,000 high school students, the largest AI deployment in a U.S. school district at the time. In February, the district announced a continued collaboration with Google to give all its high school students free access to full-length SAT practice exams through Gemini.

But on Sept. 16, the Florida Board of Education voted to require public school districts and charter schools to set rules for AI use. Schools will also have to tell parents and guardians which AI tools students will interact with, so families can opt their child into a tool or request a non-AI alternative. Districts have until July 1, 2027, to comply.

Miami-Dade County Public Schools did not respond to questions about how it will adapt to Google’s policy and the new Florida rules.

Former Federal Trade Commission chair Lina Khan criticized Google’s decision to tie Search to its AI summaries. In a Nov. 3 post on X, she said the company is using its search monopoly to “coerce schools into adopting AI.”

“Multiple courts have found Google liable for violating antitrust laws, and this latest move shows it remains unchastened,” she said. “As the public increasingly pushes back against the use of AI in schools, Google is making it clear it will punish those who don’t fall in line.”

Khan isn’t the only one questioning whether the move is legal. Zephyr Teachout, a professor at Fordham Law School, said she does not find Google’s claim that it cannot unlink AI Overviews from Search “credible,” and that the company could be found to violate antitrust laws governing how companies tie the sale of two products.

In antitrust law, “tying” is when a company with significant market power requires customers who want one of its products to take another as well. Google’s parent, Alphabet, is No. 5 on the 2026 Fortune 500. According to the FTC, tying raises antitrust questions when it “restricts competition without providing benefits to consumers.”

“Google ran search for decades without AI, has filters that allow people to see results without AI summaries, and has been inconsistent in how it has responded to the tying claim — merely asserting they are ‘deeply integrated’ is not a legal argument,” Teachout said. “The company has on/off controls for K-12 administrators and could have an on/off control for search and AI.”

Google has lost a tying case before. In April 2025, U.S. District Judge Leonie Brinkema found that Google had been “willfully acquiring and maintaining monopoly power” in two parts of the online advertising market and that it had illegally tied them together by pushing websites that wanted full access to its ad exchange to also use its ad server.

But the company has also won a case involving AI Overviews. In September, U.S. District Judge Amit Mehta dismissed a lawsuit by Penske Media over Google’s use of its content for AI Overviews, finding that Penske had not plausibly shown that Search and AI Overviews were separate products.

Teachout said schools are different, pointing to the “clear evidence of a separate market demand in schools for search as distinct from AI.”

Whether a court ever takes up such a case, or agrees with Teachout, is an open question. Google’s changes take effect early next year, and until then, districts have to decide whether keeping Google Search is worth accepting the AI that comes with it.

Airline Captain Defends 20-Year-Old Female Copilot After Fatal Learjet Crash

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

121pilot, our resident commercial airline pilot on Live And Let’s Fly, examines what we know about the crash of Learjet 60 N722BD and pushes back against the rush to blame its young first officer, Nathalia Filgueiras.

The crash of N722BD, a Learjet 60 operating on a charter flight, has drawn enormous attention. Much of that is understandable. We have extremely dramatic video of the aircraft descending almost vertically at a very high rate of speed, along with equally dramatic ATC audio recordings.

As is typical of the aviation YouTube community, many of whom I respect, people have been rushing to get videos out about this accident. What I really don’t care for, however, are the clickbait titles. Titles like “Could Any Pilot Have Saved This Learjet 60?” or “Lear 60 Florida Crash: It Happened Before” or “Expert Pilot Explains What Likely Caused SHOCKING Plane Crash in Florida” by Megyn Kelly.

These kinds of titles make me angry because, regardless of the actual content of the videos, they are misleading. The simple fact is that we know very little about this accident at this point, and certainly not enough to be speculating about a probable cause.

The aircraft, a Learjet Model 60 with nine people aboard, reported moderate turbulence and icing at Flight Level 350 and requested a climb to Flight Level 370. Moments later, the aircraft departed controlled flight, eventually entering a descent at an extraordinarily high rate of speed.

We know that the NTSB has recovered components of the horizontal stabilizer that separated from the aircraft, and widely circulated video appears to show the aircraft without this crucial component.

What we DON’T know is whether the aircraft flew into a thunderstorm. The weather imagery I’ve seen to date comes from sources that have time lags built into them and don’t necessarily represent the real-time weather picture. We don’t know what caused the horizontal stabilizer to separate, or even when that happened. Did it separate during the dive, or was its failure what initiated the dive? That’s an important distinction, and right now it’s an unknown.

We know the Learjet 60’s wing is sensitive to ice accumulation and that the aircraft is susceptible to a phenomenon called Mach tuck. We don’t know whether either played any role in this accident, or even whether the aircraft’s critical surfaces had accumulated ice.

We know the aircraft had been operating at higher altitudes, with a reported descent from Flight Level 410 to 350 before the final sequence of events. But without knowing the aircraft’s speed, configuration, weight, and other performance parameters, we cannot draw meaningful conclusions about its operating margins.

We know the captain was 55 years old and very experienced. We know the first officer was 20 years old and relatively inexperienced, but fully qualified to act as a second in command.

We know the CVR, or cockpit voice recorder, has been recovered, but we don’t yet know whether its contents can be successfully recovered or what information it might provide. We also know the aircraft was not equipped with a flight data recorder, or FDR. That means information about the aircraft’s speed, heading, pitch, roll, system status, and other parameters may remain unknown or will have to be reconstructed using external data sources and whatever information can be gleaned from the CVR.

But perhaps the most disturbing part of this entire event, aside from the tragic loss of life, has been the reaction of so many people who couldn’t explain the difference between a slat and a flap, yet have felt perfectly qualified to judge the performance of the first officer based on her age and gender.

In the Megyn Kelly video, she questions whether someone who isn’t even old enough to drink should have been acting as a pilot.

I think the better question is why we allow 18-year-olds to join the military and go into combat, but apparently don’t think they’re old enough to have a beer. Why can a 20-year-old warrant officer fly a Black Hawk full of troops into the maelstrom of a hot landing zone, but we think he or she can’t be trusted with alcohol?

Even more disgusting have been some of the reactions to the ATC recordings.

First, let me suggest that if you haven’t already listened to them, don’t. Second, and most importantly, as a 56-year-old male airline captain, I hear absolutely nothing in that recording, either in her voice or in her actions, that merits criticism.

I don’t think people who haven’t experienced it can appreciate just how incredibly violent and terrifying that initial descent must have been. In one recording, we go from a seemingly calm situation, with the crew requesting a climb, to a loss of control and a reported descent of approximately 8,000 feet per minute in about 10 seconds. Just 16 seconds later, the aircraft is in an overspeed condition, and the first officer reports a descent rate of 20,000 feet per minute. That’s an absolutely eye-watering number. It defies belief and would be utterly terrifying to any pilot. About 10 seconds later, she reports that they are going to crash into the ground. Not long after that, someone can be heard in the background reciting the Lord’s Prayer.

I don’t know this for certain, of course, but my suspicion is that somewhere during this sequence the crew became aware that the horizontal stabilizer had separated and that they had lost the ability to recover the aircraft.

If there is anything to be said about her performance as a pilot, it’s that she was clearly doing her job right up to the end.

We teach Aviate, Navigate, Communicate. Aviate means flying the airplane. Navigate means determining where you need to go and how to get there. Communicate means keeping the appropriate people informed.

In this situation, the captain was presumably concentrating on flying the aircraft while the first officer was communicating with ATC. That is exactly what we expect from a two-person crew in an emergency, assuming those were their respective roles at the time.

What’s more, in the absence of an FDR, the information she provided to ATC may prove absolutely crucial to understanding what happened. We may eventually discover that her actions and communications were instrumental in allowing the NTSB to piece together this accident. That information could well save lives in the future.

I also find it interesting, and troubling, that contrary to the media’s usual fixation on the captain, as though he’s flying the airplane solo, the exact opposite has happened here. The first officer has become the focus of an extraordinary amount of attention, even though we have not established who was flying the aircraft.

It’s been my absolute privilege to fly with a number of female pilots throughout my career, and not one of them has ever done anything less than a sterling job. Frankly, the airplane doesn’t care about your gender or the color of your skin. And when it comes to aviation, that’s exactly how it should be. We should care about qualifications and competence. That’s it.

If I step onto my jet tomorrow and find a young female first officer waiting for me, I’ll be just as happy to have her there as I would a young male. At this point in my career, flying with a young and relatively inexperienced first officer gives me the opportunity to act as a mentor and pass along some hard-won flying wisdom. That’s something I genuinely enjoy. Nathalia had earned her place in that cockpit. Not one iota of the solid evidence we have right now leads me to believe that this accident can or should be blamed on her.

It may well turn out that crew errors figure prominently in the probable cause. We simply don’t know yet. But if that turns out to be the case, it’s important to remember that the captain was the pilot in command. He was responsible for the operation of that aircraft, and the first officer was serving under his command. If poor decision-making put the aircraft into a situation from which there was no escape, the captain would bear the primary responsibility for those decisions.

But that’s a hypothetical. And right now, we don’t know that any such decisions were made.

We owe the nine people who lost their lives, their families, and the people who will eventually investigate this accident something better than speculation, sensationalism, and a rush to judgment.

Liverpool captain Virgil van Dijk remains uncertain of whether he will remain at the club beyond the end of the season.

Social Security's Trump Bump-Led 2027 COLA Is the 5th Largest in 35 Years -- but There's a Dark Side to This Outsize Increase as Well

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Though Social Security checks are about to get bigger, the projected timeline for sweeping benefit cuts may have accelerated.

By Sean Williams – Nov 21, 2026 at 6:36AM EST

For Social Security’s nearly 72 million traditional beneficiaries -- retired workers, workers with disabilities, and survivors of deceased workers -- there are few, if any, announcements more anticipated than the annual cost-of-living adjustment (COLA).

Social Security's COLA is the near-annual "raise" given to beneficiaries to account for inflation (i.e., rising prices). If the price for the goods and services that seniors regularly purchase kept climbing without an adjustment in benefits, the buying power of Social Security income would steadily decline over time.

Social Security's announced 2027 COLA offers something of a mixed bag for recipients. On the one hand, it's set to deliver one of the largest raises over the last 35 years. This larger payout comes courtesy of President Donald Trump and a handful of his policies.

President Trump's policies offer a good-news/bad-news scenario for Social Security. Image source: Official White House Photo by Molly Riley.

But this outsize Trump bump isn't without its consequences. Larger payouts in the new year can make the program's precarious financial outlook that much worse.

On Oct. 14, the Social Security Administration announced that monthly payouts would increase by 3.6% in 2027. For the average retired-worker beneficiary, their monthly payout is expected to climb by $75. Meanwhile, the typical worker with a disability can expect their monthly Social Security check to rise by $59 in the new year.

A 3.6% raise is well above the norm. It marks a tie for the fifth-largest percentage increase over the last 35 years, surpassed only by COLAs of 4.1% (2006), 5.8% (2009), 5.9% (2022), and 8.7% (2023).

It's also the sixth consecutive year in which recipients have received at least a 2.5% cost-of-living adjustment. It's been 30 years (1988-1997) since benefits rose by at least 2.5% for six straight years.

While modest inflation associated with a growing U.S. economy results in a Social Security raise most years, next year's outsize COLA was pumped up by two of President Donald Trump's policies.

New from the New York Fed: "By February 2026, tariffs had contributed 2.9 pp to goods price inflation, and without them goods prices would have fallen slightly." About one quarter of every point in higher tariff rates shows up in consumer prices within one year. • A 10% across-the-board tariff yields a 2.6% rise in consumer goods prices after one year. • About two thirds of the increase comes from pricier imports, which get passed through quickly. • Another third comes from U.S. made goods, as producers face higher input costs and less import competition. This takes longer, about 6-12 months, to filter through.

To begin with, each of the last two Social Security COLAs has been influenced by Trump's tariff and trade policy. Adding duties to imports directly raises their price and can increase manufacturing costs for domestic producers.

A recently published report by three New York Federal Reserve economists writing for Liberty Street Economics ("How Fast Do Tariffs Pass Through into Consumer Prices?") found that Trump's 2025 tariffs increased goods price inflation by an estimated 2.9 percentage points by February 2026. Without tariffs, the NY Fed economists estimate that goods prices would have otherwise fallen.

The other major policy contributing to Social Security's 2027 Trump bump is the Iran war. Closing the Strait of Hormuz has resulted in the largest energy supply disruption in modern history, sending gas and diesel prices notably higher.

With Trumpflation (inflation driven by Trump's policies) becoming entrenched in the U.S. economy, it's quite possible that these policies provide a third consecutive year with a Trump bump in 2028.

Unfortunately, outsize Social Security COLAs come at a potentially steep cost to America's leading retirement program.

Every year since 1985, the Social Security Board of Trustees Report has warned of a long-term unfunded obligation. In simpler terms, the projected income to be collected in the 75 years following the release of a report is expected to be insufficient to cover outlays (primarily benefits, but also the administrative costs to oversee Social Security). As of 2026, this long-term unfunded obligation had ballooned to $29.3 trillion.

However, this isn't the most pressing concern for the program or its nearly 72 million traditional beneficiaries.

According to the latest Trustees Report, the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance trust fund (OASI) is forecast to exhaust its asset reserves by the fourth quarter of 2032. The OASI's asset reserves represent the excess income collected since inception that is invested in special-issue, interest-bearing government bonds, as required by law.

US Old-Age and Survivors Insurance Trust Fund Assets at End of Year data by YCharts.

The positive news is that Social Security isn't going bankrupt and is in no danger of halting benefits. More than 91% of the program's income is collected through the 12.4% payroll tax on earned income (wages and salaries, but not investment income). As long as Americans keep working, the payroll tax will continue generating income for disbursement to eligible beneficiaries... even if the OASI's asset reserves are depleted.

But just because Social Security isn't going bankrupt, it doesn't mean everything is peachy. If the OASI's asset reserves run dry over the next six years, as predicted, it would indicate that the current payout schedule, inclusive of annual COLAs, isn't sustainable. Sweeping benefit cuts of up to 22% may be necessary for retired workers and survivors of deceased workers.

The concern is that Social Security's Trump bump may have materially altered the trajectory to the OASI's asset reserve insolvency.

When the Trustees Report issues its short- and long-term outlooks, it takes into account a long list of variables, including birth rates, death rates, net migration rates, and even annual COLAs. The Trustees assume that annual COLAs will be modest -- but as we just saw, a 3.6% annual raise is anything but average.

Social Security's outsize 2027 raise risks draining the OASI even faster than initially forecast.

President Trump's "Big, Beautiful Bill" already moved up the projected OASI asset reserve insolvency timeline by one quarter (from the first quarter of 2033 to the fourth quarter of 2032). It's entirely possible the 2027 Trump bump further accelerates the timeline to sweeping benefit cuts.

Millions could lose paid benefits due to Congress ignoring decades of Social Security warnings: experts

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Andrew Biggs, a senior fellow at American Enterprise Institute, and Romina Boccia, Cato Institute’s director of budget and entitlement policy, warned that Congress’ 50 years of action have led Social Security to the cliff of insolvency.

A panel filled with top economists, including a Nobel Peace Prize winner, warned Congress decades ago that their reforms to Social Security could spell disaster.

Now, experts tell Fox News Digital that group from a bygone era was right, as the safety-net program hurtles toward a crisis that could affect millions.

"Social Security benefits were put on autopilot back in 1977," Andrew Biggs, a senior fellow of the American Enterprise Institute, told Fox News Digital. "Since then, Congress has done as little as it possibly could."

Senate Budget Committee Chair Ron Johnson, R-Wis., unveiled a report on the decades-old program that blasted it as a "Ponzi scheme," and warned that Social Security’s slide into insolvency began in the 1970s, when, despite warnings, lawmakers opted to tie benefit growth to wages rather than inflation.

AMERICANS MISLED ON SOCIAL SECURITY FOR DECADES AS CLOCK COUNTS DOWN ON BENEFITS, EXPLOSIVE REPORT REVEALS

President Jimmy Carter shakes hands with Louisiana Sen. Russell Long after signing the Social Security bill at the White House in Washington on Dec. 20, 1977. (Getty Archive)

Johnson wrote in his report that at the time, an expert panel was formed that warned lawmakers that if they were to tie benefits to inflation, "Social Security would likely be sustainable today."

Still, lawmakers at the time ran with their own amendments to the program, which were signed into law by former President Jimmy Carter in December 1977.

"What they said in 1977 was if you index benefit growth to wages, you're effectively building a solvency crisis into the program," Biggs said. "You are over-promising, you're going to build in benefits that the current system can't afford to pay, and you're committing future taxpayers to pay rates of taxes that the taxpayers in 1977 weren't willing to pay."

In the decades since, Congress has done little to change the program.

Romina Boccia, the Cato Institute’s director of budget and entitlement policy, told Fox News Digital that’s largely because lawmakers have not had the political will to do it.

She agreed with Johnson that Social Security was effectively a "Ponzi scheme," and said "there's this misconception that a lot of Americans think that Social Security is a retirement savings program."

"But for that, you would actually need to have savings occur in the system, and there would need to be assets that would be invested in earning returns," Boccia said. "That's not the case. We're talking about a pay-as-you-go program."

One of the side effects of Congress’ lack of action to rein in the program is that the wage growth formula relies on workers in the economy contributing with their paychecks.

Boccia noted that as the decades have gone on, "We've seen a severe decline in the number of workers compared to the number of retirees collecting benefits as a result of a decline in fertility."

Sen. Ron Johnson, R-Wis., makes his way to votes in the U.S. Capitol on Sept. 30, 2026. (CQ-Roll Call, Inc. via Getty Images)

Congress’ last real successful effort to reform Social Security came in the early 1980s, Biggs noted, when lawmakers were forced to step in to deal with the "funding crisis" hitting the program.

"They raised taxes. They increased the retirement age. So they did this with months to spare to fix Social Security," Biggs said. "It's not something they wanted to do, but they were forced to do."

"Now in 2032, we're going to face the same situation, where Congress will be forced to take action sometime before the trust fund runs out in 2032, not because they want to, but because they're forced to do it," he continued.

If Social Security’s trust fund runs dry by 2032, Americans’ benefits would be slashed and the onus of paying for the shortfall would add trillions more to the nation’s debt.

But Johnson, in his report, declared that the Social Security problem could not be fixed between now and then because of Congress’ inaction for the last several decades, and he suggested that the solution would be to repeal a possible benefits reduction as the program’s trust fund heads toward insolvency in just under seven years.

Biggs, who was part of former President George W. Bush’s effort to reform Social Security over two decades ago, said that had Congress taken action "25 years ago, you could fix Social Security in barely noticeable ways."

"There is no easy fix to Social Security today, and that stems entirely from the fact that every year, when Congress has had the opportunity to fix Social Security, they chose to duck the issue for political reasons and kick the can down the road," Biggs said.

Researcher Tommy He built tufazip, and the full package, at 105.39MB after optimization, includes a small, 4.33MB pre-trained starter model — stored at low precision, or quantized, to save space — that’s needed to unpack the file. The full model is a 12-layer Transformer, which means there are 12 stages for each guess to go through. The starter stores just two already-trained stages, copying them back and forth to fill all 12 once the full model spins up in memory; each copy keeps learning on its own.

This doesn’t work like a traditional chatbot or cloud AI but is, rather, a self-contained language model that learns on the fly. Notable here is that this compression is lossless, which means there’s no risk of any data being lost in the process. This means that predictor updates must be “fully deterministic.”

Training happens in two stages. The starter, a 34-million-parameter model, is trained offline, before any compression begins. Then, the full model begins to learn online, by predicting each token and training on it, with the unpacking side repeating the same steps, with the model itself being rebuilt alongside the text. For this reason, the total size must include, besides the compressed text, only the unpacking program and the files it needs, including the starter, but not the full trained model.

There is no traditional, hand-built compression model that assists, setting it apart, Tufa Labs says, from all 12 entries that now rank above it on the benchmark, which mix a Transformer with hand-built models. Its first, online-only version ranked second when submitted in early September; the 105.39MB version was 13th as of the benchmark’s Nov. 19 update. Emphasis should be placed on this being a text-only benchmark, for those imagining media files.

While this sounds more exciting than, say, WinRAR, it’s for research purposes and not a product, and that’s underlined by what you’d need to actually run it. Currently, two Nvidia B200 GPUs — plus two Intel Xeon Platinum 8570 CPUs in the test machine — would need 631,548 seconds to compress and 645,748 seconds to decompress the 105.39MB version, or about 7.3 and 7.5 days, respectively, not counting the starter’s offline training.

‘Project Hail Mary’ Almost Had a Completely Different Ending for Rocky

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In a historic move, actor and puppeteer James Ortiz is eligible for a Best Supporting Actor nomination at the 99th Academy Awards for his standout performance on Project Hail Mary. He’s the mind and movement behind the blockbuster’s lovable alien creature, Rocky, who led a team of puppeteers on set, essentially performing “Waiting for Godot in space” opposite Ryan Gosling with take after take of improvisation.

From filmmakers Phil Lord and Chris Miller, Project Hail Mary took audiences out of this world in an Andy Weir adaptation, penned by Drew Goddard, that wasn’t just visually stunning, but was a surprisingly moving two-hander between a man way in over his head and a creature made of rock, both on a dangerous mission to save their home planets.

At this year’s SCAD Savannah Film Festival, Collider’s Steve Weintraub had the pleasure of moderating an in-depth Q&A with Ortiz to discuss the artistry of bringing one of 2026’s most memorable characters to life. Alongside Gosling’s Ryland Grace, Rocky becomes Project Hail Mary’s beating heart, and during this conversation, Ortiz shares the behind-the-scenes work it took to bring them from the page to the screen. We learn all about the practical puppetry, the limitations that allowed their creativity to soar, how Rocky’s most iconic moments and quips came to be. Don't miss the full Q&A in the video above, with a transcript provided below.

COLLIDER: Let's talk a little bit about your background and how you got involved in the project.

JAMES ORTIZ: So I was cast as an actor a thousand years ago by the casting director, Jeanne McCarthy. You may have seen her name at the end of this film. I was cast in a pilot that no one ever saw because it never aired. It was for a network that we don't have to mention.

It was a great experience, but shortly after that, in New York, where I'm based, I was developing this sort of side career that turned into my full career, which is puppeteering and designing puppets for the theater. It kind of became this funny opportunity for me to play characters against my type. I could play things that I would never, ever be cast as because not every day you need, like, I don't know, a gay mad scientist. So, sometimes you need other flavors, and it was so exciting to be able to do that.

So Jeanne remembered that I existed, and she would bring me in for stuff, but oftentimes I would go, "Oh, I can't come in because I have this puppet thing." And she would go, "Puppets?" And long story short, she gave me a phone call. She told me about this story, and I went, "This sounds incredible, but do they really mean a puppet? Do they mean that?" And I had a great meeting with Phil [Lord] and Chris [Miller]. It was love at first sight. We absolutely adore each other. And then we were off to the races, and I was on set trying to make a faceless five-legged creature lovable.

If Rocky could watch Project Hail Mary, what would he complain the filmmakers got wrong about him?

ORTIZ: I think Rocky doesn't think that they're funny, so I think Rocky would hear everybody laughing and go, "Not funny, not funny." I think that'd probably be the biggest thing.

If you could sit down with Jim Henson, what would you want to talk about?

ORTIZ: I don't know if I would say anything. I would just sort of sit there like a loser. I've been really fortunate; I've met a lot of his children because they run different puppet organizations all over the country, and it's been really, really special. I did a show called The Woodsman a thousand years ago in New York, and it was really, really special. Cheryl Henson moderated a talk-back very much like this, and she told me very effusively how much her father loved the Oz stories, and it was really meaningful to hear that.

How many voicemails have you recorded for friends and family as Rocky?

ORTIZ: The limit does not exist. I couldn't even begin to give you a number.

How do you feel about doing the voice in public, like in moments like this?

ORTIZ: Yeah. What are we trying to communicate?

I'm sure there are some people in the audience that would like a voicemail from Rocky.

ORTIZ: I think what it would probably be would be, "You reach voicemail of this human. Make no sense. Leave message after beep. Beep now. It's time for a beep. Where is beep, question? Make no sense. Make no sense. Uh, uh, uh." And then it gets cut off, because I don't think Rocky fully understands what's happening.

When was the first time you said, "Fist my bump," and did you realize what it was going to become?

ORTIZ: I had no idea. We worked on the movie. We worked so hard, and I left, and I had such a great experience. And then I really had no sense that anybody would like it, frankly. That's maybe a failing of mine, but I've been around long enough to know that even if everybody that worked on it did everything right, it doesn't always translate. It doesn't always land, and the fact that it has is pretty, pretty wild. So, I didn't really think that far ahead, to be honest.

Frankly, also, I was only meant to be the person on set to help Ryan [Gosling], to help the DP. I was the steward of the character. I was just borrowing Rocky. And the fact that they lent him fully to me towards the end is still something I'm going to be processing for the rest of my life.

I'm going to dig down a little bit, but do you remember when you first said, "Fist my bump?"

ORTIZ: It had to have been in one of the scenes. It probably was in an improv that we were just kind of going. I was working so hard on just trying to make Rocky as truthful as possible that sometimes I would forget that there were catchphrases I had to hit. So most of the way that we were shooting is that Phil and Chris had an earpiece in my ear, and they could offer thoughts in between, in takes. So sometimes they would go, "Say fist my bump," and you're like, "Right. ‘Amaze, amaze, amaze,’” or whatever, right? So I can't actually tell you when I did it, but I know it would've been at some point then.

You said you and Ryan would sometimes improvise for incredibly long stretches. What's something Ryan did or said as Grace that completely caught you off guard, and how did you respond as Rocky?

ORTIZ: [Laughs] The only thing that's coming to mind right now is because when we shot it, remember that meme that went around three years ago that was “very demure?” There was one day where we were just going and riffing and riffing and going, and at some point, we were talking about Rocky or something, and he went, "It's very demure." And you saw him in real-time go, "No,” and try to backpedal. And in real-time, I think I went, "What is demure, question?"

We just kind of would go. We would just waste camera time.

We're at SCAD, and there are a lot of students in the audience, and I am 100% sure there are a few people out there that would love to do what you do. What advice would you like to share? And part two is, what advice were you given that was absolute horseshit?

ORTIZ: I may have to curse to answer this question, too. I was told by an acting professor a long time ago, "This is one motherfucker of a business." And, yes, it's hard, but I don't think that means it's bad. I think the most rewarding things have been the things that have been hard-won. That's what I would say.

But I would also say about the first question, if you want to play characters that are not easy to define, act more. Act all the time. Try to get opportunities to play characters that you wouldn't be cast as. Read roles that you're like, "I don't think this is for me. Let's try." And also, I think puppetry is an inherently physical art form, so I would also say invest in using your body to play the character.

Something that I've been saying endlessly all over this process has been saying that Rocky's movement is actually Rocky's subtext. The thing that Rocky is feeling is actually what is coming out in his body. The thing that he's saying is the best the translator can manage. So, that was something that I was always tracking: What is Rocky feeling at any given moment? Even though I don't know if Rocky has a deep sense of that themself. They don't. And over the course of the story, we get to see that Rocky actually learns what feeling is through Grace. So, that was another whole evolution.

But I guess the answer is movement theater, playing wacky parts, and letting your imagination not be limited.

I would also say, just from my perspective, I don't think people realize you really have to work your ass off to do something. You've got to give almost everything of yourself to something that you love to be successful.

ORTIZ: Yeah. I think that's completely right. I knew when I got the job that it wasn't going to be me at the end, but what does that matter? My job was to be of service. My job was to make the film work. My job was to make this character that is totally untenable — five legs, no face, speaks in this way, comes from a culture that is unimaginable — and I just went, "I want to always deliver something that feels consistent, that feels alive, that feels surprising, and I want to help Greig Fraser, who's shooting this movie, who has to emotionally find where the camera should go, and emotionally help Ryan in these long scenes where he has so much to do." Can you imagine him trying to do this to a tennis ball? It would've been a nightmare.

So, it was always the objective of just going all the way, and I think I have a “too much” gene as it is, so I couldn't help it.

Was there a moment during filming when you realized Rocky had stopped being a puppet and had become a real character in everyone's eyes?

ORTIZ: Everybody was talking to Rocky and not me. [Laughs] It was exactly that.

The first few weeks of the process, we were still finding our sea legs and figuring some stuff out. Phil gave me some great advice. We shot a scene that actually didn't make the cut — it's on the deleted scenes. My first day of shooting was this scene where we were in these two bunk beds. It was a great scene, and I loved doing it, but you could feel everybody on set who might've been skeptical that this would work, you could feel every crew member's shoulders just relax and lean in.

But Phil pulled me into his little tent with all my other Rocketeers, and we looked at some of the footage from that day, and he was like, "I think you can trust stillness more," because I was overcomplicating the fact that he doesn't have a face means you have to communicate through movement. But I think I was doing too much, and he was like, "Remember, this is in IMAX, so you can't be like [thrashes around] the entire time.”

So, it was a really cool lesson. It was a little bit like going to the Michael Caine school of acting, of never blink, always look at that side of the camera, not that. It was all that stuff that I was learning on a creature with no face.

By the way, that advice that Michael Caine said about not blinking is dead-accurate.

If any of you are acting students, I swear to you, you've got to learn how to not blink, and if you look at people's performances, like great actors, you will notice they do not blink. They're very specific when they blink because they feel that it's like a character moment.

I swear it's a little thing that you should absolutely practice.

ORTIZ: There's footage you can find of Michael Caine explaining this. Do it. It's so valuable because it's about using the medium. Theater people know how to use their whole body, and a lot of film acting is like, "This is now your proscenium. What are you going to do? How do you use it?" And, yeah, that's always the exercise.

Let's talk a little bit about what the physical rig was like. How many people does it take to operate Rocky, and how do you keep the team in sync?

ORTIZ: Great question. So it was me — I was basically always on the carapace, the main body. There were handles coming out of his mid-back, sort of like a fighter pilot, and that was how I could animate the movement of the head. There were sometimes three to seven people operating his other appendages, and I was always sort of leading the charge on what that was.

So we spent eight weeks basically learning how to work together as kind of a company. But the thing that would keep us tethered together, because puppetry can inherently be very technical — I promised Ryan the first day that we started talking about this project, and I had already gotten the part, I did say, "Listen, puppetry can be technical, but I guarantee you can improv with me, and I'm not going to leave you hanging, and I'm not going to let the technical side win." Right? It has to be an emotional performance.

And sometimes when I could feel my other performers trying to do one thing with a leg, and it wasn't going to happen, and they were getting frustrated, all of that, I would just say, "Let's not try to aim for perfection. Let's just focus on the feeling." So sometimes we would finish a take, and I would go, "That was awesome. Let's try it again, and this time it's Christmas morning, and you're tiptoeing down the stairs, and you're so excited." That has nothing to do with the scene, but that's the emotional beat that we were trying to sort of land.

So, that would be the one way, especially when we would be improvising and had to just lean in.

Was there ever a scene where the limitations of the puppet made the performance better?

ORTIZ: [Thumbs down] So, I think it's been well documented that basically they were like, "So he's going to do a thumbs up," and I was like [shakes head], because Rocky's elbows don't rotate. Rocky doesn't have wrists, which was something I would say all the time, and they'd go, "What does that mean?" And I was like, "Well, you can't do a thumbs up without a wrist." And we would try to do his finger, but that looked like something else. That looked like, "One minute!" So that didn't work.

That was something that we sort of showed on the day, and it was something that one of my puppeteers was like, "Wouldn't it be funny if he goes, 'Thumbs up?'" [Thumbs down] And it is! So that was one sort of happy accident, as Bob Ross would say.

Phil and Chris are known for finding movies in the editing room. Did they do anything with the editing that really surprised you about Rocky's performance?

ORTIZ: Yes. What I loved about working with them is that, again, I was a theater person from nowhere. The theater credits don't always transfer in the world of movies, and they would shoot every scene until basically everybody's ideas had been wrung out. It wasn't just like, "Let's do the scene on paper." Which, by the way, Drew Goddard wrote an incredible script, and we would do that, and then Ryan would have a cool idea, or I would have a cool idea, or Phil and Chris would go, "We were thinking it would be fun if..." And we would just shoot. Sometimes it would be a three-line scene, and we would shoot a take for 45 minutes, going back and going back, and, “Try it again, and add this.”

So, we had so much material that frankly, I left every day going, "I have no idea what we just did." I completely blacked out. I was just trying to stay in the scene and trying to stay alive. So when I saw different cuts of the movie, they in fact were very different films from my estimation. The way that the scenes would be juxtaposed next to each other meant something else.

When I saw the final cut, I actually did go, "I think I want to try to get into the ADR booth and maybe try to complete some of Rocky's arcs a little bit more." Because we didn't have the real estate. The movie is from Ryan's perspective. Literally, you're inside of his head most of the time. When he realizes that Rocky speaks that way, we suddenly can see in Rocky Vision, right? So there wasn't a lot of real estate to put Rocky's arc in there.

The TLDR of what I'll just sort of say here is that we didn't have this moment where Rocky's sort of traumatic 80 years left alone on a spaceship was ever paid off. Through a series of just trial and error, we were able to find something, and I sort of raised my hand and said, "I think we need to change this line in this scene where I give him the astrophage.” And Rocky saying, "Grace say Grace will die, Rocky fix" was something that we added truly a month before the movie came out. And it was something that really made me so happy because, again, it's that moment when I was going, "I feel so much for this character. I really want to do him service." And we hadn't landed the plane on, well, where does that trauma go? We never addressed it. And I think the trauma has to turn into something positive. Rocky then takes the awful experience that they had, which we don't really talk about, and turns it into help for the person that he cares about the most.

So anyway, that's a roundabout way of answering that.

It has to be really crazy to be a part of a film like this that means so much to so many people. What has it been like meeting fans, and what do they always want to talk to you about?

ORTIZ: Oh, it's incredible. Like I was saying, we made this movie kind of in a bubble, and then it came out — and what we don't have time for is the time that I met Steve the first time. Do you want to do it? Can we do it?

This is your Q&A. You do whatever you want.

ORTIZ: Let's do this because I think it's real charming. So basically, we were doing a press junket in LA before the movie came out, and the only people that had seen the movie were press. We were doing this thing where they were being interviewed downstairs, and I was up with the Rocketeers doing basically a meet-and-greet with Rocky. So press was coming in. There were little bits of footage. The footage was still coming out. And it was good, but it was quick. It was two minutes with each person.

We should say the room that you were in was in a hotel, and there were curtains, so you could not see past the curtain to know what was inside. And the publicist would only say, "We have something we want you to do." We were not told what was inside that room. At least for me, they were like, "We just want you to go in the room."

ORTIZ: And basically, there were cameras set up that people could see Rocky because Rocky was on a table and alive, and, "Hello, friend!" And we would interact, and it would be a two-minute sit in the chair, take a picture, have a video, move on. And it had become kind of a little rote, and everybody was like, "I loved the movie," and that is kind of a kiss of death. We're like, "Did you?"

And this human walked in, the curtain parted, and Steve's face went [looks concerned]. And we were trying to engage, like, "Come over here and take a picture." He went [holds up hand and shakes head], and he just sort of moved towards us, and it was so moving. I told you this already. It was so moving because it was the first inkling I had that maybe this character worked because you were so affected. I had to break character. I was still puppeteering, and I went, "Steve, do you want to sit down?" It was so moving. We went long just to let you have your experience. It was really meaningful to me.

I'm very, very lucky with what I get to do, and as I told you backstage, I've interviewed Kermit and Miss Piggy. Literally, you just become a child. You do not see the puppeteers. Meanwhile, Advanced Micro Devices' (AMD -13.25%) GPUs continue to gain traction. The company has improved its software stack, and its chips' larger memory capacity makes them ideal for inference.

If the gap between AI chips continues to shrink and AI models can be quickly shifted to run on any AI accelerator, this is very bad news for Nvidia. Not only will the company continue to lose market share, but, more importantly, its pricing power will erode, and its margins will shrink. The Nvidia premium can be seen in its gross margins, which are in the mid-70% range, versus AMD’s in the mid-50% range. Meanwhile, all of Nvidia's largest customers, outside of SpaceX (SPCX -7.69%), have been developing their own custom AI chips to try to break free of Nvidia's high prices.

All is not lost for Nvidia, as the company still holds some advantages. It has created a cohesive hardware, software, and networking ecosystem that should still be able to command a premium. It also has major current supply chain advantages, having locked up more access to constrained high bandwidth memory (HBM) supply and having more manufacturing and packaging capacity with Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing (TSM -9.48%). While Nvidia may struggle, these three AI stocks look set to benefit.

Lower AI accelerator prices and more Nvidia GPU alternatives could boost overall AI chip demand, which would benefit Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing. It would also lower TSMC's customer concentration and could actually help with pricing power and gross margins.

Whether a hyperscaler, neocloud, enterprise, or government buys Nvidia GPUs, AMD accelerators, or ASICs through a company like Broadcom (AVGO -12.19%) or Marvell Technology (MRVL -11.69%), TSMC still wins at the end of the day. That makes the stock a buy.

As both a cloud computing giant and a developer of TPUs, Alphabet is one of the best-positioned companies to benefit from the commoditization of AI chips. Alphabet has more than a decade's head start with custom chips and is creating an ecosystem to rival chipmakers like Nvidia and AMD. This lets it get better utilization out of its data centers and gives it an advantage over other companies using their own custom AI chips.

Meanwhile, because its offerings cost much less than Nvidia's, it should be able to gain share with external customers without sacrificing margins. This ultimately could make Alphabet one of the biggest winners in the AI space during this transition.

Starting from a much smaller market-share base with an operating model already built around slimmer gross margins, AMD should benefit as AI chips become more commoditized. Between its lower prices, improving cost performance, and the recent introduction of a complete AI rack system (Helios), AI chip pricing normalization and chip fungibility actually become a tailwind rather than a headwind for a smaller Nvidia competitor like AMD.

Meanwhile, the market is heading in AMD's direction with inference and agentic AI, with customers caring far more about memory capacity, central processing unit (CPU) integrations, and cost-per-action, which is where its chips shine. With its new Helios system, which combines its GPUs and CPUs, AMD has a big opportunity to establish itself as a meaningful alternative to Nvidia in the coming years. This makes the stock a buy.

Indian Auteur Girish Kasaravalli Looks Back on 50 Years of Cinema at IFFI Masterclass: ‘All My Films Talk About Othering’

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Indian auteur Girish Kasaravalli looked back on his career in Kannada-language cinema at the 57th International Film Festival of India (IFFI) in Goa, in a masterclass titled “The Moral Universe of Parallel Cinema: 50 Years of Girish Kasaravalli.”

The 75-year-old spoke about the moral contradictions of society and the relationship between cinematic form and social content. He also argued that even entertainment is a political act. The session was moderated by Variety‘s Asia editor Naman Ramachandran.

Across films such as “Ghatashraddha” (1977), “Tabarana Kathe” (1986), “Thaayi Saheba” (1997), “Dweepa” (2002) and “Gulabi Talkies” (2008), Kasaravalli has kept returning to one question: how societies exclude individuals and communities, and how people who are themselves victims of oppressive structures can also become complicit in perpetuating them.

Asked whether the 1977 Kannada classic “Ghatashraddha,” which examines religious orthodoxy and social exclusion, still spoke to contemporary India, Kasaravalli said: “All my films talk about othering. I always talk about how society looks at some people from a different lens, and in that process they make them ‘other.'” He described this as “the biggest cruelty an individual or a group of people can impart on another person.”

Asked whether he considered himself a political filmmaker, Kasaravalli said: “Whoever makes something, whether he makes mainstream cinema or art cinema, he is making a political statement, either in support of the dominant ideology or opposing the dominant ideology. He cannot avoid it.” There is nothing called only entertainment, he added, and even entertainment is a political form.

On his characters, he said: “I don’t want any of my characters to become a kind of a mouthpiece to a certain ideology.” Drawing on Jean-Luc Godard, he compared understanding a character to buying a house: one must examine it from the outside and then from within before making a judgment. Similarly, a filmmaker must inhabit a character and then step back to assess it. For Kasaravalli, characters are too complex for a filmmaker to easily take sides.

On the evolution of his cinematic language, Kasaravalli described the visual construction of “Ghatashraddha,” as “ritualistic cinema.” “The style is not imposed on it; it comes from within the content itself,” he said. Since “Ghatashraddha” was about a ritual, he felt its cinematic form should reflect that structure.

Kasaravalli said he could not write a script until he had clarity about its images. “For me, the film is not merely making statements; it’s an attempt by the filmmaker to impart an experience.” He described writing detailed instructions for his crew, down to which hand a character uses to lift a glass, because even that changes the composition of a shot.

This attention to the relationship between character and environment shaped “Gulabi Talkies.” Kasaravalli told cinematographer S. Ramachandra that the coastal community had to be as important as the protagonist. He wanted the images to feel like “actuality coverage,” not a predetermined visual construction. Rejecting conventional scenic shots of the sea and rocks, he told his cinematographer: “Film should not have any pretty image.” They chose 16mm, allowing the grain to contribute to the film’s sense of actuality.

The discussion of visual aesthetics brought him to Cuban filmmaker Julio García Espinosa’s idea of “imperfect cinema,” which, for Kasaravalli, challenged the assumption that Western aesthetics should define cinematic perfection. “I don’t want to take your aesthetic sensibility as my sensibility,” he said, quoting Espinosa. Kasaravalli said each country and each region has its own aesthetic concerns. “We should make films according to that condition; we should never imitate the West. Why should I be perfect? I should be loyal to my own milieu.”

Asked whether parallel cinema belonged to a particular period, Kasaravalli disagreed, pointing to notable films being made across the country today. The larger problem, he said, was reaching audiences. Of streaming platforms, he said: “We all thought it is going to be a solution for all of us. But OTT [streaming] is exactly doing what the mainstream distribution network was doing earlier.”

In the audience Q&A, he addressed open endings and whether a filmmaker should provide answers. “A filmmaker who says that he knows answers for all the questions in life is telling a lie,” he said. “He is also as confused as you are. He only throws up options so that you can start thinking. There is nothing called closure… life is always open.”

As the session concluded, IFFI festival director Ashutosh Gowariker described Kasaravalli’s work as “textbooks,” saying he had returned to “Ghatashraddha” while developing a new film. He also singled out the filmmaker’s close-ups. “You let your actors simply be. You make the audience think and anticipate what the character is going through,” Gowariker said.

Brazilian Court Orders Arrest of the Buenavista Over Corazul Dispute

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A Brazilian court ordered the arrest of Corazul’s the Buenavista at the Port of Santos after the cruise line’s exclusive Brazilian distributor claimed it was owed about $270,000 (R$1.48 million), according to a court decision dated Nov. 13.

Judge Frederico dos Santos Messias of the São Paulo State Court of Justice’s specialized maritime court granted the request from R11 Travel Viagens e Turismo against GLCL Global Cruiseline Ltd., the Cyprus-registered company that operates as Corazul.

The judge issued the order without first hearing from Corazul, the decision showed.

The order barred the Buenavista from leaving Brazilian waters. The court said the ship was anchored off Santos for repairs.

The judge set security at about $323,000 (R$1.78 million) and said the ship would be released if Corazul deposited that amount or posted a bank guarantee.

According to the decision, R11 said Corazul owed about $1.27 million (R$6.96 million) under a four-year exclusive distribution agreement signed in March. That figure included about $780,000 (EUR 675,000) in unpaid quarterly marketing contributions. After subtracting about $1 million (R$5.48 million) in passenger funds it held, R11 put the net balance at about $270,000.

The filing, as summarized by the court, described a series of disruptions including the cancellation of the Buenavista’s European season in June; the cancellation of its Oct. 23 inaugural Brazilian cruise nine days before departure and complaints about onboard service on the Oct. 30 sailing. The company has since suspended operations and R11 has stopped selling cruises on the ship.

R11 said Corazul demanded $400,000 on Nov. 4 before it would operate the Nov. 6 departure. R11 refused.

The court noted that only two of the 11 sailings scheduled between Oct. 23 and Nov. 27 operated. Corazul also suspended operations until Nov. 30 and moved the end of its Brazil season up to March 5, 2027. The judge wrote that Corazul’s own communications showed a “serious liquidity collapse.”

The dispute is headed to arbitration in São Paulo under AMCHAM, the American Chamber of Commerce for Brazil. The decision stated that R11 must file within 30 days or the order lapses. Corazul has five days after being served to contest it.
