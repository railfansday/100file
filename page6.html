Anthropic’s IPO is reportedly oversubscribed as founders sell $1.75bn

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anthropic’s seven founders are selling $1.75bn of stock, and Amazon and Google stand to gain hundreds of billions. Retail investors in Europe, Australia and Japan can join the Anthropic IPO.

Anthropic’s share sale has drawn orders for several times the shares on offer, Bloomberg reported on 11 November, citing people familiar with the matter. Large, anchor-size orders drove the demand, the people said.

The day before, the Financial Times reported that bankers were preparing to raise the valuation to $1.5tn or more. That would top the $1.4tn ceiling Anthropic set on 10 November. Details of the deal could still change, Bloomberg’s sources said.

The deal is set to price on 18 November, Bloomberg’s Bailey Lipschultz reported. Before that, Dario Amodei is due to join JPMorgan chief executive Jamie Dimon for a livestreamed event on Friday, according to Bloomberg.

Some early investors are already reselling. JonesTrading is marketing a block of up to $500m at a fee of 3% to 5% over the IPO price, Bloomberg reported. Anthropic says it does not recognise share transfers it has not approved.

Anthropic first disclosed its third-quarter loss on 2 November. Its amended filing of 10 November adds more detail on the quarter.

Anthropic put the move from a second-quarter operating profit down to two costs. They were $5bn of short-term compute and a $2bn charge for its staff donation-matching programme. Price cuts on new models cost about $600m in revenue between the two quarters. Adjusted operating margin fell to 15% from 19%.

The filing also says that 5% to 7% of revenue in the first nine months came from customers later found to have broken its usage policy or commercial terms. Anthropic tightened enforcement in August and September and expects that to weigh on growth.

Anthropic held $95.6bn in cash and $36.3bn in short-term investments, the filing shows. The Information’s Aaron Holmes reported about $10bn of new cloud commitments with Microsoft and Google since the last filing. The filing also says the $35bn Lambda deal can currently be ended, though Anthropic has no intention of doing so.

The seven co-founders are selling 23,648,646 shares between them, according to Anthropic’s roadshow announcement. That is 3,378,378 each, or about $250m apiece at the $74 midpoint, $1.75bn in total.

The sale is about 1.5% of each founder’s stake, Dylan Sloan reported for Bloomberg. Each would be worth about $18bn at the midpoint, according to the Bloomberg Billionaires Index. All seven have pledged to give away 80% of their personal equity.

“The market has gotten more comfortable with founders and large shareholders taking some money off the table,” Jay Ritter, an emeritus professor at the University of Florida, told Bloomberg.

Amazon and Google, Anthropic’s largest outside shareholders, together own more than a quarter of its shares, Karen Weise reported for The New York Times. At $1.4tn, Amazon’s $18bn investment could be worth more than $250bn, and Google’s $33bn about $140bn.

“It is a loaded bet, but I don’t think anybody wants to be left out of the bet,” Aswath Damodaran, who teaches valuation at New York University, told the Times.

Nvidia is putting about $10bn into the private placement. Its chief executive, Jensen Huang, resigned on Thursday, and Nvidia reports earnings on 17 November.

Anthropic is selling to retail investors in several countries without listing outside the US. Its European offer covers Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Ireland, the Netherlands, Norway, Spain and Sweden. Germany’s financial regulator, BaFin, approved the prospectus, which also covers Swiss retail investors.

Australia’s retail offer opened on 11 November and closes at 5pm Sydney time on 17 November, CNBC reported, citing broker Stake. Commonwealth Bank’s CommSec leads a group of eight brokers.

In Japan, Anthropic plans to sell 38.5m to 50m shares, worth $3bn to $3.5bn, Bloomberg reported. The filing also covers Canada and the UK.

Craig Coben, a former head of equity capital markets at Bank of America, told The New York Times that everyone has an interest in the deal’s success.

“A successful I.P.O. is arguably required to validate the valuations of stocks in the A.I. ecosystem,” Coben said.

Jack Selby, who runs the venture firm Copper Sky, warned of the opposite on Bloomberg Television.

“An underperforming Anthropic IPO has to be a very prominent player in what will be a pretty massive correction in the market with respect to AI,” Selby said.

He also pointed to the end of lock-up periods, when early holders can sell. Up to 316m shares can trade from the first day, the filing shows.

MotoGP U.S. Rights Head to ESPN in Deal With Liberty Media

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The motorcycle racing circuit, a sister brand top Formula 1, was most recently with Fox Sports.

ESPN is back in business with Liberty Media.

The sports media juggernaut has inked a deal to broadcast MotoGP, the motorcycle racing circuit owned by Liberty, beginning in 2027.

It’s a two-year deal that will cover both the U.S. and Latin America, where ESPN already had MotoGP rights. MotoGP’s most recent U.S. rights were with Fox Sports, and they had previously been with Warner Bros. Discovery and NBC Sports.

ESPN say that the races will air on all of ESPN’s platforms, as well as on Disney+.

Liberty Media, which is led by Derek Chang, owns both MotoGP and Formula 1, which left ESPN this year to move its U.S. rights to Apple TV. Liberty bought MotoGP in 2024 at a $4.5 billion valuation with a goal of building it into a worldwide brand as it has done with F1. The U.S. market, for obvious reasons, is a top priority, so ESPN’s reach is seen as a boost to that effort.

“This agreement with ESPN marks an important milestone in MotoGP’s growth strategy across the Americas,” said Carlos Ezpeleta, deputy CEO of MotoGP. “ESPN’s position as one of the world’s leading sports broadcasters, combined with its strong connection with audiences across the United States and Latin America, makes it the ideal platform for us to continue expanding MotoGP’s reach and bring the sport closer to millions of people. This is an exciting opportunity to introduce MotoGP to new audiences while continuing to serve our passionate existing fan base through one of the most influential sports media brands in the world.”

“ESPN and MotoGP have a strong relationship that dates back nearly 25 years, when we began bringing the championship to fans across Latin America,” added Freddy Rolón, head of global sports & talent office for ESPN. “This is a great example of how relationships that begin in one region of our international business can grow across ESPN’s global portfolio, creating new opportunities to connect different audiences with world-class sporting events.”

Every Show in Marvel’s WandaVision Trilogy, Ranked

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

VisionQuest has finally aired its series finale, bringing closure to the unexpected trilogy that began in 2021 with WandaVision and continued in 2024 with the even more unexpected Agatha All Along. At face value, the saga is a tale about how a witch and a synthezoid deal with the loss of their makeshift family, set within the confines of a superhero cinematic universe. In reality, the WandaVision trilogy is about two main things: the first is grief, how we process it, what it does to us, and how we eventually learn to live with it. The second is family. It's a story about grieving mothers, struggling fathers, and lost children, all coming together to find purpose and belonging.

It wouldn't be an overstatement to say the WandaVision trilogy is the single best, most cohesive and narratively impactful thing the MCU has ever produced. For starters, every show prioritizes meaning over spectacle and character over action; the result is a tale of multiple wandering characters who connect through shared experience, learn to process their pain, and ultimately gain new insight about the world and themselves, entering the next chapter of their lives as updated versions of themselves. Declaring which of them is the best is not an easy task, but someone has to do it. Here, we rank every entry in the WandaVision trilogy, not as a way to bring them down, but to celebrate what is undoubtedly Marvel's boldest, most compelling and cathartic creative swing.

The trilogy began in 2021 with WandaVision, written and created by Jac Schaeffer. Set in the aftermath of Avengers: Endgame, the show sees Wanda Maximoff (Elizabeth Olsen) create an idealized version of her life inspired by her love for classic sitcoms. In this world, Vision (Paul Bettany) is alive, and they even welcome twins. Of course, Wanda's life is complicated by the presence of S.W.O.R.D. and a mysterious nosy neighbor named Agnes (Kathryn Hahn). Oh, and there's also the fact that she enslaved an entire town to create this pretty little life, so there's that.

WandaVision is, first and foremost, a character study on Wanda Maximoff, and Elizabeth Olsen makes the most of it, delivering what might just be the best performance in the MCU. The writing is sharp and insightful, using Wanda's trauma to continue her character arc and keep the larger MCU storyline going. Bettany and Hahn are excellent support, as is Teyonah Parris as Monica Rambeau. The show focuses on the destructive power of grief, but also its ability to change and help us grow, finding a perfect way to include the superhero aspect of the story. Alas, WandaVision is not perfect, and it's pretty much undone by its rushed, borderline unsatisfying finale. The subsequent Doctor Strange in the Multiverse of Madness also did a disservice to the show by pretty much committing character assassination with Wanda, which certainly doesn't help.

The most recent effort, VisionQuest, is also the last chapter in the trilogy, and it does a wonderful job tying everything together. Set about 18 months after the events of Agatha All Along, the show centers on Vision as he settles into his new reality with the memories from the old Vision. Several AIs are also present in his programming, including E.D.I.T.H. (Emily Hampshire), F.R.I.D.A.Y. (Orla Brady), J.A.R.V.I.S. (James D'Arcy), and Ultron (James Spader). Vision's life is put in danger when a bounty hunter is sent after him, causing him to reunite with his lost son, Tommy (Ruaridh Mollica).

VisionQuest is a pretty perfect finale to the story, putting the spotlight on fathers and their complicated relationships with their sons. The Vision-Tommy bond is great, but the real center of the story is the Vision-Ultron father-son dynamic, with Bettany and Spader not only sharing excellent chemistry but also making the most of an unexpectedly powerful screenplay. Spader, in particular, is very clearly having the time of his life here, bringing an electrifying energy to the show. The AIs are a worthy addition, too, even if they're used a bit too much like a deus ex machina that benefits from using a lot of technical jargon that may or may not make actual sense. Yet, at its core, VisionQuest is a tale of self-discovery and coming to terms with one's role in life as a father, brother, and son. It continues the family angle while finally giving the spotlight to Vision, one of the MCU's most reliable yet sidelined characters.

Who would've thought the show that was seemingly inspired by a catchy tune would be the best in this trilogy? Alas, that's precisely the case. Agatha All Along picks up two years after WandaVision, with Agatha Harkness (Hahn) breaking out of Wanda's spell with the help of a young Teen (Joe Locke) who wants her help to walk the Witches' Road. Recruiting a coven, the duo ventures onto the Road, followed by the Salem Seven, a group of witches hell-bent on revenge against Agatha. The mysterious Rio Vidal (Aubrey Plaza) also has unfinished business with Agatha.

Agatha All Along is the most cohesive, well-planned, and best-executed series, not only in the WandaVision trilogy but in the MCU as a whole. What might seem like a gag show is actually a very tender and thoughtful exploration of motherhood and grief from the perspective of the MCU's most volatile antihero. Agatha is evil, selfish, and ruthless, a killer through and through, but she's also unexpectedly loving, and her bond with the Teen is as sweet as it's hilarious. Speaking of Teen, Locke is a revelation as Billy Maximoff, bringing a lot of nuance and plenty of sass to his performance. Narratively, Agatha All Along takes bold swings, centering its entire story on a song and delivering some of Marvel's most interesting episodes, most notably the endlessly creative "Death's Hand in Mine." With a terrific ensemble including heavy-hitters like Patti LuPone, a charming visual approach that has a high school play vibe (the Road is very much a high school play of sorts), and a thematic richness seldom found in other superhero properties, Agatha All Along is truly one of a kind.

The amazing disappearing exoplanet

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

An exoplanet seems to stopped transiting in front of its host star.

One nice thing about discovering exoplanets is that, once you work out how long their orbits take, they continue to show up like clockwork. The timing is so regular, in fact, that we can use variations to detect the gravitational influence of additional planets in the same exosolar system. And the precise timing is useful in that we can rely on it to point telescopes at the system when we know some of the light we detect has passed through the planet’s atmosphere.

On Monday, researchers reported on an exoplanet that simply stopped showing up. TOI-2535b is a hot Jupiter-sized planet we’ve been observing as it transits between its host star, TOI-2535, and Earth for about seven years now. But it recently stopped showing up, and researchers suspect that’s due to an unseen companion orbiting in a different plane.

TOI-2535 is one of the many stars with exoplanet candidates identified by TESS, the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite. It’s a K dwarf, meaning it’s somewhat smaller than the Sun. And TESS found signs of a Jupiter-sized planet orbiting it every eight days, an orbit close enough to make TOI-2535b a “hot Jupiter” exoplanet. At that point, there was nothing especially unusual about it.

But a closer look at the data showed two very strange things. First, the timing of when its transits occurred could shift by as much as six hours, a substantial variation for an orbit this brief. That’s typically a sign of gravitational interactions with another planet; depending on the details of that second planet’s orbit, it may slow the first one or accelerate it through a transit more quickly. Since the second planet doesn’t show up in TESS data, it appears to orbit in a slightly different plane, so it doesn’t pass between its host star and Earth.

The second oddity is that the transit duration grew progressively shorter in more recent observations. This suggests that, while early observations saw the planet transiting across the star’s equator, later ones involved transits near one of the poles, ensuring that the planet spent less time between the star and Earth. This trend eventually reached its logical end point: TOI-2535b simply stopped transiting in the most recent observations.

Obviously, gravitational interactions with a planet orbiting in a different plane could potentially alter TOI-2535b’s orbit so it no longer passes between TOI-2535 and Earth. There is also some precedent for the converse happening: a star around which no transits were originally detected but where transits began appearing in later observations. Still, as a Jupiter-sized planet, TOI-2535b should not be that easy to shift, and as the researchers note, “This is the fastest rate of inclination change observed for an exoplanet orbiting a single star.”

The second, unseen planet “would need to be either extremely massive or on a highly eccentric orbit,” the researchers noted. So they decided to figure out what sorts of configurations could lead to such rapid changes.

To do so, they modeled 300,000 different starting configurations and ran them forward in time. They found there wasn’t a single favored configuration; as long as the planets were in an orbital resonance, things were relatively stable, at least over relatively short periods. So they took the 100 best solutions and ran them forward for a million years.

About two-thirds became unstable during that period, which ruled out a couple of the potential resonances. The remaining third shared a couple of features. One is that the second, undetected planet has to be pretty massive—roughly Saturn-sized at a minimum. The second is that the angle between the orbits of the two planets can’t be that large. If it is, the difference would shrink over time, causing their orbits to become increasingly eccentric. Eventually, this results in a close approach between the two planets, followed by instability.

The University of California, Los Angeles has posted a video of one of the better models if you’re having a hard time visualizing all of this.

While a second planet hasn’t been detected via the transit method, its gravitational pull on the star should create changes in the radial velocity we could potentially detect. Right now, however, we don’t have enough clean data to detect it. The researchers also say there’s enough noise in the transit timing variations that a third planet in the system can’t be excluded.

The question we probably can’t answer with further observations is how this system got into its current state. Large planets are thought to form farther out from the star and then migrate inward because of friction caused by the gas in the planet-forming disk. (This inward motion tends to stop when orbital resonances develop.) But this friction should also limit the angle between the planets’ orbits. Without some additional event that mixed up the orbits, it’s not obvious how this system could have come to be.

Can we please stop calling it a ‘honey trap’?

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When Jensen Huang told Nvidia employees on November 8 that he had been “unfaithful” to his wife, there’s a phrase that quickly became omnipresent in the media: Huang had been the victim of a “honey trap”, laid by Alice Huang (no relation), a Singapore-based tech executive identified as a Chinese spy by U.S. intelligence agencies.

“Honey trap,” seriously?

The story behind the phrase is that a good, hardworking man was minding his own business when a serpent of a woman lured him off the righteous path by dishonest means and propositions he, being a man, simply couldn’t refuse. It is assumed that male sex drive is some kind of force of nature that no adult can reasonably be expected to control. So there’s a double sexist stereotype here. Can a 63-year-old billionaire CEO really be supposed to have no say over his sex life? Like a bear wouldn’t be able to resist honey?

Anyone who, like me, grew up in the 1980s knows a close relative of this idea: the “homewrecker,” a word aimed at the other woman and never at the husband who cheated. One of the decade’s most successful films, Fatal Attraction (1987), tells the story of a married man (Michael Douglas) whose affair turns into a nightmare when the woman (Glenn Close) refuses to stay discreet and starts asking for more. The film portrays the mistress as mentally unstable and dangerous. The film ends with the wife killing the mistress and family values restored. The husband’s betrayal is never really an issue. I’m ashamed to admit that when I first watched it, some time in the 1990s, I sided completely with the poor husband, hounded by a woman the film had led me to see as a witch.

Both ideas have their roots in Genesis, where Adam, asked why he ate the forbidden fruit, answers that the woman gave it to him. It is easy scapegoating: powerful men keep their reputation for rationality while some snake of a woman takes the blame for their lapses of judgment. There’s a paradox that I find difficult to swallow. For centuries, women were judged too foolish to vote or hold office, yet the same culture credits them with enough cunning to bring down kings and CEOs. Men, meanwhile, are supposed to be fit to lead nations and markets while being too weak to resist the first temptation that comes their way.

Deadly clichés

The term comes from spy fiction: dictionaries credit John le Carré with popularizing “honey trap” in Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy in 1974. Espionage has long been a man’s world, and in its novels a female spy is very often a seductress. Real Cold War espionage looked quite different, since East Germany’s best-known seduction program used men, so-called “Romeo” agents sent west to seduce secretaries with access to government files. 

In the Nvidia story, much of the coverage of Alice Huang also draws on images Western culture has attached to Asian women for more than a century: the mysterious seductress, the Dragon Lady. In the U.S., they go back at least to the Page Act of 1875, the country’s first restrictive federal immigration law, which was enforced on the presumption that Chinese women were prostitutes. Any woman arriving from Asia had to prove she wasn’t one.

In March 2021, a white man killed eight people at three Atlanta-area spas, six of them Asian women, and police relayed his description of his victims as “sources of temptation.” Asian women end up being blamed for men’s desire. And Atlanta showed that this cliché can be deadly. Coverage casting Alice Huang as a seductress is anything but harmless.

Nobody trapped the CEO

A CEO’s sexual relationship with someone working for one of his company’s biggest customers is a clear conflict of interest. Alice Huang’s employer, Megaspeed, is said to have bought about $4.8 billion of Nvidia hardware over the past two years. In most companies, a sales manager sleeping with a key client’s buyer would be expected to disclose it and hand the account to a colleague. Nobody would describe that sales manager as “trapped,” and the CEO of one of the world’s most valuable companies, who has far more power, deserves no more indulgence.

The phrase also shrinks Alice Huang: if she is only “honey,” she cannot be much of a professional. Yet she ran a business that bought chips at enormous scale, and if the allegations hold, she will have to answer for export violations and espionage, serious crimes committed with the very agency the word denies her.

Call it what it is

The honey trap trope has consequences in the workplace. In 2019, a LeanIn.Org and SurveyMonkey survey found that 60% of male managers in the U.S. were uncomfortable mentoring, working one-on-one with, or socializing with a woman, and 36% of men said they had avoided doing so for fear of how it would look. A woman alone with a powerful man is seen as a danger to him, even though she is far more likely to be the one harassed. She also pays in lost dinners, business trips and sponsorship, the informal moments that decide who gets promoted.

Other words would be more honest. A CEO had an affair with an executive at one of his largest customers, a woman U.S. officials reportedly believe was working for Chinese intelligence, and his board is now examining whether the relationship compromised the company. It is less catchy than “honey trap,” but it treats both people as adults responsible for their choices. Isn’t that the standard any employee would expect?

Anthropic has formally decided that it meets its own threshold for AI dramatically speeding up its research. It based the call on “what we expect from future models and our overall rate of progress,” not on its current models, it wrote in its amended IPO filing on 10 November.

The wording hardened over three filings in 15 days, as Alex Wilhelm noted in his newsletter Cautious Optimism. The AI research threshold sits in Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy, the company’s own rulebook for when stronger safeguards apply.

The policy counts a “dramatic acceleration” as double the rate of progress seen without significant help from AI. It also requires that AI automating research could plausibly explain the doubling.

The first S-1, filed on 26 October, said Anthropic’s evidence on AI-driven acceleration came mostly from lagging indicators.

“As a result, we may not identify a significant acceleration in our rate of development until after it has occurred,” the filing said.

The first amendment followed on 2 November. Anthropic wrote that it had “in the past not identified” such a speed-up until afterwards. Its estimates for future models showed it “may imminently meet” the threshold, it added. It had not designated any current model as exceeding it.

Three days later, the evaluation group METR published an independent assessment of Anthropic’s research pace. Its staff worked on Anthropic devices from 12 August to 29 October, with what METR called “unprecedented access” to internal data.

METR found that Anthropic’s models were improving on well-specified tasks at around twice the rate of March 2024 to October 2025. Its central estimate was 1.9 times, within a range of 1.5 to 2.8.

“We guess that a majority (perhaps ≈80%) of the current (October 2026) acceleration is due to AI,” METR wrote.

METR also found that AI cannot yet fully automate substantial parts of Anthropic’s model training pipeline. Still, it saw “a significant risk that further capabilities progress could lead to even faster acceleration over the coming year.”

The report carried caveats. The speed-up might be limited to well-specified tasks, METR said. Anthropic also held redaction rights over all non-public information in the report.

“Anthropic redacted a majority of the evidence we found most informative for these conclusions,” METR wrote.

Anthropic’s stated aim was to remove confidential information that could help other AI developers, according to METR. The group said it took no payment, though Anthropic supplied free tokens.

On 9 November, Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.2 alongside Mythos 5.2. Its system card said Mythos 5.2 does not double the internal rate of AI research progress.

The second amendment, a day later, made the call. President Trump signed the final steel beam at the White House ballroom the day after the midterm elections. Andrew Harnik/Getty Images hide caption

President Trump's efforts to remake Washington, D.C., have dominated his second term, even after his party's resounding midterm defeat.

He started his election week at the scandal-plagued Reflecting Pool and ended it with an order declaring his gilded Oval Office a national monument to "make it impossible for anybody ever to destroy this incredible space."

That proclamation, like any executive order, could be undone by a future president. But many of Trump's construction projects are changing D.C. in more permanent ways.

His administration has already downed hundreds of trees at East Potomac Park and demolished the East Wing of the White House to make space for a ballroom — which he called "the safest place in Washington" as he showed it to reporters the day after the midterms.

Trump has taken a uniquely unilateral approach to construction, packing regulatory commissions with his allies and bypassing Congress entirely. Most of his projects — from the triumphal arch to the Kennedy Center — are now playing out in court.

Critics accuse the Trump administration of skirting federal laws and misleading judges in its effort to progress as quickly as possible before the end of his term.

Democrats have vowed to use their soon-to-be congressional majority to push back on what they describe as Trump's "vanity projects."

When asked about that, White House spokesperson Davis Ingle told NPR in an email that "no other president has done more to beautify our Nation's interior than President Trump." He cited park cleanups, the Reflecting Pool and the White House helipad and ballroom as examples, and said Democrats "should work with President Trump to make our nation's capital beautiful again."

Rep. Jared Huffman, center, poses with activists protesting Trump's triumphal arch at Memorial Circle in May. He will soon become the chair of the committee with legislative and oversight authority over public lands. Andrew Harnik/Getty Imags hide caption

Rep. Jared Huffman, D.-Calif., told NPR in an interview that the Trump administration has "played this game of Catch Me If You Can, where they roll ahead with the bulldozers and the tree cutters … and they wait for courts to shut them down." He says a Democrat-led Congress will fight back.

"There's no way that we could look away while these sacred, iconic places are being permanently changed in ways that violate the law and could have really terrible lasting impacts," said Huffman, the top Democrat on the House Natural Resources Committee, which has a legislative purview over public lands and national parks.

Huffman says blocking Trump's construction projects — and holding people accountable for potential wrongdoing — will be a top priority when he leads the committee. Earlier this week, he sent letters ordering dozens of administration officials and private companies to preserve their records for a future investigation.

"Whether it is the arch or … or the East Potomac golf course or any number of other examples around the United States capital, that is federal land, and we have a direct statutory responsibility to either authorize those changes or to stop them if they're proceeding without the proper authorization," Huffman said.

What would that actually look like? Democrats have many tools at their disposal, from legislation to lawsuits. But experts say only two are likely to succeed: funding and oversight.

Trump made a final pre-Midterm Day pitch to voters from the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool, whose renovations took months longer than anticipated due to algae blooms and peeling paint. Saul Loeb/AFP via Getty Images hide caption

Congress could — at least in theory — pass laws to block specific projects from moving ahead. Some Democratic lawmakers have already introduced bills to protect the Kennedy Center from demolition and prohibit the construction of an arch at Memorial Circle.

But Trump has promised to "veto everything," and Democrats won't have a big enough majority to override him on their own.

"Legislation in principle is the cleanest way of doing it, but it's also not achievable," said David Super, who teaches administrative and constitutional law at Georgetown. "So what they're likely to do is cut off funds."

The Constitution says that the federal government may spend money only if is appropriated by Congress. But Trump has neither sought nor received congressional authorization for projects like the arch and the White House ballroom — he maintains he doesn't need it.

Rep. Chellie Pingree of Maine is one of many who disagree. She's the top Democrat on the House committee that oversees funding for the Interior Department.

"Certainly Congress should have been involved, because we have the power of the purse," Pingree told NPR. "That is our ultimate tool. And Republicans have refused to use that. But we can use that in very many ways to block him or to use it for leverage."

Pingree says a Democratic majority will be able to write appropriations bills with clear language from the outset about where money can and can't be spent — and potentially take the president to court if he doesn't follow it.

Super expects Congress would prevail in such a challenge, because it has clear-cut authority over federal funds.

For example, he noted that some of Trump's D.C.-area beautification projects have been funded at least in part by diverted national park entrance fees. Congress could "cap how much money can be used in the greater Washington area, and force the money to be used in the Everglades and Yosemite and Great Smoky Mountains," he said.

Congress will have to make some appropriations decisions in the coming weeks, since it needs to pass a short-term funding bill by Dec. 11 to avoid a government shutdown. Democrats could use that opportunity to try to limit funding for at least one of Trump's construction projects.

Huffman has proposed language that would prevent Trump from using federal funds for the East Potomac golf course renovations, limiting them instead to repairs of the park's crumbling seawall. Pingree said she will join him in fighting to negotiate that into next month's bill.

Earlier this year Pingree introduced an amendment that would have outright blocked construction of the arch; it failed along party lines. She wonders whether any Republicans might vote differently after the midterms, given the message Americans sent at the polls.

"They want the president to be focused on the issues they're dealing with: the cost of health care, the cost of food, the cost of gasoline," she said. Voters made clear they want lawmakers to focus on ending the Iran war and making life more affordable, big asks that may take a lot of political capital.

"And that's not corruption or Democrats not doing their job," Leavitt said. "It's just the natural trade-offs of dealing with who you're dealing with, and understanding that what matters to Trump more than anything seems to be building the arch, building the ballroom … remaking the Kennedy Center."

Whether or not these projects move forward, Congress can provide a fuller public accounting of how they came to be — in the hopes of preventing more in the future.

"They can gavel in hearings. They have agenda-setting power," Leavitt said. "They determine what the public hearing roster is. They decide which witnesses to invite. And that means they have a unique opportunity to draw negative public attention to the Trump administration."

"The frustrating part about congressional oversight is Congress can draw attention to a problem, and they can demand accountability for something that's already been done," Leavitt said. "But it's very hard for Congress to stop something from happening that the president is determined to do."

Super agrees that Democrats are playing a long game. He expects to see more bipartisan support after Trump leaves office for preventing future presidents from following his playbook.

"The process in the past has been built on trust, and President Trump certainly broke that trust in numerous ways," he said. "Laws can be written so that we don't depend on trust, we depend on law. And I'm guessing we're going to do that."

The steep fall in Nvidia’s stock that started with revelations about CEO Jensen Huang’s romantic relationship with an alleged Chinese spy, and accelerated following his resignation on November 12, makes Nvidia’s shares look like a historic buy. At least that’s the conclusion from analyzing its numbers by conventional metrics. The GPU-maker’s shares peaked on October 5 at $243. At that point, Nvidia sported the world’s biggest market cap at $5.85 trillion. Wall Street analysts following the stock were almost universal even then in acclaiming Nvidia as a cheap stock. And, it appeared, for good reason: Nvidia was selling at 30 times its earnings over the previous four quarters, a modest number for an enterprise growing its bottom line at an annual clip of 100%.

Today, Nvidia looks like a far greater bargain based strictly on the numbers. Since the summit in early October, it’s tumbled 34% as of mid-morning on November 12 to $161, a downdraft that slashed nearly $2 trillion from its valuation, the biggest dollar drop by far, in a condensed period, in the history of capital markets. As a result, Nvidia’s PE has dropped to 20, a figure that given its current stats, tags it as practically the ultimate value play. For example, Apple and Amazon carry a PE of 40 and 22 respectively, and are expanding at a fraction of Nvidia’s speed.

Indeed, despite its immense size and sorcerous growth rate, Nvidia has so far shown no signs of falling victim to the law of large numbers. In the quarter ended October 31, its sales jumped 26% over the previous three-month span, beating the around 20% jumps of the two previous quarters. What about profitability? In the October quarter, gross margins hit a sumptuous 75%, beating its FY 2026 posting of 71.3%.

Make a sober assessment of Nvidia’s overall condition, however, and the best conclusion is that the market is probably right in imposing this giant discount based on Huang’s departure. Nvidia’s wild profitability arises from its virtual monopoly in the top GPU segment. According to a top industry executive who’s an Nvidia customer, it is 18 to 24 months ahead of all-comers in the quality and performance of its top chips. The rub, says this person, is Nvidia’s dependence on Huang as both an unmatched innovator and excellent operator. “Huang was the tech visionary and he also got Nvidia to operate incredibly well,” says this person. “The question is how much depended on Jensen, and how much of what he did can another person do?”

This executive stresses that Nvidia has maintained its huge margins by keeping several paces ahead of the field, and never slowing, one of the greatest feats in the annals of tech. “That continuing advantage in chips is absolutely essential to Nvidia. It’s an industry where you can’t miss a step. If you do, others will catch up fast. Intel fell behind, and it never caught up.”

Plus, competitors who regularly complain about the high cost of Nvidia chips are racing to create their own, in-house competitors, a list that encompasses AMD, Google, and Amazon.

It’s also revealing to parse how Nvidia’s deploying its huge free cash flow to stay in front. In the past four quarters, it’s generated $105 billion in FCF. In the past, it’s channeled a huge portion of that bounty into share buybacks. But in the 12 months ended in October, repurchases fell to just $32 billion. Nvidia’s cash Hoard of $100 billion in relatively modest, and growing slowly.

So where is all the rest of that capex going? Nvidia is famously making big investments in its customers, including SpaceX, CoreWeave, OpenAI, SoftBank’s SB Energy, and Intel (for CPUs). It’s also provided multibillion-dollar credit guarantees for projects built for hyperscalers and neoclouds where it’s providing the GPUs. The practice of funding its own customers is often questioned as “circular financing.” The question is whether Huang felt obliged to follow that course to protect Nvidia’s future. The reason: Locking in future sales as rivals hatch competing products at lower price points. In other words, Huang sought to forestall the commoditization of Nvidia, the threat that’s eventually hit the profitability of all tech hardware giants.

And that’s just the problem. Huge prices and margins inevitably attract cheaper new supply. The market judges that Nvidia’s margins and profit growth will look far lower in the future than in the past, and that losing the genius who could hold commoditization at bay longest was a big, big loss.

A former CEO whom I spoke to took a contrarian stance by arguing that the Nvidia board made a big mistake in not making every attempt to keep Huang. “I would have liked to see more facts, and maybe the board’s got them,” says this person. “The board was not thinking about shareholders. They were thinking about their reputations and not getting sued. They should have punished Huang and made it a very visible punishment like no equity or bonus for a couple of years. They should have found a different way to punish the guy that didn’t damage shareholders.”

This person admits that this is probably a minority view. “People love to get on their high moral horse and that’s what we’re seeing,” says the observer. “You can’t just plug and play to replace someone so critical to the creation of a company’s one-of-a-kind products and operation. A number of CEOs who couldn’t say it publicly would probably say that the Nvidia board forgot what the role of a board should be, to protect shareholders, not themselves.”

That breathtaking, $2 trillion loss in value may be an enduring number, and not just the result of panic selling that most on Wall Street claim.

Seattle-based Constellation Space is rolling out its flagship software platform, which uses machine learning to streamline operations for fleets of satellites and the ground networks that serve them.

“We build models in-house for things like weather, attenuation, signal jamming, demand … and synthesize all of that to help operators understand what their ground contact plan looks like in the future, and how to optimize their fleets,” CEO Kamran Majid told GeekWire.

The Constellation Platform is tailor-made for a growing number of satellite constellations in low Earth orbit. Keeping track of thousands of satellites — and the best opportunities for downlinking data from each of those satellites — is becoming an increasingly complicated task.

Constellation Space, which was founded last year, draws upon Majid’s experience as a NASA engineering intern and as a software engineer working on SpaceX’s Starlink satellite network.

“At Starlink, I saw firsthand how manual mission-control processes break down as constellations scale,” he said. “I started Constellation to help smaller teams get ahead of link failures instead of spending their time rebuilding pass plans and recovering from outages.”

Majid’s fellow co-founders include chief technology officer Raaid Kabir, a veteran of Jeff Bezos’ Blue Origin space venture, and Omeed Tehrani and Laith Altarabishi, who were previously software engineers at Capital One. “We’re a team of six people right now,” Majid said.

One year into its existence, Constellation Space has already gained traction. Several months ago, the startup said it raised $7.5 million in a seed round backed by Y Combinator, Nvidia, OpenAI, Samsung Next and other investors.

Majid said an early version of the Constellation Platform has attracted more than 10 customers, including Amazon Web Services. It’s also used by more than 20 academic institutions. “Our annual contract value this year is $2.25 million,” he said.

The platform combines forecasts for factors affecting satellite transmissions — including space situational awareness, weather conditions, signal-to-noise ratios, signal jamming and bandwidth demand — to predict how many gigabytes of data can be transmitted during a given pass. Operators can then use that data to optimize transmission settings and schedules.

Its machine-learning model is pre-trained on data from a global network of sensors. Once it’s deployed, the model adapts to each fleet’s specific hardware and ground stations. Constellation Space says satellite operators can boost downlinked data per pass by as much as 36%.

Majid said the journey from SpaceX to startup has been “really interesting.”

“The mindset is very similar,” he said. “I was previously an engineer. Now I’m finding myself in more of a business role. The mindset is just ‘first-principles thinking’ about everything. Eliminate all the requirements that don’t make sense before you even start to think about solving the problem. Those core tenets have stayed as we’ve progressed as a venture, and I think that’s been really important to our success so far.”

Setting up the startup in the Seattle area, where more than half of the world’s satellites are built, has also been interesting.

“We are a Y Combinator company, so we were in San Francisco for a couple of months, and there was a lot of pressure from the community in SF to have everyone be in San Francisco. We definitely felt that a little bit,” Majid said.

“But operating in the satellite sector of the space industry, Seattle is becoming more and more of a hub for the space industry in general,” he said. “Space companies are either completely moving here, opening up headquarters here, or just branching out here. It’s that, mixed in with Seattle just being known as a tech hub — and also, we all love Seattle.”

Last year, the New York Times reported that U.S. officials were looking into whether Nvidia chips meant for data centers in Southeast Asia had ended up in China.

The chips in question had been bought by Megaspeed, a Singapore company that rented out computing power. U.S. export rules limit China's access to some of Nvidia’s most advanced AI hardware, to protect Nvidia's trade secrets and the United States' competitive edge over China in the AI arms race. Singapore police were also looking into whether Megaspeed had broken local laws, but at the time, Nvidia said it had no reason to believe the chips had been sent elsewhere.

The Times' report referred to Megaspeed's "mystery C.E.O." and noted U.S. officials were looking into the company's ties to China.

Things were quiet for a while; until two weeks ago, when the Times reported that U.S. intelligence officials suspected Alice Huang, a former Megaspeed executive, of gathering information for Beijing. Eco-Friendly Hardscaping Ideas for a More Sustainable Garden

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The increasingly common term “eco-friendly” encourages us to think more carefully about the products and practices we use every day. When applied to hardscaping, it brings to mind materials and techniques that minimize environmental impacts and reduce our carbon footprints.

It’s exciting to have such a vast and diverse selection of landscape materials available for our designs and garden improvements, but many of those options have a significant impact on our planet through their creation, shipping, and installation. By thinking about how our hardscaping choices affect natural resources, pollution, and local ecosystems, we can create landscapes that are attractive, functional, and potentially more affordable.

With 30 years of experience as a landscape designer, I’ve recommended countless decks, patios, paths, fences, and other hardscaping elements to my clients, admittedly with minimal guidance about the actual materials used. In recent years, I’ve seen a notable uptick in clients who have expressed strong interest in locally sourced materials and in repurposing or recycling materials for these features.

Some have even accumulated reclaimed bricks, large concrete pieces, and other materials with the intent of incorporating them into the design. This is exciting to me, as designs should balance the durability and longevity of products with a focus on resource efficiency and environmentally friendly options. These creative approaches are becoming more readily available and are being sought out by eco-savvy gardeners.

Read More: How Space and Structure Create a Living Oasis

Conventional hardscape materials have a “production history” that often comes with a heavy environmental cost. This might include the lumber we purchase, edging materials, or the pavers and path materials we specify. Their history includes not only energy and resources used in production but also shipping and transport, and ultimately, their disposal or reuse at the end of their useful lives.

Finding lower-impact materials takes some research, but sustainability doesn’t have to come at the expense of durability or functionality. Interestingly, eco-friendly options can be more cost-effective over the long run because of lower maintenance needs and durability.

Hardscaping elements such as decks and fences are great examples to explore. Decades ago, the question for clients in my Midwest location was “cedar or treated pine” for most fences or decks. Now, the evolution and wider availability of composite decking and recycled plastic lumber have created increasingly popular alternatives.

Of course, the manufacture of composite lumber isn’t carbon neutral, and its environmental benefits depend on the materials and manufacturing processes involved. However, products incorporating recycled materials can reduce demand for virgin resources, and their durability may offer advantages over time.

Another option for traditional wood features is to seek out responsibly sourced, third-party-certified lumber, such as wood carrying Forest Stewardship Council (FSC) certification. This certification means the wood and products were harvested, processed, and tracked according to established environmental and sourcing standards.

Patios are another popular garden feature, and while I appreciate a nicely installed patio made with concrete, new pavers, or bricks, there are some other options available. It is important to consider the permeability of our pavement selections as challenges of runoff and erosion come into play.

Permeable pavers, open-grid pavers, and some types of pervious concrete can allow water to pass through the surface and into underlying layers, helping reduce runoff when properly installed and maintained.

Repurposing brick or other paving materials from another project is also an opportunity to add a vintage look and possibly save money. I have a client who obtained more than 4,000 free vitrified clay pavers from the early 1900s (used for streets!) that we used for his paths and patios, and they look fabulous. Reusing these durable materials also kept them in circulation rather than requiring newly manufactured pavers.

Don’t neglect to check out the wide range of energy-efficient lighting options for your landscape as well.

I’ve also been involved with some rustic patios made of upcycled concrete pieces that were fitted together like puzzle pieces, the gaps filled with plants. Research “urbanite” patios and paths, and see the amazing results!

It’s a great use of a common material that frequently ends up in our landfills. Crushed concrete is another useful material for paths. It can also be used as a base layer for paths and patios, provided it is suitable for the intended application. Again, local sourcing can reduce the carbon footprint associated with shipping and transportation.

Our reasons for supporting eco-friendly hardscaping decisions in our landscapes include, of course, preventing waste, conserving resources, and protecting our local and regional ecosystems and habitats.

While the convenience of buying readily available new materials may be appealing, taking the time to look into salvaged, recycled, locally sourced, and responsibly manufactured alternatives can yield equally attractive results.

Whether you’re planning a new patio, replacing an aging deck, or adding a garden path, find out what materials might already be available for reuse. Explore these opportunities!

The FBI names 8 fraud schemes aimed at older Americans, from fake suitors to trusted caregivers. See how each works and how to stop it.

Criminals go after older Americans on purpose, and a single successful scheme can take a serious share of what a victim has saved. The average victim over 60 reported losing more than $38,000, according to the FBI. Someone living on retirement savings may never be able to earn back a loss that size, so knowing how to stop these schemes matters as much as knowing how they work.

Losses from cybercrime make up only one part of the money taken from older people. Victims over 60 reported losing more than $7.7 billion to cybercrime in 2025, according to the FBI. Financial exploitation of every kind, not only crime committed online, costs older adults an estimated $28.3 billion a year, according to an AARP study reported by the Federal Reserve. Complaint totals count only the victims who come forward, so some of the money taken from older people may never appear in a complaint at all. The way these crimes are sorted makes the losses even harder to count. One scam often hands its victim off to another, so a loss filed under one heading may have begun under a different one. Many of these schemes are also run by organized networks that work together, rather than by lone callers, and those networks make stolen money harder to trace and to recover.

The FBI set out to identify the typical fraud schemes that criminals use against elderly victims, from fake suitors on dating sites to con artists at the front door to trusted relatives and caregivers. Nearly every one of these schemes depends on trust the scammer hasn't earned. The scammer poses as someone the victim has reason to believe, such as a romantic partner, a grandchild, a government official, a charity, a contractor or a family helper. Each scheme is larger, more organized or harder to undo than its short description suggests, and each has a defense that can stop it. Reporting is part of that defense, because losses no one reports never reach the official count. Anyone targeted by a romance scammer or a similar con can report it online at ic3.gov, the website of the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center. Victims can also call the National Elder Fraud Hotline at 833-372-8311, according to the NIA. Every report brings the official count closer to the true cost.

Romance scammers don't count on carelessness. They count on loneliness. The FBI says fake suitors on dating sites exploit seniors' wish for companionship. That emotional need is an expensive weakness: people 60 and older reported $584 million in losses to romance and confidence scams in 2025, according to HousingWire. That total counts only the money filed under romance scams.

Judged by dollars alone, romance scams are far from the costliest fraud against seniors. People 60 and older lost $3.52 billion to investment fraud in 2025, according to HousingWire. The two categories overlap. A fake suitor met online can steer the conversation toward a crypto or gold "opportunity," and the loss may then be filed as investment fraud even though it began as a courtship. The romance figure likely understates how much older Americans lose through romance-style contact, so its smaller size shouldn't reassure anyone.

Money sent to these scammers rarely comes back. Scammers favor payment methods built for speed, and across all scams aimed at people 60 and older, they collected $257.5 million through crypto ATMs and kiosks in 2025, according to HousingWire. Cash fed into one of those machines can be converted and gone before a victim has second thoughts. Even then the losses may not stop. Fraudsters come back posing as officials or law firms and offer to recover the stolen money for a fee, and seniors lost $540 million to these recovery scams in 2025, according to HousingWire. A romance victim can lose money to the suitor, then to the "investment," then to the fake rescuer, and each loss is harder to reverse than the last.

Older adults can spot this scam before any request for money, at the moment a new contact pushes to leave the site where the two met. The FBI names social media and dating websites as the places where romance scammers find their victims. "The scammers typically initiate contact through text messages, social media sites, advertisements, or dating applications and then quickly move the conversation to a messaging platform," the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center said in its annual report, as reported by HousingWire. The defense is to keep the conversation where it started and never send cryptocurrency to someone known only online.

The cheapest time to stop a romance scam is at the first odd request, because after the first payment little money is left to save.

A pop-up warning or a phone call that offers to fix a broken computer is a request for access, not an offer of help. Victims lost over $1.3 billion in 2023 to fake helpers who "fixed" computer problems that did not exist so they could get inside the victims' devices, according to the FBI. Once inside, a scammer can see sensitive information, the kind of details that lead from a computer to a bank account. No one loses a sum that large to bogus repair fees. Losses on that scale come from scammers who go after the savings behind the screen, and the computer is simply their way in.

In the scheme's advanced form, the scammers stop working only through the screen and collect money in person. Scammers who open with a promise to fix a computer take payment in cash, gold, precious metals or wire transfers, the Post and Courier reports, and couriers pick up those payments at victims' homes or at other locations. Cash or gold handed to a stranger is very hard to get back, and a courier sent to collect shows that more than one person is working the victim. That gives older people one firm warning sign. When a computer problem leads to a request to hand cash or gold to a courier, the scam has shown itself, and that is the moment to stop.

Even a single state's losses reach into the millions. South Carolina residents 60 and older told the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center that tech support scams took over $9 million from them in the 11 months from January through November, according to the Post and Courier. Those losses came from one state and one age group in less than a year. The scammers want the money, so the first call should go to the bank, not to a repair shop. Anyone who has let a "helper" into a device should contact their financial institutions to protect their accounts.

Shutting the laptop won't stop this scam, but locking down the accounts will.

A grandparent scam caller doesn't need to fool anyone for long, only long enough to get money sent before anyone checks. The FBI describes impostors who claim to be a relative urgently needing money, exploiting family trust. The NIA warns that scammers may threaten people or pressure them to act immediately. In this scheme, that urgency is the main weapon. A grandparent who loves the caller believes the story. A grandparent told to act now feels that stopping to check would mean abandoning a grandchild in trouble. The pressure aims at a grandparent's loyalty rather than their financial judgment, so someone who handles money carefully everywhere else has no special protection here.

The defense is to refuse the deadline. Before sending anything, hang up and talk the request over with someone you trust, such as another family member. A second person ends the private, rushed conversation the scammer needs. A relative who is really in trouble can wait a few minutes while someone checks, but a scammer is usually exposed as soon as someone calls the real grandchild. That is why the caller pushes hardest against any delay.

Shame keeps many victims quiet afterward, because a person fooled through family trust may feel embarrassed in front of that same family. "Don’t be ashamed if you think you or someone you know has been a victim of a scam — it can happen to anyone," the NIA said. Advice from a federal health agency treats falling for the call as something that happens to ordinary people, not as a personal failing. Victims who decide to speak up have several places to start, including local police, the state attorney general and the Federal Trade Commission, according to the NIA.

Telling someone is the one thing this scam is built to prevent, whether before the money is sent or after.

Government impersonation scams work because people fear the government's power to punish them. The FBI includes this scheme among the typical frauds against older people because fake officials threaten arrest or prosecution to frighten victims into paying. The fake official often joins a scam that another criminal has already started, the FBI reports, with a second caller posing as a government official or a bank representative. That second caller's job is to make a fraud already under way look legitimate. A victim who believes they have finally reached someone who can help may be speaking with another member of the same group.

Scammers create panic on purpose to rush victims into payments that are hard to trace or reverse. "Scammers create a sense of urgency to produce fear and lure victims into immediate action," the FBI said. In these schemes, scammers demand cash, gold, precious metals or wires, according to the FBI, and couriers collect the payments using passcodes. The passcode makes the pickup feel like an official procedure rather than a stranger taking someone's savings. Once a courier carries cash or gold away, the victim has no bank transfer to stop and no card charge to dispute.

The type of payment demanded reveals the scam, however convincing the threat sounds. The FBI says that no genuine U.S. government agency asks to be paid with gift cards, in cryptocurrency or precious metals, or by wire transfer. A victim can apply that rule without judging a caller's tone or title. Anyone claiming to be an official who demands gold, wires or gift cards to keep someone out of jail is not acting for any real agency.

A caller can claim any title, but a demand for gold or gift cards is reason enough to hang up.

Scammers open this con with good news about a prize or a worthy cause, and the victim wants to say yes before any doubt sets in. The FBI says these fraudsters pose as charities or promise foreign winnings that can be collected only after paying a fee. People told they have won a contest or lottery then learn they must pay fees, according to the FBI. In every version, the victim has to pay before anything arrives, and nothing ever arrives. The prize exists only to justify the fee. The fake charity appeal works the same way, trading on the trust people give to good works. The payment is still the whole point. A request to pay for something a person has already won is not a step toward the prize. It is the theft itself.

Anyone can receive this pitch, including people who know fraud from the inside. Scammers have aimed it at William Webster, a former director of both the FBI and the CIA. "If it can happen to me, it can happen to you," Webster said. Scammers send the pitch to everyone, so protection can't depend on being too smart to fall for it. It has to be a habit everyone keeps.

A fee paid to collect winnings from abroad is almost certainly gone for good. Ron Miller, a special agent in the FBI's Washington Field Office, says victims are unlikely to get back what they lose to scams, and the odds are worse when the scammers operate overseas. That leaves the moment before payment as the only one that counts. A scammer can demand a fee only from someone who responds, and Miller advises people to ignore the call, letter or email altogether.

Nobody has to pay to collect a real prize, a real charity will wait while a donor checks it out, and hanging up is free.

Home repair con artists skip the inbox and the phone line and knock on the front door with an offer to fix the house. They show up at homes, collect payment up front and never do the work, according to the FBI. The homeowner pays for a promise and loses the deposit itself, with no half-finished job left behind to dispute. Described that way, the scheme sounds like one bad hire and one overpaid bill. It sounds like a local nuisance, not a serious threat to retirement savings.

The stranger on the porch can be the visible member of an organized network that works one community and then moves on to the next. CBC News reports that police in the Waterloo region of Ontario, Canada, say some people tied to these networks come from abroad and travel from town to town, pushing seniors into contracts with misleading sales pitches. A crew that moves between communities can do far more damage than one bad job, and victims in a single investigation in the Waterloo region lost more than $500,000 combined.

Some of these con artists add a badge to the contractor's pitch. People who pose as police officers exploit how readily the public trusts officers, according to remarks by Const. Chris Iden of the Waterloo Regional Police Service reported by CBC News. An older homeowner who faces a uniformed stranger in person on the doorstep has little room to stall or check the story.

Homeowners have one clear defense in how they settle the bill. In the scheme the FBI describes, the con artist takes the homeowner's money before a single repair is made. A homeowner who refuses to hand over the full cost at the start leaves much less for a con artist to take. The safest rule is to pay in stages, and only for work the homeowner can see and check, so a crew that disappears leaves with as little as possible.

A real contractor can wait to be paid for work that is done.

The FBI reports that some scammers reach older Americans indirectly, through television and radio advertising, without ever contacting them directly. The scammers speak to a broad audience at once, so they don't single out anyone by name. Reaching people through a broadcast gives a potential victim fewer reasons to be suspicious. No stranger calls out of the blue, and no unexpected email lands in an inbox to put anyone on guard. Viewers and listeners hear the pitch between ordinary commercials, and nothing marks it as different from the ads around it.

To sound trustworthy, these advertisers attach their pitch to genuine products that many older people have already heard of, such as credit repair or a reverse mortgage. The products themselves are legitimate, and the dishonesty lies in the advertisements. A real product says nothing about whether the person advertising it can be trusted. An older viewer who recognizes a familiar product such as a reverse mortgage may also hear a dishonest offer as routine.

The defense starts by keeping the product and the ad separate. An older viewer can trust that a service is real and still doubt the advertiser selling it, even when the offer sounds familiar and sensible.

The ad deserves the doubt, not the service it names.

Most fraud against older people needs a stranger to earn the victim's trust, but this scheme starts with someone who already has it. The FBI warns that trusted relatives or acquaintances exploit older people and take their money. Regulators treat this as a crime separate from a scam. FinCEN described elder theft as the taking of an older adult's money "by a trusted person," apart from scams that send money "to a stranger or imposter," as reported by the Federal Reserve. The usual advice against fraud is to hang up, check the story and never pay anyone whose identity can't be confirmed. That advice fails when the thief is the person the victim would ask for confirmation. A relative or helper may also have legitimate access to the accounts, so withdrawals that drain the victim can look like routine errands done with permission.

This kind of theft leaves the faintest trail in the official figures. From June 15, 2022, to June 15, 2023, financial institutions sent FinCEN 155,415 filings about suspected exploitation of older people's money, according to the Federal Reserve. Those filings cover theft by trusted people as well as scams by strangers. The FBI, however, gives no loss figure or case count for the family and caregiver scheme. When the person taking the money is also the one the victim relies on to manage it, the victim may have no one else watching the accounts.

The protection that fits an insider threat is a second, independent person who can spot trouble, not more suspicion of strangers. Naming a trusted contact at the bank adds that check without giving anyone new access to the money. A trusted contact can't see the accounts or act on them unless the account holder separately grants more access, according to the Federal Reserve. That makes it safe to choose someone outside the circle of people who already handle the victim's money.

With this scheme, the question to ask is not who is calling but who already has access to the money.

Astronomers trace a black hole’s powerful jet to a surprising source

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

A fast-feeding black hole in the galaxy 3C 120 launches its jet from a hot gas cloud where its disk ends, a billion miles from the center.

Some of the fastest-feeding black holes in the universe launch jets of plasma at nearly the speed of light.

Those jets come from the region around the black hole, and standard models struggle to explain how they form when so much gas is pouring in.

Now astronomers have looked closely at one of them, and the disk of gas feeding it stops about a billion miles (1.6 billion kilometers) short of the black hole.

Inside that gap, the gas appears to form a hot, swollen cloud. The measurements suggest the jet originates within this hot region, matching the arrangement theory predicts for launching a jet.

Hirofumi Noda of Tohoku University and Kazuhiro Hada of Nagoya City University in Japan led the work with colleagues across East Asia.

The result rests on one galaxy observed over a few weeks in 2025, so whether every fast-feeding black hole with a jet works this way is still open.

“By looking at the same black hole with state-of-the-art X-ray and radio telescopes, we were able to see both the hot plasma surrounding the black hole and the jet emerging from it,” Noda said.

“This allows us to directly connect the extreme environment near the black hole with the formation of the jet.”

The leading explanation for a black hole jet is that magnetic fields threaded through the black hole tap its spin.

Gas near the black hole has to hold those fields in place, and only a hot, thick flow of gas can hold enough of them.

A black hole that feeds slowly, like the one in the galaxy M87, has that kind of flow and fires a famous jet.

Fast feeding is a different case, because in standard theory it builds a cold, thin disk that reaches almost to the black hole.

Computer models find such a disk can’t carry strong magnetic fields inward or squeeze a jet into a narrow beam.

The galaxy 3C 120, in the constellation Taurus, breaks that rule. Its black hole has the mass of about 63 million suns.

Its surroundings shine at roughly 10% to 20% of the brightest a black hole that size can get. It fires a powerful jet anyway.

The team pointed the Japanese X-ray satellite XRISM at 3C 120 for about 55 hours in March 2025.

XRISM measures the energy of each X-ray far more precisely than earlier satellites, and the team used it to read the X-rays given off by iron atoms in the disk.

Near a black hole, that iron signal gets smeared out. Motion in the disk shifts it both ways, and the black hole’s gravity drags the whole signal to lower energies.

How badly the signal is smeared tells astronomers how close the disk’s inner edge comes to the black hole.

Five weeks later, 18 radio dishes around the world observed the same galaxy for 20 hours as the Global Millimeter VLBI Array.

Linked across more than 5,900 miles (9,500 kilometers), they resolved details about 50 millionths of an arcsecond across.

The Very Long Baseline Array and the East Asian VLBI Network traced the jet farther out.

The iron signal showed the thin disk ending at about 17 times the black hole’s gravitational radius, give or take six.

For a black hole of 63 million suns, that is roughly a billion miles (1.6 billion kilometers) from the center, farther than Saturn orbits from the Sun.

Inside that edge, the gas has changed into a thick, hot flow that reaches hundreds of millions of degrees.

Earlier X-ray instruments had hinted at a gap like this but couldn’t pin it down.

The jet narrows as it approaches the black hole.

When the team traced that narrowing all the way in, the beam was no wider than about 7 gravitational radii at its base.

That places the inferred jet origin inside the hot zone, rather than in the cold disk.

The X-ray data gave one more number: the outer disk is tilted about 31 degrees to our line of sight, while the jet is tilted only about 19 degrees.

If the jet follows the black hole’s spin axis, the different viewing angles suggest the outer disk is tilted relative to that axis.

The hot inner flow may be tilted too, and a tilted inner flow should wobble slowly around the spin axis, like a top.

The team saw a possible flicker in the galaxy’s X-ray brightness repeating about once a day. Only four or five cycles were caught, though, and they call it marginal.

A wobble isn’t the only candidate, though. Magnetic fields packed tightly around a black hole can also erupt in bursts, and that could make the X-rays pulse on a similar schedule.

A hot inner flow appears necessary for a jet, but the team doesn’t think it’s sufficient.

Some galaxies with fast-feeding black holes show the same kind of truncated disk and launch no jet at all, which leaves black hole spin as the likely deciding factor.

This study couldn’t measure the spin of 3C 120, because the disk ends too far out for the X-rays to carry that information.

Whether the inner flow is as magnetically loaded as the theory needs is also unconfirmed. The once-a-day flicker would help settle that, but it needs a longer X-ray watch than 55 hours.

The team is now observing a heavier black hole the same way.

“Our next goal is to build a more complete picture of how matter, magnetic fields and black hole rotation interact in these extreme environments,” Noda said.

For now, 3C 120 offers a clearer view of how a rapidly feeding black hole can still launch a powerful jet.

The full study was published in the journal The Astrophysical Journal Letters.

Scotland’s waterlogged landscape hides thousands of years of carbon

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

A satellite image of Scotland’s Flow Country reveals a waterlogged landscape where peat bogs have stored carbon for thousands of years.

Today’s Image of the Day from NASA Earth Observatory features the Flow Country, a peatland landscape in the Scottish Highlands.

Landsat 8’s Operational Land Imager captured this view on March 4, 2024, recording a spring scene in one of Europe’s most important blanket bog regions.

Dark brown ground stretches between scattered lakes in northern Scotland, broken by green fields and sharply outlined blocks of trees.

From above, the contrast between bogs, farmland, and tree plantations becomes clear.

What the image cannot show is the deep store of ancient plant material beneath the bogs, where carbon has been accumulating for thousands of years.

The brown expanses mark boggy ground dotted with lakes.

Around Thurso, on the right side of the image, lighter green farmland contrasts with the darker green shapes of conifer plantations closer to the center.

Those geometric tree plantations stand out against the irregular outlines of water and peatland.

Together, the patterns show how farming and forestry occupy parts of a region whose distinctive character comes from persistent wetness.

At ground level, the bog’s surface has a texture that the satellite view cannot fully resolve.

Mossy hummocks rise above hollows, while pools interrupt the vegetation. Grasses and heathers grow across this uneven, largely treeless terrain.

“Blanket bog” describes how peat spreads over the landscape, covering slopes and hollows rather than remaining confined to a single basin.

Its formation requires conditions that keep the ground wet for long periods.

Northern Scotland has a useful combination of climate and terrain for building bogs.

Rain arrives frequently from the Atlantic, while rocks, soils, and landforms slow its escape.

Cool conditions also limit water loss through evaporation and plants.

Under these circumstances, water collects faster than the landscape can shed it. Bog plants grow, die, and leave their remains in saturated ground.

Usually, microbes break down dead vegetation, returning much of its carbon to the atmosphere.

Waterlogged soils restrict oxygen, slowing that process. Some plant material remains only partly decomposed and gradually becomes peat.

The growing peat then helps retain more water, sustaining conditions in which additional bog plants can flourish.

Over time, this relationship between vegetation and water builds a thick organic layer beneath the living surface.

Sphagnum mosses play a central role in that process. Their contribution is easy to overlook beside a forest’s towering trees, yet these small plants help create an ecosystem capable of preserving carbon far beyond a single growing season.

Parts of the Flow Country have been forming peat for around 9,000 years.

In places, deposits exceed eight meters in thickness, preserving the accumulated remains of countless generations of plants.

That depth also holds evidence of environmental change.

Pollen and plant fossils within the peat record earlier vegetation, offering researchers a way to investigate how the landscape developed and how people influenced it.

In 2024, the Flow Country joined UNESCO’s World Heritage List.

The designated property covers approximately 1,870 square kilometers across seven component areas, recognizing an exceptional example of an actively accumulating blanket bog.

Its value extends beyond what lies underground. The mixture of pools, ridges, wet hollows, and vegetation supports distinctive wildlife communities, including breeding birds.

More than 30 species of sphagnum moss occur here, adding variety to ground that can appear uniform from a distance.

The Flow Country illustrates a much larger global pattern.

Peatlands occupy only about 3% of Earth’s land surface, yet the United Nations Environment Programme reports that they store roughly twice as much carbon as all the world’s forests.

That comparison concerns carbon already stored, rather than the amount absorbed each year.

Much of a peatland’s importance comes from protecting material that accumulated long before modern climate records began.

Drainage can disrupt that protection. As peat dries and oxygen enters, decomposition accelerates, allowing stored carbon to escape as carbon dioxide. Burning can also release carbon accumulated over centuries.

UNESCO identifies historical drainage and woodland creation among the pressures that have affected parts of the Flow Country.

Restoration is therefore part of protecting the landscape’s future. Seen from orbit, the bogs offer few obvious clues to their age or depth.

The brown surface is simply the visible top of a much older system, still growing wherever water allows plant remains to accumulate.

SoftBank’s billion-dollar loss may have put a cap on AI’s natural gas ambitions

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

SoftBank might not be the first to invest in a hot trend, but when it does, it goes big.

Witness its $30 billion bet on OpenAI in early 2025 or its announcement in February that its SB Energy subsidiary would spend $33 billion on a 9.2-gigawatt natural gas power plant. If you can’t be first, be biggest, I guess.

That philosophy apparently spilled over into procurement, where orders were also supersized. Starting about two years ago, SoftBank reportedly put down deposits on more than 13 gigawatts of turbines, almost 50% more than it needed.

The decision to over-order undoubtedly helped secure turbines and speed up SoftBank’s projects.

“SB Energy did not have a choice except to overbook turbines in the future if they wanted some turbines right away,” Peter Gardett, CEO of Noreva, told TechCrunch via email.

The energy world was caught off guard by the swiftness and magnitude of AI’s rising power demands. Queues for turbine orders now extend into the early 2030s. As natural gas was shaping up to be a go-to power source for the AI boom, CEO Masayoshi Son bet that by deploying his investment firm’s significant war chest, he could corner a portion of the market. If the AI world wanted power for its data centers, it would have to come to him.

Now, that bet appears to have fizzled. SoftBank announced a $7.1 billion quarterly loss on Tuesday, with over half of that stemming from losses related to power-generating equipment. The investor had paid turbine manufacturers nonrefundable deposits to secure orders, and now it considered much of those worthless.

The move was also a costly bit of price discovery, Gardett said. Rather than overpay and potentially still be short of turbines, SoftBank overpaid and over-ordered.

“This charge is just that price discovery finally happening,” Gardett said.

The magnitude of that misplaced bet — over $3 billion — reflects some of the electricity mania that has overtaken the AI world. Faced with years-long waits to connect to the grid, many hyperscalers and developers have turned to natural gas as a sort of energy security blanket, something that is proven and familiar.

As AI data centers spend more time in operation, their operators are getting a better feel for what’s required to keep them operating. Natural gas might seem like a safe bet, but there are still risks, including winter curtailments when supplies dip and demand spikes, Brent Nelson, senior managing director of markets and strategy at Ascend Analytics, told the Equity podcast Wednesday.

In light of that, Nelson said the grid might still be their best bet. Most data centers would be best served by a grid connection, albeit a smaller one than they might normally request. To make up for the shortfall, large batteries are a cost-effective choice, Nelson said. They’d help shave peak loads or ride out requests from the utility to curtail electricity use.

Data center operators might be coming to that same conclusion, leading the natural gas market to develop more slowly than SoftBank expected.

SoftBank’s natural gas-related losses are unlikely to have a domino effect, leading to mass cancellations of gas power plants. AI’s power demand appears to be too large for that. For now, natural gas is riding high, though SoftBank may have found its ceiling.

9 foods in the Mediterranean diet and the trades behind them, per Mayo Clinic

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Mayo Clinic's Mediterranean diet advice works as a set of trades. See what 9 foods replace and how firm the evidence behind each one is.

Most diets are known by what they forbid. The Mediterranean diet makes more sense through what people who follow it eat at almost every meal and what they save for a few nights a week. Produce, whole grains and olive oil are everyday foods in this way of eating, while fish and eggs are among the foods kept for only a few meals each week. How often a food appears is a sign of how much it matters to the pattern, and the pattern sets proportions across a week rather than a fixed menu. That schedule turns most food choices into trades. When a reader moves one food into a daily slot, another food has to leave the plate, and the health case for the change often depends on which food left.

The diet earned its reputation for heart health from people who ate this way as a whole culture, not from people who added one food to an otherwise unchanged diet. The research behind it comes mostly from Mediterranean populations, according to StatPearls. That evidence describes a whole way of eating more than a set of portions. For that reason, health advice on the diet is about starting a pattern of eating, not taking a dose of any single food, and the benefit belongs to the whole pattern before it belongs to any one item. A reader can turn that into a working rule: follow the shape of the diet faithfully, and treat any single serving number as a target to aim near rather than a line that guarantees a result.

Mayo Clinic lays out practical steps for starting a heart-healthy Mediterranean eating pattern, covering foods from seafood and nuts to eggs and herbs and spices. Each of those steps comes down to a trade, and the evidence behind the trades is not equally strong. Some swaps have direct proof behind them. Others are cautious limits set where scientists still disagree. A reader who knows which is which can decide where to be strict and where to be flexible. The slides that follow take each food in turn and look at what it replaces, how strong its case is and how closely a reader needs to follow the amount attached to it.

The vegetable target looks very different on the plate depending on how the vegetables are prepared. One serving of leafy greens is two cups, while one serving of cooked vegetables is half a cup. The same four servings can be a heaped salad bowl or a modest side dish. A reader who struggles to finish large salads can reach the target with smaller portions of cooked vegetables.

Eating produce every day is common ground, but the exact counts are less fixed than the habit. Sharon F. Daley of Cape Cod Hospital in Hyannis, Mass., and Melissa R. Hinson of Conemaugh Memorial describe a guideline framework in StatPearls that differs from Mayo Clinic's daily serving counts for vegetables and fruit. A reader can hold firm to eating produce every day and to fruit in place of dessert, and let the balance between fruit and vegetables follow taste, the season and the budget.

Whole grains belong on the plate every day because their benefit is broad rather than dramatic. Mayo Clinic advises eating them each day, and the research points the same way. A European Heart Journal analysis of over 6,500 adults links whole grains to possible improvement in a group of heart risk factors rather than in just one, according to Harvard Health. Small gains across several risk factors can add up to more than a large change in one. People get that kind of modest benefit only by repeating the habit, so whole grains fit an everyday meal better than an occasional one.

The case for whole grains has limits. The research tracks risk factors, not heart attacks or strokes. It supports whole grains as a sensible staple without showing that they prevent heart disease, which makes them a well-founded bet rather than a settled cure. Mayo Clinic measures whole grains by the ounce, and 1 ounce is about one slice of bread. A single slice of whole-wheat toast at breakfast therefore counts as only one serving. A reader who stops there keeps the daily habit in name more than on the plate.

Eating more than a slice or two is easier when bread is not the only source. Mayo Clinic also names bulgur, barley and farro, which a cook can use as the base of a salad, a grain bowl or a side dish. With those grains on hand, a large daily helping becomes part of an ordinary meal rather than a chore.

Make whole grains the base of the plate at every meal, and count each slice of bread as just one ounce toward the day.

Cooks get the heart benefit of olive oil only when they use it in place of butter. The oil has a reputation as a health food in its own right, but Mayo Clinic includes unsaturated plant fats in the diet because people who use them instead of butter-type fats cut their heart disease risk and their LDL cholesterol. The gain depends on the fat that leaves the pan more than on the oil that goes in. A cook who adds olive oil to salads but still cooks with butter has added a food without making the trade.

The proven payoff of the swap is narrow, and it centers on bad cholesterol. Fats from plants that are unsaturated appear to bring down a person's LDL cholesterol along with total cholesterol. Replacing butter with plant oils and spreads had no effect on HDL cholesterol or triglycerides, according to USDA NESR. The improvement is real, but it touches one part of the blood lipid picture rather than all of it.

The heart-disease benefit rests on softer ground. "Replacing saturated fats with unsaturated fats may help lower the risk of heart disease," Mayo Clinic staff wrote. Research on trading butter for plant oil does not settle the question, USDA NESR reports, and the studies differ on whether the swap raises or lowers cardiovascular risk and by how much. People can count on the swap to improve their cholesterol and should treat any heart protection beyond that as a likely bonus rather than a promise.

Some plant fats belong on the butter side of the trade. Blood pressure stayed the same when palm oil, coconut oil or cocoa butter gave way to unsaturated oils, according to USDA NESR. These tropical fats still count among the ones to swap out, though a reader should not expect lower blood pressure from the change. At the store, the useful test is whether a fat is unsaturated, as olive oil is, not whether it comes from a plant.

Make liquid plant oil the everyday cooking fat in place of butter, and count it as a cholesterol step rather than a cure for heart disease.

Fish is one of the proteins the diet saves for a few nights a week, and part of its value lies in the meat it replaces on those nights. The health case for the fish itself is firmer for some benefits than for others. Mayo Clinic says the omega-3 fats in seafood may curb inflammation and stroke risk, and the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements reports that intake of EPA and DHA, two omega-3 fats, lowers triglyceride levels. The triglyceride effect is stated as established, while the stroke and inflammation benefits that justify eating fish are worded only as possibilities. Mayo Clinic links the omega-3 fats to fatty fish, yet no recommended amounts have been established for EPA and DHA. Omega-3 is a plausible reason to eat fish, not a measured prescription, so the safer bet is the fish itself, eaten as a meal.

Mayo Clinic asks for more fish than the heart association does, setting a target of 2 to 3 seafood meals a week. The American Heart Association calls for 1 to 2 servings weekly, according to the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements. Either number makes sense only as a swap. A fish dinner helps when it takes the place of a meat dinner, not when someone adds it to an otherwise unchanged week. Each fish night also calls for only a modest portion, since an adult serving is 3 to 5 ounces. A reader who reliably eats fish instead of meat at the heart association's lower level is already making the trade, and Mayo Clinic's range is the level to aim toward.

Fish oil does not stand in for fish. The diet ties the benefit to fatty fish. Unless a person's cardiovascular risk is high, the American Heart Association doesn't advise taking omega-3 pills, according to the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements. A person who takes a capsule still eats the meat dinner, so the main part of the trade never happens. The cooking method matters too. "Stay away from deep-fried fish," Mayo Clinic staff said. Frying a fillet turns a fish night back into the kind of meal the pattern is meant to replace.

With seafood, the number is negotiable but the swap is not: grilled or baked fish on a night that would have been meat is the habit that counts.

Nuts belong in the Mediterranean diet as a backup source of fat, not the main one. Extra virgin olive oil does most of the work of supplying unsaturated fat at every meal, and nuts rank behind it as a supplier of that same fat. That supporting role keeps the portion small. The diet calls for nuts in four quarter-cup servings a week, according to Mayo Clinic. A quarter cup is a small handful, so each serving adds a modest amount to the fat a cook already gets from the oil in the pan. A reader who sprinkles almonds on a salad or eats a handful of walnuts in the afternoon is adding to the fat the oil already supplies, not building meals around nuts. Nuts matter here for the healthy fat they contribute to the whole pattern, more than for any food they replace.

The advice also covers a specific kind of nut, not every product that contains them. Only raw, unsalted nuts count toward the weekly target, so a can of salted cashews or a seasoned snack mix doesn't meet it as written. That gives the reader a clear way to divide the advice. The weekly count works as a target to aim near, much like the other serving numbers in the diet. The choice at the store is a firm requirement, and it is the one part of this advice that the reader controls completely every time they shop.

Nuts are the diet's measured extra: a small weekly portion that counts only when it is bought plain.

Mayo Clinic advises eating dairy only in moderate portions and choosing low-fat versions over higher-fat ones. The trade therefore happens inside the dairy case, with leaner products going in and richer ones coming out. For most people, the change would show up at breakfast, since whole milk and 2% milk are the products the advice asks them to cut back. The call for moderate portions and the call for low-fat products do not rest on equally firm proof, and a reader should know which one to hold tightly.

Recent research has called the low-fat rule into question. Participants in a recent trial ate full-fat dairy three times a day, according to the University of Toronto, and their results did not show the harm the low-fat rule is meant to prevent. "Those that had three servings of dairy didn't have adverse levels of blood cholesterol or lipids or evidence of insulin resistance," said Harvey Anderson, a professor of nutritional sciences at the Temerty Faculty of Medicine at the University of Toronto. Blood pressure also improved in the groups eating dairy, so on that measure the full-fat eaters did better rather than merely no worse. For these markers, choosing low-fat looks more like a precaution against a harm the trial did not find than a proven requirement.

That challenge still leaves moderation standing. The trial lasted only 12 weeks, according to the University of Toronto. That is long enough to show early changes in cholesterol and blood pressure but too short to show what years of full-fat dairy would do. Even the researcher questioning the low-fat rule does not argue for more dairy, since Anderson favors eating many different foods and limiting how much of any one goes on the plate. His view and Mayo Clinic's call for moderate portions point the same way, and the trial's three daily servings were an amount researchers tested, not a license.

Moderation is the rule worth keeping, though Mayo Clinic gives no daily or weekly count for it. Its one concrete gauge is the size of a single serving, which matches four dice, and readers have to judge for themselves how many of those servings still count as moderate.

Keep dairy portions modest even without a set number, and treat low-fat as a sensible default that researchers have reopened for debate, not as a rule to obey at all costs.

The Mediterranean diet asks people to eat less red and processed meat, but it stops short of banning it. Mayo Clinic advises reducing both, and the heart benefit of doing so depends on what takes the meat's place. Among people who ate pulses instead of all their red meat, the risk of ischemic heart disease was 10% lower, according to EMJ. The gain belongs to the trade, not to the cut alone. A plate with the meat simply removed does only half the job, and the empty space needs beans, lentils or other pulses.

The limit worth holding firmly is the one on processed meat. EMJ reports that ischemic heart disease risk ran 15% lower when pulses took the place of processed meat, and cardiovascular disease risk ran 13% lower when they stood in for processed poultry. Both results involve processed foods, whether red meat or poultry, so the common thread is processing rather than the color of the meat. These findings center on processed meat and say nothing on their own about whether fresh cuts carry the same risk. That fits the diet's own guidance, which still allows lean cuts in small portions.

The findings come from a large group of adults in the U.K., 118,272 people in the UK Biobank, according to EMJ. A link of this kind supports the trade, but it doesn't prove that eating pulses instead of processed meat is what lowers the risk.

The practical rule is to be strict about processed meat and to fill the gap with pulses. Lean fresh cuts in small portions are an allowance the diet makes on purpose, not a lapse to feel guilty about.

Eggs sit on the limited side of the Mediterranean diet, and the limit falls on the yolk rather than on the egg as a whole. Mayo Clinic allows up to four whole eggs a week, and it sets that limit because of the saturated fat in the yolk rather than because of any doubt about the egg's nutritional value. For most people the cap asks for little change, since typical intake already runs three to four eggs a week, according to TIME. A reader who follows it mainly avoids drifting toward an omelet every morning.

The heart risk behind the cap is real but small for any one person. In 2019, researchers estimated that an extra half egg a day would mean about 11 additional heart disease cases among every 1,000 people across 30 years, according to TIME. Nearly everyone in that group would see no difference, which makes this an effect that appears across a population rather than a sharp danger to each eater. The four-egg figure is a nutritionists' suggestion rather than a number taken from a study, and the science underneath it is open. "The existing literature is still controversial and inconclusive for nutrition experts and researchers to conclude the safety of eggs," said Victor Wenze Zhong, a postdoctoral fellow at Northwestern University's Feinberg School of Medicine and a co-author of the study behind that estimate. A fifth egg in one week breaks no rule. A steady climb past four, though, moves toward the intake the research flags.

The most useful trade happens inside the egg itself. Mayo Clinic's cap counts whole eggs and targets the fat in the yolk, while egg whites contain no cholesterol, according to TIME. Zhong himself recommends whites as a stand-in for whole eggs. A scramble made with one yolk and several extra whites keeps eggs on the plate without adding to the weekly yolk count.

Be strict about how many yolks you eat and relaxed about how many eggs.

Herbs and spices earn their place mainly for what they let a cook leave out of a dish. Mayo Clinic lists them among the foods the Mediterranean diet is most often high in, which places seasoning among the diet's regular foods rather than its occasional ones. The diet calls for them because a cook who seasons food richly needs less salt. The CDC names spices, garlic and citrus juice as substitutes for salt in cooking, so the swap is shared health advice rather than one diet's quirk. A cook who keeps those on hand has several familiar ways to make food taste full without the shaker.

The swap does less than it seems to, because the saltshaker was never where most of the salt came from. Packaged foods and restaurant meals supply most of the sodium people eat, the CDC reports, and bread adds more because people eat it often even though each serving carries only moderate sodium. Seasoning at home therefore trims only a minor share of a person's salt. Bread is also one of the foods the diet encourages, so a cook who seasons generously still takes in salt that no herb can remove. The full trade has two parts: season richly at home, and cut back on packaged foods, sauces and takeout.

Mayo Clinic sets no serving target for herbs and spices, so a reader has no amount to measure against. That fits the trade itself, because success shows in the salt left out rather than in the amount of seasoning put in.

Here a reader can be as generous as they like, and the strictness belongs to the salt hidden in the rest of their food.

A few weeks ago, I rented a big cabin outside of the city with friends. It was the first cold weekend in New York, the kind where you might actually need to throw on the heavy jackets that were too much for those 55-degree fall days. Nestled in this mountain house, we cozied up around a fire in fleece sweatshirts and fuzzy blankets. Before bed, we turned the heat on. I slept cozy like a baby in the womb, but immediately upon waking, my skin felt tight and dry. I coated myself in moisturizer, but it was no use. My face—which is usually oily and acne-prone—was dull and rough, like it’d never produced a drop of oil in its 29 years.

At first, I thought it was a sign of the times. I’m about to turn 30, and my skin’s just getting drier. But when I headed back to Brooklyn, where my stuffy apartment produces enough natural heat on its own to not need the radiator cranked up, my skin went back to normal.

I’ve heard of this phenomenon before—when your skin gets dehydrated once you turn on the heat in your house—but this was my first experience. And I was determined to get to the bottom of it before my radiator goes on for good and the winter uglies take over. Two dermatologists weighed in—and it looks like this isn’t necessarily preventable but totally treatable.

“Indoor heating lowers the relative humidity in the air, which makes moisture evaporate from your skin more quickly,” says dermatologist Annette Czernik, MD. “Our skin naturally produces oils that help protect it and keep moisture in, but sometimes that's not enough when the air is really dry.” You might apply moisturizer and feel like you didn’t even do anything, she adds.

You’re also not always in the clear if you have oily skin, says Dr. Czernik. “A lot of people don't realize that your skin can be oily and dehydrated at the same time,” she explains. “This is because your skin can still produce oil while losing moisture from the dry air.”

You may also feel tightness, roughness, stinging, flaking, and even itching. “As your skin loses more moisture, its protective barrier can become weaker,” says Dr. Czernik, hence why you can experience some irritation as your skin isn’t as strong enough to fight it off. The air can also make your skin look dull or even feel more sensitive, since it’s working so hard to hold onto the moisture it needs, explains Dr. Czernik. “In more severe cases, you might experience redness, peeling, or even small cracks in your skin,” she adds.

Naturally, with colder air outside, your skin might get drier too, only exacerbating the issue. “When the temperature drops outside, the air usually holds less moisture,” says Dr. Czernik. “Then, when you go indoors, you're exposed to heated air that can also be very dry.” You’re basically losing moisture on both sides, no matter what you do. “Going back and forth between the cold outdoors and warm indoors can make that dryness more noticeable,” she adds. “This is why someone might have perfectly normal skin during the summer but struggle with dryness all winter.”

“When you turn the heat on, the air inside your home can become very dry,” explains dermatologist Ariel Ostad, MD. And one of the easiest ways to add moisture back into the air is a humidifier. “This can help slow down how quickly your skin loses water throughout the day or night,” Dr. Ostad adds.

He recommends getting a bedside humidifier, like the Canopy Bedside Humidifier, for your bedroom and running it while you’re sleeping. “You may notice that your skin feels less tight and dry when you wake up in the morning,” he says. “It can also help with dry lips and irritated skin.” But remember to clean it regularly, as bacteria and mold can build up in your device.

“If your skin starts getting drier, I would first look at the products you're using every day,” says Dr. Ostad. Both Dr. Ostad and Dr. Czernik recommend switching to a thicker moisturizer. Ideally, find one that both moisturizes (meaning it contains ingredients that pull water into your skin, like squalane, hyaluronic acid, panthenol, and glycerin) and seals in hydration (aka ingredients that act as a physical barrier to reduce moisture loss, including ceramides, oils, butters, and silicones). But you could also opt for two moisturizers nightly. I prefer to use a lightweight moisturizer, then follow it up with a barrier cream.

“It's also a good idea to apply your moisturizer right after washing your face or getting out of the shower,” adds Dr. Czernik. If your skin is still damp, even better. This helps pull all that water into your skin (which helps plump it up and decrease dullness), plus it eliminates time for moisture to evaporate from your skin.

Just as hot air can pull water from your skin, so can hot water, explains Dr. Czernik. It doesn’t mean you need to do a daily cold plunge, but opt for mild to warm water temperatures, as scalding water can also increase skin irritation.

Some of the products in your routine could actually be making your dryness worse. Put down all the strong exfoliants, whether it’s your salicylic acid cleanser, an acid-based peel pad, or a retinol serum.

Instead, Dr. Ostad suggests using a gentle, fragrance-free cleanser “that won't strip your skin of its natural oils.” And if you love your exfoliants, you don’t have to ditch them completely, he adds, but you might want to cut back to once or twice per week at most. “You don't need to completely change your skincare routine, but making a few small adjustments can really help during the colder months,” he says.

“If your skin is just a little dry, you can usually manage it at home with a good moisturizer,” says Dr. Ostad. “However, if the dryness continues for a few weeks despite changing your skincare routine, I would recommend seeing a dermatologist.” If you notice your skin becomes extremely itchy or irritated, that’s a good sign to see a doctor too. “Cracking, bleeding, or painful peeling are signs that something more serious could be going on,” adds Dr. Ostad.

First, look at the cuff: You want an upper that’s stiff enough to support your joint if you stumble or step on a loose rock, but not so stiff that you can’t maintain a natural stride on the tail. Which of those you prioritize depends on your objectives: Long days on relatively smooth trail call for softer support, while stiffer boots may help prevent injury on steep, loose ascents. Pay attention to the padding on the cuff, which should cradle your ankle rather than squeeze it.

Midsole construction can also make a big difference in how a boot feels on the trail: An EVA midsole will feel bouncier and softer, more akin to a running shoe, while a PU midsole will provide a somewhat stiffer, more supportive ride, though often at a weight penalty. A shank, often made of a material like carbon fiber, will provide additional stiffness and structural support to the sole, though it should still allow your foot to flex naturally.

Finally, pay attention to the outsole; while it may not play a role in supporting your ankle directly, a grippy tread can help prevent stumbles and slips, which can lead to injuries. Softer rubber will grip better on hard surfaces, though it will shorten the boot’s lifespan as it wears away faster. A harder, less pliable rubber may feel more slippery on surfaces like slickrock, but can grip well on soft trail, allowing its tread to “bite” into the path.
