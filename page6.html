The U.S. House Financial Services Committee has advanced H.R. 8957, known as the American Reserve Modernization Act of 2026, bringing the proposed legislation one step closer to a full House vote.

Introduced on the 21st of May 2026 by Republican Representative Nick Begich of Alaska, alongside Democratic Representative Jared Golden, the bill seeks to give the U.S. government’s Strategic Bitcoin [BTC] Reserve a formal legal foundation.

It proposes that the U.S. Treasury Department establish two separate structures within 180 days of the legislation becoming law. This includes a Strategic Bitcoin Reserve exclusively for Bitcoin and a Digital Asset Stockpile for other cryptocurrencies held by the federal government.

Under the proposal, Bitcoin owned by the federal government through criminal or civil asset forfeitures and other qualifying sources would be transferred to the Strategic Bitcoin Reserve.

Here, the most significant provision is a 20-year minimum holding requirement, which would prevent the government from selling, swapping, auctioning, or using reserve Bitcoin as collateral during that period. At the same time, the bill also allows the Treasury Department to explore budget-neutral ways of acquiring additional Bitcoin without increasing the national debt.

Separately, the legislation proposes a Digital Asset Stockpile to manage other cryptocurrencies held by the federal government. Lastly, it also introduces requirements for transparency, auditing, and congressional reporting to ensure proper oversight of these assets.

The bill is particularly significant because it seeks to give the U.S. Strategic Bitcoin Reserve a statutory foundation. For its part, President Donald Trump had already established the reserve through an executive order in March 2025.

However, H.R. 8957 aims to formalize its structure and introduce additional safeguards, including the proposed 20-year holding period.

As of now, twenty months have already passed, and the U.S. government reportedly holds around 328,000 BTC, largely from seizures and forfeitures, while governments globally hold about 435,000 BTC.

Henceforth, if H.R. 8957 becomes law, the reserve would receive statutory backing, making it more difficult for a future administration to reverse the policy unilaterally.

The bill received approval from the House Financial Services Committee on 16th September 2026 in a 28-21 vote, with all Republican members voting in favor and all Democratic members opposing it.

Then, on the 24th of November, the committee formally reported the revised legislation to the full House, placing it on the Union Calendar and making it eligible for a floor vote. However, its chances of becoming law remain uncertain.

This is because the 119th Congress ends on the 3rd of January 2027, leaving limited time for the bill to pass the House, clear the Senate, and receive presidential approval.

GigaChat 4 Debuts With 1M-Token Context and Two Model Sizes

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The GigaChat Team announced GigaChat 4 on November 26, 2026, releasing 708B- and 37B-parameter mixture-of-experts models with native one-million-token context windows. Both activate only a subset of their parameters per token, support tool calling and multi-step agent tasks, and allow Instant or Reasoning responses to be selected per request within one checkpoint.

The flagship 708B-A41B uses approximately 41B active parameters per token, compared with roughly 3.8B for the compact 37B-A4B. Both were trained on sequences extending to the full context length for work with long documents, collections of sources and large codebases. This arrangement replaces GigaChat 3.5’s separate instant and reasoning checkpoints.

Both models use a 3:1 mix of Gated DeltaNet layers and multi-head latent attention layers enhanced with DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA). Gated DeltaNet maintains a fixed-size recurrent state, while DSA selects up to 2,048 earlier positions for each query rather than computing attention over the entire sequence. The companion technical report says indexer and selection overhead can outweigh the attention savings at short contexts or small decode batches.

The flagship has 64 decoder layers and 256 routed experts, selecting eight per token together with one shared expert. The compact model has 32 layers and 192 routed experts, using the same combination of eight routed experts and one shared expert.

For the 708B model, context extension progressed from 32K to 128K tokens before indexer training and joint optimization at the native length. After supervised fine-tuning, specialist models underwent reinforcement learning in mathematics, coding, tool use and multi-turn agent workflows. Multi-teacher on-policy distillation then consolidated their capabilities into one model offering both response modes.

Under the team’s evaluation protocol, the 708B model scored 71.26% on Terminal-Bench 2.0 in Reasoning mode and 60.47% in Instant mode, versus 30.30% and 12.94% respectively for GigaChat 3.5. Its reported Reasoning results also include 80.89% on SWE-bench Verified and 79.55% on BFCL v4, although GLM-5.3 is listed higher at 86.67% on SWE-bench Verified.

The compact model’s reported Reasoning results include 85.00% on LiveCodeBench v6, 70.80% on SWE-bench Verified and 72.00% on BFCL. For the flagship, evaluation combined public runners and scoring code with the team’s own implementations, including seven-hour timeouts for specified agentic benchmarks.

The team’s demonstrations emphasize tasks that chain artifacts and tools: the flagship modified a Mario cartridge’s palette in its binary file and ran the game on a Mac M4, produced an STL vase for 3D printing, and used MCP access to the Cadenza music workstation to compose a track before building an accompanying visualization site.

The flagship model card lists FP8, BF16 and GGUF instruction-tuned variants, along with base and intermediate checkpoints, and releases its instruction-tuned checkpoint under the MIT License. The compact model card lists the same variant categories but uses the Noncommercial License for Individuals 1.0. The detailed technical report currently covers the 708B model; a dedicated report for the 37B model is planned for later.

Both cards document SGLang and vLLM serving, per-request mode selection and OpenAI-compatible tool calling. They recommend Pi for agentic tasks, with Codex CLI and DeepSeek Harness as alternatives for interactive terminal work.

The flagship’s documented FP8 configuration uses two nodes with eight H100 80 GB GPUs each. The compact model’s example uses one H100 80 GB GPU, with approximately 40 GB required for its weights before attention caches, recurrent states and activations.

GigaChat 4 makes deployment terms part of the model choice alongside benchmark scores: the flagship combines MIT licensing with a two-node example, while the compact model offers a single-GPU path under noncommercial terms. For prospective users, the compact checkpoint’s usage restrictions are therefore as consequential as its lower hardware requirement.

It takes the risks in the order that hurts users most, with what Andrej Karpathy, Pieter Levels, Patrick McKenzie, Jeff Atwood, Arvid Kahl, Simon Willison and Joel Spolsky said about each one, every record linked to its source.

Before you invite more people, close the risks that would hurt them first. Take every API key out of the code and revoke any that leaked, confirm payments on your server and leave card data with the processor, cap what one user can trigger, and prove you can restore a backup. Then have a fresh reviewer audit the whole app, because vibe coding security comes down to the checks the AI did not run for you.

GitHub's own description of vibe coding separates the quick prototype, where little of the result gets read or tested, from the human-led kind, where you review, test and refine. Real users move you into the second kind, whether you planned it or not.

An API key in your code works like a password for anyone who can see the code, and the bill or the breach lands on you. The processor keeps cards off your server; McKenzie's lesson is that it does not keep fraud off your store, so plan some detection of your own.

Principle: Accept non-zero fraud for conversion. McKenzie holds that merchants should trade off conversion rate against fraud rate rather than minimize fraud, since every anti-fraud step also reduces the customers a business can sell to.

This is the counterweight. Every check you add also turns away some honest buyers, so choose the few that stop the attacks you actually see.

So treat fraud control as a dial, not a wall. Start cheap: limit how many payment attempts one account or address can make in a short time, watch for bursts of small failed charges, and look at failed payments often during your first weeks. Tighten only where you see abuse, and watch your sign-ups when you do.

Principle: Design assuming users will be evil. Atwood holds that social software must be designed from day one assuming some users will be malicious, because naive designs like trackbacks eventually make the whole community suffer.

Jeff Atwood, The Day The Trackbacks Died (2025-06-04)

Trackbacks are his example of a feature built for good-faith users that the whole community paid for once others abused it.

Principle: Never mix untrusted text with tools. Willison holds that an LLM application must never combine untrusted input such as emails or scraped pages with access to tools or private data, because any attacker text can take control of the output.

Simon Willison, Imitation Intelligence, my keynote for PyCon US 2024

If your app reads emails, web pages or user uploads and the same model can also call tools or see private data, text written by an attacker can steer what it does.

If your app puts a model inside the product, such as a chatbot that searches user records or an assistant that sends messages, check each one against this rule. Where the model reads text you did not write, take away its tools and private data, or split the job so the part that reads the text cannot act. Test it yourself by pasting instructions into a message or a page the model will read, and see whether it obeys them.

"Don't rely on your host or anyone else to back up your important data. Do it yourself. If you aren't personally responsible for your own backups, they are effectively not happening."

Jeff Atwood, International Backup Awareness Day (2025-09-11)

A host's backup is a promise you have not checked. A copy you control is the one you can count on.

"The minimum bar for a reliable service is not that you have done a backup, but that you have done a restore ."

Joel Spolsky, Lets Stop Talking About Backups (2009-12-14)

A backup you have never restored from is a guess. Do one restore before you invite more users and see what comes back.

What should I check first in an app built with AI?

Start with keys: make sure no API key sits in your code or in a public repository, and revoke any that did. Then check that payments are confirmed on your server, that one user cannot trigger unlimited emails or charges, and that you can restore a backup.

Revoke the key at the provider and create a new one before anything else. Removing it from the Git history is slow and often unnecessary once the old key no longer works, and GitHub secret scanning can check the whole history for others.

Is code written by AI less secure than code written by hand?

The risks are the familiar ones, such as hardcoded credentials, insecure endpoints and unvalidated input, but they are easier to miss when you did not write the code. Review and test the result yourself, and ask a fresh reviewer for evidence, not just a verdict.

Should my repository be public or private?

A public repository can be seen by everyone on the internet, so make it private unless it is meant to be open. Never upload keys either way.

Do I need to store credit card numbers to take payments?

No. A payment processor can hold the card and give you a token, and your server confirms the payment with a key the browser never sees, after checking that the amount matches.

Google's crawler docs now list pageburst, which renders pages for Google Ads and ignores robots.txt, plus more existing user agents to check in server logs.

Google identified pageburst as a user agent that renders pages to measure Google Ads quality and landing page safety and ignores robots.txt rules. Its two strings look like a browser’s and do not contain “Googlebot”; a bot rule created using “Googlebot” will not capture them.

Pageburst is one of 12 existing user agents Google added to its crawler documentation on Nov. 23, along with a corrected entry for APIs-Google. Google’s crawling documentation changelog explains:

“These crawlers and fetchers are not new; we’re documenting existing high-volume clients that are more likely to show up in server logs and that we’ve received questions about.”

The 12 belong to Google products other than Search, such as Google Ads, Gmail and Google Cloud.

Google’s special-case crawlers page, which lists crawlers used by specific Google products, says pageburst “renders web pages for Google Ads quality, landing-page safety, and page understanding.” The entry adds that “it’s unaffected by crawling preferences.”

The desktop string identifies as Chrome on Linux and the mobile one as Chrome for iOS on an iPad, and both include “compatible; pageburst.”

AdsBot, which checks web page ad quality for Google Ads, has its own robots.txt token. Pageburst’s entry lists no token and says the user agent “ignores robots.txt rules.”

The highlights show what to look for in server logs and where Google states its robots.txt policy.

Google’s documentation says user-agent strings can be faked. Special-case crawlers use the IP addresses defined in Google’s special-crawlers.json; these have a reverse-DNS match of rate-limited-proxy-***-***-***-***.google.com. The pageburst entry does not say what occurs when a website blocks the Google Ads check.

Besides pageburst, each of the five other newly documented special-case entries says the user agent “ignores robots.txt rules.”

Google’s user-triggered fetchers page covers the six newly documented fetchers and says that because a user requested the fetch, these fetchers “generally ignore robots.txt rules.” Three of those (AppEngine-Google, Google-Apps-Script and Google-Cloud-Tasks) send requests for code running within Google’s platform-based products. The remaining three are GoogleImageProxy, which is used to load image content into email applications such as Gmail, along with Google Web Preview and the PageSpeed Insights fetcher, both with strings ending in Chrome-Lighthouse.

Several of the newly listed user agents seem to use standard browser strings. For example, a string from GoogleImageProxy appears similar to Firefox 11 on Windows NT 5.1; the strings for Google Web Preview and PageSpeed Insights, and one of PlayStore-Google’s, were created using Chrome strings. A filter that only identifies “Googlebot” would miss all of these.

Google has moved APIs-Google, which makes requests to the webhook notification systems developers create within Google Cloud and Google Workspace APIs, from the list of special-case crawlers to the list of user-triggered fetchers. APIs-Google’s push requests, the changelog says, “come from the user-triggered fetcher IP ranges and ignore robots.txt rules.” Google added a missing semicolon after “APIs-Google” in its documented string and tells developers to modify any code that was previously using a hardcoded version of the earlier string.

Prior to the Nov. 23 update, Google had included robots.txt as a method to block requests from APIs-Google in its documentation. In an Oct. 21 capture, the robots.txt token was present on the special-case page. On Oct. 30, a capture of the APIs-Google page showed “Use robots.txt” as one of two methods to block requests from APIs-Google. Now the APIs-Google page says that it “ignores robots.txt rules”; for controlling the delivery of notifications, the page points to either the developer who created the notification system or Google Cloud’s abuse form for unexpected requests.

Pageburst renders pages for ads in Google and does not follow robots.txt. Check for “pageburst” in the log entries for your servers and CDNs, verify with Google’s procedures that the IP addresses are in its special-crawler ranges, and see if your pages were delivered to the verified requests, or if a challenge or a 403 was returned.

Roger Montti covered Google’s advice on Nov. 18 that general bot blocking may keep Googlebot from crawling content when moving a CDN; the same advice should be applied to other Google clients that do not have the name Googlebot.

Google’s special-case and user-triggered pages both say their lists aren’t exhaustive and cover the requestors “more likely to show up in log files.” The crawling documentation changelog, which is where Google logs additions like these, has an RSS feed.

Nepal: Predatory Lending Abuses Exacerbated by Conflict in the Gulf

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Loan shark victims demand justice after 38 days of protest in Kathmandu, Nepal, March 7, 2024. © 2024 Skanda Gautam/SOPA Images/Sipa via AP Photo Thousands of borrowers face exorbitant interest on fraudulent loan agreements taken out to fund labor migration and other needs.The Nepali government has criminalized abusive practices, but police and courts often side with loan sharks against distressed borrowers.Lost job opportunities due to war in the Gulf region have deepened the crisis facing many borrowers.

(Tokyo) – Authorities in Nepal should hold illegal moneylenders accountable for abuses against borrowers, including unlawful land dispossession, arbitrary eviction, threats and acts of violence, and sexual exploitation, Human Rights Watch said today. Conflicts in the Gulf region, where many Nepalis are migrant workers facing exorbitant recruitment fees, have made more people vulnerable to extortion by illegal lenders.

“Despite government commitments to protect borrowers, illegal practices are rife in Nepal’s informal credit sector, leaving those with the fewest resources dependent on moneylenders who can take everything they own,” said Meenakshi Ganguly, Asia deputy director at Human Rights Watch. “The government of Prime Minister Balendra Shah should carry out the government’s commitments to ensure that the law against fraudulent and abusive lending is consistently enforced.”

Human Rights Watch interviewed 18 borrowers, as well as local government officials, lawyers, and activists from six districts in the Terai region where informal money lending is rampant, reviewed government reports, and examined over 50 cases in Nawalparasi district.

Human Rights Watch found that many families are struggling with unmanageable debt particularly as migrant workers in the Gulf States during 2026 have faced reduced incomes or involuntary travel delays ahead of starting jobs due to the conflict between the US, Israel, and Iran. War-induced disruption amplified pre-existing abuses, including reliance on unauthorized individual agents and unreasonable recruitment fees. Human Rights Watch previously found that companies in Gulf states play a critical role in driving these illegal costs by refusing to cover costs and fees to recruiters that they know will be borne by workers.

Government investigations have shown that, under Nepal’s informal credit practices, a borrower signs or thumbprints an unsecured personal loan contract, known locally as kapali tamasuk, which can falsely list a principal amount three to ten times more than the cash received. These loans, known as meter byaj, in some cases compound at annual rates ranging from 36 percent to 60 percent or more, compared with less than 10 percent charged by banks.

As low-income households that had relied on remittances disrupted by the war have shifted their limited resources toward expenses for food, fuel, and healthcare, they have been increasingly unable to repay their loans, leading to a surge in defaults. In response, some moneylenders are forcing evictions or land transfers, including through violence, in the worst cases tying people into generational debt. Nepal authorities are not pushing for effective long term solutions beyond encouraging and facilitating debtors and creditors to enter pre-court settlements.

Following the outbreak of conflict in the Persian Gulf, in March 2026, the Nepal government suspended both institutional and individual labor permits to protect its citizens, including to Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, and Kuwait, with an estimated 2,000 Nepalis denied labor permits daily. Thousands were stopped from travelling at the international airport despite possessing valid medical clearances, verified visas, and binding employment contracts, while administrative delays also led foreign firms to cancel labor contracts. However, the authorities failed to step in to protect aspiring migrant laborers who still had to service their high interest meter byaj loans, leaving them exposed to violent enforcement by loan sharks.

“They did not just hit my father to get the money,” said one victim. “They beat him in the middle of the road during the day so that everyone else in the village would see what happens when you cannot pay the meter byaj. The local goons told us, ‘If you don’t sign over the land title today, your family will receive a corpse tomorrow.’”

While abuses, including violent attacks and forced evictions, appear to be increasing due to conflict related disruptions, many moneylenders have long wielded political and economic power over borrowers. Claiming that the justice system routinely favors the moneylenders, borrowers have staged three extended protests in Kathmandu over the last four years—the longest for 442 days—and three long marches. Each time, the government agreed to resolve debt issues but failed to honor its commitments to protect victims. Consequently, the borrowers have regularly protested at district and provincial levels as well.

Although government has criminalized such loans, borrowers and activists in Madhesh Province said that police have repeatedly refused to file reports when victims seek protection. Instead, they said, the police typically dismiss abuses and intimidation by moneylenders as “private civil disputes.”

“When the moneylender came to break our door, we ran to the police, but the officers told us to go home and settle personal transactional disputes ourselves,” said a Rautahat district migrant worker.

The police also back moneylenders when they use signed blank checks and banking laws to threaten defaulters with arrest, or use informal deeds as the basis to seize land and evict families. “The lender made me sign a blank check when he gave me a loan, and later on he filled it for 10 lakhs (US$6,500) and sent the police to threaten me with jail when the check bounced,” said a Siraha district farmer.

Borrowers also cite cases in which the authorities do not intervene even when lenders use patently unlawful loan agreements to obtain asset foreclosure orders. “The lender told me openly that he owns the courts and the administration,” said Shiv Shanker from Gaur, Rautahat district, who after facing abuses from a moneylender, is leading protests. “When the state shuts its doors on a poor farmer, death feels like the only place left to hide from this meter byaj.”

On May 5, 2023, the government criminalized informal loans as an “improper transaction,” and declared that forging loan contracts, threatening borrowers, or fabricating loan amounts, are punishable by up to seven years in prison. However, enforcement remains weak. Successive governments have established inquiry commissions to address the crisis, but over 65,000 complaints registered with inquiry commissions remain unresolved due to a lack of legal provisions and administrative bottlenecks.

These failures have contributed to deadly outcomes including suicide. Santidevi Mahato from Biratnagar died by suicide on June 21, following threats from a moneylender because she could not service her debt, people interviewed said. After borrowing 150,000 Nepali rupees (US$975) in October 2015, she had repaid 400,000 Nepali rupees (US$2,600) in installments. Yet, violating their agreement, the moneylender seized her land and threatened to evict her from her house.

Under international human rights law and standards, governments have the obligation to take steps to protect people from serious rights abuses caused by private actors, such as moneylenders, including through investigating and prosecuting serious crimes.

On July 17, the government led by Prime Minister Balendra Shah concluded a nine-point pact with protest leaders to eliminate predatory lending, after debt victims began a march to Kathmandu. The government should take immediate legal and policy steps to institutionalize its commitment, Human Rights Watch said. Nepal’s donors and international partners should assist the government to establish systematic debt relief, and grievance mechanisms that can support debt restructuring.

“The Balendra Shah government was elected with the promise of change, to overturn existing oppressive structures,” Ganguly said. “More closely regulating moneylenders and ending their chokehold on farmers and laborers is a crucial step in ensuring lasting social justice.”

Loan Sharks and Migrant Workers from Nepal

Nepal’s informal credit system is rooted in its feudal history, where local elites used predatory debt to trap peasants in tenancy and systems of generational servitude such as Kamaiya, Haliya, and Haruwa-Charuwa.

The present-day meter byaj system evolved around a spurt in labor migration during the decade-long civil war in Nepal, from 1996 to 2006, and rapidly spread after the 2007 political unrest in the Terai region. An estimated 7 million Nepalis work abroad, about half in India and a majority of the rest in Gulf countries, contributing a third of the national GDP. For many households, migration became one of the few viable ways to earn a living, while the absence of accessible and affordable institutional credit left them dependent on informal lenders to finance the costs of securing employment abroad.

Some local moneylenders and corrupt agents demanded exorbitant fees to help rural youth find jobs abroad, particularly in the Gulf States and Malaysia. Land or property was borrowers’ main collateral, but they often later discovered that their contracts were fraudulent, including falsified sums and forged documents. The schemes exploited bureaucratic complicity at government land offices to transfer property titles despite contracts being illegal, and borrowers lost their assets due to a lack of protection from the government.

In interviews, debt victims described devastating cycles in which migrant workers are forever bound to serving a predatory moneylender, while the authorities fail to intervene. Ramnath Musahar from Susta, Nawalparasi district, said the family borrowed to send two of his brothers to work in the Gulf States in 2015, but despite servicing the loan, their debt had grown enormously:

My brothers sent back Rs. 1,200,000 (US$8,000) from their wages, and we sold our plots [of land] to pay an extra Rs. 1,800,000 (US$11,000). But the lender went to the district court with a fake contract and claimed we still owe him Rs. 5,000,000 (US$33,000). The court looked only at the paper and our thumbprints; it did not look at our tears. The police came and threw our belongings into the dirt. We went from being independent farmers to beggars overnight.

Conflict Between the US, Israel, and Iran Exacerbates Debt Risks

Since the US and Israel began their assault on Iran on February 28, 2026, and Iran subsequently blocked and attacked ships traveling through the Strait of Hormuz and attacked sites across the Gulf States, the fallout is being felt far beyond the region.

In Nepal, UN agencies warned that “rising fuel prices, closure of shipping routes, and growing financial uncertainty are placing mounting pressure on economies, labor markets and vulnerable households.” According to the World Bank, Nepal’s real GDP growth could fall from 3.67 percent in 2024 to 2.3 percent in 2026 due to reduced tourism, higher transport costs, and supply chain snags. Rising inflation, a weakening currency leading to a rise in import costs, a spike in fuel costs, and stalled fertilizer supply have placed additional pressure on low-income households, and increased demands for overseas employment.

Hoping to seek jobs to support their families, many rural youths have taken loans from loan sharks, who have responded by raising rates. Many families were stuck servicing those loans, even as people were unable to travel because of conflict-related disruptions.

Workers already employed in the Gulf States are facing job losses, unpaid leave, wage reductions, and other disruptions that have reduced their ability to keep sending remittances. Compared with the same period the previous year, Gulf-bound labor approvals plummeted by 52 percent—from 228,165 down to 109,249—between the outbreak of the war in late February and mid-September, according to Department of Foreign Employment data. At least two Nepali workers have been killed due to the conflict, and 15 injured.

Many are returning to Nepal stripped of their anticipated income, and unable to service their loans. The authorities have failed to act as moneylenders crack down on defaulters, beating them, evicting them, and forcing land or asset transfers. The lenders are also seeking court-ordered asset foreclosures and even arrest warrants based on the loan contracts, including contracts that borrowers alleged contained fabricated loan amounts. As a Mahotari district borrower observed, “The law says meter byaj is a crime, but police demanded receipts I never got, so the thief walks free, and I am made the criminal.”

Women-led households, Dalits (considered an “untouchable” caste), and Indigenous ethnic communities have even fewer protections from violence such as beatings and sexual violence due to their caste, gender, and discrimination. One woman from Dhanusha district said she was raped by a moneylender under threat of eviction.

My husband was trapped in a labor camp abroad and couldn’t send money. The lender came to my house with the contract and said he would evict my children the next morning unless I “compromised” with him. He held our roof over my head like a weapon. I had to endure his abuse in silence because a poor woman in this village has no voice against a powerful man who holds her land paper.

Abuses by Moneylenders

While the conflict has exacerbated the crisis, debt-ridden families had already proliferated in Nepal, particularly in Terai districts. The authorities have failed to protect these families.

Chirai Chamar, a widowed Dalit worker in Paklihawa, Nawalparasi district, while struggling to gain control of her inherited family land, had borrowed 20,000 Nepali rupees (US$130). A moneylender offered to help with the legalities in exchange for a portion of that land. “A moneylender, from whom I had borrowed Rs. 20,000, came to me saying he will help me to acquire all the papers required to own my family land, and in return I need to give him two katthas (650 square meters) of land,” she said.

He then grabbed an additional four katthas (1336 square meters), paying Chamar a grossly undervalued sum of Rs. 150,000 (US$970). When rights activists intervened to reclaim Chamar’s land and restore her livelihood, local thugs threatened them and destroyed Chamar’s crops. Despite complaints, the police and the local officials failed to assist her.

Ashamaya (name changed), a resident of Thecho, Lalitpur, supported her family by running a small tea shop. However, when her father-in-law fell ill, she had to close the business to care for him. Left without income as the medical expenses mounted, she borrowed money from a moneylender, who charged an extortionate annual interest rate of 120 percent, which she could not pay. Under pressure, she took on another usurious loan simply to pay off the old one.

Although she settled that debt by selling a piece of land, she had to borrow again, this time to cover her husband’s medical costs, but she no longer had enough land for collateral. The moneylender coerced her into sex with multiple men after he forced her to sign a new contact for Rs. 2500,000 (UDS$16,167), amounting to five times her initial debt.

Seeking a Remedy

Debt victims have started to organize under various banners and groups, demanding that the government protect them from abusive loan sharks. In 2022, after hundreds walked barefoot for weeks from districts including Siraha, Dhanusha, Mahottari, Sarlahi, Kapilvastu, and Nawalparasi to stage protests in Kathmandu, the government at that time establisheda three-member Commission for the Investigation of Loan Sharking (CILS) in April 2023, to collect complaints, study structural issues, and resolve loan-sharking disputes.

By May 21, 2023, when the District Administration Offices stopped accepting these complaints, over 28,000 had been filed across 67 districts, with an alarming 21,552 cases concentrated in Madhesh Province. However, the commission only resolved and closed 5,115 loan disputes. Several victims reported abuses by district offices. “We went to the Chief District Officer expecting justice, but instead, the police and the lender ganged up on us, warned us that going to court would mean total land auction, and scared us into signing an agreement to pay half the inflated sum,” said a woman from Siraha.

As complaints persisted, including of violent encounters in which the police failed to intervene, the authorities largely resorted to symbolic agreements with the victims, followed by the repeated formation of new commissions. A commission was set up in March 2024, led by former high court judge Tej Bahadur Karki, and another in March 2025, led by Baburam Regmi. In November 2025, the Regmi Commission reported a total of 41,409 complaints registered in 43 districts. Of these, 6,393 cases had been resolved, while 35,016 complaints remain pending.

Each commission exposed the vast scale of the crisis but political commitments to break the stranglehold of moneylenders remain unfulfilled. Nawalparasi’s Deputy Chief District Officer Poshan Raj Bhandari told Human Rights Watch that, “The existing law will not be enough to resolve this problem; the government needs to think of a separate legal process to resolve this.”

When Prime Minister Balendra Shah’s government was elected in March 2026 with a large majority, victims had cause for hope. However, instead of the government ensuring a collective debt moratorium, victims say that moneylenders have convinced the police to issue a surge of arrest warrants against debtors.

In response, victims have organized new protests. Nigra Nabin, a leader of the protest movement, said that based on “past experiences” the victims “had three long-standing demands” including the nullification of all illegal agreements and the underlying loan contracts that support them; a new law to institutionalize these actions and establish special legal procedures; and a separate entity dedicated to ensuring justice for the victims.

On July 9, the anti-loan shark movement began an 11-day justice march from Janakpur to Kathmandu. On the sixth day of the march, Home Affairs Minister Sudhan Gurung traveled to Nijgadh to hold direct talks with the protesters. Following three days of negotiations, the government accepted the three core demands and signed an updated nine-point deal, leading the protesters to call off the march.

The victims are waiting. One of them, Sobita Devi Mahato, said: “If this is not resolved, the only resort I have is to go to the Home Ministry and hang myself there.”

The US has announced a fresh raft of tariffs as President Donald Trump pushes ahead with his signature trade policy.

The new taxes charged on imports to America are set to hit many of the country's biggest trading partners - China, the European Union and 12 other jurisdictions, including Japan, South Korea and Taiwan.

The White House began investigating the trade practices of other countries in March after the US Supreme Court ruled Trump's previously announced Liberation Day tariffs were illegal.

China is facing the highest tariff rate of up to 40%, followed by Vietnam at up to 35%, with others facing either 20% or 25%.

The US Trade Representative (USTR) announced the proposed tariffs together with extensive reports of how each jurisdiction's trade practices purportedly impact American commerce.

The new duties are not expected to take effect until next year and have to go through rounds of public consultation and, potentially, hearings.

Tariffs are taxes levied on imported goods. The amount is typically calculated as a percentage of a good's value and paid to the government by businesses importing products.

Some of these rates will be effectively reduced because of caps on tariffs Trump has agreed to. Individual countries also face different levies.

The proposed tariffs will affect goods from China, the European Union, Singapore, Switzerland, Indonesia, Malaysia, Cambodia, Thailand, Korea, Vietnam, Taiwan, Bangladesh, Japan, and India.

The USTR's Jamieson Greer said the latest tariffs issued would "level the playing field for American workers and chart a new, more sustainable course for the world economy".

He said that many of America's trading partners had "for decades" adopted industrial strategies which included "targeting manufacturing, wage and labor suppression".

They also employ "other market distorting practices that tilt the trade balance in their favour while eroding America's manufacturing base", Greer said.

But economists have warned tariffs can fuel inflation and push prices up for consumers if businesses pass on higher costs, as well as dampen global trade.

Trump has repeatedly threatened or imposed tariffs during his second term, arguing they will boost US manufacturing and create jobs.

Despite a poor result for the Republican party in the midterm elections, the president has continued to press on with his flagship trade policy.

Trump is also seeking to cut the US trade deficit - the gap between the value of goods it buys from other countries and those it sells to them.

Trump's so-called Liberation Day tariffs were struck down by the Supreme Court earlier this year

These new levies are build on a "stronger legal foundation" under US law compared with Trump's April 2025 Liberation Day tariffs, Singapore Management University economics lecturer Chang Pao-Li said.

Trump previously invoked emergency presidential powers to impose tariffs that have since been struck down, while his latest approach relies on the USTR's authority to impose tariffs to address certain foreign trade practices, she said.

The levies are still likely to face legal challenges, but there has been a "historical precedent" for such tariffs remaining in force, Chang said.

Many tariffs imposed under Section 301 - a law that allows the US to probe and retaliate against foreign trade practices - during Trump's first term remained in effect during his successor Joe Biden's administration.

However, Stephen Brown, chief North America economist for Capital Economics, said the latest tariff announcement was "not as alarming as it looks", due to the taxes "essentially replacing" previously announced tariffs made in the summer.

"The increase in the effective tariff rate looks set to be a touch lower than we've been assuming for our forecasts, although the new tariffs will still provide a marginal boost to prices," he said in a note.

The additional tariff burden is likely to be small because a limited number of goods are subject to the new duties, Brown added.

Jurisdictions affected by the new wave of tariffs have voiced their disapproval over the Trump administration's move.

Washington has also proposed a 10% tariff on goods from Brazil, as part of a separate investigation over what it says are "harmful trade practices" undercutting American workers.

Brazilian authorities expressed their "outrage" at the announcement, saying that the move is aimed at pressuring the government to change regulations around issues like digital trade.

The South American nation faces duties on finished aircraft and sugar sold to the US.

"Brazil will not yield to such threats," President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva's office said.

The European Commission rejected the new US tariffs, saying it would investigate the reasons for bringing them in. It pointed out that tariffs are limited to the 15% previously agreed.

The latest tariff rate is "not consistent" with the reality of bilateral economic-trade cooperation between the US and Vietnam, Vietnamese Deputy Prime Minister Bui Thanh Son said.

The Japanese government said the US tariffs were "regrettable".

C.V. Rao, who helped shape the production and post-production pipelines behind S.S. Rajamouli‘s “Baahubali” films and “RRR,” has become the first technologist from South India to be named a Fellow of the Society of Motion Picture and Television Engineers (SMPTE).

“It also reflects the tremendous technical talent available in India,” Rao tells Variety. “We have developed strong capabilities in adopting and implementing advanced filmmaking technologies, and we believe the next opportunity is to contribute more actively to innovation, technology development and international standards.”

“Baahubali” brought challenges well beyond what the studio had expected. The scale of the production demanded major upgrades in grading, processing power, storage, bandwidth and technical staff. For “Baahubali 2,” the team set up a dedicated peer-to-peer link between Hyderabad and Los Angeles to send large volumes of material straight to Imax. The experience also led to the launch of ANR Sound & Vision, a one-stop post facility that takes projects from data management and editing through VFX, sound, grading and final mastering.

The Dolby Cinema version of “RRR” had its roots in Las Vegas, where a Dolby Cinema demo at NAB led to talks with cinematographer K.K. Senthil Kumar, who pitched the idea to Rajamouli.

“We had to travel to Germany for the Dolby Cinema processing of ‘RRR,’ which raised an important question: why should Indian filmmakers depend on overseas facilities for these capabilities?” Rao says. “That experience became a major motivation for establishing Dolby Cinema processing capabilities at Annapurna Studios.”

Rao points out that India had skilled VFX artists and capable post houses well before either film. What Rajamouli’s productions brought was a new order of scale and complexity, which pushed studios to grow their capabilities, strengthen their teams and get departments working together more closely. Both films also relied on international partners. Attempting projects of that size entirely within India 10 years earlier would have been far tougher, he says, given the gaps in specialized infrastructure, processing capacity and experience at the time.

“The most significant impact was the confidence these projects created across the industry,” Rao says. “They demonstrated that Indian studios could handle increasingly complex work and encouraged greater investment in our technical ecosystem.”

Rao started out as a graphic designer and 3D artist. From there he worked his way through documentary and feature editing, scanning, lab work, visual effects and color grading. That time spent across departments taught him where crews run into trouble during the shoot and in post, and at Annapurna he has used it to find weak spots in workflows and build infrastructure around what productions actually need.

“We have always believed that understanding the filmmaker’s requirements is just as important as understanding the technology itself,” he says.

Annapurna has put money into virtual production and one of India’s largest motion-capture stages. Interest is growing, but uptake has lagged expectations. During COVID, the studio saw virtual production as a way for filmmakers to stay inside controlled environments and cut down on location work and travel. The format, though, demands heavy preparation: digital sets have to be built, and camera moves and lighting mapped out, before anyone shoots a frame.

“In our industry, approvals and shooting schedules often get finalized very late,” Rao says. “Even when filmmakers are interested, there may not be enough time to prepare everything properly. Under these circumstances, conventional shooting becomes the easier option.”

Motion capture is gaining ground on complex action scenes, digital characters and performance-driven VFX. Rao expects both technologies to become standard tools once more productions have hands-on experience and learn where they genuinely save time.

On infrastructure, he maintains that Indian facilities hold their own against established international hubs and can access most of the same advanced technology. Several major Indian productions now finish large chunks of their post work at home. Where India trails is research and development. Mature markets, he says, benefit from close collaboration among studios, technology companies, research institutions and creatives, while India needs to spend more on homegrown technology and on testing new workflows.

“We strongly believe infrastructure alone cannot deliver quality,” Rao says. “The knowledge, experience and creativity of the professionals behind it make the real difference.”

Annapurna has also set up Dolby Vision and Dolby Atmos mastering facilities. Rao finds big-budget productions increasingly sold on premium formats, while mid-budget filmmakers are still weighing whether they pay off commercially. With streaming and high-end TVs giving audiences quality viewing at home, he sees premium theatrical formats as a reason to get people back into cinemas.

“However, there needs to be better alignment between mastering capabilities and theatrical exhibition infrastructure,” Rao says. As more premium-format screens open across India, he expects producers to commit to these formats earlier in their release plans.

AI is already proving useful in concept development, storyboarding, previsualization, restoration and repetitive VFX tasks, letting teams test ideas faster and automate slow work.

“However, storytelling, performances, emotional expression and final artistic decisions must remain under human creative control,” he says.

He sees AI working alongside existing tools rather than replacing them. Complex digital characters driven by performance may still need precise motion capture, with AI handling supporting jobs.

Whether any of this lowers costs depends on the project. Virtual production can trim location, travel and scheduling expenses, but it requires investment in infrastructure, digital assets and prep, and without proper planning it can end up costing more. AI is getting easier for smaller filmmakers to access, yet consistent professional results still call for skilled teams, computing power and quality control.

“We believe smaller productions can benefit from shared facilities and specialized technology services rather than investing in expensive infrastructure independently,” Rao says.

Having overseen technical pipelines for more than 500 feature-film deliveries, Rao traces most post-production trouble back to poor planning. The most frequent error is bringing post teams in too late, when VFX, sound, grading, data management and delivery specs should ideally be on the table in pre-production. Late creative changes are the other big problem. Changes come with the territory, he acknowledges, but major revisions near delivery put artists and technicians under enormous strain.

“Teams may need to work extended hours, repeat completed tasks and rush important processes to meet deadlines,” Rao says. “This creates unnecessary stress, increases costs and can sometimes compromise the quality of the final product.”

For India to land complete high-end international productions, Rao wants a friendlier business climate, consistent government policy, competitive incentives and simpler approvals, along with deeper partnerships with global studios and more spending on research, training and integrated facilities.

“The opportunity is to position India not merely as a cost-effective outsourcing destination, but as a reliable creative and technical partner capable of handling complete productions to international standards,” he says.

Training is part of that equation. Rao wants studios to offer structured internships and mentorship, educational institutions to rework curricula alongside working professionals, and government to back specialized training centers and research.

As an SMPTE Fellow, he plans to take part in technical discussions where India’s experience can help. An industry that works across many languages and production scales, under demanding workflows, has lessons to offer in digital cinema, post-production and data management, he says. He frames the fellowship as a two-way exchange, bringing ideas from global engineers and researchers back home in return.

Looking five years out, Rao expects AI, real-time rendering, virtual production and motion capture to reshape filmmaking as they increasingly converge. Annapurna will keep upgrading its infrastructure, exploring new workflows and training teams, judging each new technology on whether it solves real production problems rather than on novelty.

“Ultimately, we believe technology investments should be driven by the value they bring to filmmaking, not simply by technological advancement itself,” Rao says.

Hong Kong: Fire Anniversary Underscores Growing Repression

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image A memorial near the site of the deadly fire at the residential complex at Wang Fuk Court in Tai Po, Hong Kong, December 2, 2025. © 2025 Ng Han Guan/AP Photo

(London) – The Hong Kong government should ensure meaningful accountability for the devastating fire that killed 168 people one year ago and end its suppression of peaceful criticism, Human Rights Watch said today.

On November 26, 2025, Hong Kong’s deadliest fire in decades engulfed seven residential high-rise buildings at Wang Fuk Court, Tai Po. The authorities initiated an independent investigation and arrested three dozen people implicated in crimes. The Housing Minister resigned. But the authorities have also threatened and arbitrarily arrested those engaging in criticism and activism concerning the fire.

“The Hong Kong government responded to the horrific Wang Fuk Court fire by using Beijing’s tactics of shutting out the voices of the victims and promising an elusive accountability,” said Elaine Pearson, Asia director at Human Rights Watch. “The authorities need to recognize that the only way to prevent similar tragedies in the future is to allow people to speak out safely and hold those responsible fully accountable.”

The authorities have engaged in apparent acts of repression of free expression in the weeks ahead of the anniversary:

The media reported in November that the police made inquiries with Catholic churches planning masses to commemorate victims of the fire.A church in Tai Po subsequently removed references to the fire from its announcement and instead described the service as a mass for “the brothers and sisters who died in the past year;” hundreds nevertheless attended.A priest at another Catholic church posted on Facebook that three police officers had inquired about a planned memorial mass marking the anniversary, even though the church had not publicly announced the service.On November 22, a group working with migrant workers who survived the fire cancelled a book launch and drama event scheduled ahead of the anniversary, citing “unforeseen circumstances.” On November 23, the online news platform Boom News said it would remove some videos featuring interviews with survivors, quoting the whistleblower Edward Snowden on “censorship and fear.” The Boom News founder, Tang Ho-wing, had been arrested in September for alleged “sedition” for reporting on another politically sensitive anniversary.

Police Commissioner Joe Chow said police routinely asked organizers about upcoming public events, while decisions on whether to proceed “rested with the organizers themselves.”

Amid continued restrictions on freedom of expression and peaceful assembly in Hong Kong, Hong Kong communities around the world have planned commemorations of the fire.

Deep concerns surrounding the 2026 anniversary follow a year-long government crackdown on criticism, reporting, and civil society-organizing related to the fire. Immediately following the fire, hundreds of people gathered near Wang Fuk Court to donate and distribute supplies to victims, but as the volunteer effort grew, police told them to leave and government workers took over the site.

Authorities targeted people calling for accountability. Just four days after the fire, national security police reportedly arrested a university student, Miles Kwan, for “seditious intent” after he initiated an online petition calling for an independent investigation into the fire, and two others on suspicion of “inciting hatred against the government.”

Hong Kong and Beijing authorities subsequently accused “foreign forces” and “anti-China destabilizing forces” of using the fire to “incite hatred against the government.” Within hours of these announcements, two prominent online commentators, Ellie Yuen and Hailey Cheng, who had been frequently quoted in foreign media, said they would stop reporting on the fire.

National security police also arrested the online commentator Wong On-yin in December after he planned to hold a news conference about the Wang Fuk Court fire and discussed on YouTube his subsequent questioning by police. The authorities prosecuted him for allegedly obstructing a national security investigation and publishing “seditious material.” He remains in custody awaiting a verdict in January 2027.

Between April and May 2026, the authorities barred Wang Fuk Court residents from displaying banners from their homes or being accompanied by journalists as they were briefly permitted to return to their apartments to retrieve belongings.

Also in May, the authorities arrested the former Wang Fuk Court owners’ committee member Kong Cheung-fat and his wife on allegations of fraud and money laundering. Kong had joined other owners in seeking a meeting to discuss insurance and other post-fire issues. While out on bail, he said he would no longer give interviews or comment publicly. Some residents said his arrest made them more cautious about speaking publicly.

An independent committee, set up by Hong Kong Chief Executive John Lee, published a report on October 29 that found wide-ranging regulatory failures across four government bodies, including inadequate oversight of renovation work and fire safety, weaknesses in building maintenance regulation, and supervisory failures by contractors and other professionals. That day, Lee and senior officials formally apologized to the victims of the fire and their families while promising to implement “systemic reforms.” Housing Secretary Winnie Ho later stepped down from office while the authorities arrested 35 people in connection with the fire and possible corruption in the renovation project.

“An apology and promises of reform are no substitute for ensuring that people can freely question and criticize the authorities,” Pearson said. “The Hong Kong government should act on the independent committee’s recommendations and provide adequate compensation and other support for victims and their families.”

The 2026 Rural Ambulatory Care Report (PDF) analyzed athenahealth network data across 12.3 million rural patients between 2021 and 2025, as well as more than 30,000 clinicians and 1,861 rural practices across the entire U.S. Factors such as appointment activity, workforce composition, payments and more were examined.

Athenahealth CMO and athenaInstitute co-chair Nele Jessel, M.D., said in a statement the findings show while rural practices are not “standing still in the face of enormous challenges,” there are “limits to how much more they can absorb as patient needs continue to grow.”

“They are adapting how they deliver care and finding ways to keep patients connected to the care they need,” Jessel said. “With renewed focus on rural healthcare, there is a real opportunity to build on what is working by investing in the workforce, technology and models of care that can help these practices continue serving the communities that depend on them.”

Primary care physicians in rural areas have to wear multiple hats, as specialty care needs at practices are higher in rural communities.

The report found that primary care handles 68.7% of visits involving anxiety or depression diagnoses versus 55.1% in non-rural areas. Moreover, 25% of rural patients with documented anxiety or depression had a substance use disorder—up from 19.8% in non-rural communities.

Data shows rural and non-rural healthcare practices collected roughly the same share of what they were owed by insurers in 2025, but analysts note a difference in patient balances.

Rural practices collected about $70 of every $100 owed, while non-rural practices collected about $78—which analysts say is about a 10% gap.

Another potential source of financial vulnerability for rural practices is Medicaid. Medicaid covered 27.5% of rural visits, which analysts note leaves these practices more exposed to eligibility changes set to begin in 2027.
