Netflix might want its new AI search to drum up more “quality” viewership

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Netflix‘s new strategy for combating YouTube‘s commanding share of living room TVs is to insist that it’s not the quantity of viewership, but the quality.

And it might be trying to pump up those quality views with a new recommendation tool.

Of course, the times being what they are, the tool is AI.

AI Discover is intended to curate Netflix’s offerings in a hyperpersonalized manner, so people only see what they like. If this sounds familiar, it’s because Netflix is doing what YouTube, X, and Meta have already done: shunt the responsibility for a quality recommendation algorithm onto viewers and their input.

“There’s more to enjoy and discover on Netflix than ever, but what sounds right for a quiet night in may look different for family movie night or a few spare minutes,” Netflix said in an announcement post. “We’ve spent years refining how you discover, with recommendations tailored to your tastes, from what you Thumbs Up to what you add to ‘My List.’ Now, AI Discover joins the mix as a new way to explore.”

Once a theme is in place, Netflix’s bot brings up a bunch of suggestions, and also additional narrower themes within the first themes. Netflix says there will be a process of “tuning” AI Discover’s recommendations until it “find[s] your next obsession.”

Users who think the bot is getting too far off what they want can refresh the whole deal. Netflix also lets people tell it titles they don’t like.

Unlike the aforementioned social media platforms, Netflix doesn’t seem to have included a ChatGPT-esque discussion aspect to this tool. Users just click through and select what they like, and what they don’t.

Makes sense, since YouTube, Meta, and X have their own in-house chatbots they can shove into everything; Netflix doesn’t have a Gemini or Grok or Muse equivalent, so a yes/no system is its best option. It did partner with OpenAI in 2025 to test an AI search feature that was rumored would replace its regular search function, but that never materialized in full across the service. Maybe this is the version of that idea it ultimately ended up with, sans GPT chatting.

Netflix does say AI Discover is “powered by a new intelligence layer we’ve been building that responds to what members tell us in real time and keeps learning.”

This brings us back to what we said at the top of this article: Netflix is looking for quality viewership. And, well, if it can gather an immense amount of information about viewers’ preferences with this new “intelligence layer,” it might be able to more accurately match them with what they want to watch.

That in turn could drive up the amount of time people spend watching a movie or a TV show–and Netflix might start shooting for average view duration as a way to supercede YouTube.

For now, Netflix’s new AI discovery is only available on TVs in the U.S. (interesting choice), and only in English. Netflix says it won’t appear on kids’ profiles. Per Variety, the streamer plans to expand to more international users in the coming weeks.

Should Investors Buy Into the Chipotle Turnaround?

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Chipotle continued its turnaround with another quarter of positive same-store sales growth.

Chipotle Mexican Grill (CMG +5.83%) continued its recent turnaround with its third straight quarter of comparable-store sales growth. The solid results lifted the stock nearly 9% on Oct. 29, although its shares are still down more than 15% on the year.

Let's dig into the fast-casual Tex-Mex restaurant operator's quarterly results and prospects to see if the stock's rebound can continue.

After seeing declining same-store sales growth throughout much of last year, Chipotle has now turned in positive same-store sales growth each quarter of 2026. This started with a modest 0.5% increase in the first quarter, and then a 2.2% rise in the second. It followed those gains with a 2.1% comparable-store increase in the third quarter.

Transactions rose 0.6%, while it said pricing was up about 2.7%. It started to see industry headwinds in mid-July that slowed its momentum, but trends normalized in late August. It then saw solid momentum pick up after the launch of Pollo Asado and Chili Lime Chips in September, although October trends have softened.

Chipotle also saw a boost from its reward program, which it revamped in April. It now has over 24 million active members, and it is seeing strong in-store sign-ups. It said over 33% of its sales now come from loyalty members.

Importantly, the company said that it expects its same-store sales momentum to pick up starting next month. It is looking for comparable-store sales to climb by 2% or more in the fourth quarter, with an exit rate for the year around 3% to 4%. The growth is expected to be fueled by a combination of marketing initiatives and the November launch of its popular brisket limited-time offering.

Overall, Chipotle's revenue climbed by nearly 10% to $3.29 billion in the quarter, while its adjusted earnings per share (EPS) fell 7% to $0.27. That was generally in line with analysts' expectations for EPS of $0.29 on sales of $3.29 billion, according to LSEG.

Restaurant-level operating margin fell by 120 basis points to 23.3%. This is one of the most important metrics in the restaurant industry, as it shows how profitable individual restaurants are. The company has been feeling pressure from wage inflation, higher labor usage, and increased prices, and as such, it plans to raise prices by 3% in Q4.

Chipotle continues to add new restaurants, opening 84 company-owned locations in the quarter and five international ones with partners. During the quarter, it opened restaurants in Mexico, Saudi Arabia, and South Korea. It said Mexico has exceeded expectations, while Saudi Arabia opening volumes set a new record.

It plans to add between 350 and 370 new restaurants in 2026, with between 10 and 15 of them in international markets with partners. It ended the quarter with 4,267 restaurants.

Looking ahead, Chipotle plans to add another 340 to 360 U.S. and Canada company-owned locations and 15 to 25 international company-owned and partner-operated restaurants in 2027.

Chipotle continues to show signs of turning around its business with solid same-store sales growth. However, inflation continues to run high, and its sales growth has not yet reached a level to help it stabilize its restaurant-level margins. This is leading to reduced earnings power.

A 3% price increase set for the end of 2026 and 2027, combined with increased traffic, could be a recipe for improvements in this area, which is what you really want to see to get excited about the stock. Until then, an investment in the company is more about its turnaround potential than its growth.

With the stock trading at a forward price-to-earnings (P/E) multiple of 23 times 2027 analyst estimates, Chipotle's stock looks appropriately valued in my book. Whether it is a buy is all going to come down to execution. At this time, given the uncertainties, I'd prefer not to chase this recent rally.

Companies need to fortify supply chains for “a flock of black swan” events, says Toll Group executive chairman Thomas Knudsen

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Companies must build supply chains for robustness, not resilience, because “a black swan event has become a flock of black swans,” Thomas Knudson, executive chairman of logistics company Toll Group, said Tuesday.

Supply chain shocks have piled up since 2020, following the COVID pandemic, Russia’s 2022 invasion of Ukraine, Trump’s 2025 tariffs and the end of de minimis, and finally the U.S. war with Iran and the closed Strait of Hormuz.

Before 2020, Knudsen said resilience meant reacting—waiting for a crisis, then calling for help. “And then we call our banks, our logistics companies, and we say, ‘Hey, can you help us get out of this problem?’” he said at the Fortune Southeast Asia 500 Summit in Ho Chi Minh City, Vietnam.

“Now suddenly you’re looking at your tier two, three, and four suppliers,” he added. “We’ve got to build a supply chain that’s much more robust.” Toll Group, founded in 1888 and currently owned by Japan Post, runs freight forwarding and contract logistics across Asia.

In practice, robustness means spare capacity: backup routes by truck and rail alongside ocean and air, and sea-air combinations that fly cargo part of the way and ship it the rest. “Today we’re doing trucking. Tomorrow we’re doing rail. We’re complementing with ocean and air,” Knudsen explained.

But robustness has limits. For low-value goods like cement, he noted, building a resilient supply chain is difficult simply because of the cost.

Tariffs have also forced Chinese companies to look to new markets and manufacturing hubs. “Is that going to move back? I don’t think so,” Knudsen said.

Standard Chartered’s Future of Trade report—a survey of 2,100 senior corporate leaders across 27 markets—reports that ASEAN executives citing geo-economic actions among their top three trade concerns has risen from 49% over the past year to 57% over the next three to five years.

James Nesbitt, managing director and head of global subsidiaries for Singapore and ASEAN at Standard Chartered, said the bank’s corporate clients are making long-term strategic commitments to the region.

“This isn’t simply about a foreign company setting up a factory in Indonesia, waiting for that factory to produce, and then kind of moving on,” Nesbitt said.

Established in 1992, the ASEAN Free Trade Area has fostered regional integration by eliminating tariff barriers among Southeast Asian countries. ASEAN’s 11 economies together are worth about $4.5 trillion and growing about 4.5% a year, according to the IMF—yet only about 22% of the bloc’s trade happens within it, versus roughly 60% for the European Union.

Knudsen pointed to the EU’s single currency and open borders as the model for ASEAN. “We saw when the UK stepped out of the EU on Brexit, that friction became real,” he said. “It wasn’t just a little obstacle.”

Nesbitt said ASEAN’s diversity could also create opportunities to connect its payment systems. Each market has its own real-time payment network, he said, and an integrated overlay could enable them to work together.

“What if there was an opportunity not to create bilateral ties between ASEAN, but instead have an overlay where all of these different payment infrastructures can operate real time?” he asked. “Can blockchain solve that? Is there an opportunity for tokenization? Or stablecoins to solve for that?”

What the Democrats' midterms win means for the US economy and Trump

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

From high oil prices and data centers to tariffs and mounting debt, economic concerns played a key role in the crushing defeat of President Donald Trump's Republicans. So can the Democrats now make a difference?

For the millions who cast their ballots in the US midterm elections, one issue ranked above all others: the economy.

So what does the Democrats' stunning capture of both the US Senate and House of Representatives mean for the economy and economic policy, given that President Donald Trump still has two years of his term to go?

Markets have rebounded after initially trending negatively. Global stocks rallied as investors appeared to take heart from the Democrats' victory.

With Democrats now holding comfortable majorities in both houses of Congress and Trump still in the White House, it could lead to gridlock in Washington. Trump has already said he would become "Mr Veto," meaning he would block any legislation the Democrats advance.

"The magnitude of the gains for the Democrats in the midterms does not change too much for the near-term policy outlook, given the party still lacks a filibuster-proof majority in the Senate and President Trump has veto power to block legislation," Stephen Brown, chief North America economist with Capital Economics, said in a note.

"I think we are looking at two years of complete 100% gridlock," Jacob Funk Kirkegaard, nonresident senior fellow with the Peterson Institute for International Economics, told DW after the results came in. "Nothing will get done of any kind."

Some investors appear worried that the Democrats could force through AI regulation, especially if they also win the White House in 2028. Rising opposition among large majorities of the US population to data centers — the key infrastructure behind AI — helped secure victories for many Democratic candidates.

Kirkegaard, who is also a senior fellow at Bruegel, a European economics think tank, said the vote showed the extent of that opposition.  Even if the Democrats can't force regulation, he thinks local opposition will make it harder for data centers to be built, which could hit the valuation of big tech firms that have benefitted from the AI boom.

"This election shows that the political underpinning of the rapid, aggressive AI growth story is shaky," he said. "It doesn't mean it's going to stop or be made illegal, but the dynamic of the growth story has shifted."

If the Democrats were to win the presidency in 2028, Brown said, they would be perfectly placed to make major policy changes. "That includes the potential for tax hikes on corporations and high-income individuals as well as regulation that could delay the AI rollout," he said.

According to polling conducted in early October by the Pew Research Center, 56% of Americans say Trump's tariff policies will have mostly negative impacts on the country, with only 20% saying mostly positive.

That sentiment clearly drove many Democrats to victory in the midterms — particularly in states where tariffs have had a particularly negative impact. That includes those with sectors reliant on exports, such as Texas, Michigan, Iowa and Illinois, where counter-tariffs have hit and those that have high trade volumes with Canada.

Yet, barring an unprecedented climbdown from Trump — who has been ideologically wedded to tariffs since he first became a public figure almost 50 years ago — it's not clear how the Democrats could force substantial change to the president's signature economic policy.

"Trump will not be moderated," Deborah Elms, head of trade policy at the Hinrich Foundation in Singapore, told DW earlier this year. "Expect him to continue his trade disruption and tariff policies for as long as he remains in the Oval Office."

Though the US Constitution gives Congress the power to collect and organize tariffs, various laws passed over the years have given presidents power to implement tariffs when they cite certain conditions, such as national security or economic emergencies.

Trump has already used such avenues for his tariffs and can continue to do so, despite some legal rebukes. However, while Congress can't necessarily force an end to Trump's existing tariffs, it can now take further legal action against them to prevent greater escalation.

Though Trump and the Democrats are clearly not going to make common cause, Kirkegaard said, the scale of the Republicans' defeat might force Trump to be less erratic in his policymaking, from escalating his trade war with Canada to his Iran policy.

Trump said in the aftermath of Tuesday's defeats that he would "work with" the Democrats and told the Financial Times in the run-up to the vote that he was unlikely to escalate the war in Iran.

Were a deal to be struck with Iran, soaring energy prices — a pivotal election issue — could finally fall, helping voters with their cost-of-living concerns.

"You've got to get a deal with Iran, and you've got to bring down gas prices," Kirkegaard said. "From a political point of view, that's the most important thing now."

George Saravelos, an analyst with Deutsche Bank FX Research, thinks the markets are beginning to become more optimistic in that area. "With oil flows through the Middle East having materially recovered, the market is pricing a large imminent risk premium of re-escalation and energy supply disruption," he said in a note.

Despite the scale of the Democrats' victory, the fact that Trump remains in the Oval Office will complicate all efforts to force a change to signature economic policies and issues.

Yet Congress can now exert considerable pressure. Another issue vital to voters was corruption and the belief that the Trump administration has engaged in corruption and personal enrichment while in office.

That raises the prospect of the new Democratic-controlled Congress launching legal investigations and potentially pursuing another impeachment of Trump, which could force the president to negotiate on some economic issues to secure his political survival.

"Can you find 10 or 12 Republican senators who, in the face of overwhelming corruption, would be willing to vote for articles of impeachment? I wouldn't rule it out," Kirkegaard said. "And that's a scenario in which Trump might suddenly be much more accommodating to, for instance, abandoning most of his tariffs."

Despite voters' clear rejection of Trump's economic policies, the Democratic Party's newly minted senators, congressmen and congresswomen face an uphill battle in trying to reverse them.

Delta Must Face Lawsuit Alleging Over 1 Million Flyers Paid For 'Window Seats' With No Window

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Delta Air Lines must face a proposed class action lawsuit over allegations that it charged passengers extra for window seats that have no window. The lawsuit alleges that Delta sells certain seats on its Boeing 737, Boeing 757 and Airbus A321 aircraft as window seats without revealing that some are positioned next to a blank cabin wall.

It is estimated that more than one million Delta passengers may have paid for these seats. A judge has now rejected the airline's attempt to have the case thrown out, following a similar ruling in July that allowed an almost identical lawsuit against United Airlines to proceed.

On Wednesday, November 4, US District Judge Ramon E. Reyes Jr. of the Eastern District of New York ruled that the passengers' breach of contract claims are not preempted by federal law, rejecting one of Delta's central arguments for dismissing the case. Delta, represented by law firm King & Spalding, had argued that the lawsuit should be dismissed on several grounds.

According to Law360, Delta's defense centered on its Contract of Carriage, which it says makes no promise to provide passengers with a window. It also explicitly states that seat assignments "are not guaranteed and form no part of this contract." Delta added that any claims based on seat map labels, tickets or marketing materials fall outside the contract and are therefore preempted by the Airline Deregulation Act (ADA).

On the other hand, the plaintiffs — represented by New York law firm Greenbaum Olbrantz — argued that Delta's use of the word "window" is a commitment to provide an actual window, particularly when it charges an additional fee for the seat. The plaintiffs’ lawyer, Carter Greenbaum, said when the suit was first filed:

"They sold customers window seats and ended up seating them next to a wall."

The case was originally filed on August 19, 2025, as Meyer v. Delta Air Lines, with Brooklyn resident Nicholas Meyer leading the lawsuit. According to the original complaint, Meyer paid extra for window seats on a trip from LaGuardia Airport (LGA) to John Wayne Airport (SNA) in Orange County, connecting through Delta's hub at Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport (ATL).

However, when he boarded a Boeing 757-200 for the second sector of his journey, he discovered that seat 23F was next to a blank cabin wall rather than a window. As a result, he spent the roughly four-and-a-half-hour flight without the view he had paid for. The lawsuit states that some seats on Delta's 737s, 757s and A321s have no windows because of air conditioning ducts and other equipment behind the panels, but this was not made clear during booking.

The plaintiffs say they would not have paid for these seats if Delta had made their lack of windows clear. They also argue that Delta could easily prevent the problem by updating its booking system. The complaint points out that the airline already identifies other seat limitations during the selection process, such as restricted recline or a lack of under-seat storage, but does not identify seats without windows.

This is unlike rivals American Airlines and Alaska Airlines, which clearly inform customers during seat selection if a 'window seat' has no window. Southwest Airlines, which introduced assigned seating on January 27, 2026, has also added a warning to its seat selection page.

United clearly believes the lawsuit has no merit.

United must also face a similar lawsuit to Delta's after losing its own bid to have the case dismissed in July. United had claimed that the term "window" describes a seat's position next to the cabin wall, rather than promising passengers an actual view. It also argued that federal law prevented the plaintiffs from bringing their claims.

US District Judge James Donato rejected both arguments, stating that United's ticketing terms "plausibly establish that United expressly agreed to provide a seat with a window." Both lawsuits are seeking millions of dollars in damages, along with changes to how the airlines identify and advertise seats that have no window.

FX's 27-Episode Sci-Fi Masterpiece Deserves Kudos For Breaking Every Storytelling Rule

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

For all that sci-fi television has become much more experimental narrative formulas in the age of streaming, one corner of the genre remains surprisingly attached to tradition: superhero shows. Even the most adventurous and ambitious superhero TV shows seem to eventually fall back into old storytelling habits. Even the series that deliberately lean into strangeness for a selling point do so with the promise that everything will eventually click into place and the true heroes and villains of the story will meet for a final showdown no matter how morally ambiguous they’d been up until that point.

That makes an often overlooked 2017-2019 FX series is deserving of much more recognition. Before Disney+ explored superhero surrealism through MCU shows like WandaVision and VisionQuest and Prime Video lambasted the entire genre with The Boys and Invincible, Legion was already dismantling expectations. Creator Noah Hawley was tasked with making a Marvel adaptation, and instead delivered a series that treats every scene as a chance to experiment. The show’s 27 episodes are dripping with confusion, discomfort, and unpredictability, and it makes for some thoroughly entertaining viewing.

In terms of the show’s premise, Legion follows David Haller (Dan Stevens), a psychiatric patient who discovers his mental health struggles are linked to extraordinary mutant abilities. While the series is loosely connected to Fox’s X-Men movies and draws from Marvel Comics mythology, Legion’s plot stands on its own, and Noah Hawley used that independence to turn what could have been a standard superhero TV show into something truly unique.

From the moment it begins, literally nothing about Legion plays into expectations surrounding its plot and narrative structure. For example, most superhero shows spend time establishing a status quo for reality before introducing something that threatens it. Legion makes reality itself the problem. Memories can be manipulated, seemingly ordinary spaces become mental prisons, and it’s always unclear whether a scene is actually happening or just playing out inside someone’s head. Because David’s mental state means he can’t trust his own experience, the same applies for viewers too.

This combination of atmospheric surrealism and an incredibly unreliable narrator defines how Legion’s story is told. The show’s 27 episodes include some incredibly avant-garde installments. Season 1's “Chapter 7” turns escape from a mental institution into a black-and-white silent film sequence, while “Chapter 10” in season 2 depicts the confrontation between David and central antagonist The Shadow King using dance. This psychedelic approach gives Legion’s psychic conflict its own unique style, making the show’s superpowered struggles more unsettling than a simple exchange of energy blasts or city-leveling fist fight.

It’s not only the scene-by-scene storytelling in Legion that flips expectations on its head. The wider narrative also dismantles a core theme of almost all superhero shows: discovering immense power means inheriting a heroic destiny. David eventually comes to see himself as burdened with glorious purpose, but his self-perception as a savior and the reality of his place in the world couldn’t be more different. His certainty that he deserves love and redemption increasingly justifies the harm he causes others, inverting the traditional message that power, heroism, and responsibility all go hand-in-hand.

Several superhero shows that arrived after Legion have also embraced unreliable perspectives, unconventional formats, and morally compromised "heroes". Few, however, are able to sustain the sense that even their storytelling methods can unexpectedly change at any moment across three whole seasons. Legion may not be the most accessible series out there, but its experimental style shapes the entire experience. Its underlying uncertainty is inseparable from its explorations of what ideas like “truth” and “reality” even mean when everyone’s perception is subjective, and this thematic interest in turn means it breaks almost every narrative rule.

Calling Legion a superhero show is accurate as a description of its source material. The show incorporates X-Men characters such as David himself, the Shadow King, and even Professor Charles Xavier. As a description of actually watching it, however, the “superhero TV show” label is incredibly misleading. Sure, there are genre staples like mutants, secretive organizations, and potentially catastrophic powers, but the show itself has little interest in using these elements to construct a good vs. evil superhero narrative about saving the day.

If one were to assess Legion in a vacuum without considering its X-Men roots, psychological horror is a much more appropriate genre label. David’s mind is a thoroughly unsafe place where childhood memories conceal terrifying intrusions from outsiders, and apparent understanding is often just a trick to disguise more secrets. Alongside psychological horror, Legion also works as an experimental sci-fi series, using concepts like psychic spaces and, eventually, time travel to explore identity, responsibility, and the desire to rewrite painful experiences.

There’s also romance to be found, but once again, this isn’t the kind of love story usually found in superhero shows. David’s relationship with Syd Barrett (Rachel Keller) initially seems like it will be the source of a glimmer of hope, that it’s going to be a tale of two isolated people finally finding connection. However, like everything else in Legion, the love story soon gives way to some unsettling explorations of themes like consent, bodily autonomy, and manipulation. Since David and other characters can violate boundaries in ways ordinary people physically cannot, this leads to some truly disturbing moments.

That makes Legion the perfect superhero TV show for anyone suffering from superhero fatigue. It’s not a superhero story: it’s a deep and complex drama that just so happens to center on characters who first appeared in comic books. Its three-season arc is a psychedelic tragedy that uses its character’s spectacular abilities as tools to explore some truly dark ideas. Legion may technically be a superhero series, but it’s one that feels ideal for viewers who openly balk at them.

A new UK law banning porn featuring choking and “fauxcest,” or simulated incest, may have unintended consequences for content creators and smut fans all over the world, some experts warn.

As of November 6, amendments to the Crime and Policing Act will outlaw the “possession or publication of pornographic images of strangulation or suffocation”—a law intended to curb violence against women and girls. Yet some experts warn that the legislation is written broadly enough that it could potentially criminalize far more innocuous content, including “breast smothering,” “jeans facesitting,” and even potentially oral sex, ostensibly because people could have their airways impeded during these activities.

“I would encourage both content creators and consumers to underscore the risks of this law,” says Myles Jackman, an obscenity lawyer in the UK. “Because to me, it genuinely seems like one of the greatest acts of suppression of free speech in global history.”

The new amendments were inspired by a lengthy pornography review authored by Baroness Gabby Bertin, a Conservative member of the UK House of Lords. The legislation bans various depictions of sex acts, including “pornographic images of strangulation or suffocation,” as well as content showing performers role-playing as minors under the age of 16.

It also takes aim at “fauxcest,” an extremely popular porn niche that depicts simulated sexual relations between family members who are not related by blood, such as stepsiblings or stepparents. In Bertin’s review recommending the ban on such content, she cites “anecdotal evidence” suggesting that fauxcest “can be used between an abuser and child to permit interfamily child sex abuse,” describing it as a “fig-leaf” of sorts for actual sexual abuse.

Under the new law, those caught distributing or in possession of such content face up to five years in prison and a fine.

The impending UK legislation has prompted panic among some adult creators, many of whom are currently combing through their content libraries and removing clips that could get swept up in the ban.

“The problem is always how broad [the legislation] is,” says Alexandra Snow, an adult content creator and educator. “And we won't know how they're going to interpret it until they start making people's lives hell.”

Snow says she is particularly concerned about how the aspect of the legislation pertaining to “suffocation or strangulation” will affect kink creators, who may make content related to collaring or leather fetishes. She’s also worried about AI image-recognition software, which is already in widespread use among the platforms, potentially flagging content that isn’t violative at all. “They use a very broad, heavy-handed approach,” she says.

Cami Strella, an OnlyFans creator in the US who estimates that 60 to 70 percent of her content depicts some form of role-play, including step-“fauxcest,” is also concerned about the legislation. She says that the content she makes is “just playing a fantasy,” and that more sex education and literacy is required to teach people about the risks of various behaviors.

“I don’t think banning it is going to do anything,” she says. “If anything, it’ll just push even worse content that is actually illegal or immoral underground, so you’ll have to access it away from these platforms. This is just a slippery slope we’re getting into.”

The new UK law is seemingly targeted at the trend of choking in porn, which has been linked to real-life instances of fatal choking during sex. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services finalized sweeping Medicare payment reforms it says will shift the program from volume-based "sick care" toward prevention, primary care and accountable care.

The 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule rule includes a roughly 2% reduction in baseline physician payment rates with targeted increases and new payment opportunities for accountable care, primary and chronic care and behavioral health. CMS finalized steeper physician payment cuts than it had proposed in July.

An unpublished PDF version of the 2,468-page final rule can be found here.

In a press release, CMS said it is "prioritizing prevention, strengthening primary care, cracking down on duplicate billing and reinforcing fiscal stewardship across Medicare."

The final rule modernizes physician payment structures to "reward health maintenance and coordinated primary care rather than disjointed, volume-based intervention," CMS said.

"Medicare should reward clinicians for keeping people healthy," HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, Jr. said in a statement. "Under President Trump’s leadership, we are putting prevention and primary care at the center of Medicare, closing payment loopholes, and cracking down on waste. This rule directs resources toward coordinated care that puts patients first. We are changing what Medicare pays for to change what our health system delivers."

Under the final rule, CMS will officially sunset the traditional Merit-based Incentive Payment System (MIPS) reporting option beginning with the CY 2031 performance period. Clinicians will have through CY 2030 to transition to the MIPS Value Pathways (MVPs), which already cover approximately 98% of clinicians, or Advanced Alternative Payment Models. This reflects two additional years of transition time compared to the proposed rule. The rule also finalizes three new MVPs focused on diabetes, hypertension, and hospitalists, and requires clinicians to report at least one specialty-specific "Core Measure" starting in CY 2027.

For physicians, while the proposed rule includes statutory payment increases, Medicare payment rates face an overall baseline decrease for 2027 due to the expiration of a temporary 2.5% legislative increase from the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) in 2026.

Under the Medicare Access and CHIP Reauthorization Act (MACRA), the CY2027 conversion factors reflect statutory updates of +0.75% for qualifying Alternative Payment Model (APM) participants and +0.25% for non-qualifying participants. The changes to the PFS conversion factors for CY 2027 include these updates as required by statute and an estimated -0.12% adjustment necessary to account for proposed changes in work RVUs for some services, according to CMS in a fact sheet.

For clinicians who don't qualify through an advanced alternative payment model, the conversion factor drops from $33.40 to $32.63, a 2.31% reduction. For qualifying APM participants, the conversion factor drops 1.83% from $33.57 to $32.95. In the draft rule issued in July, CMS projected cuts of 1.68% and 1.19%, respectively.

CMS is also addressing what it sees as overpayment and duplicate reimbursement when multiple services are delivered during the same patient encounter. Under the final rule, when an office visit and minor procedure occur on the same day, CMS will reduce payment by 20% for the lower-cost service, reducing duplication in payment by patients and the Medicare program for clinical work already covered in the procedure’s valuation.

CMS had initially proposed paying the higher-cost service in full and reducing payment for the other service by 50%.

Physician groups were quick to criticize CMS' payment changes in the final rule.

"Notwithstanding modest policy improvements to enhance value-based care through MSSP, MGMA is disappointed by CMS’s decision to finalize several destabilizing policies in the 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule," Anders Gilberg, senior vice president of government affairs at the Medical Group Management Association, said in a statement.

"Conversion factor cuts of 1.83% for qualifying APM participants and 2.31% for all other clinicians further degrade an already fragile payment system and amplify severe financial challenges caused by years of inadequate Medicare reimbursement," Gilberg said.

Compounding this ongoing reimbursement decline, CMS will cut payments by 20% when office visits billed with modifier 25 are performed on the same day as certain procedures, "interfering with physician practices’ ability to offer same-day care," he noted.

MGMA is urging Congress to intervene by immediately fixing cuts to the conversion factor, along with changes to modifier 25. "To prevent annual disruption, Congress should prioritize comprehensive MACRA reform by passing the Patients First Act," Gilberg said.

The agency is overhauling the office/outpatient evaluation and management visit complexity add-on code, HCPCS code G2211. CMS is transitioning HCPCS code G2211 from a flat add-on payment to a modifier that would increase the payment of the associated E/M code by 16%. That would maintain an equal percentage increase across all levels of E/M codes, CMS said.

The agency also finalized a new modifier that would offset the costs incurred by practitioners in accountable care organizations (ACOs) when providing longitudinal care and care coordination. The modifier would only be available for practitioners participating in a Shared Savings Program ACO or participant providers in a Long-term Enhanced ACO Design (LEAD) Model ACO and would increase payment of the associated E/M visit by 32%, CMS said.

The final 2027 PFS rule also establishes new 340B reporting requirements. Covered entities will have to report certain Medicare Part D claims involving drugs purchased through the 340B Drug Pricing Program to the Medicare Part D Claims Data 340B Repository beginning with claims with a date of service on or after Jan.1, 2027. The deadline for the first quarterly submission is delayed until Sept. 30, 2027.

In the final rule, CMS tightened remote patient monitoring and remote therapeutic monitoring requirements but backed off an immediate restriction on contractors.

Beginning in 2027, as CMS outlined in the proposed rule, RTM services can only be furnished to established patients, and providers must provide a separately reportable initiating visit before billing for RPM or RTM services. CMS initially proposed only allowing payment for RPM or RTM services when performed by clinical staff employed by the practice and not when those services are delivered by third-party vendors. The agency provided a two-year reprieve on that requirement as it goes into effect Jan. 1, 2029, "giving practices that rely on contractors two additional years to adapt," CMS said.

Health tech companies, providers and lawmakers pushed back on the proposal to restrict third-party vendors, urging CMS to reconsider the proposed changes to preserve patients' access to these tech-enabled services. Many rural, small, and independent practices, as well as both urban and rural health systems, rely on specialized clinical and technology partners to make remote monitoring services available to their patients.

CMS finalized changes to the Medicare Shared Savings Program, the nation’s largest value-based payment program, to "accelerate accountable care service delivery, further CMS’ goal of aligning spending and value in Original Medicare, and support achieving other related strategic objectives," the agency said in a fact sheet.

The overhaul to the MSSP program aims to make ACO participation more financially attractive, encourage organizations to take on risk, expand beneficiary participation and ease some quality-reporting requirements.

The agency says the changes are projected to grow the program by adding up to 1 million assigned beneficiaries in an average year and produce $5.5 billion in net federal savings over 10 years. CMS also projects the changes will generate an overall increase in shared savings net of losses to ACOs by $1.1 billion over a decade.

The final rule changes the Shared Savings Program’s benchmarking and financial methodology to balance incentives between Level E of the BASIC track and the ENHANCED track. The shared savings rate for Level E of the BASIC track increases 50% to 60% and reduce the maximum weight used in calculating the positive regional adjustment for lower-spending ACOs participating under the ENHANCED track from 50% to 35%.

The agency is increasing the prior savings adjustment's scaling factor from 50% to 75%, allowing more of an ACO's previous savings to be reflected in future benchmarks.

Under the final rule, CMS will risk-adjust the 5% cap on benchmark increases, potentially allowing ACOs that care for sicker, higher-cost patients to receive larger upward adjustments.

Data shows that patients aligned with an ACO are less likely to experience uncontrolled diabetes or hypertension and are more likely to receive recommended preventive care, according to CMS. In performance year 2024, 75% of the 476 participating ACOs earned shared savings totaling $4.1 billion, while generating net savings for the Medicare Trust Funds of approximately $2.5 billion compared to projected spending benchmarks.

Under the final 2027 PFS rule, approved ACOs may reduce or eliminate Part B cost-sharing for many items and services for assigned beneficiaries, directly lowering out-of-pocket costs for essential care. That change goes into effect April 1, 2027.

The rule also increases shared savings rates for eligible ACOs, provides new incentives for first-time participants and sets more predictable spending targets. And, the rule simplifies electronic health record reporting requirements, streamlines patient notice mandates and removes outdated administrative hurdles.

Executives at Amwell, formerly known as American Well, touted the company's platform strategy, government contracts and higher-value clinical services as the main drivers of future growth during the company's third-quarter earnings call Tuesday.

Rising healthcare costs are pushing health plans and employers to adopt tech-enabled care solutions, but they are looking for measurable engagement and outcomes from digital health vendors, not just access, according to Ido Schoenberg, M.D., Amwell chairperson and CEO.

The proliferation of point solutions, accelerated by AI, is creating more vendor sprawl and member confusion, Schoenberg told investors and analysts during the call.

There's also a lack of proof with many digital health tools, he noted. "It is hard to see which programs work and harder still to replace one without disrupting members. That sprawl is accelerating. AI has made it faster and cheaper to build a clinical program. Investors have backed hundreds of new digital health companies this year, many of them built on AI. More patients are turning to AI for health guidance, but those interactions don’t necessarily lead them to appropriate, covered care, creating the risk of poor clinical decisions and unnecessary costs for health plans, Schoenberg said.

"This is the problem Amwell is determined to solve for our customers. We help them improve clinical and financial outcomes. We do it through activation, getting more of their members into technology-enabled care in place of conventional care that often costs more. We give our customers one platform under their brand with one integration. It provides reliable, scalable rails to bring in a program, exchange data with it, and tests whether it works. The member's experience stays consistent, simple, and engaging. A common data structure proves the outcomes and improves performance over time," he said.

As AI adoption grows, a unified care platform becomes more valuable, Schoenberg asserted.

"We are not selling an AI feature. We are the trusted platform where AI-powered care runs with clinicians in the loop," he said during the Q3 call.

Amwell's customers represent more than 90 million covered lives, and the company also serves 9.6 million beneficiaries of the Military Health System.

Amwell's platform can help drive member engagement and utilization of digital health tools, executives said.

"Most of the health plans we talk to still see engagement in the single digits. We address that with always-on engagement, outreach to members through our customers' own channels within the rules they set. When a customer switches it fully on, members use the platform at more than three times the rate we see when it's off," Schoenberg said.

The platform runs on a common data structure, he noted. "It sends a member into a program with the right information. It collects outcomes back, and it reports value in the customer's own terms, whether that customer is a health plan or an employer that the plan serves. That is how a program proves its return on investment and how it gets better over time," he said.

In response to a question from an analyst, Schoenberg suggested pricing for the solution could evolve from fixed Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) fees toward outcomes-based arrangements in which Amwell shares in the savings it produces for health plans.

Energy Drinks, Stimulant Supplements Linked to Higher Heart Risks in Young Adults

New research suggests that caffeinated energy drinks and stimulant supplements are associated with significantly higher risks of serious heart problems, including cardiac arrest and heart attack in young adults.

The findings, to be presented at the American Heart Association (AHA) Scientific Sessions 2026 November 6–9 in Chicago, are preliminary and can’t prove that energy drinks cause heart problems.

Still, the results highlight concerning cardiovascular effects of these popular products, particularly among adults under 35 who consume them regularly.

“These products are marketed as everyday pick-me-ups and sold next to soda and bottled water, so most people assume they carry no real risk,” study author Sai Samayamanthula, a fourth-year medical student at the University of Virginia School of Medicine in Charlottesville, VA, said in a news release.

“For many healthy people, if these products are consumed occasionally, the risk is probably small. However, individuals who consume energy drinks or other stimulant-containing supplements regularly can quickly exceed the recommended daily limit for caffeine, so it’s important for people to know that there may be a risk of cardiovascular harm with these products,” Samayamanthula continued.

Researchers from the University of Virginia School of Medicine analyzed 28,345 reports submitted to the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) between 2017 and 2025.

Of these, 854 involved caffeinated energy drinks or stimulant supplements, while the remaining reports involved non-stimulant products.

Compared with non-stimulant products, cardiac arrest was reported approximately 11 times more often with energy drinks and stimulant supplements. Heart attacks were reported 4.5 times more often, while ventricular arrhythmias and palpitations were reported about four times more frequently.

Nearly 45% of heart-related reports involved adults ages 18 to 34.

“Our findings support the case for more systematic safety monitoring of caffeine- and stimulant-containing products. In addition, healthcare professionals should remember to ask their younger patients who have palpitations, chest pain or fainting spells if they recently or regularly consume these products,” Samayamanthula said.

But we are now in an era where a lot of SQL is drafted by AI assistants, and they are very good at producing queries that return the right answer. They are much less good at knowing your data. A model will happily add COALESCE, TRIM, LOWER or DISTINCT "just in case", because in the abstract each one is safer. On a 10,000-row QA table you'll never notice. On a 10-million-row production table, each one can turn an index lookup into a full scan.

The result being correct does not mean the assumptions behind it are. Before a query ships, check it against the data's real shape: null counts, value distributions, duplicates, and how skewed the busiest keys are. Or ask a database engineer to review it. It never hurts.

A note on IN versus EXISTS: PostgreSQL already turns IN (subquery) into a semi-join, so the two often plan the same way. The DISTINCT adds nothing, since a semi-join never returns a row twice, and it can push the planner toward building the full list first. Switching to EXISTS helps. The bigger win is adding the filter. If the outer query is limited to something you also know about the subquery's rows, say so in the subquery too. Don't rely on the planner to infer it.

PostgreSQL has a neat shortcut here. As the CREATE AGGREGATE documentation explains, a MIN- or MAX-like aggregate can be answered from an index, because SELECT max(col) FROM tab is equivalent to SELECT col FROM tab ORDER BY col DESC LIMIT 1. The planner walks the index backward and takes the first non-null value. It also works on partitioned tables and UNION ALL views, by merging each child's index. It does not work through a join. The planner source (planagg.c) limits the shortcut to queries that reference exactly one table, so the join branch had to build the whole join just to find its largest value.

The same review turned up a second easy win. The ceiling was global, but the job ran it once per scope, inside a loop, so it returned the same value several times per run. Computing it once per run cost nothing to change.

After the rewrites, I had a list of index candidates that would have made every remaining query near-instant: one on password_changed_at, a partial index for a rare account type, and a few more. Before building any of them, I added up what an hourly run actually cost without them:

Every index you add costs writes, storage and maintenance forever, and here the database was a replication target that would pay for each one on every write. So most of the candidates were dropped. That is a fine outcome. Not every slow-looking query needs an index; sometimes it needs nothing.

One query was different, because its cost multiplied. It loaded full account details in chunks of 100 IDs, and each chunk joined org_members on account_id, a column with no index. Every chunk scanned a 9-million-row table: about 0.7 s.

That is the test I use now: don't ask whether a query is slow, ask whether its cost grows with the data or the backlog. Fixed costs can usually be tolerated. Costs that multiply need an index. We added exactly one: org_members (account_id).

The new index worked exactly as planned: the org_members step went from scanning 5.4 million rows to reading 2,217 buffers. Then I looked at the total for the chunk query. It had gone from 723 ms to 26.9 seconds.

With org_members suddenly cheap, the planner switched to a nested loop and probed memberships once per organization row through an index on org_id. That index holds every membership in the organization, one per member account. The accounts in this chunk belonged to very large organizations, so each probe walked about 14,000 entries to find the one primary membership it needed.

The planner expected roughly one row per probe. When it knows a value in advance, PostgreSQL can look it up in its most-common-values statistics. Here each probe's value came from the outer row at run time, so the planner estimated an average number of rows per organization. The handful of very large organizations didn't fit that average, and re-running ANALYZE doesn't change how the estimate is made.

The fix was another index, but a very small one: a partial index containing only the rows the query asks for.

It holds about 15,000 rows instead of 650,000, so it is small and cheap to maintain, and each probe now lands directly on the row it needs. PostgreSQL uses a partial index only when it can prove, at planning time, that the query's WHERE clause implies the index predicate. The docs point out that a bind parameter such as membership_type = $1 can't prove that. Because this query uses membership_type = 'PRIMARY' AND is_deleted = false as literal values, the planner can match the partial index without any code change.

The lesson isn't "avoid indexes". It's that an index changes the plan around it, not just the step it targets. Re-run the full EXPLAIN ANALYZE after every index, and read the total, not just the line you were fixing.

Both indexes went in with CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY, so writes kept flowing. A few things I check every time:

1. Look for old transactions first. Per the CREATE INDEX docs, a concurrent build waits before each table scan for transactions that have modified the table, and after the second scan for any transaction holding a snapshot older than that scan. My pre-check found 65 sessions from a replication tool sitting idle in transaction for 28 days. Before cancelling anything, check whether they actually matter:

Ours held no snapshot, no transaction ID and no locks on the target tables, so they couldn't block the build or hold back vacuum. That's a cleanup item, not a blocker.

2. Run with autocommit on. CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY cannot run inside a transaction block, and some SQL clients wrap scripts in one.

3. Decide on lock_timeout deliberately. It lets a build fail fast instead of queueing behind a long transaction, but a build that fails partway leaves an invalid index behind. I left it unset and watched the progress view instead.

4. Check for invalid indexes afterwards. CREATE INDEX ... IF NOT EXISTS sees an invalid leftover's name and skips it with only a NOTICE. Drop it with DROP INDEX CONCURRENTLY and build again, or rebuild it with REINDEX INDEX CONCURRENTLY.

5. Build smallest to largest, and watch replication lag during the big ones.

This is what I now run through for any SQL headed to a large production table, whether a person or an AI wrote it.

Looking back, the biggest gains came from questions, not configuration:

None of that needed database expertise the developers couldn't learn. It needed someone to explain how the planner reads a predicate, why a function hides an index, and why QA data isn't evidence for production. Once that was clear, the developer rewrote the queries himself, and the run time in QA went from 30–50 minutes to under 30 seconds.

Some tuning really is just common sense and basic troubleshooting: read the plan, check the data, and ask what the query is for. If your team writes SQL, especially with an AI assistant drafting it, get a database engineer to look at it before it meets production data. It never hurts, and sometimes it saves you an hour-long first run, or a 27-second query you didn't know you had.

If you freelance as a developer, you'll probably be asked for an hourly rate, a sprint price, a monthly retainer and a fixed project quote, sometimes all from the same client. They can all come from one number. This post builds that number from your own costs, then turns it into each kind of price, with a short section on keeping scope creep from eating the margin.

All dollar amounts, hours and percentages below are made-up example figures, picked so the math is easy to follow. They aren't typical, average or recommended rates. Swap in your own. Tax and buffer percentages here are planning aids, not tax or accounting advice.

Start with what you want to take home in a year, then add what the business costs to run. For a developer that might be a hardware replacement fund, IDE and SaaS licences, hosting and domains for your own projects, insurance and accounting.

Example: take-home goal $67,200, plus expenses of $400/month × 12 = $4,800, so $72,000 in total.

Part of each invoice may need to go to tax, and a buffer can help with slow months and gaps between contracts.

Example: 25% tax set-aside + 15% buffer = 40%, which leaves 60% for income and expenses:

$72,000 ÷ 0.60 = $120,000 revenue needed (example figure)

Some of a freelancer's week probably can't be billed: sales calls, proposals, invoicing, keeping your own tooling up to date, learning a new framework for the next contract. Holidays and sick days come out too.

Example: 35 working hours a week, 10 of them non-billable, so 25 billable hours × 48 working weeks = 1,200 billable hours a year.

$120,000 ÷ 1,200 = $100 an hour (example figure)

That's your floor, not a target. Every other price below starts from it.

Some clients, especially teams already running Scrum, may find a per-sprint price easier to budget than an hourly one. Multiply the billable hours you'll commit per sprint by your rate.

Example: a 2-week sprint at 25 billable hours a week = 50 hours × $100 = $5,000 per sprint.

Say in writing what a sprint includes (planning, demo, a set number of hours) and what happens if the client adds work mid-sprint. For example, it waits for the next sprint, or it's priced as a change.

After a launch, a client may want someone on call for bugs, dependency updates and small fixes. A monthly retainer turns that into a fixed number of hours at your rate.

Example: 10 hours a month × $100 = $1,000 a month for maintenance.

Things worth writing down before you agree:

Some clients may prefer a fixed price for a whole project. Break the work into tasks, estimate each in hours, then add a contingency for the unknowns: the third-party API with thin docs, the legacy code nobody has touched in years.

That's 64 hours. Add a 25% contingency and you get 80 hours × $100 = $8,000 (example figure).

To spread the risk, split payment into a deposit and milestones.

Example: a 25% deposit of $2,000 before work starts, then the remaining $6,000 in three milestone payments of $2,000 each (say, accounts done, API done, launch).

A fixed quote holds up best when the scope does. "Can we also add a CSV export?" sounds small, but a few requests like that can push the hours well past the estimate.

Example: if the $8,000 project ends up taking 100 hours instead of 80, the effective rate drops to $8,000 ÷ 100 = $80 an hour, below the $100 floor from step 3. That can be a signal to tighten scope, raise the contingency, or re-estimate next time.

Tens of millions of voters cast their ballots Tuesday without widespread disruptions despite concerns of interference by the Trump administration.

Around the country, election administrators responded to relatively common Election Day issues in some communities, from delayed poll openings and long lines to poor signage and voting equipment problems.

Polls remain open in much of the western United States until later Tuesday.

Flooding in parts of Florida and in the Four Corners states of Arizona, Colorado, New Mexico and Utah forced local officials to relocate some polling sites and resort to emergency ballots.

The most high-profile technical problem occurred in Westchester County, N.Y., which includes part of the state's competitive 17th Congressional District. A ballot scanner issue took much of the day to get sorted out, meaning voters had to give their ballots to election workers for scanning at a later time. Some right-wing commentators online claimed the issue was a sign of fraud, but both the Republican and Democrat running in the district told voters not to be deterred.

"Every ballot will be counted," Mike Lawler, the Republican incumbent in the district, wrote in an X post after news of the issue arose.

The Trump administration's Justice Department had announced it planned to have around 1,000 monitors at polling places on Election Day, and shortly after the first polls opened on the East Coast Tuesday, the department said it was sending monitors to observe voting in every state.

"Election integrity has been one of the top priorities for this Administration and this Division," Harmeet Dhillon, the head of the Justice Department's Civil Rights Division, said in a statement.

Sending out election observers has been a routine way of documenting any racial discrimination at the polls under past administrations since the passing of the Voting Rights Act of 1965. But ongoing, baseless claims of widespread voter fraud from President Trump and other federal officials had heightened worries among many local officials and election experts that this administration's DOJ election monitors would interfere with voting at the polls.

As of Tuesday evening, however, no such activity has been reported.

But the New Mexico secretary of state's office received reports of DOJ monitors asking unusual questions and questioning voters in Bernalillo County, which is home to Albuquerque. "The New Mexico Department of Justice reached out to the DOJ Team Lead on our office's behalf to address this reported behavior, and we received assurances it would be addressed," spokesperson Lindsey Bachman said in a statement.

Concerns about Immigration and Customs Enforcement activity and law enforcement presence near polling sites also did not come to pass, as of 9 p.m. ET.

Instead, voters exercised their rights on what many voting observers considered to be a fairly normal Election Day.

Their ballots on Tuesday joined more than 61 million that were already cast during early voting. Nationally, early in-person and mail-in voters cast 34% more ballots compared to the last midterm general elections in 2022, according to the latest data compiled by the University of Florida's Election Lab. The pre-Election Day surge was driven mainly by in-person voting.

As the counting of ballots continues, voting rights advocates are now looking ahead to any potential controversies over close races and the certification of results. Some election officials have also warned about ballot seizures, especially after the FBI earlier this year seized election 2020 materials from Fulton County, Ga.

"Election Day is the last day that you can cast your ballot. But election season continues after today, and we're going to continue to remain vigilant," said Shaylyn Cochran, deputy executive director of the Lawyers' Committee for Civil Rights Under Law, during a press call on Tuesday.
