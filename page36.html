Crystal Palace are Thomas Frank's third London club after previous spells with Brentford and Tottenham

"When the time is right I will look forward to my return as a manager, ready to embrace the job with great energy and dedication," said Thomas Frank in June.

That time is now after the Dane was confirmed as the new manager of Crystal Palace, who are two points and a goal difference deficit away from safety at the bottom of the Premier League.

Former boss Pierre Sage was sacked last week after just 16 league and cup games.

The role offers Frank an opportunity to rebuild his reputation after he was sacked by Tottenham in February after eight months.

Frank described it as "an honour" to be "in charge of this fantastic football club" as Palace announced his arrival.

"Crystal Palace is an iconic club in England and in the Premier League," Frank told Palace's website.

"I will do everything I possibly can to help and support the players and everyone around the place, and I'm really looking forward to meeting them."

Palace chairman Steve Parish said Frank was the "perfect fit for our club".

But how big is the challenge at Selhurst Park that awaits the man who made his name at Brentford?

"It's going to be a bit of a shock for Thomas Frank," former West Ham and England goalkeeper Rob Green tells BBC Sport.

"He had his success at Brentford - a very well-run club where players are brought in with a lot of thinking and planning to take over the players they sell for profit.

Sandisk Is Splitting Its Stock 8-for-1. Here’s the Number I’d Check Before Buying.

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sandisk’s profit margin matters more to the investment case than its new share count.

A stock split always generates buzz. So, when Sandisk (SNDK -7.58%) recently announced an 8-for-1 stock split, investors cheered.

Yet while stock splits can make headlines, investors may want to focus on another figure -- its gross margin -- rather than its new, lower stock price. Here's why.

First, let's talk about stock splits. While they may seem exotic, they don't actually change anything fundamental about the business. The best analogy is culinary: If you take a whole, uncut pizza and cut it into eight slices, you've done nothing to change how much food you have. You've simply divided it up.

Stock splits work the same way. Fundamentals, like revenue, net income, and free cash flow, do not change when a company splits its shares. When Sandisk shares split on Nov. 20, no one receives anything for free; they will simply see what they already own split according to the 8-for-1 principle.

For example, say you own 10 shares of Sandisk today. As of Nov. 13, each share trades for around $858, for a total market value of $8,580. After the split, someone who owned 10 shares will own 80 shares, and each share will drop to $107.25 (assuming the price stays steady between now and then). The total market value of 80 shares at $107.25 per share? $8,580, the same as before.

Granted, the price of Sandisk shares will fluctuate between now and Nov. 20, but the principle remains the same. The market value of shares going into Nov. 20 will still equal the market value after the split; the only thing that will change is the share price.

Rather than focusing on the stock split, I think investors should take a closer look at Sandisk's gross margins. Gross margin is the profit remaining after deducting the cost of making a product.

Imagine Sandisk sells a memory chip for $100. If it took $15 to physically assemble the product, its gross margin on that product would be 85%. Bear in mind that the company doesn't get to keep the full $85 – it still has administrative and research costs, not to mention taxes.

However, gross margins are useful because they strip away these costs, allowing investors to see how profitable a business is without them. Sandisk's gross margins exploded since 2024, increasing from near 10% to around 85%.

SNDK Gross Profit Margin (Quarterly) data by YCharts

However, the big question is whether the company can maintain these incredible margins, or whether those margins will come crashing down.

For Sandisk, its gross margins are driven more by the price customers are willing to pay than by the input costs for its products. Therefore, it may be helpful to think of Sandisk's business like a hotel. The same rooms are far more profitable at $400 per night than at $150 per night, with many of the hotel's costs remaining steady.

Similarly, the supply-and-demand dynamics of the memory market have an enormous effect on Sandisk's gross margins. If memory demand were to pull back, perhaps as a result of slowing AI infrastructure investment, Sandisk's gross margins could tumble. On the other hand, if the company's long-term memory contracts prove durable, Sandisk's gross margins may remain resilient.

Stock splits alone do not make a stock an attractive investment. The split changes the unit size; profits determine the investment's appeal.

As a result, Sandisk must maintain its impressive gross margins in future quarters in order to justify its appeal. The stock already trades at a price-to-sales (P/S) ratio of 4.6. That's down significantly from its peak of over 25 in June, but it remains far above its 2025 average multiple of around 1.7.

The market has already priced a major step into Sandisk's profitability. If that fall doesn't materialize, the stock could be primed for a move higher. That's why I'm intrigued by Sandisk's gross margins, not its upcoming stock split.

Why scammers try to keep you on the phone (and how to spot their tactics)

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

If something feels off, hang up and call the company back on their official number.

That unexpected call from someone claiming to be from your bank can be worrisome, especially if they're calling to alert you about a suspicious money transfer. Your savings are at risk, but fret not, the caller who somehow got hold of your number says they can stop it — as long as you stay on the line.

But more than the emergency itself, their instruction to stay on call is the real catch. Scammers are counting on you to not hang up, as that valuable time helps them earn your trust. It then leads to turning up the pressure, which eventually leads you toward a decision you otherwise wouldn't take. That's when they might ask for a verification code, or straight up ask you to make a transfer. Keeping you occupied just long enough is the plan, so you don't end up calling the bank for a second opinion.

Dedicated scammers are known to stay on the line as the victims login to the accounts to transfer funds, buy gift cards or make a visit to the nearby ATM to withdraw cash. Some even go as far as to school you on what to tell bank employees when questioned about that odd transaction. When the person "helping" you protect your savings is deterring you from speaking to anyone else, that's a pretty obvious sign that something is off.

One possible way in could be a compromised bank account. A creative scammer could inform you about open accounts under your name or even scare you with an arrest warrant. The plan is to make you feel that following their instructions is the only way out, so hanging up doesn't feel like a plausible option.

Sometimes, the whole script includes more than just one scam artist. Back in August 2026, the FBI reported that scammers posing as bank employees told their victims that their information had been used to open an account to purchase illegal firearms. The scammers then transferred the victims to someone posing as an FBI agent, but disconnected after the victims were encouraged to verify the number.

Shortly after, they received a call displaying the FBI's Boston office number, which had been spoofed. These "FBI agents" could then ask the victims to continue the conversations using an encrypted messaging app. The FBI didn't specify how many people were targeted by this scheme.

But the threatening villain persona is not the only way these scammers are out to get you. Some of them act professional, stay patient and even go a step further to sound sympathetic. Now, when these friendly voices start to discourage you from reaching out to the organization directly or getting a second opinion, that should get your alarm bells ringing. After all, con artists don't want all of their efforts to result in failure.

Once the small talk is done, call handlers may switch to a dictating tone in order to get the outcome they want. This is a clear warning sign. When the caller demands you stay connected or instructs you to keep the conversation private, be sure to trust your instincts and end the call.

When they ask you to move funds to a "safer" account, it's time to hang up. Any request for passwords or even one-time verification codes should also be met with a call termination. Since scammers are often savvy enough to spoof authentic phone numbers, caller ID isn't all that reliable either.

Once you hang up or end that text conversation, reach out to the organization, but only using its official app or the number on your bank card or statement. Steer clear of contact numbers that the caller gives you, and trusting the customer-service numbers from sponsored search results isn't safe either. If it's a bit late to heed this advice and you've already shared sensitive information or worse transferred money to the scammer, contact your bank or payment provider right away.

Did you know your phone can filter out some of these unwanted calls? On iPhones running iOS 26 or newer, head to Settings > Apps > Phone > Screen Unknown Callers to enable extra incoming call checks, which include silencing unsaved numbers or asking callers to state who they are before they're connected.

If you own a supported Samsung Galaxy phone, try out spam filtering under Phone > Settings > Caller ID and spam protection, and Google Pixel owners can set up Call Screen using the Phone app's settings on supported devices in eligible regions. The downside is that legitimate callers can sometimes get caught by these filters.

Companies like Meta, Microsoft and Starlink have already worked to help take down over a million scam accounts. If you want to help save other unsuspecting consumers, it's best to report suspected scams to the FTC.

My wife, Christine, had to fly out to London for work after missing several trips due to my illness. Before she left, I had gotten my follow-up CT scan, but the radiologist hadn't read it yet. I had significant “scanxiety” about what it would show. While Christine was away, my kids did their best to help me—waking up on their own (tough as teens), fixing their own breakfast (really tough as teens), and getting out the door (really, really tough as teens). The house was quieter. The click-clack of her keyboard or murmur of her work video calls was absent.

I received the CT report a couple of days into Christine’s trip. Good news! The cancer in my right lung and a spot in my liver had shrunk. Being anxious, I got the report right away from my friend and primary care provider. That night, when I was showering, I was gripped with a sudden, unexpected sadness that verged on depression. Despite the positive results, in the back of my mind, I’d had this deep hope that my cancer would have magically vanished. Instead, the cancer, though smaller, was still there. The study my treatment was based on had shown that one patient had been miraculously noted in complete remission. It was just one point on a chart in a study, but the hope that it would happen for me had gripped my mind. I went into a negative-feedback cycle, and I was choked up, on the verge of tears. Somehow I had turned my good news into something horrible. Usually, I’d talk about the results and my hopes and fears, but she was a continent away and fast asleep. I made it through the week and to the eighth class, but the experience was a poignant reminder of how important this was to my mental well-being; sharing my experiences with others still is.

Early on in my diagnosis, I called my classmate and good friend from medical school, who practices pulmonology in San Diego. I wasn’t close to any other classmates in med school except for Elan—we bonded over Mike’s Roast Beef sandwiches while taking our physical diagnosis class. He kindly reviewed my tests and introduced me to another friend and colleague, who told me something that gives me hope every day: “You only have to live long enough for the next new treatment to come out.” That simple statement comforts me when I dwell on my late-stage prognosis, thinking about how many years (or months) I may have left.

One of the many beautiful parts of sharing my own cancer journey is meeting people going through their own cancer journeys who I would never have met otherwise. Sharing personal narratives—whether through storytelling, group discussions, or expressive writing—has emerged as a valuable psychosocial intervention in oncology. Storytelling is the communicative act of patients recounting their illness experiences through words, images, or even digital media, often revealing deeply personal emotions and insights. Across cancer types and patient populations, narrative-sharing interventions are increasingly recognized for fostering emotional expression and psychological adaptation to illness. Peer-reviewed research spanning children, adolescents, adults, and the elderly, indicates that storytelling bestows a range of benefits. These benefits include improvements in psychological well-being, coping and resilience, treatment adherence, social support, and even physiological or clinical outcomes.

Peer-reviewed research overwhelmingly supports the therapeutic value of storytelling and personal narratives for cancer patients of all ages and stages. Through sharing their experiences—whether in a support group, with a counselor, via digital media, or in a private journal—patients often achieve better psychological well-being, characterized by reduced anxiety, stress, and even depression. Storytelling fosters adaptation and coping, empowering patients with resilience and practical strategies to face cancer’s challenges. It also enhances social connectedness, diminishing isolation and strengthening support networks among those who “get it.” Notably, narrative interventions can encourage patients to stay engaged in treatment and healthy behaviors, potentially improving adherence and follow-through in care. While more research is needed, emerging evidence points to physiological benefits—improved symptom control, functional status, and overall quality of life—arising from the mind-body interplay of expressive storytelling. Taken together, these studies support the belief that the simple act of sharing one’s story is far more than talk or writing on a page; it is a meaningful therapeutic process that can help cancer patients heal psychologically, cope more effectively, build community, and perhaps even influence their health trajectories for the better. Narrative practices in oncology—from diagnosis through survivorship or end-of-life care—represent a holistic approach that honors patients’ voices while delivering measurable benefits across multiple dimensions of well-being.

Scans, treatments, family, and faith shape mental health; this can get wrapped into the stories people tell themselves when the future becomes a moving target. Narrative medicine is not a soft add-on to “real” care; it is part of the human need to communicate and be heard.

When U.S. President Donald Trump touches down in Shenzhen, China, this week for a three-way summit with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin, he will be embarking on the highest-level diplomacy he has ever undertaken at his weakest point in power.

The problem is not merely the devastating midterm election losses for his Republican Party, in which he urged supporters to turn out and cast their votes as if he were on the ballot. In recent weeks, Trump has displayed startling new levels of incoherence and evasion of responsibility for a sitting president. This seemed to reach a crescendo last week with two national crises for which Trump had no suitable answers.

The first of these crises brought news that Jensen Huang, the head of Nvidia, at once one of the world’s most valuable and strategically important companies, had had an affair with a suspected Chinese intelligence asset. Trump pleaded ignorance, denying well-sourced reports that he had been briefed about the matter as long ago as May, including by his national security advisor and secretary of state, Marco Rubio. The only thoughts the president seemed able to summon reflected credulity about Huang’s claim that he had never suspected the woman could have been a spy and personal sympathy for a man who had, until his sudden resignation from Nvidia last week, belonged to a club that means more to Trump than any other: the exclusive circle of the world’s richest and most powerful men. Perhaps for that reason, on Monday, Trump told reporters he “would not mind” if Huang was reinstated.

For all of Trump’s envy of powerful authoritarians, if the situations were reversed and Huang were a Chinese national leading a company as important as Nvidia, it is impossible to imagine Xi treating a man under such suspicions this way, including welcoming him on a flight to his country’s No. 1 competitor and adversary. But Trump’s need for this kind of validation is an ongoing matter and not something he has learned a lesson from. Witness his decision to invite the world’s richest man, Elon Musk, to join him on this trip, if not in the meetings with his fellow heads of state.

For a U.S. president heading off to a summit such as this, the week’s second crisis may have been even worse. The image the United States presented to the world in the wake of a breakdown in the nationwide air traffic control system on Friday was one of utter dishevelment and incompetence. It put the spotlight on two of arguably the least qualified members of a cabinet resolutely hostile to meritocracy: Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy, a former congressman and Fox News personality, and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin, a former congressman whose formal education culminated with an associate’s degree in plumbing and construction.

With thousands of flights canceled and a country desperate for answers and reassurance, they bumbled their way through an almost incoherent news conference that offered no real explanations. Instead, they cast blame on others, including Congress and past administrations, while exalting Trump’s leadership as if they were members of a personality cult and the public should be too. During the first 36 hours of the crisis, Trump for his part chose to lie low, making no statements about the matter, apparently fearing that doing so would mean taking responsibility for something bad that happened on his watch, which he has always been deeply allergic to.

These displays of unaccountable behavior and cluelessness are bad enough for the United States in their own right, but Americans should be worried about their implications for the upcoming summit. Even in ordinary times, Trump’s narcissism and emotional neediness make diplomacy like this dangerous because he craves fellowship and approval from men such as Putin and Xi, whom he all but openly idolizes. But with last week’s crises and domestic scandals sprouting in many directions as Democrats prepare to take over Congress, Trump is liable to make large and unwise concessions to his imagined peers in order to secure political coups that he hopes can restore prestige and momentum to his badly weakened presidency. The cliché holds that weakened or trapped animals are liable to strike out to defend themselves. With Trump, the danger is the opposite—given his self-centered view of the world, where his own ego gratification is paramount, he could give away the store.

What could that mean? Here, one feels that Trump is so easily flattered that the sky is almost the limit. He has reportedly already forsworn raising the Nvidia crisis with Xi as well as Russia’s ongoing war of aggression against Ukraine. If true, such self-imposed restrictions themselves emphasize the weakness of the U.S. president’s hand and preemptively vitiate the utility of such an unusual gathering.

What does this leave on the table for discussion? The Trump administration has said it hopes to make progress on limiting nuclear weapons armament at a time when the United States and Russia still have enormous legacy arsenals whose maintenance and modernization are costly and China is belatedly racing to catch up. Trump officials have also said they hope to engage leaders of the world’s other two big powers on the United States’ stop-and-go war against Iran, which by now has turned into a quagmire.

But what could the latter possibly yield? That Trump has already displayed weakness and enormous gullibility on Iran in his dealings with both powers does not bode well. Russia has supplied intelligence and munitions support to Iran throughout the war, enabling that country to target U.S. military bases throughout the Middle East with devastating effect. Consistent with his general enthrallment to Putin, Trump has publicly downplayed the importance of this, making clear that maintaining warm relations with Moscow is his priority. China, if more cautious than Russia, has also hewed to a line that has enhanced the Iranian state’s position. It has done this by continuing to purchase as much Iranian oil as it can while also reportedly supplying electronic components essential to that country’s missile and drone production. Genuine progress toward administration goals on Iran would require concrete commitments from both powers to stop what they are currently doing. Trump appears unlikely to demand this, and even if he did, neither is likely to follow through.

Putin has already mesmerized Trump into treating Ukraine as if it were the faulty party in its defensive war against Russia. That conflict may not officially be on the agenda when the three leaders sit down together on Thursday, but the specific danger in Shenzhen is that Putin will offer Trump a vague, unverifiable promise to limit support for Iran—something that Trump can present as a win—in exchange for Trump’s deeper complicity in ending the Ukraine war on Russian terms. It would be a trade of something real and irreversible for something hollow and unenforceable. Because of Trump’s unique weakness for glad-handing and grandiosity, it may take little more than that. A more lucid and competent U.S. president would never allow such confusion between the two crises.

What might Xi seek, and how do Trump’s growing vulnerabilities play into this? Beijing’s biggest goal is progress toward exerting control over Taiwan, if not taking over the island altogether by the eventual end of Xi’s longtime hold on power. Toward this end, Trump has already gifted Xi in important ways without receiving anything in return. At the level of official rhetoric, Trump has weakened America’s traditional support for a peaceful resolution of the dispute between Beijing and Taipei over the island’s future. In fact, he has come close to saying the United States would have no business defending Taiwan in case a war broke out between the two sides.

This by itself is an immense gift, and it would be enhanced further if Trump delayed arms sales to Taiwan to ingratiate himself with Xi. Already, the U.S. military posture in East Asia has been dramatically weakened by the prolonged deployment of aircraft carrier battle groups near Iran and by heavy depletion of advanced weapons systems during the war with that country. Trump has also weakened U.S. alliances in Asia through tariff demands and unpredictable behavior. By nature, Xi seems much more cautious than Putin. But sensing Trump’s weakness and need for some kind of foreign-policy achievement, however illusory, he could try to induce concessions through displays of strongman camaraderie and vague pledges of cooperation in areas of U.S. need.

If we cannot know in advance what these meetings will produce, we should know one thing already. Trump will emerge from them making great claims about how well he gets along with the world’s two other most powerful heads of state. In fact, nothing more might publicly come out of the meetings beyond this. But the world should be wary even of any quiet understandings that Trump, at this moment of extreme weakness and vulnerability, believes he has reached. The likelihood is that he is being played due to his personal and political need, and if the details are unknowable, the costs could be great.

All the Award-Winning Photos From Nature Photographer of the Year 2026

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

All the Award-Winning Photos From Nature Photographer of the Year 2026

The Nature Photographer of the Year 2026 winners have been unveiled, showcasing the world’s best nature, wildlife, and landscape photography.

This year’s competition received 27,108 entries from photographers in nearly 100 countries, a record-breaking number for the longtime photo contest.

Finnish photographer Hannu Ahonen bested stiff competition to earn the title of Nature Photographer of the Year. Ahonen’s award-winning photo, “The One that Left and the One that Stayed,” is an extraordinary image of a water lily leaf positioned alongside its icy duplicate.

“It is not often that a photograph of a plant is crowned the overall winner of a major photography competition, but the jury could not overlook this fascinating image by Hannu Ahonen,” says jury chair Theo Bosboom. “First and foremost, the photograph captures a unique situation: a water lily leaf with an almost perfect black-and-white replica of it lying next to it in the water.”

Ahonen’s winning photo tells a compelling and unusual story. The living water lily leaf is positioned on the left, while the ice that formed on top of it and slipped off sits delicately to the side, blown away slightly by the breeze. Even the veins from the leaf are visible in the icy shell.

Nature Photographer of the Year adds that the photo reveals the “marvelous beauty of nature” while embodying its transient character. The sheet of ice will soon melt away, joining the water that keeps the lily alive in the first place. It’s a symbolic photo at a time when climate change is impacting all life on Earth, especially its ice.

“The winter ice had arrived, but it remained snowless for a long time. Some of the pond’s lily pad leaves began to crumble on top of the ice. The wind carried a few of them away, revealing delicate, frosty ornaments left behind. I eagerly captured this natural phenomenon, something I had never witnessed before,” Ahonen explains.

Ahonen has an inspiring story. He has ADHD and lives in a wilderness home in Finland with no carbon footprint. Rarely venturing more than a couple of miles from his home, he spends much of his time behind the camera in his yard, using photography as a spiritual escape and creative outlet.

Alongside the overall winner, Ahonen, the competition’s jury also selected winners across nearly a dozen categories. There are also special prizes for youth photographers and an accomplished portfolio. Ahonen took the top spot in the Plants and Fungi category, so that category is not included below.

French photographer Lucas Mugnier-Lavorel won this year’s Birds category for his environmental portrait of a Magellanic woodpecker in Patagonia.

“Environmental portraits of wildlife can make us feel closer to the animals despite being farther away from them, when done right. This image of a Magellanic woodpecker in its gorgeous habitat was a perfect example. It stood out from thousands of submissions because of its understated sense of place, beauty, simplicity, and timing,” says judge Steffen Foerster.

“Overall, it was a very successful reminder of how the portrayal of nature in its literal state can still be the most powerful of photographic expressions,” Foerster concludes.

British photographer Paul Goldstein’s foggy photo of a giraffe earned the top prize in the Mammals category.

“Quiet images can be easy to overlook in the fiercely competitive mammal category, one of the largest in NPOTY. This ethereal image of a giraffe peeking out of the mist revealed its strengths gradually. Initially a little disorienting, it offered just enough of a visual puzzle to keep the viewer engaged until the image resolved,” Foerster explains.

Judges tapped Marcus Kuan’s macro photo of a surprisingly rich and diverse little ecosystem alongside a pitcher plant in the Other Animals category. The Singaporean photographer impressed the panel with his composition and lighting.

“The lighting, colors, and unexpected gathering of species immediately caught our attention. Kuan beautifully captured a world where crustaceans, amphibians, and carnivorous plants cross paths,” explains jury member Louis Guillot.

“As judges, we were drawn to the possibilities unfolding within this single frame. Could the pitchers be digesting invertebrates? Might the frog be exploring a potential egg-laying site, while the crab continues its daily search for food among the plants?” Guillot continues.

Photographer J Fritz Rumpf from the United States won this year’s Landscape category with a silhouetted photo of Iguazú Falls in Argentina.

“What immediately caught our attention was the incredible light and atmosphere in this photo. The longer we looked, the more fascinating it became. There is so much to discover: the last light of day illuminating the mist rising from Iguazú Falls, the silhouettes of trees and rocks, the birds circling above, the sunlight streaming through an opening in the rock at the center, and the subtle waterfall on the left,” says jury member Ilse Koks.

Another American photographer, Matt Pérez, won the Underwater category with a striking photo of a turtle beneath a collection of newt eggs.

“Pérez’s photograph immediately drew us in. As a jury, we wanted to linger over it, exploring the details and wondering what we were seeing,” remarks Guillot. “Newt egg masses decorate the submerged branches like baubles on a Christmas tree, framing the scene and guiding our eyes towards the pond turtle with its piercing yellow eyes.”

Guillot adds that the scene looks “otherworldly,” which is a very apt description.

Chinese photographer Xuzhong Liu’s abstract drone shot of a winding landscape ticks all the right boxes. It has interesting patterns, striking colors, and a very pleasing composition.

Liu captured the image in a wetland area in China and says the scene strongly reminded him of peach blossoms he had previously photographed—hence the title, “Peach Blossoms in the Snow.”

“This photograph has everything that can make a drone shot interesting and fascinating. First and foremost, there are beautiful shapes and subtle colours, but there is also a sense of alienation. What am I actually looking at, and what is the scale of the landscape in the photograph?” asks the chair of the judging panel, Theo Bosboom.

Czech photographer Petr Bambousek’s winning photo depicts a sobering scene of a Malayan sun bear resting inside a garbage incinerator.

“The winning image stood out for its striking depiction of the impact human activity has on local wildlife,” explains jury member Lana Tannir. “The sleeping Malayan sun bear inside a garbage incinerator creates a powerful visual contrast, showing how habitat loss and human waste increasingly force wild animals into close contact with human environments.”

Tannir says Bambousek’s photo is “unexpected and unsettling.”

Hungarian photographer László Tóth won the Black and White category for his striking, high-contrast, abstract photo.

“This photo really stood out to us. At first, we were not quite sure what we were looking at. It sparked some wonderful discussions among the jury and made us look even closer,” says Ilse Koks.

As for what the photo actually depicts, it’s a thin layer of snow on top of a frozen lake, shaped by the wind.

This photo might be familiar to PetaPixel readers. Norwegian photographer Audun Rikardsen recently won an award for a similar underwater portrait of a polar bear peering down through a hole in the ice.

“Just when we thought we had seen about every kind of polar bear image there is, along comes this shot taken from inside a hole in the ice looking up at the bear,” says Steffen Foerster. “Once you realize what is happening, you start thinking about how this shot could possibly have been taken. Then it dawns on you that it seems almost impossible, no matter how.”

This special category, “De Lage Landen” (which translates to “The Low Countries”), celebrates photos captured in the low-lying region of northwestern Europe, including the Netherlands, where Nature Photographer of the Year is based. This year’s winning photo is by Hungarian photographer Enikő Kálovics.

“The winning image stood out for its ability to transform a familiar landscape into something dreamlike and unexpected,” says Lana Tannir. “The combination of the tiny lacewings, the autumn forest, and the purplish night sky creates a delicate, atmospheric interpretation of the nature of ‘De Lage Landen.'”

American photographer Parham Pourahmad, who was also recently celebrated in the 2027 North American Nature Photography Association (NANPA) Showcase, won this year’s Youth category with a lovely monochromatic photo of a coyote beneath a large tree.

“In ‘Tree of Life’, Pourahmad captured a striking scene that could comfortably hold its own in the adult categories,” says Guillot. “Black and white was an excellent choice, defining the coyote’s silhouette and revealing the majestic oak’s intricate network of branches. Even within this wider scene, the coyote’s downward gaze is clear. Pourahmad explained that it was searching for rodent prey, a small detail that brings the photograph to life.”

Pourahmad has also been named Youth Photographer of the Year.

The Fred Hazelhoff Award celebrates a portfolio of nature photos, rather than a single image. This year’s portfolio winner is German photographer Simone Baumeister.

“In the portfolio category, the bar is set considerably higher for participating photographers than in the individual categories. Whilst a single fantastic photograph is sufficient there, in the portfolio category you need a whole series of outstanding images that not only complement one another but also fit together well in terms of style and theme. Simone Baumeister has succeeded brilliantly in this with her portfolio on eagle owls in the city,” says jury chair Theo Bosboom.

Baumeister has been fascinated by owls for as long as she has been interested in nature photography. The eagle owl’s story is a positive one, in contrast to the plight of many species across the globe. The species, once on the brink of extinction in Germany, has made an incredible recovery. Its resurgence is thanks largely to major conservation efforts and extensive reintroduction programs.

“It was perched in twilight on a church, right below the street lamps, moving cars, and a string of people passing by, with music blaring from a pub right on the church square. Not a soul took notice of the impressive bird with a wingspan of 1.7 meters flying up the steeple, sitting enthroned right within everybody’s view,” the photographer says of the first time she saw an eagle owl in a city.

Ever since then, she has been consistently excited by eagle owls and keen to photograph them in many different places. Observing and photographing them in various cities inevitably morphed into a dedicated project.

FBI says Artex AI open-source hacking tool used to breach networks in ‘under three hours’

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The FBI is warning that hackers are relying on autonomous AI hacking tools to launch cyberattacks across the U.S. and globally. The campaigns include a widespread hack of customers of American IT company ConnectWise and across South Korea’s financial industry.

In its latest flash cybersecurity advisory, the FBI says hackers have targeted several industries in recent months, including financial services, government, tech, defense, phone and internet companies, and law firms, using open-source tools like Artex to carry out autonomous-driven cyberattacks.

Artex is one of several open-source AI-powered penetration testing platforms, a category of software built to probe a network’s defenses. Hackers can connect Artex to hosted frontier large language models (LLMs) like those from Anthropic and OpenAI, as well as to open-source models and non-U.S. models. The FBI says Artex intrusions can be “noisy,” meaning they generate reams of traffic and data that may trigger a targeted company’s defensive alerts. But the tools allow hackers to move at speeds that makes it far more harder for human defenders to keep up.

In some cases, Artex-linked attacks can move from targeting a system to active compromise “in under three hours.”

The FBI’s warning comes as AI-powered hacks are escalating, particularly by finding easy-to-find security flaws and breaking into companies with relative ease.

Oftentimes, Artex will rely on rapidly scanning a victim’s network to look for signs of weakness, and then exploit simple security bugs, such as weak protections and exposed or stolen passwords to gain a foothold. From there, Artex allows the hacker to spread across the network for databases, mailboxes, and other sensitive information.

The FBI’s advisory noted that Artex has been used in several cyberattacks since the tool’s first public debut in July. Artex was used to hack a number of South Korean financial institutions, blamed in large part on weak cybersecurity defenses and lacking protections, such as multi-factor authentication. The attacks allowed hackers to steal the personal data of at least 68,000 people.

The FBI confirmed earlier findings from Microsoft that hackers also used Artex to break into IT management giant ConnectWise and gain access to the Microsoft 365 cloud environments of at least 2,250 of its customers.

The hackers, who Microsoft says are likely Chinese, used the tool to steal data from affected ConnectWise customers.

A spokesperson for ConnectWise did not respond to a request for comment about the FBI’s claim.

Cybersecurity company Barracuda also disclosed that it was breached as part of an Artex-linked attack by financially motivated criminals, in which the tool found an exposed credential belonging to a third-party service that Barracuda had not revoked when it ended work with the vendor. That access allowed the hackers to access stores of credentials used by Barracuda to collect data from its customers’ systems. Barracuda said it couldn’t rule out access, as its logs were not detailed enough, and chose to “assume the worst” and changed all of its customer credentials.

“Defenders who rely on spotting malicious activity and then responding to it will often arrive after the attacker has finished,” wrote Barracuda’s chief information security officer Arve Kjoelen.

Barracuda did not return a request for comment about how many customers are affected by the breach.

After Artex was used in cyberattacks, the tool’s developer made the software closed-source to prevent further attacks. But the FBI says the previous open-source version continues to circulate and remains “widely accessible” for use in cyberattacks.

It’s not only companies and targeted victims that have been compromised as a result of weakened security exploited by AI tools; the tools themselves have been exposed as well, potentially allowing outsiders to access powerful hacking capabilities. Last week, researchers found hundreds of publicly exposed Artex systems online, which had been placed on the internet without a password, likely by mistake or misconfiguration — including the Artex system that was used to target South Korea.

Nokia, Telia, Verda, Nscale and Skeleton team up for EU AI gigafactory bid

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The AI gigafactory would link sites in Oulu, Iru and Valmiera, with a Swedish site still to be chosen. The EU’s tender closes on 3 December for one lot and 4 February for the other.

Mikko Hautala, Nokia’s chief geopolitical and government relations officer and chairman of Nokia Defense.

Nokia, Nscale, Skeleton, Telia and Verda have formed a group to bid for one of the EU’s planned AI gigafactories. Called AI Gigafactory Northern, it would spread one facility across several Nordic and Baltic sites, the partners said in a joint release on Monday.

The sites named so far are in Oulu in Finland, Iru in Estonia and Valmiera in Latvia. A fourth site, in Sweden, has no location yet. The partners plan to run them as one federated AI gigafactory under European governance.

The consortium plans to bid in a tender run by the European High-Performance Computing Joint Undertaking. That EU body also runs Europe’s shared supercomputers, such as Finland’s LUMI AI supercomputer. The call opened on 30 July and will pick the groups that build and run the gigafactories. The EU and its member states will then buy computing time from them.

Each gigafactory must hold three to four times as many top AI processors as Europe’s most powerful AI factories, the call says. It must also show enough power capacity and meet standards on energy and water efficiency.

The deadline for the call’s second lot is 3 December. On 5 November, the EU body moved the first lot’s deadline to 4 February 2027. The delay gives member states time to commit funding.

Everything You Need to Know About Korean Scalp Scalers

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

If you’ve ever been to a Korean head spa, you know it’s less of a hair wash and more of an entire reset. Before anyone touches a shampoo bottle, there’s usually a close-up look at your scalp on a magnifying camera, followed by a treatment that loosens all the oil, dead skin, and product reside that a regular wash leaves behind. It’s the step that makes your hair feel impossibly light afterward, and it’s called scaling.

You don’t need a spa appointment to get the same effect, though. Scalp scalers bring that deep clean home, whether that’s an acid-based treatment you leave on before shampooing or a salt scrub that lathers up in the shower. They’re especially useful if you lean on dry shampoo, work out often, or have fine hair that goes flat a day after washing.

Below, experts explain what a scalp scaler actually does, who should use one and how to keep from overdoing it, plus the formulas worth trying.

The name actually comes from the dentist’s office. “In Korean, ‘scaling’ is the same word we use for dental cleaning,” says Dr. Ellie Byun, a doctor of Korean medicine and licensed acupuncturist. “Plaque builds up on your teeth even when you brush, and in the same way, oil and dead skin build up on your scalp even when you shampoo.” For many Korean women, it’s a routine they’ve had for years. “I’ve been using scalp scalers since high school,” says Kristin Lim is the founder of Base-K.

What sets a scaler apart from a regular wash is time. “A clarifying shampoo only cleanses during the short time it’s on your scalp,” says Dr. Byun. “A scaler stays on for several minutes and chemically dissolves dead skin and oil with acids and enzymes, the same principle as an exfoliating toner for your face.” That said, plenty of Korean scalers, including several below, use salt granules instead of acids, or a mix of both. “A scalp scaler focuses on loosening dead skin and buildup, using acids, physical exfoliants or a combination of both,” says Lim. If you go the gritty route, keep it gentle. “A scrub physically buffs the surface with grains, and if you use it too often, it can make the scalp barrier sensitive,” says Dr. Byun.

“It’s especially good for oily scalps, flaking caused by buildup, fine hair that goes flat quickly, people who use a lot of dry shampoo and styling products, and anyone who sweats a lot,” says Byun. Flaking is where it gets tricky, though. “It can also help loosen surface flakes, but persistent flaking and itching deserve a closer look,” says Lim. “Dandruff and other scalp conditions may need targeted treatment rather than more exfoliation.”

The first thing most people notice is how light their hair feels afterward. “Removing that residue can leave the scalp feeling cleaner and the hair feeling lighter,” says Lim. With regular use, it can help with oil and flaking, too. “In a four-week study of a salicylic acid formula, researchers examined the scalp with a magnification camera and found that flaking, itching, redness, and oiliness all decreased,” says Dr. Byun. A calmer scalp may also mean better hair over time. “Studies show that when scalp health declines, hair quality declines too, and when the scalp recovers, hair quality recovers with it,” she says.

The biggest risk is doing too much. “Over-exfoliating weakens the scalp barrier and can cause irritation, dryness, and even a rebound increase in oil,” says Dr. Byun. It’s not the right fit for every scalp, either. “Individuals with sensitive or irritated scalps should approach exfoliating treatments cautiously,” says Joe Cho, team lead of media relations at Medicube. Even a mild label doesn’t change that. “Even a gentle formula may not suit a scalp that’s actively inflamed,” says Lim. If something feels off, don’t push through it. “If you notice sudden heavy shedding, pain, or lesions, see a professional,” says Dr. Byun.

“Some scalers go onto a dry or damp scalp before shampooing, while others are used on wet hair as part of the wash,” says Lim. Either way, the product needs to reach your scalp, not just your hair. “Part your hair in sections about an inch apart and apply it directly to the scalp along each part,” says Byun. “Then massage with your fingertips, never your nails, for two to three minutes, pressing slowly on the crown and the base of the skull.” Then rinse it out on time. “Leaving it on longer or scrubbing harder won’t improve the result,” says Lim.

Less is more with frequency, too. “I recommend once a week for oily scalps, and once every two weeks for normal or dry scalps,” says Byun. Your scalp will tell you when you’ve gone too far. “Signs you’re overdoing it include tightness, stinging, redness, or tenderness, more flaking instead of less, new itching, or a scalp that feels oilier than before,” she says. Lim also recommends sticking to one exfoliant at a time and avoiding combining several scalp exfoliants in the same wash.

Afterward, think of your scalp the way you’d think about your face after an exfoliating toner. “Korean care follows a simple idea: clear first, then nourish,” Dr. Byun says. “Use a mild, low-pH shampoo, then a lightweight scalp serum with niacinamide, panthenol, or centella, and apply conditioner only from the mid-lengths to the ends.”

Not directly. “While scalp health plays an important role in overall hair care, there’s currently limited clinical evidence that scalp exfoliation alone can directly stimulate hair growth or prevent shedding,” says Cho. Dr. Byun sees it as groundwork. “A scaler doesn’t grow hair on its own, but it does have a real, research-backed role,” she says. “So I see scaling as the first step in creating an environment where healthy hair can grow.” Lim is just as clear: “Promising that a scaler alone will regrow hair or stop shedding would be over-promising.”

Usually, scalp scales are safe for colored hair as long as you keep it where it belongs. “It’s generally fine on color-treated hair as long as you apply it to the scalp, not the hair itself,” says Byun.

Curator Christiane Paul Helped Write the History of Net Art. She’s Doing It Again with a New Survey at the Whitney.

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Editor’s Note: This story is part of Newsmakers, a new ARTnews series where we interview the movers and shakers who are making change in the art world.

Rare are the times when a New York museum has turned over an entire floor to a presentation of digital art. That makes “artport: A History of Internet Art,” opening at the Whitney Museum on Saturday, an outlier in the institution’s programming—and something of a major occasion.

The titular artport is an online gallery launched in 2001 by curator Christiane Paul, who has since gone on to commission plenty of works in the intervening 25 years, by artists ranging from JODI to Mimi Ọnụọha. Officially indexed as part of the Whitney collection since 2015, artport continues to commission artists. The most recent was Jazsalyn, who, for one work staged earlier this year, proposed what she called “Black Metatheories” as means of forming something alternative to AI and called on users to respond to a range of prompts, including one that reads: “Consider blackness. How does life persist within the spectrum of impossible possibility?” Related Articles Frieze's Kristell Chadé Discusses Why the Fair Has 'Never Been About Adding Points on a Map' Newsmakers: Studio Ma's Christiana Moss on Building Museums for a Hotter, More Expensive World

Artport’s offerings live on the Whitney’s website. Generally, however, they have not been show in the Whitney’s galleries, something that will change with Paul’s exhibition opening this week, which features more than works associated with artport. The show is yet another digital art–focused exhibition from one of the great thinkers on the subject. She literally wrote the book on digital art in 2003, and is currently in the process of writing a follow-up to it.

ARTnews spoke with Paul, an adjunct curator of media art at the Whitney, to learn more about artport’s history and how she created such an ambitious show. This interview has been condensed and edited for clarity.

ARTnews: Artport was founded in 2001, but it didn’t become a part of the Whitney’s collection until 2015. More than 10 years later, what is its place at the Whitney today?

Christiane Paul: I would not say it’s changed much. The original thinking, which we kept intact, was: It’s kind of weird to lock down net art in a collection. And that is why we decided to commission the works under non-exclusive licenses. But what changed in 2015 was really that we wanted to acknowledge the artist’s status in all of this. By making it officially its own little umbrella collection within the permanent collection, all of the artists gained lifetime membership status, and are now treated the same way as collection artists—that wasn’t the case before.

Also, the works are of course more integrated into the collection database. Before, when you searched, everything was there on artport, but it did not necessarily appear in collection searches, which also became problematic when we added more physical works by some of the artists—for example, a sculpture by Auriea Harvey [whose work was commissioned by artport in 2023]—to the collection. You would find the sculpture, but not the rest of her work.

When artport commissions an artist—let’s say, for its “On the Hour” series, which stages projects on the Whitney’s website at designated times—who is the commissioner, the Whitney or artport?

The commissioner is always the Whitney Museum, and I invite artists to submit a proposal. What we don’t do is show existing work. We did a series from 2009 to 2023, “Sunrise Sunset,” that marked sunrise and sunset through a 30-second intervention into whitney.org that played on all pages. I found the format highly intriguing, because it’s so net art to disrupt, particularly an institutional website. While it was very beautiful and poetic, there also was a concern: This was sunrise and sunset, New York time only. That prompted us to change it to make it into hourly interventions.

My invitations to “On the Hour” are often based on two criteria. First of all, who are artists who can really work with that kind of seriality intervention, in terms of vignettes? I think it’s also a great way to give younger artists a chance to do something for the museum. In general, artport has always paid a lot of attention to emerging artists, but due to the format, this lends itself to being a little more experimental.

What did you look for when you started commissioning artists? How did you know an artist was good for artport?

The main goal of artport was always to show the breadth of practice in the in the online field. When artport was launched in 2001, of course, there already was a history of net art. I mean, depending on if you’re counting by accessibility to the public, the internet launched in 1993. Artistic practice in this space started around that time and really took off in the mid-’90s. So, by the time I started, there was already a backlog of the artists who were creating the language of the field: Mark Napier, Tina La Porta, Martin Wattenberg, Marek Walczak, Amy Alexander. I was rapidly moving through those people at the time. Then I was following who was doing significant work in that area.

Certainly, when you started artport, institutions had already begun paying attention to net art. Documenta X in 1997 had a net art section, and Lawrence Rinder organized a net art portion of the 2000 Whitney Biennial. You did something similar for the 2002 edition, and Rhizome partnered with the New Museum in 2003. But it seems like we’re in a very different moment now, where net art can even be the subject of major exhibitions like the artport one. What does it mean, now, to bring net art into museum walls?

I would once again say my approach has not changed that much. There’s more technological support for doing these types of shows, so the infrastructural possibilities and budget certainly did change. But since the ’90s, there has been this discussion of: “Oh, this should only be seen online.” I always thought that was such an ironic misunderstanding because online is not on your couch in your house. Online is everywhere! We’re online in the museum. People are on their phones. Everything is networked.

You’re definitely changing the context [by showing it in a museum], but it’s important to see this art in the context of other art forms, and that was very important to me. The challenge that an exhibition like this poses is: How do you make the work easily accessible within a gallery space while also maintaining its net-ness? On the one hand, it’s about not overwhelming people with dozens and dozens of screens, which we do still have in this exhibition. But we’re also changing formats quite a bit, and at the same time, we’re not sucking out what the online environment is really about.

The way I approached it with this exhibition was to use an open floor and a landscape of browser windows as a design conceit. There are floating walls held up by poles. There’s also a lot of cabling, as you can imagine. The walls or panels suggest open browser windows on which the screens are presented. They’re not segregating works into rooms—they’re all the web, where you might switch from one tab to another. Some works have been translated into more of an installation, like Andy Deck’s Screening Circle [2006], for example, which you use online, with different people contributing to it. You use your mouse to choose pixels and create an icon within this screening circle. And in the gallery, you will see it projected onto a platform with actually four tablets for input, so four people can use it simultaneously. It will switch between them and also bring in the online work.

There are also works that have components of paintings or prints, like Michael Mandiberg, for example. He did an endurance performance, Life Study [2018], where, over the course of four years, he filmed himself doing paintings of his assistants. We’re not only showing the work and its archive, but also some of the paintings.

The exhibition is titled “artport: A History of Internet Art,” which suggests that it is a history of net art, and that there are others still to be told. What is the history you’re telling?

Julia Kaganskiy puts it nicely in her essay for the catalog. There are timestamps that are demarcating the concerns, attitudes, social norms, and media environments that have shaped our collective lives. I think any type of net art that one would have picked beyond artport from the ’90s until today would somehow tell that history. Of course, the history that artport tells is a very specific one in that there are certain choices of artists, and they tell that history in a specific way, through their personal lens. If it were a completely different roster of artists, those timestamps marking these shifts would still be there, but the story would be told slightly differently.

There’s also a new performance by the artist duo Operator: the third act of their ongoing “Human Unreadable” project, begun in 2022.

Operator has, for the past four years, created the series “Human Unreadable,” which is a generative choreography on the blockchain. It’s open source, so that people can use to do their own performances. In Act I, they translated sequences that were created by the choreography into large-scale black and white images that were sold as NFTs. In Act II, collectors could reveal the movement sequence that had generated the image. From the start, the work raised the question: How much of the body survives translation into code? In Act III, we have bodies without the mediation of the screen performing the first 100 tokens choreographic sequences. It really is a multimedia theater piece that also includes spoken word, and it is really about our relationships to technological systems, the artist’s personal story, and negotiating ambition and technology in your life. And its two matinees are live streamed: I think we now have 18 partner institutions. The live stream is not simply “Oh, here is a camera that shows you what’s happening at the Whitney.” It is more of an artwork in and of itself, with moving cameras and modifications, almost a little bit more like Nam June Paik’s Good Morning, Mr. Orwell minus the technological difficulties. [Laughs.]

A lot of younger viewers who encounter this exhibition may not entirely understand everything involved in creating the early net art surveyed here—for example, the splash pages produced for artport between 2001 and 2006, which rely on a technology that is generally out of use in our current internet. How will you contextualize those pieces for viewers less familiar with them?

There were 57 splash pages—they were done monthly—and I am only showing the ones where artists created standalone projects rather than just a splash page or gate page for their work, because that kind of browsing is better done at home. The intention is to get lost in an artist’s work and website, and that would really distract from the focus of the exhibition. That being said, we are explaining the functionality of splash pages and what they were in the section text. But I hope the art of that time, and the environment of the web at the time, really translates—to me, that is actually the most significant part of the exhibition. There are these really nice connections across the floor between these early works and later ones that show you how really our attitudes towards self-representation, identity, surveillance data processing have shifted over time.

There’s an early work by Jillian McDonald that was made 20 years before deepfakes. It’s about fan culture—she inserted herself into the film Buffalo ’66 with Vincent Gallo. I have it right across from works by Tina La Porta, who did voyeur_web [2001] at a time when live casting was so popular. People would put up webcams in their homes, in their kitchens, in their bedrooms, as this kind of gesture of returning the gaze, something that people wouldn’t do anymore today. The freedom that some of this early net artists had is amazing. It’s just not possible anymore, with so much of the internet became commodified, capitalized, and cleaner.

Kin in the Game: How the Rise of Family Office Investors Is Reshaping Media, Entertainment and Sports

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The dawn of Skydance — the film and TV leviathan that came forth from the biggest media acquisition in 25 years — was enabled by the most influential trend in private investment in a decade: the rise of the family office investor.

“Family office” is cuddly financial jargon for a specific form of holding company that ultrarich people and their relatives rely on to manage their wealth. The proliferation of near billionaires — and billionaires proper — in the U.S. since the stock market boom began in the 1980s has led to the creation of thousands of well-endowed family offices. “The size of this is extraordinary,” says Ian D’Souza, clinical professor of finance at the NYU Stern School of Business. “A lot of people don’t realize just how big they are as an investor class in a global context and their different risk-return drivers.” Related Stories 'Nobody Wants This' Renewed for Season 4 at Netflix ‘Russell Brand: Open Secret’: Netflix Doc Reveals His ‘Jokes’ About Rape, Making a Girlfriend ‘Brush Her Teeth Until They Bled’ and More

Family offices are gaining a level of clout that is having a profound impact on the ownership of media and entertainment assets. Many operate with a low profile — they don’t have the same financial disclosure requirements as other companies and investment firms. Among the most powerful entities flexing financial muscle is Lawrence Investments, the office that handles the prodigious fortune amassed by 82-year-old Larry Ellison, co-founder of software giant Oracle. Ellison is the father of Skydance chairman and CEO David Ellison, the mastermind of Skydance’s quest to bolt together studio and network assets.

The younger Ellison’s one-two punch began last year with his $8 billion acquisition of Paramount and CBS. And then he went in dogged pursuit of Warner Bros., HBO, CNN, Discovery and numerous other channels. It all culminated in the closing of the $111 billion Warner Bros. Discovery transaction on Oct. 6. David Ellison couldn’t have pulled off those head-spinning deals without the full faith and credit backing of the enormous Ellison family wealth held through Lawrence Investments, which has an estimated $160 billion in assets under management. But Ellison’s empire-building boom has been financed with debt.

The enlarged Skydance is carrying $80 billion in red ink, and if Skydance doesn’t make its aggressive debt and cost-reduction targets over the next three years, Larry Ellison will personally be on the hook for as much as $40 billion in debt payments to lenders. That’s how far the software mogul, who recently became a major shareholder in the U.S. spinoff of TikTok, went to help finance his son’s Hollywood ambition.

As demonstrated by the Ellison family’s big swing, the influence of family office owners and investors will be markedly different from that of traditional private equity investors, experts say. Among the key differences:

According to data analysis from global investor research firm Altss, the number of family offices has grown at a rate of nearly 5% a year, culminating in more than 8,000 firms managing as much as $5 trillion or more in assets. The top 50 firms have an estimated $2.4 trillion in assets under management; the top 10 alone account for $1.5 trillion.

Generally speaking, it takes about $250 million in seed money to launch a large single-investor family office. Others are formed with $50 million to $100 million, and sometimes a handful of those firms will join together in a multi-family office. One of the biggest of those is Iconiq Capital, which handles some personal investing for Mark Zuckerberg, Sheryl Sandberg, Jack Dorsey and Linkedin co-founder Reid Hoffman, among others.

The reigning company among family offices remains Walton Enterprises, the $225 billion colossus controlled by the heirs of Walmart founder Sam Walton. Elon Musk, the entrepreneur behind Tesla and SpaceX, funnels some of his personal investments through his Excession family office, valued at $200 billion. Bill Gates’ Cascade Investment is another heavyweight at $170 billion. Amazon pioneer Jeff Bezos holds his roughly 9% stake in the e-commerce giant (which encompasses Amazon MGM Studios and Prime Video) through his Bezos Expeditions office, with an estimated $107 billion in assets under management.

“The ability to create wealth over a short period of time right now is incredible, and because of that, all of a sudden you’ve got someone like Elon buying Twitter and turning it into what he wanted it to be,” says Greg Suess, co-founder and head of Activist Artists Management’s family office practice. “It was unfathomable that you would see an individual be able to buy a company of that size on his own. But that is the way it is now. A lot of these family offices are of the size where they can take swings on major entertainment assets. That has shocked the industry.”

At the same time, fortunes the size of Ellison’s, Musk’s and the Walton clan’s are still outliers — the 1% of the 1%. The more pervasive impact is coming from the many other family offices steered by less than astronomically wealthy people who are drawn to the soft power of media. Think Steve Sarowitz, the payroll-software billionaire whose Illinois-based 4S Bay Partners family office teamed with Justin Baldoni to launch “It Ends With Us” production company Wayfarer Studios.

David Steward II has also inherited significant wealth from his father, World Wide Technology co-founder David Steward. The younger Steward in recent years has invested in comic-book imprints and the Lion Forge Entertainment animation studio with the goal of bringing more diverse stories to life as family animation films. The Steward family has also worked with NASCAR and Richard Petty Motorsports to sponsor the groundbreaking driver Bubba Wallace, and it was an owner of the family’s hometown NHL team, the St. Louis Blues, from 2012 until selling the stake in 2024.

“Our mission is very much around what we call diverse stories authentically told. It’s about displaying and telling stories from varied backgrounds, whether it be racial, socioeconomic or gender,” the younger Steward says. “We’re a lot more patient with outcomes because we have a higher vision. We’re not only investing to get a return; we’re investing for change. We’re investing in people at the end of the day. So it’s a different kind of philosophy as we look at those things.

“There are three pillars to any family office that I’ve ever been around,” says Edward Hamati, a wealth management pro who is chief investment officer for the Steward Trust and the Steward Family Office. “Trust, treasury and banking — that’s one bucket. Building the corpus, that’s another bucket. Anyone can give you 50 ways that you do that. And then there’s the third bucket, which is, What is this family about and what do we have to care about because of the family? What are they motivated by? What do the kids want? Is there a lot of family politics? You have to think through all that around any family you work with.”

Joe Becher, CAA’s global managing partner and founder of its executive search practice, has felt the same demand that spurred Suess and partners to form Activist. Becher expanded CAA into family office specialty services as he found himself conducting C-suite searches for firms that had been bought out by family offices.

“Probably two to three years ago we started seeing this trend of family offices popping up everywhere, whether it be in media companies or music-catalog businesses or sports franchises,” Becher says. “Very often, instead of us dealing with an owner in isolation or a private equity firm with institutional capital, we were dealing with family offices. They needed our help to understand the assets that they’ve invested in. They needed our help to structure them the right way and build the right operating teams.”

Traditionally, the family office investment model was associated with vintage American family fortunes such as the oil money of the Rockefellers or the publishing and media empires of William Randolph Hearst and E.W. Scripps. Hearst and Scripps still operate as well-established media companies. But their governance structure makes them essentially family offices that are run by trusted staffers who ensure that the generational wealth keeps flowing to heirs for decades to come.

But nothing has animated the family office sector lately like the availability of sports teams and music catalogs. The latter is seen as a form of timeless physical media that will retain value no matter how much music consumption changes in the streaming era. On the other hand, sports franchise deals are usually driven by raw passion and bragging rights.

“There’s only so many of them, and so that sense of paying for scarcity and legacy impact are important drivers for family offices that don’t really exist for other types of pooled capital because they have a time-duration problem,” D’Souza says. Some first-generation wealth generators thrive on the challenge of turning around properties like sports teams or rehabilitating beloved fashion or retail brands. “They feel like, ‘I made it once; I can do it again,’” says D’Souza, who has served as a board member for numerous family offices over the years.

At best, a family office can bring to the table resources and in some cases synergies with other holdings for an acquisition. Steward II notes that the company’s work with NASCAR and driver Wallace led to World Wide Technology upgrading the racing circuit’s digital infrastructure to help it make the most of AI and other cutting-edge tech.

Steward II emphasizes that the company can’t completely ignore financial results. But it has the luxury of options. “We do have the investments that are a little bit colder and distant and more financially oriented. And then we have this entrepreneurial pile, and in that pile comes [ideas] by way of passion and by way of utilizing the relationships that we have,” Steward says. “Everything we’ve done in sports and entertainment has been led by those two things. We want to see change in the industry as well. So how can we best not only put our creative foot forward, but how can we leverage our relationships for change as well?”

The flood of investment and financing dollars flowing into a broad range of industry deals in recent years has turned the private investment vehicles of the ultra-high-net-worth crowd into the most courted players in global investing. “It has become recognized that families are more viable owners of various assets than they were considered to be in the past,” Activist’s Suess says. “The wealth has grown so much. They’ve become business rock stars.”

What Do We Do With Leftover Love?

He was everything you had ever wanted. Kind, gentle, thoughtful. Almost too good to be true. You imagined a future together, a home, a family, a lifetime of ordinary mornings. Then, one day, while making eggs for breakfast, you saw the messages. The pan was still warm. The sun was coming through the window. Nothing around you had changed, yet it's all gone. In a matter of seconds, the man you thought you knew disappeared. The future you had imagined vanished with him. But the love you felt still lingers.

You can throw the eggs into the bin, but the feelings cannot disappear as quickly. Love involves several interconnected brain systems. Dopamine-rich reward pathways contribute to desire and anticipation (Fisher et al., 2010); the brain's natural opioid system helps make closeness comforting and social rejection distressing (Hsu et al., 2013; Nummenmaa et al., 2016); and limbic regions, particularly the amygdala, help connect emotional experiences with memory (Cahill et al., 1996). The truth can change what you think in seconds, but it cannot instantly erase what your brain has learned over months or years, so do not let people tell you that you are "co-dependent" or "weak." You have a normal brain that might play tricks on you.

You have leftover love, and that leftover love can be weaponised against you (Lesiak & Gelsthorpe, 2025). He may use the feelings that remain to persuade you to let him stay, asking you to ignore what you have discovered, silence your judgment, and compromise the very values that define who you are.

He has already betrayed you. Now he is asking you to betray yourself for him.

You are the one expected to swallow your anger, silence your judgment, and call it "forgiveness." Look at the bargain. He gets to keep the woman who loves him. And you are expected to give up the woman who knows the truth.

Decades ago, psychiatrist Judith Herman wrote about it in Trauma and Recovery (1992):

His ultimate goal appears to be the creation of a willing victim. Hostages, political prisoners, battered women, and slaves have all remarked upon the captor’s curious psychological dependence upon his victim. George Orwell gives voice to the totalitarian mind in the novel 1984:

“We are not content with negative obedience, nor even with the most abject submission. When finally you surrender to us, it must be of your own free will. We do not destroy the heretic because he resists us; so long as he resists us we never destroy him. We convert him, we capture his inner mind, we reshape him. We burn all evil and all illusion out of him; we bring him over to our side, not in appearance, but genuinely, heart and soul.”

Read those last words again. Heart and soul.

It is not enough that you stay. You must want to stay. It is not enough that you forgive him. You must learn to see his betrayal as something you can live with. You must look at the evidence of your own eyes and persuade yourself that it no longer matters.

And if keeping him means abandoning everything you believe in, what exactly are you trying to save—and what's left—when you do that?

Some wounds are easier to bear because you know you stood by your values while receiving them. In Kraków, officers Jakub Kulik and Artur Morański were seriously wounded confronting a mass shooter. Poland calls them heroes, and people across the country are grateful for their courage and service.

Then there is another kind of wound. One that no surgeon can stitch. Amy Winehouse sings it in "You Know I'm No Good": “I cheated myself, like I knew I would.” By the end of the song, she is no longer simply confessing what she did. She is passing judgment on herself.

Imagine waking up beside a man you no longer respect, living a life you never wanted, having persuaded yourself to accept things you once knew were unacceptable. You kept him, perhaps. But somewhere along the way, you lost the ability to trust your own judgment.

That is the deeper cruelty of weaponised love (Lesiak & Gelsthorpe, 2025). He does not need you to believe he is innocent. He needs you to stop caring that he isn't.

And if manipulation or coercion brought you to that point, the shame belongs to the person who engineered that surrender, not to you.

Name the man, not the fantasy. The betrayal wasn't simply another woman. It was the months of lies that made you believe you were building a future together while he was living another life. He needed you to trust him because, had you known the truth, you might have walked away. So he made sure you didn't.

That is what a programme of deception does. It robs you of the freedom to make informed choices about your own life. When used to dominate and subordinate, deception becomes a tool of coercive control and psychological abuse. Stop reaching for words that make him sound better. A liar is a liar. A traitor is a traitor. A backstabber is a backstabber. Grieve the man he pretended to be, the image he created to win your love and use it to control you. But don't defend the man behind the mask.

The eggs went into the bin that morning. The relationship ended. The feelings are disappearing. You'll be ok! Probably you are already more ok now than when you were cheated on, lied to, and abused.

Of course, that's no guarantee that the same will happen to Nvidia.

It's also worth emphasizing that Nvidia's financial success in recent years could help it navigate the next chapter.

The company generated $127 billion in free cash flow over the trailing-12-month period. Nvidia has so much cash that it has significantly increased dividends -- by 2,400% -- and share buybacks this year. Nvidia benefits from significant financial flexibility, an asset that investors shouldn't ignore.

It's for all those reasons (and others) that I am not considering selling my shares. I am bullish on the long-term prospects of AI, and I am also convinced that Nvidia's board will do its best to find an excellent replacement for Huang.

Combined with the company's already excellent position in the industry, the stock could still deliver outstanding returns over the next decade, although there is more uncertainty now than there was a week ago. The good news is that the market has adjusted. Nvidia's shares aren't that far from their 52-week lows, and at current levels, the stock looks attractive to hold through 2036.

The Stock Market’s Biggest Risk Isn’t a Crash. It’s What Investors Might Do When One Arrives.

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A market decline hurts. An improvised response can make it worse.

Imagine seeing your nest egg lose 30% of its value. In this scenario, the market has already delivered a painful setback. Selling may seem like the obvious way to regain control, but selling also creates a new problem: When will you get back into the market?

In reality, panic selling -- and delaying reentering the market -- can lead to enormous opportunity costs for investors. Let's explore why.

Seeing your portfolio lose value is never fun. Indeed, psychological studies have shown that investment losses have a greater psychological impact than equivalent gains, a phenomenon known as loss aversion. Therefore, it's completely understandable that many people seek immediate relief when the market enters a sell-off, correction, or crash.

However, the psychological relief of liquidating investments creates a new problem: determining when "it's safe."

In practice, most investors simply wait too long. Many wait for recessions to end or for bullish headlines to appear. But bullish headlines often lag positive price action. As a result, investors on the sidelines often miss out on massive recovery rallies. As we'll see next, that can have a significant impact on long-term growth.

Consider a $10,000 portfolio. If it drops 30%, its value will fall to $7,000. To get back to $10,000, the portfolio will need to gain $3,000. Since the overall value has fallen, it will need to generate approximately a 43% return on $7,000 to reach $10,000. So to wipe out a 30% drop, it needs to gain 43%. These amounts differ because the starting amounts differ.

Beyond the arithmetic, there are other costs. Transaction costs, lost dividend payments, and taxes have to be considered. In short, selling might ease one type of headache but create several others.

Despite understanding the math and the other implications of selling, many investors can't resist the urge to sell when the stock market is falling. One way to train yourself not to sell is to imagine a similar situation in a different market.

For example, let's say you own a house. Now imagine that another house in your neighborhood sells for half of what might normally be expected. That sale will decrease the value of all homes in the neighborhood, perhaps significantly. But even if the price of your own home falls 10%, 15%, or even 25%, that doesn't mean one of the bedrooms in your home disappears.

Stock portfolios work the same way. Prices can fall, but that doesn't necessarily mean that the businesses behind the stocks have deteriorated to the same degree.

For a buy-and-hold strategy to work, investors must build a portfolio that can withstand periods of volatility. Leveraged positions and meme stocks can wreck a portfolio during a stock market correction or crash. Therefore, savvy investors know to limit their exposure, or, better yet, avoid such investments altogether. By building a diversified portfolio based around well-run businesses, an investor can avoid much of the worst effects of a market downturn.

Market crashes are terrible, but thankfully, they're also rare. However, to prepare for them, it's best to have a plan. Writing it down can help you manage your emotions, think carefully, and avoid panic. At the end of the day, the math and the studies are clear: It's best to ride out market downturns rather than sell and try to time reentry perfectly.

If you can keep a cool head and remember this in the moment, you'll likely come through the next financial downturn in better shape than many other investors.

I fell for it on my first playthrough, and I have seen plenty of others do exactly the same. A dedicated healer just isn’t all that necessary once you double down on damage or find more flexible healing elsewhere, like Shulk’s Light Heal. Sharla has been on my bench ever since, and I do feel bad about it, since none of this is really her fault.

From controversial clowns to big blue lionmen, Final Fantasy has party members you remember for being utterly useless but who are anything but!

For the longest time, I was shackled by what party composition makes sense for the story, and I still struggle with letting go of that sometimes. You will never catch me playing a Pokémon game without my starter, for example. In a similar vein, many of my Tales of Symphonia playthroughs simply ended up with Lloyd, Colette, Raine and Genis, because they are the ones who have known each other from the very beginning.

Eventually, I managed to get over myself. Since then, without fail, Genis has never been in my party again. I like the conversations between him and Lloyd, and I do believe that pairing a carefree character with a snarkier, more considerate one works out really well.

I just usually find him a bit grating. That is a harsh thing to say about a child who has been through as much as Genis has, and I’m aware of that every time I move him out of the party. It hasn’t stopped me yet.

Kongol always ends up benched. It’s common knowledge that Rose falls off quite aggressively on Disc 2, and again on Disc 4, which is unfortunate, but even she has seen more action in my playthroughs than he has. For the last surviving Giganto, that has to sting.

My issue is simple: I don’t usually enjoy very slow characters, and Kongol is incredibly strong but painfully slow. To make him properly useful, you have to boost both his speed and his magic defence quite aggressively, and I would rather spend that effort on someone who doesn’t need it. He never stood much of a chance with me.

Luckily, The Legend of Dragoon isn’t a particularly hard game at the best of times, so you can beat it with any party you like. That takes some of the guilt away. Kongol saved Dart and his friends from a falling pillar before joining them, though, and I repaid him with the better part of four discs spent sitting around.

It pains me so much to say this, because I love dogs! In Dragon Age: Origins, however, Dog is close to useless compared to anyone else. Unless you install a mod, he takes up a full party slot, just like any other companion would, and he can’t carry a fight the way they can.

He does a semi-decent job in the first few levels, since Alistair and Sten aren’t great tanks at that stage, either. He then gets outscaled quickly, as your warriors pick up their defensive talents and proper tank gear, and on Nightmare difficulty, that gap becomes very hard to ignore. Dog isn’t beefy enough to be a tank and doesn’t deal enough damage to make up for it, which leaves him without a clear role.

His Overwhelm ability is devastating against a single dangerous target, mages in particular, but that is about it. It’s a real shame, because his interactions with the other companions are often hilarious. Leaving him at camp means missing out on those, and it never gets any easier.

What is interesting, though, is that there exists a Extra Dog Slot mod that lets him tag along as a fifth party member, and it shows up on nearly every list of essential mods for the game. I am clearly not the only one with a guilty conscience.

Goofy has done nothing wrong, and I want that on record. My problem with the first Kingdom Hearts is that nearly every world offers a guest character who can take Donald’s or Goofy’s place, and I love how unique that makes each world feel. Somebody has to make room, and it was usually Goofy.

Recently, I felt really bad about that, and I wanted to make it right in my latest playthrough. Sadly, all it did was make me realise that the guest characters are a big part of the game’s charm for me. Fighting alongside Ariel or the Beast is simply too cool to pass up, and since they can’t leave their home worlds, it always feels like now or never.

So Goofy might have to sit this one out a little longer. He will take it well, because he is Goofy. That only makes it worse.

With a single action or word, there are moments in these Nintendo DS JRPGs Where Companions Can Derail the Entire Story.

Jean is the most painful example of wasted potential I can think of. His design is memorable, his personality is fun, and being the frog prince of SimaFort sets him apart from everyone else in Breath of Fire II. He believes that there are no bad people in the world, only bad hearts, and he says all of it in a faux-French accent.

Mechanically, sadly, he has very little to offer. He joins slow and underwhelming, with good defence but incredibly low agility, and his stats never match the way he looks. His Holy fusion turns him into a tall, heroic-looking frog knight who can in theory become quite formidable, but it comes too late and with too many trade-offs to justify his slot over characters who have already proven themselves.

Jean isn’t unplayable. He is a “what could have been”: a character who looks like he should be iconic in battle, but never gets the chance to live up to it. If he had been a little more like Frog in Chrono Trigger, with some healing to offer, he might have been worthwhile well before the endgame.

Yangus is easily one of the most lovable characters in Dragon Quest VIII, and I still barely use him for long stretches of the game. He is the dedicated tank, and the job gradually overwhelms him: magic can lower his armour, most of his equipment reduces his evasion, and he is very slow. He has a respectable Strength base, but not much gets built on top of it.

In the 3DS version, which added Red and Morrie as party members, that makes him the obvious candidate for the sidelines once the new recruits join. He can become useful again in the late game, but that means building his Scythe tree from the ground up. That is a lot to ask for a character I have already replaced.

It isn’t all doom and gloom, since he simply dips in the mid-game. Early on, 16 skill points in Humanity get you Nose for Treasure, which tells you how many chests are left in an area, and that is quite useful. I just wish that was enough to keep him in the party.

Nino joins with such a good story that you really want to use her and help her grow. Raised by the Black Fang, she is sent to assassinate Prince Zephiel in Battle Before Dawn, has a change of heart and ends up fleeing with Jaffar instead. By the time she joins you, it’s impossible not to root for her.

Unfortunately, she arrives as a Level 5 Mage very late in the game, when you are most likely fielding promoted units. Her growth rates are amongst the highest in the game, so she will catch up if you put the time in, but she’s a glass cannon until then, and there isn’t much game left. Unless you wrap your whole strategy around her, she gets benched immediately. This makes her a classic example of what Fire Emblem fans call the Est archetype: a unit that joins late and underlevelled, but with enormous potential.

Sometimes, it feels like her sole purpose is to stand between you and recruiting Jaffar, since he only joins if she talks to him and they both survive. This tiny mage is brimming with potential, and I have made use of it in some playthroughs. I’m just not always in the mood for that, so more often than not, she ends up on the bench.

Crisp air, hot coffee and a cozy sofa: autumn is when I reach for nostalgia, and these ten GameCube JRPGs still make the trip back worthwhile.

8 Soulslikes With Insane Difficulty Spikes

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Christian Bognar is a Contributor at DualShockers with over three years of experience covering the gaming industry. Since 2022, he has written guides and reviews for publications including DualShockers, CBR, Attack of the Fanboy, PC Invasion, and The Game Slayer. He holds a Bachelor’s Degree in Business Administration

The Soulslikes genre offers some of the greatest challenges in gaming. With difficult bosses, enemies that demand patience, and many obstacles throughout a level, meeting success in any Soulslike can be incredibly rewarding.

However, there are times when a game takes it too far and includes a harsh difficulty spike that seemingly comes out of nowhere. This could sometimes lead to areas of the game feeling a bit unfair.

It's not the genre's area of expertise, but some of its representatives are more daring, offering fascinating movement mechanics.

While the games listed in this article are some of the best entries in the genre, they do have pacing issues when it comes to difficulty. Here are eight Soulslikes with brutal difficulty spikes.

Sekiro is already a demanding game from the get-go, but its difficulty reaches a whole other level in the mid to late game. Enemies become more aggressive, minibosses hit harder, and all encounters will constantly remind players that they need to master the parry system to stand a chance.

Boss fights also become considerably more demanding, requiring players to learn lengthy attack patterns and maintain constant pressure rather than relying on dodging. Just when you thought you were getting good at the game, Sekiro: Shadows Die Twice raises the bar.

The expansion of Elden Ring, Shadow of the Erdtree, is known for its difficulty spike right from the start. As soon as you enter the Land of Shadow, you’ll realize enemies deal way more damage, and your character is much weaker overall.

With the introduction of Scadutree Fragments, items that raise attack power and defense, players essentially have to start from scratch and engage with a new progression system. In other words, even OP base-game builds get a wake-up call when starting the DLC.

Overall, Lies of P is generally on the easier side for a Soulslike. However, the game has brutal difficulty spikes in a handful of its bosses. For example, Laxasia the Complete, Simon Manus, and King of Puppets are ten times more challenging than anything else in the game.

Each of these bosses is a true test of reflexes and endurance, where even the smallest mistake can prove costly. What makes these encounters particularly jarring is the stark contrast in difficulty, as the areas leading up to them are relatively easy to breeze through.

There’s no denying that The First Berserker: Khazan is a very challenging game right from its first level. Still, despite it being a very tough experience through and through, it still features an insane difficulty spike in its second half that can sometimes feel pretty unfair.

Late-game bosses are some of the most demanding encounters the genre has to offer, featuring relentless combos that leave very little room for mistakes. Khazan’s combat is super satisfying, but players who haven’t mastered its mechanics will quickly discover just how unforgiving it can be.

If you're looking for the heir to Soulsborne games, look no further than this list of ten Soulslikes that are unexpected masterpieces.

While the 2023 version of Lords of the Fallen never reaches the difficulty heights of 2014’s, it’s definitely not an easy experience. The game reaches its peak in difficulty during Bramis Castle, an area filled with the hardest enemies in the entire game.

Bramis Castle has been heavily regarded as unfair by many players due to the absurd amount of elite enemies. It was so hard that there were multiple post-launch patches to make it feel more reasonable. Still, despite the patches to make it easier, Bramis Castle remains an insane difficulty spike.

The original Hollow Knight was a fairly well-balanced experience from beginning to end, and while Silksong is a phenomenal game, it’s not as consistent as the original. For example, Bilewater increases the difficulty tenfold due to poisonous waters and brutal platforming.

Additionally, Act 3 of Silksong introduces an entirely new set of challenges as the Void corrupts Pharloom, making enemies considerably more dangerous while changing areas and levels. Fortunately, Act 3 is completely optional, meaning players who aren’t interested in tackling this difficulty spike can simply ignore it.

Wuchang: Fallen Feathers features several notoriously sharp difficulty spikes, mainly in its boss fights. Wuchang has the tendency to make the player feel incredibly powerful while exploring a level and then completely ripping that feeling away during some of the main bosses.

One of the earliest examples of this is the Commander Honglan boss fight, the first brick wall players will hit in a playthrough. All earlier bosses are a walk in the park compared to Commander Honglan, and everything that follows involves bosses that demand fast reflexes and perfect performance.

Nioh 3's difficulty spikes can be particularly unforgiving, especially when players enter the Heian Period. This period involves some of the most challenging locations and bosses in the game, such as Ibaraki Doji, Kajiwara Kagetoki, and many more.

Pretty much, once the player enters the Heian Period, the real Nioh 3 experience begins, and the rest of the adventure is a true test of skill. Bosses hit hard, are faster, and the importance of leveling equipment increases dramatically.

There's nothing more fitting for this season than being destroyed by the same boss fifty-seven times in a row.

David Duchovny on Books, Band and ‘The X-Files’

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

David Duchovny has been famous for as long as I have been alive. From 1993 to 2002, The X-Files made government conspiracies a staple of primetime television. Duchovny’s FBI special agent Fox Mulder, with his skeptical counterpart, Gillian Anderson’s agent Dana Scully, personified the imminent breakdown of consensus reality in the coming century, delivering the paranoid fringe into 20 million American living rooms. The show belongs to a world of monoculture that no longer exists, and it launched Duchovny into a realm of visibility that’s hard to imagine now, when famous people are produced and consumed at an industrial rate. A few weeks ago, as my boyfriend shopped for a suit jacket at Crowley Vintage in Brooklyn and I edited this story on my phone, I watched a college-age boy put on a long, gray trench coat and announce to his girlfriend: “Mulder mode.”

Duchovny went on to star in Californication, a raunchy dramedy about a divorced novelist navigating the sexually and professionally parasitic Hollywood studio system of the 2000s. Many viewers interpreted Californication as a kind of autofiction, although Duchovny did not write for the show. The actor has written a number of novels, but it’s apparent to anyone who has spent 30 seconds with him that this soft-spoken, reluctant celebrity has nothing to do with the character Hank Moody. Still, Californication did anticipate something about art, celebrity, and self-commodification: Anybody who writes today must contend with marketing an artistic persona for a public that does not, as a rule, read.

In addition to his six books, Duchovny has released four records, and written and directed a number of feature films. He greets the degraded state of American arts with a charming blend of rigor and self-deprecation. “I’m not Jonathan Franzen,” he says of his fiction-writing process. “But if I work on something every day, it deepens. And eventually, hopefully, I can achieve one tenth of what Jonathan Franzen achieves.” But he has an aversion to the sort of publicity that might make his various artistic ventures more lucrative. He tells me he finds it “spiritually impoverishing.”

I meet Duchovny with his band—millennial slackers half his age—at their practice space in Carroll Gardens, where they’re preparing to tour their fourth LP, Prince of Pieces. He wears a white Hanes T-shirt, tight underneath his athletic arms, and he has a tattoo on his ankle—a compass pointing west, something he got when his daughter, West, was hospitalized as an infant. (She’s 27 now, an accomplished actor. “She wanted to be a doctor,” he says. “I was so happy when she told me she was going to pursue acting. Thank God.”)

The albums are composed of subdued, easy-to-listen-to folk-rock tracks, not dissimilar from early Kurt Vile. It’s the band’s first rehearsal in ages, and one of their only chances to prepare in advance of a European tour. In person, they play the songs harder and heavier, sprinkling in crowd-pleasing covers: “Godzilla” by Blue Öyster Cult, “The One I Love” by REM, and—“sadly only one time,” Duchovny says—“Hotline Bling.” The energy in the room is high. These men like one another. Duchovny’s manager offers me a beer from the cooler I’m perched on. I gesture to the tallboy in my purse. He looks disappointed. “You didn’t trust us,” he says. Duchovny steps aside to take a sip of water and picks up the microphone.

“You can sing along,” his keyboardist, Colin Lee, says.

At 66, David Duchovny remains a sex symbol. He’s in better shape than he was on The X-Files. He’s in better shape than the boys in the band. The night after practice, during a Q&A for a screening of his 2023 film Reverse the Curse, he mentions that the wardrobe department had to fashion a “BDSM harness” for him to wear under his clothes to remind him to hunch like an old man. He plays a Red Sox fan who bonds with his estranged son over baseball, marijuana, and their shared lust for a beautiful, young death doula. Duchovny’s character has terminal cancer. “I’m in my dying era,” he tells the crowd. “I die in every film. ”

For the most part, the audience at Westchester County’s Irvington Theater is old enough to have watched The X-Files as it aired. At least two esoteric-looking old men carry David’s poetry collection like a prayer book. There are a handful of young people. A college-aged girl in a KN95 mask raises her hand to ask about Bucking Fucking Dent, the novel adaptation of the Reverse the Curse screenplay, which Duchovny wrote while the movie was in production purgatory. “In the book, Ted and Marty’s last name is Fullilove. In the movie you change it to Fullaker. Why did you do that?”

“Good question,” Duchovny says, stalling. “Full of love. Full of shit. It’s all the same.”

Inevitably, someone asks about the new X-Files. Ryan Coogler is rebooting the show for Hulu. Duchovny will later suggest to me that Chris Carter, the show’s original writer, has designs on a reboot of his own—“He has plans, and wants to end with the original show.” As for Coogler’s version, he says, “I know he said he wants me to come do something, but we haven’t figured out what yet.” During the audience Q&A, Duchovny seems to have misheard the question, and spoils the ending for The Exiles, his forthcoming Taiwanese crime film: “I die in that one too.”

Duchovny’s latest book, About Time, a collection of poems published last year by Akashic, is also largely about dying: the death of his father, the death of his first marriage, the ambient death that hung in the air at the beginning of the decade. He started writing the poems when his therapist encouraged him to talk to dead people. “He was talking about Rudolph Steiner’s belief that you can talk to the dead. And he said: You can talk to your father and you should. My dad and I didn’t connect like we might have,” Duchovny says. “When you’re younger, you think you have all the time in the world to get the relationship you want. But the relationship doesn’t end.” In conversation, he is cerebral without being annoying. “There’s a power struggle when you read a really good novel,” he says. “Somebody’s mind is taking over your mind. I think about language a lot and how we take it for granted as being an accurate signifier. When I was younger I thought communication was possible. I’m not so sure now. But it’s fun to try.”

Duchovny’s humility complicates the sexual charisma that still lives at the center of his public persona. His introduction to About Time begins, “I know what you’re thinking: Just what the world needs now—a bunch of poems from an actor.” There’s a sense that he wants to get ahead of the reader’s skepticism. But there’s also plenty of perceptive lines in the poems themselves: “You dreamt you wrote something so good, / so ambivalent and destructive, so creative, / so lacking in morals, decency, or instruction, / so sad and so funny, / so useless and old, / so familiar and new, / so full of theft and power / that they had to name a god after it.” I ask him if good writing is necessarily amoral. He says, “Probably. Which is why any politically correct work of art is usually super successful and really bad. Usually artists are difficult people, and I have no problem separating the art from the artist. But people do now.”

In college, Duchovny wanted to be a poet. “I thought it was an actual occupation you could have,” he says. When an underclassman turned up in his workshop who was better than him, he quit writing poems on the spot. “It was actually Walter Kirn,” he says. “He showed up at 16-years-old. He was a genius. I was like okay—I’m not a poet.”

The two men have become close in the last decade. Kirn, who wrote Up in the Air and founded the newspaper County Highway, tells me what Duchovny was like at Princeton: “He was golden. He had an aura. I was a little starstruck by him.” Duchovny has agreed to play paranormal radio host Art Bell in American Nights, a biopic that Kirn is developing alongside director Alex Lee Moyer. Bell’s Coast to Coast, a cult AM show during the ’80s and ’90s, was beloved by long-haul truckers and compound preppers alike, who called in to report UFO sightings, cattle mutilations, and crop circles. “I, like the rest of my generation, grew up on The X-Files,” Moyer tells me. “It made a massive impression on me, and I’ve been a fan of David ever since. The synchronicity of him being a part of this is not lost on me.”

I ask Duchovny if he always needed to be the best. “I at least needed to be the best in my class,” he says.

In LA, I meet Duchovny in a quiet restaurant to talk about his books. He describes his first novel as “an act of cowardice.” Holy Cow is narrated by an anthropomorphized barnyard animal who uncovers the conspiracy theory that is factory farming. “I thought: Well, they’re not gonna cut me off at the knees on this. Because I’m clearly not that serious. It was calculated. I didn’t want to try for the great American novel the first time as an actor.” The books that followed are more ambitious, each one better than the last. By the time he was promoting his fourth book, 2021’s Truly Like Lightning, he made his ambition plain. “I want to be taken seriously,” he said on Marc Maron’s podcast. “That’s my weakness. I want that book to be taken seriously.”

“You don’t want people to be like the guy from The X-Files wrote a book,” Maron said.

“That’s what they’re gonna say,” Duchovny said, “but I hope and I believe that eventually people will see that I mean business.”

Truly Like Lightning centers on a Mormon polygamist family forced to enter the godless world of Los Angeles when their homestead is upended. “When I started to read about Mormonism, it struck me as super optimistic. Everyone’s gonna get their own planet and everyone’s gonna be able to propagate souls. As opposed to a European perspective, which was always about the end of something, being 2,000 years removed from this thing. The Mormons said no, this is still happening.” The novel takes cues from the literary critic Harold Bloom, who wrote that Mormonism was an “American original” and called its founder a “religious genius.” Duchovny studied under Bloom at Yale, where he earned some of an English PhD. “Everything goes back to Bloom,” he says. “And you never really leave grad school. I’m not sure I’ve left grad school. They might still be waiting for my dissertation.”

But Duchovny did leave grad school. He signed with a talent agent one summer to try to earn some money acting in commercials. His agent told him that if he wanted to go out for theater or film roles, he’d have to get into an acting class. “I said: I’m not doing anything. When I stepped into acting class, something happened. I wasn’t a demonstrative person—I was an intellectual person. So it was like: Yeah, be unreasonable. Be irrational. It opened up a different world, a world of fiction, a world without consequences. I’m doing a scene, and I’m yelling at this person and I’m going to murder them, but I don’t have to go to jail.”

For someone with half a dozen good books and an enormous public profile, it’s surprising there haven’t been more reviews. Johnny Temple, who published Duchovny’s two most recent books on Akashic, tells me, “It’s certainly frustrating that there hasn't been more review coverage of David’s books. For any well-known actor, musician, or other type of public figure, there’s always going to be obstacles to being taken seriously in a different medium.” Kirn tells me, “That makes writing fiction a much purer achievement in my mind. You’re doing this because you love it, and you know that two thirds of your fans will only remember you as a TV character. It’s a brilliant, Sisyphean task. Also, he’s a superb writer.”

I ask Duchovny whether he still thinks about being the best. “I think now I can acknowledge that it’s a hollow designation,” he says. “Especially in the arts. Even though we give awards, which is silly. Holy Cow made the Times bestseller list. Ever since, everything I write sells less and less.”

“It’s hard for someone to champion it,” he adds. “If I wrote a memoir, I’m sure it would get reviewed in the Times.”

Talking to Duchovny, one is reminded that wanting to be taken seriously and being in on the joke aren’t mutually exclusive. As band practice winds down in Brooklyn, the guys are excited to cover Lou Reed’s “Perfect Day.” Duchovny crosses his arms in front of his lap like a schoolboy as he sings. To my ear it sounds just fine, but Lee, the keyboardist, asks the mixer to lower the reverb on David’s voice.

“Maybe lower David’s mic in general?” the bassist, Mitchell Stewart asks.

“Lower than zero,” Duchovny says. “Less than zero.”

A few days later, he texts me. He just screened an early cut of Soapbox, a Hollywood satire he wrote and stars in opposite Laverne Cox. At first, he’s bummed that the test audience has decided that his character shouldn’t get killed in the final moments of the movie. But a couple hours later, he writes me again: “People don’t want me to die. I guess I should be flattered.”

You Can Try WhatsApp's New Disappearing Text Messages Early

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Like Snapchat messages, WhatsApp's "View Once" feature is a great way to send things you don't want living on your friends' devices. You can send voice messages, photos, and videos that will "self-destruct" after they're opened, limiting (if not eliminating) the risk of your friends keeping them forever. However, View Once has a major limitation: While WhatsApp will let you send voice memos and images that way, you can't use it to send texts, meaning every one you send will live on (unless you unsend it yourself).

That's now changing. As reported by WABetaInfo, WhatsApp is now beta testing View Once for text messages, too. Soon enough, you'll be able to send voice memos, photos, videos, and texts as one-time-only messages. That way, you can send sensitive information via text—say, a password or an important account number—without worrying about it persisting. Here's how it works: You craft your text message as you usually do, but rather than tap the send button, you hold it. You'll see the usual "Schedule message" option, but once the feature rolls out, you'll also find a new "View Once" option as well.

As WABetaInfo highlights, the first time you use this feature, WhatsApp will present a pop-up explaining the feature, how "View Once text gives you more privacy," and that when you send the message, it'll disappear from the chat after the recipient opens it. WhatsApp also explains that recipients cannot share, forward, save, or take screenshots of View Once text messages. Recipients won't see the text appear in the usual chat interface, either; instead, your recipient will need to open the message in a separate window. Once they close this page, the message disappears.

As mentioned above, this feature is currently in testing, so it's only available to WhatsApp beta testers. What's more, only the Android version of the beta has access at the moment, so iOS beta testers are out of luck. (That said, the WhatsApp iOS beta is perpetually full, so there's not much you could do even if WhatsApp made this test available on iPhone.)

If you have an Android device, you can enroll in the WhatsApp beta to try out "View Once" text messages today. According to WABetaInfo, the feature is live as of WhatsApp version 2.26.45.6. To enroll, open the WhatsApp beta page on the Google Play store and enroll your account in the beta. If you don't have WhatsApp on your phone already, download it here; if you do, reload the page and install the latest update. This will update your existing WhatsApp app to the latest beta version.

“We had been talking about going out there to sea, sit on real boats and shoot on Imax,” cinematographer Hoyte Van Hoytema explains.

A large amount of the film was shot on the open ocean, where Nolan, his cast and crew spent four months capturing how hard the journey would have been as they endured crashing waves. And while the climactic moment ended up being shot in Los Angeles, Nolan didn’t spare any expense in pulling off the sequence, relying instead on old-fashioned filmmaking.Speaking with Variety for Inside the Frame, Van Hoytema explains the scene provided a challenge: “How to replicate something and make it feel visceral and real? Van Hoytema’s chief lighting technician and gaffer Adam Chambers built light boxes that would emulate thunder and lightning. The boat was then put on a gimbal, all while being shot on an Imax camera. “It was like shooting a real storm, just under safety supervision,” Van Hoytema says.He adds, “We did a lot of different setups in that whole scene. It is designed to feel extremely chaotic; it’s designed to sort of not understand really what’s going on.”The lighting design was something he also had to think about. “It was interesting because I was thinking, if you’re out at sea at night 2,000 years ago, without electricity and you end up in a thunderstorm, what would that feel like? I was thinking, if you can see everything around you, that’s going to look fake.”

A CGI storm didn’t feel right because the entire crew had been meticulous about keeping everything as grounded and as experiential as possible. Van Hoytema says the team built overhead lighting rigs that were approximately 60×30 feet, hanging on cranes above the ship. “It meant that you would see flashes that progressively move from behind them to the front of them, as it opens up the sky. “What you see is short bursts and there’s just darkness or a little bit of a glow. It’s like trying to see this relentless endless sea, this weather, this saltwater and they’re just so close to death. It’s extremely claustrophobic at the same time.”

When it came to framing Odysseus, Van Hoytema was wired in to keep him in place on the gimbal. His Imax camera on his shoulder was also wired in.

“I was not trying to be in any way precious about framing whatsoever. I was just trying through with a jet engine blowing, gallons of water in my face. It was not a situation in which you could just start a take or not. Every take was an event.”

Van Hoytema says the camera starts to roll and the jet engines start. “They rise, and at some point, they sound like two fighter jets are next to your ear. And it gets really wild because they start moving the gimbal as well, and you’re holding on for your life. The camera gets turned; it’s the original Imax camera, not the blimp and the Imax camera is very loud. However, in that situation, you don’t even know if it’s turned on. You do a take, and you have a crazy powerful adrenaline rush.”

In contrast, Van Hoytema recalled a moment when the crew was out to sea, in open waters and a pod of bottlenose dolphins appeared. It was completely unplanned.

I Took a Mystery “Decision Detox” Trip With Zero Planning. Here’s What Happened.

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I was completely at peace. The best part: I hadn’t made a single decision for the entire weekend. In fact, until just a couple days prior—the day of my trip departure—I had absolutely no idea where I’d be on this gorgeous Sunday morning.

The morning of my trip, I tore open a sealed envelope that held all the information about the hotel where I’d be staying (Chaminade Resort and Spa), my itinerary (forest therapy, spa treatments, dining at The View and Lago di Como), and even the destination itself (Santa Cruz, California). This was all part of the “mystery travel” experience I eagerly agreed to, facilitated by Benchmark Resorts & Hotels and Pack Up + Go, an agency that plans completely curated surprise vacations. Here was the deal: I filled out a survey about my preferences (my priorities while traveling, the types of hotels I liked) along with budget, then they planned the entire trip, and I wouldn’t find out details (beyond some notes about weather and packing suggestions) until I opened my envelope the morning of my departure.

As an avid traveler, eldest daughter, and strategic planner (particularly in the context of trips), I thought this format might stress me out—but I found there was a sense of freedom and lightness in not pouring into pre-trip research or curating my own itinerary. All I had to do was show up. This brand of mental surrender actually speaks to a rising travel trend: “decision detox.”

Modern life can often feel like an endless series of choices. Between social media, the internet, and now AI, “we are inundated with information and access to it,” says Morgan Cutlip, PhD, psychology educator, author, and researcher. “This sends us regularly searching for the ‘right’ way of doing things rather than trusting our instincts or talking to a friend and doing what they do.”

And that absolutely applies to trip planning. “When there are so many options for something like a vacation, the more information you consume and the more reviews you read, the more uncertain you can become about your decision,” she says. “Searching out accommodations, looking up flight options, thinking through food—it becomes a ton of micro decisions packed into this one major thing that feels like it’s supposed to be extra magical, extra fun, and low stress, so the stakes feel high.”

In fact, while vacations are meant to be a time to unwind and enjoy life, a 2025 survey of 1,500 Americans from YouGov found 70 percent of U.S. vacationers find their booking experience to be stress-inducing. That’s where “decision detox” trips come in, where you completely relinquish control of trip planning. The intention here is for travelers to take a break from the mental overload of everyday life and never-ending choices, in order to take a true vacation.

This travel trend is on the rise. A research report published by Lemongrass, a certified B-Corp marketing agency, found a clear shift in traveler preferences for curated, all-inclusive itineraries that remove planning fatigue; a desire to visit quieter, less-crowded destinations; and a pull toward peaceful relaxation over highly stimulating experiences.

And while traditional travel agents have long been planning accommodations and transportation, decision detox trips take that concept one step further with an emphasis on fully planned retreats and completely handled logistics. In my case, this meant a complete surprise trip and fully embracing the thrill of adventure that comes with not knowing your destination until the moment you depart.

“For some people, this might sound like handcuffs on a holiday, but for [people] burdened with making seemingly every decision in their families’ lives all the time, it’s heaven in the form of a pre-booked massage and meals that magically appear when desired,” Jools Sampson, founder and owner of Reclaim Yourself Retreats, wrote in the Lemongrass report.

Women are likely disproportionately affected by this kind of decision fatigue, as they tend to take on the lion’s share of trip planning. In fact, data from research organization Skift found that 82 percent of all travel decisions are made by women. This can be particularly draining for moms, who are constantly managing the logistics of life, says Cutlip, whose work often focuses on mental load discrepancies in relationships. “And if she’s the one planning it, she’s almost certainly coordinating with her partner too. Not being able to fully make decisions on her own, having to negotiate and talk each one through, adds yet another layer of work.”

But no matter what role someone takes in a family structure, or what the underlying reason is for mental fatigue, leaning into decision detox travel can come with a whole slew of mental health benefits.

“You eliminate all of the fatigue, overwhelm, and unnecessary stress that can come with planning a trip,” says Cutlip. “If you’re somebody who has the capacity to let go and you’re comfortable putting someone else in the driver’s seat, it frees you to focus on everything else a vacation requires: preparing to leave your home, getting work off, taking care of the pets, cleaning out the fridge, and then showing up ready to relax.”

Benchmark decided to partner with Pack Up + Go after reading YouGov’s survey about traveler planning fatigue. “It’s a different way to approach a vacation,” says Evan Crawford, vice president of marketing for Benchmark Resorts & Hotels.

It also leads to travelers visiting locations they may not have otherwise thought to book—such as Woodinville, Washington, or the Columbia River Gorge, says Crawford. “Maybe you’ve never even heard of the place before, but now you can go there and get exposed to it and have this extraordinary experience.”

And while a complete surprise vacation is one approach, it’s not the only way to try out a decision detox trip.

A reprieve from decision-making is at the heart of Duvine Cycling, which curates bike tour vacations that take guests everywhere from the vineyards of Burgundy to the Southern Lakes region of New Zealand.

“Basically, all you have to do is show up on a trip. You don’t have to think about anything,” says CEO and co-founder Andy Levine. These active, planned trips inherently encourage people to unplug and make lasting connections with their fellow cyclists. “[We act as] their concierge, and we get to make these really cool things happen, all while on a bike.”

Then there’s Selar, a sustainable polar expedition company launching in early 2027. For anyone seeking a grand adventure—without all the intricate planning that often goes into these types of trips—Selar offers a chance to take a journey way up north with itineraries across Norway, Svalbard, and Greenland. Or take HX Expeditions, which, similar to a cruise model, offers all-inclusive voyages that cover excursions, meals, drinks, and gratuities—but unlike a classic cruise, these itineraries are led by onboard naturalists and historians, and their packages also include lectures, hands-on experiences, and expedition gear.

At Vermejo, a Ted Turner Reserve in New Mexico, each guest’s nature-focused itinerary is completely handled by their Reserve Ambassadors. There are more than 45 guided activities available, ranging from horseback riding through vast prairies, fly-fishing for Rio Grande cutthroat trout, or taking a UTV tour with the opportunity to spot elk, bear, and other big game.

Of course, numerous wellness resorts have been hopping on this trend for some time now, acknowledging that a true mental reset is intrinsically linked to less decision-making. BodyHoliday, an all-inclusive wellness resort in St. Lucia, asks guests to “give us your body for a week, and we’ll give you back your mind;” Sensei Lanai in Hawaii offers guided stays that cater to your individual health needs and goals; and at Renew Retreat at Calamigos Ranch in California, you can take part in a preset wellness retreat.

There are also a number of retreats specifically designed for staying active. Take a running retreat with Run Wild, for example: these women-only trips are designed so you simply choose your pace and place of interest and select an already-set itinerary.

The crux of these trips is rooted in analog experiences, which can help with the mental fatigue and technology overload we experience in everyday life. “I think people are really craving that opportunity to be in the moment, to be human,” says Crawford.

Surrendering complete control is never easy—but in the case of my trip to Santa Cruz, I found it wildly therapeutic. By not stressing about every detail, both before and during the trip, I was able to feel fully present with every item on my itinerary, including a hike through redwood forests, idly reading by the pool in my (pre-reserved) cabana, and savoring meals set to stunning views of the treetops and ocean beyond.

Because I opted for a road trip rather than air travel, the Pack Up + Go team even chose my rest stop on the way from Los Angeles (Paso Robles), along with recommendations for restaurants and shops to explore in the area. And this detailed research extended to my final destination in Santa Cruz, too: the team provided a meticulously curated list of shops, experiences, and more for me to explore during my final day in the area.

Before embarking on your own decision detox trip, take stock of whether this will feel freeing or cause you more stress. “My advice would be to reflect on whether you’re somebody who can actually let go of control,” says Cutlip. “If you’re going to turn it over to someone else and then micromanage the process, just do it yourself. If you can turn over control, fully embrace it and enjoy it.”

For me, it was a practice in letting go of control and allowing outside help—something I think many of us could benefit from on occasion. And while I won’t necessarily be handing over the reins for every future trip I take, it was a beautiful opportunity to give my mind a bit of reprieve, to stop overthinking for a while, and simply take in the wonder of a beautiful place.

COP31 Leaders’ Summit Spotlights Electrification & Transition Away from Fossil Fuels

Antalya, Türkiye — The COP31 Leaders’ Summit concluded today, with several new announcements and speeches from heads of state and the UN Secretary-General. Developments included country endorsements of the COP Presidency’s 35×35 electrification pledge, growing support for the transition away from fossil fuels, and new pledges from philanthropies to the Tropical Forest Forever Facility, among others.

Following is a statement from Ani Dasgupta, President and CEO, World Resources Institute:

“One message came across loud and clear from world leaders: climate action has shifted from an environmental movement to a core economic and security imperative. The conversation is moving beyond high-level commitments to the practical challenges of how to transition away from fossil fuels while supporting workers and communities and how to put entire economies on a cleaner, more resilient path.

“Electrification and the transition away from fossil fuels rightly took center stage. These are two vital, intertwined goals, and progress on one must reinforce the other. Early support for the 35×35 electrification pledge is encouraging, but many more countries need to endorse it and turn their commitments into credible national plans backed by concrete policies and investment.

“President Lula deserves credit for keeping the transition away from fossil fuels at the top of the international agenda and delivering a global roadmap that countries can adapt to their own circumstances. Australia and Türkiye now have an opportunity to lead by example, putting forward their own transition plans and encouraging others to follow.

“Vulnerable countries delivered an equally clear message: access to affordable finance is critical to them implementing their climate ambition. High borrowing costs and unsustainable debt are holding back investment in the very countries that need it most. With the $1.3 trillion finance goal established, the focus is shifting to how all sources of finance can work together to deliver investment at scale and how we ensure affordable capital reaches those who need it most. There are encouraging signs, including an influx of pledges from philanthropies for the Brazil-initiated Tropical Forests Forever Facility.

“Adaptation and early warning systems received much-needed attention after a year of crushing heatwaves, fires, and floods. But that attention now needs to turn to the investment in systemic resilience as the best way to make economies and societies stronger and improve people’s lives. Building more resilient cities was a major focus and will take deep collaboration between national governments, cities, and states. Cities are on the front lines of delivering the climate solutions and essential services that make people’s lives better, and demonstrating these benefits is critical to accelerating the transition. Local leaders are on the front lines of climate impacts and the delivery of essential services that make communities safer, healthier, and more resilient, yet too often lack the resources to respond. Cities and local communities especially need greater access to adaptation finance.

“It was disappointing to see no dedicated ocean event at the Leaders’ Summit, despite over 90% of countries including ocean action in national climate plans. The ocean is on the front lines of the climate crisis, but it could also be one of our greatest allies in tackling it. Ocean-based solutions can provide up to one-third of the emissions cuts needed by 2050 to keep 1.5°C within reach. This ambition for ocean action must be reflected in any COP31 cover decision and translate into concrete progress, especially for vulnerable coastal and island nations.

“Some leaders also delivered a resounding defense of multilateralism, even amid deep geopolitical tensions. International cooperation on climate may be changing shape, but it is still alive and needed now more than ever. Even in a fractured world, leaders recognize that no country can secure its economic future, build resilience, or prosper without working together on shared climate and environmental challenges. That spirit of cooperation must now carry through the negotiations and deliver a strong, ambitious outcome at COP31.”

Grazing sheep in fields with solar arrays

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Two new research papers shed light on the growing practice of grazing sheep in fields with solar arrays. One demonstrates that, in the right numbers, sheep grazing increases populations of beneficial insects like bees and lady beetles compared to mowing around solar panels. The other research describes a decision-making support tool to help farmers profitably grow their solar grazing herds.

A team from the College of Agriculture and Life Sciences (CALS) found that when two sheep per hectare graze around solar arrays, the abundance of wild bees, honey bees and lady beetles jumped by 38%, compared to a control plot that was mowed. The increase was driven primarily by an 83% increase in honey bees alone, according to the team’s findings in “Low solar-grazing densities support beneficial insects in agrivoltaic systems,” published Oct. 7 in Environmental Research Letters.

“Sheep grazing and beneficial insect habitat are two prominent conversation topics around solar development right now,” said Leana Zoller, the paper’s first author and a postdoctoral associate in natural resources in the Cornell CALS Ashley School of Global Development and the Environment. “But what’s been missing is research that treats them as parts of the same system. When we talk about sustainable solar development, integrating these discussions into a single framework is really helpful to farmers and community decision makers.”

For three years, Zoller and a team of entomologists, ecologists and animal and plant scientists tested various stocking rates – how many sheep per hectare are allowed to graze a certain plot of land – using Cornell’s teaching sheep grazing at the university-owned Cascadilla Community Solar Farm in Dryden, New York. At four sheep per hectare, they found beneficial insect abundance was similar to the mowed control site, while vegetation management effectiveness was greater than at two sheep per hectare. At eight sheep per hectare, vegetation management was maximized and sheep flock health remained high, but honey bee abundance dropped by 45%.

“We zeroed in on stocking rates because that’s a concrete number that sheep farmers understand and work with every day, and we want this research to be useful,” said Steve Grodsky, paper senior author and assistant professor of natural resources in the Ashley School. “With solar grazing, people, energy, agricultural animals, plants and insects are all part of this ecosystem. We’re trying to understand the connections between those pieces so that when solar developers, municipalities looking at solar development, or farmers are faced with a decision, they can have scientific information about potential trade-offs and synergies.”

Grodsky is also assistant unit leader of the U.S. Geological Survey New York Cooperative Fish and Wildlife Research Unit. Additional co-authors are sheep farmer and solar grazing consultant Nikola Kochendoerfer, Ph.D. ’20; Antonio DiTommaso, professor in the School of Integrative Plant Science; John Losey, professor of entomology; Scott McArt, Ph.D. ’12, associate professor of entomology; Paige Muñiz, formerly a technician in McArt’s lab; and Todd Ugine, Ph.D. ’04, research associate in Losey’s lab, all in CALS. Sarah Carson, director of Cornell’s Campus Sustainability Office, was key in coordinating site access between researchers and the solar company that manages the Cascadilla solar farm.

Another Cornell research team, from the Cornell SC Johnson College of Business, has developed a tool that helps farmers whose sheep graze in solar arrays to make better decisions about their operation. The team described the tool in “Flock Management for Solar Grazing,” which published Sept. 28 in Manufacturing & Service Operations Management.

After a chance encounter with Caleb Scott, Ithaca-based sheep farmer and founder of United Agrivoltaics, Vincent Slaugh, assistant professor at the Cornell Peter and Stephanie Nolan School of Hotel Administration and first author of the paper, began thinking through the economics and logistics of solar grazing. He recruited Linwei Xin, associate professor of operations research and information engineering, and Shane Henderson, Charles W. Lake, Jr. Professor in Productivity, both in the Cornell Duffield College of Engineering, to work on the topic.

Together, they developed a spreadsheet-based model that helps sheep farmers to optimize procurement and sales decisions, based on factors like target flock size, costs, anticipated revenues, and expected vegetation in particular solar sites; if a farmer doesn’t have enough sheep to keep the grass down, they’ll have to cut it some other way. It also helps solar developers understand growers’ needs and develop contracts that are more profitable and sustainable for all parties long-term. United Agrivoltaics has already incorporated the tool into its onboarding process.

Slaugh sees opportunity for U.S. shepherds to grow their operations – especially with solar contracts offering a steady source of income and animal feed. Current domestic sheep production for meat and wool doesn’t come close to meeting domestic demand; about half of all lamb and mutton consumed in the U.S. is imported, primarily from New Zealand and Australia.

“If we grow the sheep industry, will the American diet eventually include more lamb? Could that be a side effect of the solar energy revolution? I think the answer is very likely yes,” Slaugh said. “And then when that happens, we’d expect processors and supporting industries to come back, for banks to be willing to give more loans, and for supply chains to follow. All of that grows the economy, and rural economies in particular.”

Kochendoerfer, who grazes 700 ewes on solar installations in New York and Vermont, said that economy is already growing.

“I work with a lot of farms that are now scaling up because of solar,” Kochendoerfer said. “There is a new wave of sheep farmers who are capitalizing on this access to good land. With solar grazing, you get paid to graze as opposed to having to pay to graze. That leads to sustainable, meaningful economic income that these farmers can use to expand, build a barn, buy equipment or hire someone. It’s enabling a lot of folks to build sound, viable agricultural businesses.”

Funding for these research projects came from the Cornell Atkinson Center for Sustainability, the National Institute for Food and Agriculture and the National Science Foundation.
