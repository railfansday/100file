Jude Bellingham, star of England’s men’s soccer team and Real Madrid, admitted his skills are limited to the pitch, and that he hasn’t yet learned how to drive or cook.

Jude Bellingham may be one of the greatest footballers of his generation.

But as it turns out, Team England’s star midfielder’s basic everyday skills—or lack thereof—help level the playing field, bringing him closer to us mere mortals.

“There’s loads of things I don’t know how to do,” Bellingham told British Vogue in an interview published Nov. 10. “I can’t cook, I can’t drive. I’m good at what I do, but everything else? Not that great. I rely on my friends, my family to help me do stuff—chef, cleaner, mum, whoever else. That’s where I’m at.”

In fact, he’s been so busy keeping his eye on the literal ball—and doing a really good job at it—he may have lost track of life’s other skills.

“I work hard every day and all day, and then when I get home,” the 23-year-old said, “I’ve probably neglected learning how to do some of the more basic things.”

In his defense, not only is learning those life skills par for the course for many early twenty-somethings, but Bellingham’s daytime job also takes up a lot of his time.

“I feel mature in a football context because I’ve played nearly 400 games,” he reflected. “But in a life context I don’t feel that mature now, to be honest.”

But, as he noted, “There’ll be a time and a place for that, I’m sure.”

In the meantime, he’s focused on his skills on the pitch, where Bellingham’s natural talents already having brough him to the World Cup twice, in 2022 and 2026. And in their most recent outing, England finished third—the country’s best result since 1966.

“The World Cup, for me, was probably the best experience I’ve had in my life, full stop,” the Real Madrid player said. “While I was in it, I really made the conscious decision to enjoy this.”

After all, “It doesn’t come around very often,” he added. “It’s rare.”

And while Bellingham’s World Cup performance won him fans around the world—as did his endearing bromance with former Borussia Dortmund teammate and Norway’s star Erling Haaland—nowhere was more grateful for his performance than England.

As he noted, “I’ve seen a lot of people say, ‘I don’t think I’ve ever had a night when I was so happy and so proud to be English.’”

Warriors reveal Stephen Curry injury diagnosis after MRI on abdomen

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Golden State Warriors were already without one star. Now they'll be without their Most Valuable Player candidate, Stephen Curry, for the short-term future, too.

The Warriors announced Thursday, Nov. 12 that an MRI confirmed Curry suffered an oblique abdominal tear and added that he would be reevaluated in two weeks. The injury took place Wednesday, Nov. 11 in an eventual 115-108 loss against the Houston Rockets.

Curry walked off the floor midway through the first quarter, clearly grimacing and favoring his right side.

The sequence took place just 5:35 into the game. Curry had scored 3 points on 1-of-3 shooting by that point. He did not return to the floor for the rest of the first quarter, and he was not back at the bench area to start the second period.

ANALYSIS: What does Curry injury mean for Warriors? Can they weather the storm?

The Warriors announced minutes into the second quarter that Curry would not return for the rest of the game with right abdominal soreness.

After the game, Warriors coach Steve Kerr said that Curry said he "tweaked something in his abdominal area," which Curry told Kerr he had never done before. Kerr added that "concern is high" though he reiterated that he had no idea of the extent of the injury.

During the game, Curry did have a collision with Rockets forward Amen Thompson earlier in the game, when Curry had driven to the hoop on an attempted layup. Then, Curry had been fighting off a screen against Rockets center Alperen Şengün. As soon as that sequence ended, Curry once again grabbed his side.

Finally, just moments before he left the game, Curry curled around a screen and put up a 3-point shot. As soon as he landed and stepped backwards, he grabbed at his right side, near his hip.

Curry had been in the midst of a renaissance and had been elevating the play of the Warriors, who entered Wednesday night 8-3, which was tied for the third best record in the Western Conference.

Prior to suffering the injury, Curry was averaging 30.6 points, 4.1 rebounds and 4.3 assists per game in nine appearances. He was converting 3-pointers at a blistering 45.7%, which would mark a career-high. His scoring total entering Wednesday night had ranked second in the NBA, behind only Luka Dončić's historic 39.3 points per game, for all players with at least five games played.

The Warriors were already without Jimmy Butler, who has missed the start of the season as he rehabs the torn anterior cruciate ligament he suffered in January 2026.

The Saga of Former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang’s Fake Leather Jacket

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The internet loved his outrageous new look. There was just one problem.

There are several ways to respond when you've admitted to an affair, your company's shares are tumbling, and even the White House is discussing your private life. Turning up in a spectacularly studded Saint Laurent leather jacket is one of them. Resigning is another.

For a while, it seemed like Jensen Huang, the 63-year-old former CEO of Nvidia, had opted for the former. A photograph showing the tech billionaire in an archival Hedi Slimane YSL biker jacket went viral this week. The internet was delighted. There was just one problem: The photograph was fake.

The resignation, which came just this morning, was real.

As CNN reported, the image had been doctored from a genuine photograph taken at a Seoul airport in June. Nvidia, which one might imagine has other things to deal with right now, confirmed it wasn't real.

But perhaps the reason so many people believed it was that Huang already dresses like this. Leather jackets are his uniform. He reportedly owns 30 or 40 of them, including a snazzy reptile-effect number worn to deliver his company's keynote at the Consumer Electronics Show.

And Hedi Slimane was the perfect designer to bring into the fantasy. The Frenchman, who ran Saint Laurent from 2012 to 2016, made his name dressing skinny rock stars and turning their disheveled, devil-may-care attitude—later rebranded as so-called “indie sleaze”—into expensive luxury fashion. His leather jackets were practically a uniform for men who were determined to look as though they didn't care what anyone thought and/or having a midlife crisis.

For a billionaire in the middle of a spectacularly public scandal, it was quite a look. One Instagram commenter described Huang as the first person to achieve "negative aura" in archival Hedi.

There's another irony. Huang has previously said his wife Lori—i.e., the wronged party in all this—helps choose his clothes.

And then there's the technology itself. Nvidia makes the chips that power the AI revolution, including the tools capable of ... producing convincing fake photographs.

In conclusion then, the image wasn't real. But as a portrait of a billionaire in the middle of a spectacularly public crisis, we so wanted it to be.

Paris-based Orakl Oncology has raised a $34M Series A co-led by Norrsken VC and Singular. It grows copies of patients’ tumours in the lab, tests drugs on them and uses AI to predict trial results. It will expand into lung and gastric cancer.

Paris-based Orakl Oncology has raised a $34M Series A to predict how cancer drugs will perform before they reach patients, European Biotechnology reported. The round was co-led by Swedish investor Norrsken VC and French firm Singular, taking total funding to $50M.

Orakl was spun out of French cancer centre Gustave Roussy in 2023. It grows small copies of patients’ tumours in the lab, called organoids, and tests drugs on them. It then combines those results with patients’ medical records to train AI that predicts how a drug will do in clinical trials.

Norrsken says the system is 90% accurate. An April preprint, not yet peer-reviewed, tested the model on 23 past colorectal and pancreatic cancer trials. Its forecasts of how long patients went without their cancer worsening were off by 0.82 months on average.

It also picked the better treatment in seven of eight cases. But those tests looked back at trials that had already finished, and most involved standard chemotherapy. How the model handles newer drugs still needs to be proven.

The money will go into producing five times more lab data, expanding into lung and gastric cancers, and opening teams in the US and UK. Norrsken says 18 of the world’s 20 biggest drugmakers are in talks with Orakl, and in July it won rights to test four of Sanofi’s cancer drugs.

Orakl joins a wave of Paris startups using AI in biology, including Rivercell, which raised $25M in October. Singular also led a $20M round in Paris health startup Lucis this year, while US drug-design startup Chai Discovery raised $400M in July.

These 10 HBO miniseries tend to be forgotten about in the glut of amazing limited shows the network has made, but on review, they're much more watchable than they're given credit for. HBO still holds the title as the go-to streamer for prestige television and shows you won't regret watching till the end.

Still, that doesn't mean every HBO show is a hit. Even their vaunted collection of miniseries has many titles that received the critical and audience scores you might associate with other, less rigorous networks and streamers. The difference is that a forgotten, underappreciated miniseries on HBO is still very much worth your time.

Elizabeth I is a two-part co-production between HBO and Channel 4, directed by Tom Hooper of The King's Speech fame, and starring Helen Mirren as the titular Queen Elizabeth I of Britain, who ruled from 1558 to 1603. The series covers the back half of her reign, which is noted in part for her refusal to take a husband and bear any children.

The series focuses first on her relationship with the Earl of Leicester (Jeremy Irons), and the second on her relationship with the Earl of Essex (Hugh Dancy). Elizabeth I is totally Mirren's show. She disappears into the Queen and turns every piece of dialogue or look of melancholy into something amazing and forceful.

House of Saddam is a biographical docudrama that depicts the rise and fall of Saddam Hussein, the Iraqi dictator who was executed for crimes against humanity in 2006. The series begins in 1979, shortly after the Iranian Revolution, and ends with his death by hanging.

The series covers a huge portion of Hussein's life, and at times, it feels like we're only getting a surface-level story. Still, the parts we do get to see are incredible and shocking. Yigal Naor's portrayal of the infamous figure is amazing, but there's nuance to it as well that keeps you engaged with the character.

Jude Law stars in The Young Pope, a Sky Atlantic, HBO, and Canal+ production that follows Cardinal Lenny Belardo (Law), a New Yorker, who is suddenly elevated to the position of pope. Now styled as Pope Pius XIII, Lenny starts making drastic changes in the Vatican and within the Catholic Church.

His efforts to change the perception of Catholicism, and possibly reinstitute the Papal States, serve as the backdrop for an overly melodramatic but surprisingly funny series that often has its tongue in its cheek. The production design and costuming alone are reasons to watch the series.

Gunpowder is based on the 1605 Gunpowder Plot in London, when English Catholics attempted to assassinate the Protestant King James VI of Scotland and I of England by blowing up the House of Lords. Though Guy Fawkes (Tom Cullen) is most associated with the conspiracy, it was led by Robert Catesby (Kit Harrington).

The series is remembered for some explicit and difficult-to-watch instances of torture. Accurate or not, they suggest a grimmer tone than the series is actually operating in, which is much more of a spy thriller. If you can stomach those scenes, or fast-forward through them, you'll find a compelling and well-acted historical action drama.

Steven Soderbergh loves to get creative with the film form. In 2024's Presence, he filmed a horror story entirely from the point-of-view of the ghost. In Ocean's Twelve, he had Julia Roberts play Tess Ocean play the real-life Julia Roberts. He's happy to go avant-garde, and in Mosaic, he tries out an interactive dimension in filmmaking.

This six-episode murder mystery was released as a drama series on HBO, and as a mobile app, which allowed viewers to see the same scene from multiple viewpoints, look through clues, and engage with the story in a way more akin to a murder-mystery dinner party than a miniseries.

I Know This Much Is True is based on Wally Lamb's 1998 novel of the same name and follows twin brothers, Dominick and Thomas Birdsey, both played by Mark Ruffalo, which required him to film his scenes twice, once as Dominick, and then again six weeks later and 30 pounds heavier as Thomas (via Variety).

Medieval settings can make for interesting stories whether it's Viking Europe or Kublai Khan's Asia. Here are the best medieval TV shows available.

In 1990s Connecticut, Thomas Birdsey has paranoid schizophrenia but manages his symptoms with medication. However, occasional episodes result in events like Thomas cutting off his own hand, requiring the intervention of his brother. It's dark and difficult, with not much of a light at the end of the tunnel, but it's powerful for it.

Philip Roth's 2004 novel of the same name serves as the inspiration for another Ed Burns and David Simon HBO TV show. The Plot Against America is set in an alternate history where Charles Lindbergh, a real-life aviator and noted xenophobic populist, is elected president, shaping America.

In this new world, America has pivoted towards fascism in the '40s. The election of the popular celebrity is met with elation by some, and quiet discomfort by others. What begins with a neutral stance on the Nazis ends with rampant antisemitic violence, martial law, and the collapse of the old America. It's a troubling series that hits painfully close to home.

Luca Guadagnino's We Are Who We Are is a coming-of-age story set on a fictional US military base in Chioggia, Italy, where 14-year-old Fraser Wilson (Jack Dylan Grazer) has just moved to from New York City. There, he meets Caitlin Poythres (Jordan Kristine Seamón), who has been on the base for years and is beginning to question her gender identity.

The picturesque Italian coastline provides the backdrop for an achingly modern series that takes on some of the stickiest modern cultural and political topics. As Guadagnino is wont to do, he never shows his cards all at once, preferring to take on these questions from multiple angles.

Woody Harrelson and Justin Theroux star in White House Plumbers as E. Howard Hunt and G. Gordon Liddy, the two men who organized and directed the break-in at the Watergate building which led to the Watergate scandal, which ended in Richard Nixon's resignation.

The series does its best to stick to the history of what really happened in the events leading up to the break-in and the fallout, and by doing so, White House Plumbers does feel a bit overstuffed. Even so, Theorux and Harrelson's chemistry and careful sense of tone make this a historical drama that's a blast to watch.

Kate Winslet's The Regime is a black comedy set in a fictional, unnamed, Central European country. Chancellor Elena Vernham (Winslet) is the vain and corrupt dictator of the country, but in recent years, her government has begun to crumble thanks to her laws and impositions on the country.

Increasingly paranoid, Elena attempts to expand her power further, only hastening her downfall. The Regime tries for some sharp-tongued satire that doesn't always hit, but is more than made up for with Winslet's performance. Her bold decisions make this an HBO miniseries that feels unique among the ones she usually appears in.

10 Ocarina of Time Remake Changes That Break the Hearts of Nostalgic Fans

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Covering the video games industry since 2017, with experience in news, articles, and reviews. Currently a writer at DualShockers and gaming magazines.

The remake of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time has brought one of the most beloved and iconic adventures in gaming history back to life with a revamped visual presentation, plenty of gameplay improvements, and a surprising number of small changes that make exploring Hyrule feel fresh all over again. From quality-of-life improvements to the inventory and equipment systems to subtle environmental tweaks, there's literally a lot to appreciate about Nintendo's new take on this timeless classic.

However, as someone who grew up playing the original (and has an admittedly unhealthy amount of nostalgia running through my veins), I couldn't help but notice that some of the little things I loved are no longer around – or they are, but in a very different way.

Thomas Mahler believes Nintendo missed an opportunity to improve the level design in the Zelda: Ocarina of Time Remake.

Don't get me wrong, I absolutely adore this remake and described it as a lovely modern remake with an old soul, but that doesn't mean I'm not allowed to miss a few things from my childhood, right? So, please allow me to put my nostalgic glasses on for a moment and revisit ten changes that have left a tiny hole in my heart.

One of my favorite things about the original Ocarina of Time was starting a new save file and giving Link the most ridiculous name I could think of. It was such a simple feature, but it opened the door to some quite hilarious moments, especially when characters started addressing the legendary hero using whatever silly name I'd come up with.

One of my personal favorites was naming him “Bro.” Suddenly, every conversation in Hyrule sounded like everyone was part of the most laid-back adventure imaginable.

Unfortunately, the remake has decided to put an end to our creative freedom by locking the protagonist's name to Link. I can understand the reasoning, especially considering Nintendo's efforts to make the franchise more accessible to new generations and expand its presence across other media (since the live-action movie is coming hot). Still, I'll always miss my little adventures with Bro. You will be missed, buddy.

One of the coolest easter eggs in the original Ocarina of Time was hidden in the courtyard where you first meet Princess Zelda. As Child Link, you must sneak past the castle guards to reach the princess without getting caught. Once you've had your little chat with her, however, there's nothing stopping you from exploring the area and discovering a few fun secrets.

Among them is a window you can shoot with your Slingshot, prompting a particularly grumpy guard to throw a bomb in your direction (how rude!). But that's not all. If you look through the other windows, you'll find three familiar faces from the Super Mario universe: Mario, Luigi, and Bowser, all sporting their Super Mario 64 appearances.

I was honestly expecting Nintendo to bring this little crossover back using the characters' more modern designs, perhaps even their Super Mario Odyssey models. Instead, the entire reference has been removed from the remake. It's a shame, considering how charming these little Nintendo Easter eggs can be. At least the guard is still there to throw a bomb at your face. Some traditions must be preserved, I suppose.

If there's one thing every Ocarina of Time veteran knows, it's that Rupees are important, and there's no shame in taking advantage of a good farming opportunity. One of the earliest places where you could stock up on some extra cash was a room inside Hyrule Castle's Market, conveniently filled with breakable pots.

As Child Link, you could simply walk inside, smash everything in sight, collect the Rupees, leave, and come back to find the pots replenished. Rinse and repeat until your pockets were sufficiently full.

Unfortunately, the remake has decided that we shouldn't be allowed to get rich quite so easily. While you can still find some pots containing Rupees in the room, they no longer respawn simply by leaving the castle and coming back inside. Instead, you'll have to wait quite a while and spend some time doing other activities before they return. I guess we'll just have to go back to cutting grass like the rest of Hyrule's hardworking citizens.

I know what you're thinking – why on earth would anyone miss Kaepora Gaebora being annoying? But hear me out, I promise there's a reason behind my questionable opinion!

I also ain't a fan of the original owl. In fact, I probably spent more time than I'd like to admit accidentally answering “No” when he asked whether I wanted to hear his explanation again. And if you've played the original, you know exactly how frustrating that could be. However, there was something strangely entertaining about collectively hating this overly talkative bird. Kaepora became one of those characters that practically every Ocarina of Time player had a story about, and that shared frustration was part of the experience.

Now, the remake has given him a much cuter appearance, and he's also significantly less annoying, behaving much more like the wise and mysterious owl he's supposed to be. I mean, where’s the fun in that? I can't help missing the days when simply seeing him fly toward me filled me with dread.

Ocarina of Time has plenty of memorable exploit tricks, but one of my personal favorites involved getting a Heart Piece much earlier than Nintendo originally intended.

In the original game, you could perform a particularly well-timed backflip from the top of Death Mountain, near the entrance to Goron City, and land on the roof of Dodongo's Cavern. This allowed you to grab a Heart Piece that was normally intended for Adult Link, long before you were supposed to have access to it. It was just one of those silly little tricks that made exploring Hyrule feel even more rewarding.

Well, those days are over. The remake has adjusted the backflip's height, making it considerably less effective, and even tweaked the position of Dodongo's Cavern entrance to prevent players from pulling off the same trick (not that I tried to do it or anything).

From suspicious house designs to familiar songs, fans think they've found clues.

There's an unwritten rule among players: the moment you unlock the Hookshot, you must immediately equip it and start shooting at everything in sight. And one of your first destinations will probably be the rooftops of Kakariko Village.

This was really fun because it offered more freedom than simply using it to reach the obvious targets. You could experiment with different surfaces, discover unexpected ways to navigate the environment, and generally mess around with an incredibly useful piece of equipment. Apparently, however, that wasn't quite what Nintendo had in mind. The remake has restricted the Hookshot's functionality, meaning it can now only latch onto specific designated targets, such as stone markers and certain treasure chests.

I understand that this might have been done to prevent unintended shortcuts or make progression more controlled, but I can't help feeling that something has been lost in the process. One of the most enjoyable aspects of Ocarina of Time was experimenting with your equipment and discovering creative ways to use it. So, please, Nintendo, give us our old Hookshot back!

One of my most vivid childhood memories of Ocarina of Time involves something that probably sounds completely ridiculous to anyone who didn't grow up playing the original: stepping into Hyrule Field for the very first time and desperately running toward Hyrule Castle like a lunatic.

That happened because, if night fell before we reached the castle gates, they would close, leaving Link stranded outside until morning – and Hyrule Field at night was not exactly the safest place to be.

And you’ve guessed: the remake has removed this particular inconvenience, meaning you no longer have to worry about being locked out of Hyrule Castle after sunset. My adult self appreciates not having to deal with that little problem, but the child in me misses those frantic nighttime sprints.

There's something wonderfully weird about the little laboratory at Lake Hylia. Inside, you'll find a rather eccentric scientist who spends most of his time complaining about his back pain while standing next to a water tank.

He'll even offer you a reward if you manage to dive deep enough to touch the bottom of the pool. Naturally, being the helpful hero that you are, you'll want to give it a try. But once you reach the bottom, you'll discover something rather unexpected: a shark inside a cage.

What made this little discovery even funnier in the original game was the shark itself! Rather than featuring a detailed 3D model, it was represented by a hilariously low-resolution, static image. It looked so out of place that it became one of those bizarre little details you simply couldn't forget. While the remake has kept the shark in the aquarium, unfortunately, it has also given it a proper visual upgrade. Sure, it looks considerably better now, but I miss the ridiculous charm of that pixelated little creature.

If there's one thing Ocarina of Time does exceptionally well, it's turning a seemingly innocent adventure into a horror game without any warning! One moment you're exploring a dungeon and collecting treasures, and the next you're desperately running for your life from something that looks like it escaped from your worst nightmare.

Few enemies capture that feeling better than the Wallmaster, a giant disembodied hand that suddenly drops from the ceiling, grabs Link, and sends him all the way back to the beginning of the area or dungeon.

And what made this enemy so terrifying in the original was the anticipation. You couldn't simply look up to check whether it was coming, so you had to rely on an increasingly unsettling sound effect and the shadow that gradually appeared above Link. The remake preserves most of these elements, but introduces one rather important difference: a spotlight that illuminates the area whenever it’s about to appear. Combined with its shadow, this makes the enemy practically announce its arrival in the most dramatic way possible.

I appreciate the warning, but I honestly miss the uncertainty that made these encounters so frightening in the original. Thankfully, the Dead Hand is absolutely nightmare-inducing in the remake, so I'll let this one slide.

Remember the Water Temple? If you played the original Ocarina of Time back in the day, there's a good chance that simply reading those two words made you feel a little uncomfortable.

For years, this dungeon had a reputation for being one of the most frustrating experiences in the entire game. Between constantly changing the water level and switching between the Iron Boots and other equipment, it was enough to test even the most patient players.

There was also a particularly nasty Small Key hidden underneath the floor of the central pillar, which could only be accessed after raising the water level. It was incredibly easy to overlook, and if you ever found yourself seemingly stuck with no idea how to progress, there's a good chance this little key was the culprit!

Despite keeping most of the original dungeon's layout intact, the remake has made things considerably easier by removing this easily missed Small Key altogether. This means players no longer have to spend ages retracing their steps and wondering whether they've somehow managed to softlock their game.

Of course, the Water Temple is still a memorable dungeon, and I'm certainly not complaining about a more streamlined experience. But I do find it funny that an entire generation of players had to suffer through those confusing corridors just so newcomers could have a slightly more pleasant time! You're welcome, younger Zelda fans. We fought in the trenches so you wouldn't have to.

Karin Slaughter Said “Absolutely Not” to Adapting Her Novel ‘The Good Daughter.’ Then She Wrote the Whole Series

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The bestselling novelist initially turned down Bruna Papandrea, wrote all six episodes of the Peacock series on her own and only then pitched it: "I wanted to make sure I could land the plane."

Some stories refuse to stay on the page. The Hollywood Reporter’s Beyond the Book column explores what happens when books make the leap to screen and beyond — unpacking what stayed, changed, how it was done and why it matters with the creatives who made it.

Karin Slaughter never planned to adapt The Good Daughter.

When producer Bruna Papandrea asked whether she’d be interested in bringing one of her novels to the screen herself, the author’s answer was immediate. “I said, ‘Absolutely not,’” Slaughter tells The Hollywood Reporter. “I don’t have time.” Related Stories TV 'The Good Daughter' Review: Rose Byrne and Meghann Fahy Elevate Peacock's Overly Familiar Southern-Fried Thriller General News Foundation Books Unveils First Titles in Macmillan Partnership; John Goodman to Narrate Animated Short Adaptation (Exclusive)

She’d already watched two of her books become hits in other hands: Netflix’s Pieces of Her, adapted by Charlotte Stoudt, and ABC’s Will Trent, run by Liz Heldens. Though she declined, Slaughter couldn‘t move on, and the idea that she might do it herself stuck. So she started writing the scripts, and she told no one.

“I wrote them completely on my own because I didn’t want to tell anybody if I’d done a bad job,” she tells THR. When she finally told her agent, she made one request: Don’t tell Papandrea — or anyone for that matter — yet. “Because she’d want an option. I just wanted to do this in secret.”

The secrecy also gave her time to take on the creative challenge. “I know from my novel writing that the beginning of a story is important, but just as important is the end,” she says. “People can read a book they think is mediocre, and that’s fine. But if it starts out really great and craps out at the end, they’re furious. So I wanted to make sure I could land the plane.”

With the scripts done, Slaughter went back to Papandrea. “I said to Bruna, ‘This is the show. If you’re interested, that’s great. If not, obviously I’ll take notes, but I haven’t wasted your time and money on an option.’ She read it overnight and said, ‘Let’s go, let’s do this.’”

The result is The Good Daughter, Peacock’s six-episode adaptation of Slaughter’s 2017 bestseller, which drops on the streamer in full on Nov. 12. Slaughter is the sole writer on every episode and serves as showrunner and executive producer.

The series stars Rose Byrne and Meghann Fahy as Samantha and Charlotte Quinn, sisters still living with the aftermath of a single event of violence during their childhood, with Brendan Gleeson as their father, Rusty. The cast also includes Shayna Benardo, Olivia Williams, Harper Steele, Jake McDorman and Michael Dorman. Made Up Stories, Papandrea’s banner, produces with Fifth Season.

When the team took the project to streamers, Peacock “just got it,” Slaughter says. “They had thoughtful questions, and they seemed like people who would be good partners.”

Though an author taking on the showrunner role for the first time, Slaughter‘s résumé shows why she could afford to skip the usual option-and-develop process. Will Trent was renewed by ABC this spring for a fifth season and had 10 million cross-platform viewers last season, making Slaughter’s catalog one of the more reliable book-to-TV pipelines. The Good Daughter, though, is the first time she’s been in charge of that pipeline herself, and she set ground rules early. Drawing on her long relationship with her book editor, she made a request of the network about notes.

“I said, ‘Obviously there are going to be notes, but I need you to speak with one voice,’” she recalls. “You can have all the meetings in the world and discuss it, but I don’t want a note that Kevin from accounting suggested and you don’t agree with.”

She also wanted the reasoning behind each note on the table, so she could interrogate it. “I’m not a ‘note behind the note’ person — I want to know the note. So let’s have a conversation so I can figure out what you want, how to do it and how to make sure we’re both happy with the outcome,” she says.

As a first-time showrunner, the biggest adjustment, she says, was losing a novelist’s room to explain things. “With a book, I get 500 pages to tell you what people are thinking,” she says. “So it was figuring out how to convey information without making it clunky. I never wanted anything to feel written. As a novelist, dialogue is probably one of my strengths, so it felt natural.”

To keep the mystery working and span across six hours, she made clue sheets as her way “to figure out how to make the puzzle work and give all the pieces.” When a note came in on a script, she could show exactly where a detail paid off. “I’d say, ‘We need this piece of information because it pays off in [episode] five or six,’” she says. “After a while, you develop a certain level of trust.”

She also kept the season concise. “I learned very quickly that as much plot as a novel has, there’s not enough for eight episodes. I made the decision early on: This is only going to be six episodes, because I’m not going to do a filler episode,” she says. “Pacing is a hallmark of my writing. I wanted you to be emotionally invested and on the edge of your seat. There’s the tension you get from the plot, but this is a story that relies on emotional tension, and I needed those emotions to hit at the right times.”

She also pushed for Peacock to release every episode at once. “I was scared they’d say no and parcel it out.”

The reasoning for the immediate rollout is because Slaughter thought of the series as “a six-hour movie.” That interpretation also shaped the choice of director Steph Green (The Americans), who directs all six episodes. “She understood that it was a movie and not standalone episodes,” Slaughter says. Each installment has its own genre — one is a legal drama, another a family drama then a murder mystery — but Green kept a “symmetry of place, setting and atmosphere” across all of them, Slaughter explains.

“I think the first thing I said to her was, ‘I’m so tired of thrillers where everything is dark, even in the daytime. Does anyone have a lamp in this house? I want the bad things to happen in daylight,’” Slaughter says. “That’s what we focused on, and our cinematographer, Zoë White, totally got it.”

Slaughter also fought to film in Georgia. “They made us scout in Toronto, and I was such a butt the whole time,” she says laughing. Once the team reached North Georgia, she recalls showing them the red clay — red is a key color in the story — and the kudzu. “Because we’re writing about things that happen to women and girls every single day, we needed to ground this in a naturalistic environment,” she says.

A Georgia native herself, Slaughter took the story‘s Southern backdrop seriously. “I was very clear: This is not Southern Gothic. Those words have meaning. This is not grotesque, it is not horror. It’s a story set in the South, but it’s not a Gothic story. Steph put together this beautiful look book, and it felt like a perfect fit because she got what I wanted to do and how I wanted it to look.”

The same thinking shaped the score. Composer Jonathan Sanford grew up in Maryland’s Prince George’s County but has family roots in Georgia, and he understood, Slaughter says, “that there’s a way to tell Southern stories that’s just such a trope,” with slide guitar and banjo.

“I said, ‘I don’t want to hear any of that. I want a new, crisp Southern musical language developed for this,’” she says. “As a Southerner, it was very important to me to represent the South for what it is, not all these stereotypes.”

Slaughter teases that Fahy even sings a track that will be featured on the show‘s soundtrack. “I knew she was a singer — she has a beautiful voice — and from the moment we started shooting I was working on everybody: ‘We’ve got to get Meghann to do something,’” she says. “I think they did it just to shut me up. But she did amazingly well. It’s a beautiful cover.”

Fahy and Bryne may star as the leading protagonists, but Jessica Biel was originally attached to star and executive produce the series, which scored a straight-to-series order at Peacock in March 2024. She eventually left the project in September of that year. Slaughter holds no grudge. Biel, instead, went on to star in The Better Sister, based on the novel by Slaughter’s friend and fellow author Alafair Burke. “I loved watching it. What do I have to be angry about?” she says.

Slaughter credits Bryne and Fahy for evolving her characters with Byrne’s casting as Sam inspiring a change in the character. “Sam in the book is a lot colder than Sam in the show, and that’s Rose, because she has this natural warmth,” Slaughter says. After watching dailies, she reworked lines to “lean into and support what she was doing.”

Fahy’s Charlie called for rewrites, too, particularly in her romance with Ben, played by Dorman. “In the book, Charlie is much more thirsty for Ben — she’s chasing him. Meghann Fahy is not thirsty,” Slaughter says. “So I had to find ways to express that longing for Ben in a way that was believable.”

And because Fahy handled Slaughter’s Southern rhythms so easily, Slaughter kept writing her more lines. “I’m going to write the hell out of this scene,” she remembers thinking. “It made more work for her.”

Slaughter has been candid about using violence in her books to open up a dialogue. With The Good Daughter, she hopes the series grounds the conversation in the fact that violent events happen to “real people” and that “there are real people living their lives after these things happen.”

She points to Bryne‘s Sam, who lives with lasting injuries from the shooting she experienced at a young age, including impaired vision and walking with a cane. “I watch a lot of television, and I can’t think of many shows where you see a survivor of gun violence and what their life is like,” she says. “We always hear that horrible statistic — ‘this many people died and this many survived‘ — and we think, ‘Thank God they survived.’ But we don’t think about what that recovery looks like. I wanted to show that.”

Byrne, she says, did “so much work with a physical therapist, figuring out how to move in a body like that. She has such physicality, and to distill all of that into this person recovering from a gunshot wound shows the range of her abilities. The fact that she could elevate that performance is remarkable.”

Despite taking on the showrunner title, Slaughter’s work as a novelist never stopped. After finishing the show’s scripts, she went back to work on her most recently published book, We Are All Guilty Here, and noticed the show has already changed how she writes, no longer keeping characters in one place for long stretches.

“When I write my Will Trent novels, I can have a whole scene in a car, and it’s a chapter,” she says. “But with Guilty, I was moving people around more.”

The reason was a day on the Good Daughter set. “You don’t know guilt until you’ve put Rose Byrne and Meghann Fahy in a car on a trailer in South Georgia, 10,000 degrees in August, and the air-conditioning’s not on,” she says laughing. “I was like, ‘Oh my God, why couldn’t I have set this in an office? Why are they sweating?’”

Whether she’ll take another book to the screen herself, Slaughter isn’t saying yet. For now, she’s finishing her next novel and focused on getting viewers to The Good Daughter. “It’s exactly the kind of show I’d love to watch,” she says. “I’m so proud of it!“

On Thursday (Nov. 12), the Euclid space telescope released its first major batch of data, capturing the light of more than a billion objects over an area of sky as big as 9,500 full moons. The data release lays the foundation for one of the largest-ever 3D maps of the cosmos and many groundbreaking discoveries about the nature of dark energy and dark matter.

The European Space Agency's (ESA) Euclid space telescope has been called the "Dark Universe Detective" because of its capability to investigate the dual mysteries of dark matter, the universe's most mysterious stuff, and dark energy, the force accelerating the expansion of the universe. Collectively, these cosmic elements are sometimes referred to as the dark universe.

The new data release, designated DR1-Foundation, was built from 13 months of observations and is accompanied by a multitude of images, including large sky mosaics. It marks the first major release of mission data since Euclid launched in mid-2023.

"These are far more than beautiful pictures; today marks a scientific milestone in our study of the cosmos," ESA's Director of Science, Carole Mundell said in a statement. "This major Euclid data release represents the largest space-based survey of its kind, laying the foundation of future discovery in a new regime for astronomy and demonstrating the excellence of European technology, science and international collaboration."

The ESA put into raw numbers the sheer number of celestial objects viewed by the Dark Universe Detective and contained in DR1-Foundation. That includes light spectra from 160 million sources, over 900 million galaxies, and 60 million candidates for quasars, regions of space dominated by feeding supermassive black holes.

"This is a change in scale from Euclid's previous releases, which included a few days of observations over around 60 degrees squared of sky," ESA's Euclid project scientist Valeria Pettorino said. "We're now releasing more than a year of data covering a significantly larger area. This unlocks groundbreaking science."

Euclid can see such a vast number of cosmic objects thanks to the fact that its space-based position helps it avoid interference caused by Earth's atmosphere.

This includes clusters of galaxies and even larger superclusters. The study of these objects, some of the largest structures in the cosmos, can reveal how matter came together across the larger network of gas, dust and dark matter called the cosmic web.

"Euclid doesn't just look far back in time  — it does so across huge areas of sky," Euclid Consortium member Barbara Sartoris of JL Lagrange Laboratory, Nice, France, explained. "This gives us an unprecedented view of large samples of ancient galaxy clusters that were previously out of reach. "We will explore these in the coming months to learn how these giants grew, and decipher what they reveal about the dark universe."

Another exciting element of the new Euclid data release is the 15,000 strong gravitational lensing candidates catalogued by the space telescope. Gravitational lensing is an element of the universe first explained in Einstein magnum opus, general relativity, in 1915.

General relativity says that objects with mass cause the very fabric of space to warp, and that gravity arises from this warping. But there is another consequence. When light passes this curvature, its usually straight path is curved. That means when light from a single background source passes an object with great mass, like a galaxy or galaxy cluster, a strong gravitational lens, it can arrive at our telescopes at different times.

As a result of this strong lensing, background sources can be greatly amplified. It is an effect that the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has been using to great effect to study galaxies in the early universe.

Thus, the prospect of mapping 15,000 strong gravitational lenses is monumental, especially in studying the rate at which the universe is expanding and thus the effect of dark energy.

"Strong lensing provides unique insight into the dark universe, allowing us to 'weigh' individual galaxies, including their invisible dark matter, and explore how the universe is expanding," Natalie Lines of the University of Portsmouth, U.K., and member of the Euclid Consortium, said. "Over 10 years ago, we forecast that Euclid would revolutionize the field of strong lens discovery. That day is finally here."

Arguably, the most exciting thing about DR1-Foundation is that Euclid is just getting started. The area of sky covered from spring 2024 to spring 2025 represents just 14% of Euclid's target for its entire six-year survey.

That means whatever scientists discover from this data release could be the tip of the iceberg in terms of how the Dark Universe Detective will change our understanding of the cosmos.

Nothing captures traditional Christmas decor like the glossy foliage and scarlet berries of American holly (Ilex opaca). Hardy across zones 5-9, this stately native evergreen provides year-round privacy and winter shelter for songbirds, before lending its iconic branches to holiday mantels and door wreaths. Plant in full sun to partial shade in moist, slightly acidic soil. It can reach 15-30 feet (4.5-9m) tall, so allow ample elbow room or choose compact cultivars like Maryland Dwarf.

You must plant at least one pollinating male cultivar (such as Jersey Knight) within 40 feet of berry-producing female trees (Satyr Hill or Miss Helen) to guarantee fruit set. Harvest small, 8-12-inch (20-30cm) sprigs evenly around the tree using sharp pruners. Dip stems in water overnight before wiring into garlands. Note that holly berries are toxic to pets and humans if ingested, so display out of reach of dogs and toddlers. You can buy American Holly from Nature Hills at a discount if you hurry.

For winter drama, few shrubs rival winterberry holly. Unlike its evergreen cousins, this rugged native deciduous holly drops its leaves in fall to reveal bare branches densely studded with luminous, bright red berries that persist well into midwinter. Thriving across zones 3-9, winterberry (Ilex verticillata) is ideal for damp, low-lying, or poorly drained spots where most other woody ornamentals rot. It prefers full sun for maximum fruit production, as well as acidic, organic-rich soil.

Winterberry also requires male and female plants for pollination (and hence berries). Pair prolific female cultivars like Winter Red or compact Berry Poppins with a male pollinator such as Southern Gentleman or Mr. Poppins. Cut stems look magnificent in tall floor vases, outdoor porch planters, and minimalist wreaths paired with silver-blue cedar. Harvest selectively once leaves have fallen, leaving plenty of fruit for robins and cedar waxwings. If you fancy a golden variation on the classic winterberry, you can buy Yellow Winterberry Holly from Amazon.

When winter strips color from the yard, red twig dogwood (Cornus sericea) brings blazing crimson bark that looks electrifying against fresh snow. Hardy in zones 2-7, this vigorous native tolerates everything from boggy clay to average loam in full sun or light shade. Look for vibrant Cardinal or compact Arctic Fire, which stays under 4 feet (1.2m) tall and fits neatly into small suburban borders. The most dramatic bark develops on 1-2-year-old shoots, as older wood gradually turns brown. Cut out up to one third of the oldest, thickest stems down to the ground in late fall. This renewal pruning stimulates a flush of vivid new canes next spring, while providing structural crimson wands for your holiday decor. Weave flexible young stems into evergreen wreaths for classic contrast, or arrange upright branches in weighted urns alongside white pine and pinecones.

Junipers deliver unmistakable fragrance alongside feathery, silver-blue foliage studded with dusty, berry-like cones. Thriving in zones 3-9 depending on species, junipers range from low-spreading groundcovers (like Blue Rug) to graceful upright evergreens like Eastern red cedar (Juniperus virginiana) and blue-tinged Rocky Mountain juniper (Wichita Blue). Plant in full sun and sharp, well-drained soil. Once established, junipers are drought-tolerant and shrug off winter winds. Their supple boughs are tailor-made for weaving lush garlands over doorways, shaping rustic table runners, and layering texture into mixed evergreen wreaths. When harvesting branches, prune lightly from the outer tips and never cut back into old, bare interior wood. Dunk cut sprigs in cool water for 24 hours before crafting, and wear sleeves to guard against potential skin irritation from the resinous needles.

Bridging the gap between holiday decor and festive feasting, here comes rosemary. Its pine-scented foliage brings instant charm to winter tablescapes and mini wreaths, especially paired with dried orange slices and cinnamon sticks. Rosemary (Salvia rosmarinus) is reliably perennial outdoors in zones 8-10, where it thrives in lean alkaline soil. If you are in zone 7 or colder, grow upright cultivars like Tuscan Blue or Arp in terracotta containers. You can buy Arp Rosemary Indoor Christmas Plants from Plant Addicts. Just remember to transition plants outside when weather permits.

Move pots indoors to a bright, south-facing windowsill before hard fall freezes arrive, watering sparingly only when the top two inches (5cm) of soil feel dry. Harvesting sprigs in December encourages bushy branching, but never remove more than one-third of the plant at once. Any leftover trimmings from wreath-making sessions can be used in holiday roast potatoes, focaccia, and mulled cider. Win-win!

For a modern, frosted aesthetic that elevates traditional red-and-green palettes, try silver dollar eucalyptus (Eucalyptus cinerea). Prized by floral designers for its dusty leaves and camphor aroma, this fast-growing Australian native is hardy outside in zones 8-11. In cooler climates, grow it as a chic container specimen that can come into a cool, bright sunroom or frost-free conservatory before temperatures dip below 25°F (-4°C). Eucalyptus demands full sun and gritty, fast-draining soil. In late fall, selectively prune back overly long, whippy shoots by up to a third. This shapes the plant before winter rest, rewarding you with supple stems for cascading garlands, mantel swags, and vase displays. Unlike conifers that eventually shed, eucalyptus dries gracefully indoors. Just let the cut stems dry before using them in arrangements, hanging them upside down in a cool, dark room for 2 weeks.

Eastern white pine (Pinus strobus) is quintessential holiday greenery. Instantly recognizable by its soft, feathery bundles of blue-green needles, this stately conifer thrives in zones 3-8. Plant in full sun or light shade in slightly acidic, well-drained soil. Give it generous space, too. Standard trees can soar 50-80 feet (15-24m) tall, but compact cultivars like Nana or Blue Shag suit smaller yards. Growing white pine for holiday decor rewards patience. Allow newly planted trees 3-5 years to establish before harvesting, and never cut the central leader shoot, as it can ruin the tree's natural symmetry. When mature, snip 12-18-inch (30-45cm) lateral side tips around the lower and middle branches. You can weave its flexible stems into lush banister garlands and door wreaths, while its perfume fills the home.

Don’t rush the process and harvest too much too soon, and you’ll be rewarded with years of lush, natural décor.

Astrophysicists Want to Forecast When a Black Hole Launches Its Jets

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Black holes are not just objects. Some astrophysicists prefer to call them locations. From this vantage point, they're locations where the Universe's laws of physics are pushed to their extreme limits.

Black holes are gravitational gluttons that draw everything in their vicinity towards them. When another star strays too close, the black hole can strip its gas away. The gas doesn't necessarily disappear over the event horizon and leave our observable Universe. It first gathers in a rapidly rotating accretion disk, where its temperature is raised to extreme levels. Some of this superheated material is ejected in a pair of collimated jets from the black hole's axis of rotation.

These jets are streams of plasma and particles that can reach relativistic speeds. They can have an enormous reach; the largest ones come from supermassive black holes and can extend for millions of parsecs into space. Though astrophysicists have witnessed many of these jets, their exact causes remain uncertain. The extreme gravity, magnetic fields, and temperatures around black holes is all a part of it.

Astrophysical jets shape their surroundings by heating star forming gas and moving it around on a massive scale. Stars form from cool gas, and the energy from jets works directly against that. The jets can also inflate bubbles where gas is too diffuse to collapse and form stars.

This JWST NIRCam image shows the jets coming from Messier 87, one of the most massive galaxies in the local Universe. These jets aren't from an x-ray binary, they're from its central SMBH that has billions of solar masses. It's an example of extreme astrophysical jets and an indicator of the extreme physics at black holes. Image Credit: Jan RÃ¶der, Maciek Wielgus et al., Astronomy & Astrophysics (2025)

Astrophysicists want to be able to predict when these jets are triggered so they can quickly gather follow-up observations, and new research in The Astrophysical Journal has made some progress. It's titled "Jet Forecast: Timing of Discrete Jet Ejections of Stellar-mass Black Holes Anticipated via X-Ray Properties," and the authors are Toshihiro Kawaguchi and Kazutaka Yamaoka. Kawaguchi is from the Graduate School of Science and Engineering at the University of Toyama, and Yamaoka is from the Institute for Space-Earth Environmental Research (ISEE) at Nagoya University.

The pair of researchers have been working on this problem for a while. In a 2025 study, they examined an x-ray binary consisting of a black hole and a donor star and found a link between the parameters of the inner accretion disk and jet activity. That research suggested that as the accretion disk's inner edge shrank towards its innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO), the black hole launched its jets. "This fact suggests that the jet materials originate from accreting matter from the innermost part of the disk," the authors wrote.

That was progress, but in that paper the researchers pointed out that more observations were needed. "Both X-ray spectral and timing observations are very important to understand the accretion disk states which can change very rapidly. Dense, simultaneous coverage with radio observations are also crucial to understand diskâjet interactions," the authors wrote in the conclusion to their 2025 paper.

This illustration is representative of XTE J1859+226, the system the authors studied in their 2025 paper. It shows a black hole not only drawing material away from the donor star, but also stretching the star into a distorted shape. Image Credit: J. M. Corral-Santana/Corral-Santana et al. 2011 MNRAS

Their 2025 paper was based on observations of a single black hole x-ray binary gathered during an outburst in 1999â2000. This work is based on observations of four black hole binary systems: one from the 2025 paper and three new ones.

There seem to be changes in an x-ray binary's (XRB) x-ray emissions prior to jets being launched. The spectrum is altered, and variability decreases. Astrophysicists have interpreted these changes as a signal that jets are about to be emitted. But the signals haven't been clear and reliable enough, so astrophysicists haven't been able to use them to time their follow-up observations. The researchers' previous work showed that as an accretion disk's inner edge shrank down to its ISCO, jets were launched. This could be the reliable signal astrophysicists are searching for.

This work expands their observations to more XRBs to see if this holds true. The researchers hope to develop a forecasting model for XRB jet launching so that more complete observations can be obtained.

The results show that the rapidly decreasing radius of the inner disk, called Rin, combined with a decrease in x-ray emission variability (indicated by rms), are a reliable predictor of jet launching. "All the four sources turned out to show the precursors of jet ejections (drops in Rin and rms) 1â10 days before radio activities," the authors write.

They also found that there are no reliably detected increases in the amount of mass accreted prior to the launching of jets. "They show no huge jumps in M prior to each jet ejection," the researchers explain.

In at least one of the XRBs, that rate actually decayed in the 30 days leading up to jet launching. "We examined the time evolution of the accretion rate M of XTE J1859. It turned out to be exponentially decaying during the outburst (with an e-folding timescale of â¼30 days), and shows no huge jumps around the jet ejections," they write.

But XTE J1859 did show a tantalizing hint of enhancement. "There is a hint of â¼20% enhancement in M just before the jet productions," the authors write.

But that's just a hint, and better observations are needed to support it. "Such fine structures in the time evolution of M will be examined by the better data via the âjet forecastâ in the future," the researchers write.

Another notable finding concerns the Eddington limit. It's a theoretical threshold balanced between the inward force of gravity and the outward force of radiation. In two of the four systems, jets formed below this threshold. Some models of jet ejection say that super-Eddington accretion beyond this limit is necessary to launch jets, but these results say otherwise.

Those observation would hopefully reveal some more of the extreme physics that dominate and define black holes.

"When both Rin and rms continuously decrease and when Rin is about to reach the ISCO, alerts can be circulated as a âjet forecastâ for triggering ToO (Target of Opportunity) observations," the authors write.

The Earth's protective magnetic shield deflects most of them, but some get through and trigger showers of secondary particles. Convincing evidence is thin, but some researchers still consider them a factor in the course of life on Earth.
