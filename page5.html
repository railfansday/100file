Planes, Trains And Automobiles' Deleted Ending Was Rightfully Cut

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Planes, Trains and Automobiles was originally supposed to have a completely different ending, but thankfully it was removed before release. Few comedy movies have endings quite as emotionally satisfying as Planes, Trains and Automobiles. John Hughes' 1987 road-trip comedy spends most of its running time putting advertising executive Neal Page (Steve Martin) through an increasingly ridiculous series of travel disasters, largely thanks to his unwanted traveling companion, Del Griffith (John Candy). However, beneath the hilarious arguments, unfortunate misunderstandings and spectacular misfortunes is a surprisingly moving story about loneliness, friendship and the importance of appreciating other people.

Planes, Trains and Automobiles’ final moments bring that story together beautifully. After finally reaching Chicago, Neal initially leaves Del behind, only to realize that his traveling companion has nowhere to go. He returns to the train station, discovers the truth about Del's circumstances, and invites him home for Thanksgiving. It's a wonderfully understated conclusion that allows both characters to experience a genuine emotional breakthrough.

However, this wasn't always how Hughes intended to finish the movie. The original Planes, Trains and Automobiles screenplay featured a considerably longer ending, including a more explicit explanation of Del's tragic circumstances and an extended Thanksgiving dinner with Neal's family. Although the additional material offered some interesting character details, Hughes ultimately made the right decision by cutting it.

In the original Planes, Trains and Automobiles screenplay, Neal initially leaves Del behind after they finally reach Chicago. When he boards the train home, Del somehow turns up at his destination, prompting Neal to question why he is still following him. Del eventually explains that his wife, Marie, died eight years earlier and that he has been living out of his trunk, moving between motels and occasionally attaching himself to strangers for companionship (via Den of Geek).

The screenplay included a much longer explanation of Del's circumstances, detailing his wife's illness, their inability to have children, and his increasing difficulty coping with life alone. He also explains that Thanksgiving is particularly painful because it reminds him of the family he never had. Rather than Neal figuring out the truth, this version has Del explicitly explain his loneliness and why he attaches himself to other people.

The original ending then continued to Neal's family home, where Del joined the Thanksgiving celebrations. The screenplay included an extended dinner sequence, with Del expressing his gratitude for being welcomed into a family gathering. Audiences at test screenings reportedly did not respond well to the ending, finding Del’s monolog frustrating and unintentionally funny, prompting the change.

The change to the Planes, Trains and Automobiles ending gives Del considerably more dignity. Throughout Planes, Trains and Automobiles, his relentless friendliness is frequently presented as a source of frustration for Neal. He's loud, intrusive, and seemingly incapable of recognizing when somebody wants to be left alone. However, the final revelation reframes his behavior without suggesting that his personality is simply a disguise for his loneliness.

In the original screenplay, Del's explanation risks making his invitation to Thanksgiving feel like an act of charity. Neal discovers the truth because Del effectively has to ask for help, and their reconciliation becomes dependent on an extended confession. Although the material provides valuable background, it also removes some of the emotional complexity from their relationship. Del becomes somebody to pity rather than somebody Neal has genuinely learned to appreciate.

The theatrical version is much more thoughtful. Neal doesn't need Del to explain his situation because he has finally started paying attention to him. Audiences don't need to see Del sitting around the table, meeting every member of Neal's family or explaining how grateful he is to have somewhere to spend the holiday. The simple image of the two men walking together with Del's enormous trunk before settling on Del's earnest smile communicates everything necessary,

Planes, Trains and Automobiles is at its best when its comedy and sentiment exist alongside one another rather than competing for attention. The theatrical ending preserves that balance, allowing the audience to experience the same realization as Neal. Hughes understood that the most affecting way to conclude Del and Neal's journey throughout Planes, Trains and Automobiles wasn't to explain their newfound friendship, but to show that it had finally happened.

The Formula 1 Las Vegas Grand Prix is one of the most glamorous weekends in sports. With drivers’ global followings and a long list of A-listers in attendance, the event can feel as much like a fashion show as a race.

But while many guests were focused on the designer labels, Kristin Juszczyk had another message in mind: the need for more research on women in sports.

Juszczyk is a self-taught designer whose work began as a hobby and has since made her a force in the industry. The wife of San Francisco 49ers player Kyle Juszczyk, her custom game-day outfits gained traction on social media, and she has gone on to dress high-profile names including Simone Biles, Taylor Swift, and Caitlin Clark. This year, she was also named to TIME’s 100 Most Influential People in Sports.

For the Las Vegas GP, Juszczyk partnered with Gatorade’s Body of Science, an initiative dedicated to advancing the research on women’s sports. One stat drives their research: 6 percent of sports science research focuses on women.

Only 6 percent of published research in sports and exercise journals has focused exclusively on women, per a study published in the Women in Sport and Physical Activity Journal—a staggering gap that has implications for everything from training to recovery, as previously reported by Women’s Health.

Tennis legend Venus Williams, a Gatorade athlete, wore a custom look Juszczyk designed for the paddock red carpet inspired by this staggering statistic.

“Venus is one of the most iconic athletes of our generation, and to have her being in one of my designs, is surreal,” Juszczyk exclusively tells Women’s Health.

When it came to building the design, Juszczyk says she first took inspiration from motorsport fashion while also channeling the ambience a city like Las Vegas brings to the race. She opted to use leather as the base material, feeling like it channeled the energy of the sport. From there, she designed a trench coat with a layering corset piece over it, an homage to the city’s glitz and glamour.

Designing a trench coat was a first for Juszczyk, but the corset design is what she describes as her personal “bread and butter.” True to the Gatorade brand, the trench featured piping in its iconic orange, offset with black.

That 6 percent statistic is also front and center, ensuring the message of the design is clear: There needs to be more research dedicated toward women’s sports.

Juszczyk wanted to stay true to Venus and her personal style, and did a lot of research on what she wears on the regular. She says she approaches all of her designs with intentionality, ensuring the client is wearing something they feel good in.

“I want it to feel like my design, but I still want it to be something they feel beautiful and confident in,” she says.

The look was on full display when Venus presented the winner of the F1 Academy race, Alisha Palmowski, with her trophy. The moment was symbolic: a titan of women’s sports celebrating the new generation, while calling attention to a research gap that directly affects them.

“If anyone can take anything away from it, it’s that women need as much research in sports as men do,” she says. “The world is doing so much in pushing women athletes forward, and science should back it as well.”

Private equity deals in sports doubled in the past two years—investors see it as an ‘institutional-grade asset class,’ Apollo’s Torsten Slok says

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Private equity is coming for America’s favorite pastime, America’s game and almost every other sport. Private equity firms are buying into sports at twice the pace they were a few years ago, according to Apollo Global Management chief economist Torsten Slok.

“Private equity deals in global sports have roughly doubled from about 100 a year in 2021-2023 to almost 200 in both 2024 and 2025, a sign that investors increasingly see sports as an institutional-grade asset class,” Slok wrote in a Nov. 22 analysis.

The investment boom isn’t just limited to stakes in pro teams, however. Private equity firms also target the businesses that supply, support and monetize sports, from equipment manufacturers to talent agencies and training facilities.

On Nov. 19, Swiss investment firm Partners Group agreed to acquire a majority stake in Rawlings Sporting Goods—the manufacturer of baseballs, gloves, bats and protective equipment for the MLB. The company serves as the league’s official supplier of game balls, gloves, helmets, face guards and bases. The league itself owned a stake in the company as well, meaning unlike the minority stakes leagues allow in their teams, the deal gives a private equity firm control.

According to Partners Group, MLB and Rawlings’ existing shareholders will retain meaningful ownership while also giving the Swiss firm exposure to Rawlings’ broader business—which includes brands like Easton and Combat, as well as D-BAT Academies, a network of 228 indoor baseball and softball training facilities.

“Rawlings’ iconic brands and training footprint helps reinforce participation in America’s pastime and has credibility with consumers and athletes across all ages,” Managing Director of Private Equity Goods & Products at Partners Group Thomas Clark said in a press release. “We look forward to working with Ron, the Rawlings team, and MLB as long-term partners.”

Apollo has its own sports arm as well: In August, Apollo Sports Capital entered as a “strategic investment partner” to the MLB’s most iconic franchise—the New York Yankees. The deal was a $2.6 billion financing agreement with Yankee Global Enterprises, the team’s holding company, supporting the “continued growth” of the Yankees franchise while also refinancing its existing debt.

“Teams and leagues might embrace capital for any number of reasons: to support operating costs, update aging facilities or develop new venues, invest in new technologies, or grow their global reach,” Deloitte’s 2026 Global Sports Industry Outlook report read. “But outside investment brings greater scrutiny, new challenges, and rising expectations around transparency, governance, and financial rigor.”

For decades, professional sports restricted private equity firms and other institutional investors from owning stakes in their teams. Instead, franchises were controlled by wealthy individuals, families or ownership groups—mainly from the leagues’ concern about preserving control over teams and maintaining structure.

That began changing in 2019, however, when Major League Baseball became the first major U.S. professional sports league to permit private equity investments in franchises. The NBA and Major League Soccer followed in 2020 and the NHL lifted restrictions in 2021. The NFL became the last of the four major American sports leagues to approve private equity ownership in 2024.

But those permissions came with limits. According to Sportico, MLB only allowed institutional investors to own up to 30% of a franchise, with a single fund capped at 15%. The NBA and NHL permit up to 30% institutional ownership per team as well, but cap individual funds at 20%. The NFL has the strictest ownership rules, only allowing approved private equity firms to own a combined stake of up to 10% in a team—and only 10% in an individual fund as well.

And the owners have taken advantage of that influx of capital. Outside money helps owners pay for stadium construction, arena renovations, player contracts and the development of surrounding entertainment districts, all without having to foot the full bill themselves.

For example, the Buffalo Bills created an arrangement in 2024 following the NFL’s lifted restrictions to sell 21% of the team’s stake to a group of limited partners—which included a 10% investment from private equity firm Arctos Partners. The transaction came as the Bills were building their new stadium, when projected costs rose from $1.4 billion to $2.1 billion.

But the benefit for private equity owners exists as well. Professional sports teams have access to multiple revenue streams, including broadcasting rights, ticket sales, sponsorships, merchandise, licensing and stadium-related businesses. The five major U.S. professional sports leagues generated approximately $60 billion in combined revenue in 2025, according to the Congress. The estimated combined value of their franchises reached approximately $560 billion that year.

“The new model of baseball—or basketball, football—is teams becoming media companies and transcending a local marketplace,” founder and chief investment officer of Harbinger Sports Partners Rashaun Williams, which had invested a minority stake in the newly relocated Las Vegas Athletics MLB team, previously told Fortune. “They’re finding ways to diversify their revenue and monetize that fan base 365 days a year.”

How brewers can protect beers from smoke-tainted hops

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Study: Brewers can still use up to 50% of smoke-tainted hops without affecting beer taste and aroma.

The 2020 Oregon wildfires burned over 1 million acres, destroyed thousands of structures, and killed 11 people. Smoke from the fires spread across the entire region; this “smoke taint” adversely affected not only local wine production, but also hops grown for beer brewing. Since the Pacific Northwest produces more than 95 percent of US hops and about 40 percent of the global supply, the potential economic impact is significant.

That’s why Oregon State University (OSU) scientists have been studying how brewers could offset the negative impacts of smoke taint while still incorporating affected hops in their brews. Most recently, they found that it all depends on how much tainted hops are included and when hops are added during the brewing process, according to a new paper published in the Journal of the American Society of Brewing Chemists.

As previously reported, the chemistry of brewing beer is a very active area of research. For instance, in 2018, OSU chemist Thomas Shellhammer—who also co-authored this latest paper—identified the growing popularity of “dry hopping” (adding hops after fermentation, common for pale ales) among craft brewers as the likely culprit of the exploding beer bottle phenomenon.

All beer contains hops, a key flavoring agent that also imparts useful antimicrobial properties. (Beer spoils quickly without them.) Brewers mash and steep grain in hot water, which converts all that starch into sugars. This is traditionally the stage where hops are added to the liquid extract (wort) and boiled to give the beer that hint of bitterness. During the boiling process, a portion of the resins (alpha acids) in hops isomerize into iso-alpha acids. That chemical rearrangement of the molecules is what produces bitterness. Yeast is then added to trigger fermentation, turning the sugars into alcohol.

Far from being the chemically inert ingredient brewers have long assumed, hops contain starch enzymes that, under certain conditions, can boost their activity to such a great extent that excessive re-fermentation occurs (“hop-creep”) after bottling. Dry-hopped beers produce significantly higher CO2 levels and packaging pressures, because there is much more re-fermentation going on than usual. And sometimes that pressure builds until the bottle explodes.

In the wake of the 2020 wildfires, Shellhammer turned his attention to smoke-tainted hops. The primary culprit is volatile phenols (VP), which are produced when the lignin in plant cells is exposed to the high heat of a wildfire. Earlier this year, the OSU team discovered that smoke exposure while the hops are drying increased VP uptake and substantially altered the hops’ aroma; those with higher levels took on meaty, smoky, and artificial BBQ notes; medicinal flavors have also been reported. Those notes were so strong that they overpowered more desirable beery qualities, like citrus notes or aroma intensity.

For this latest project, Shellhammer worked with graduate student Cade Jobe to harvest Idaho-grown Cascade hops. Then they “smoked” the hops in kilns using wood pellets injected into the air intake systems, then dried them. They also harvested and dried non-smoked hops as a control.

Credit: Ellie Lafferty/Oregon State University Credit: Ellie Lafferty/Oregon State University

Next, Shellhammer and Jobe brewed several batches of beer, blending smoke-tainted hops with untainted hops at different levels (25,  50, and 75 percent), along with beer brewed using only tainted hops and beer brewed with no tainted hops. They did this using both a dry-hopping process and one where they added hops during wort boiling. They measured the VP concentrations of each batch of beer with gas chromatography. Finally, a panel of 24 blind tasters evaluated each beer.

Shellhammer and Jobe found that dry-hopping with smoke-tainted hops resulted in increased VP concentrations; the more tainted hops were included, the higher those concentrations. The tasters reported more intense smoky aromas in those beers as well as meaty, smoky, medicinal, and artificial BBQ flavors.

By contrast, when tainted hops were added during the boil, the resulting beer was pretty much indistinguishable from beers brewed with no smoke-tainted hops, even though the former still showed the same increases in VP concentrations. There was no discernible difference when hops were added during the wort boiling stage.

The authors suggest brewers could blend smoke-tainted hops with untainted batches when dry-hopping, keeping the smoke-tainted hops to less than 50 percent. This would still allow brewers to use tainted batches of hops, reducing the economic impact of wildfires on beer production.

“Hop growers and brewers are looking for guidance on whether and how to use smoke-affected hops,” said Jobe. “This research begins to provide some practical information to help hop growers and brewers understand how smoky hops impact beer so that they can make the best decisions.”

There’s still more research to be done to confirm the precise ratios and to replicate these initial findings. Future research should also explore the impact of hops exposed to smoke under field conditions, when the plant is still metabolically active, along with consumer studies to evaluate whether beer drinkers more generally will find the flavors of beers made with blended hops (tainted and untainted) acceptable. The findings may not extend to wine production, however, since the fermentation processes are so different, per the authors. And grapes comprise almost all the raw materials in wine, while hops are a smaller fraction of beer brewing ingredients.

This Classic Italian Sandwich Is Extra Crispy & Delicious

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Everybody celebrates Italy for pizza, pasta, and gelato, and while they're classics for a reason, we implore you to explore Italy's unrivaled gastronomic tapestry a little deeper. Trust us, there are some serious gems hidden just beneath the surface. One of our favorite Italian street foods that you need to try is porchetta, Italy's answer to crispy pork belly. Irresistibly crunchy and bursting with indulgent flavors, it makes for a seriously delicious sandwich filler.

Porchetta is a style of pork roast, but is completely unique in its preparation. It's typically made from a whole deboned pig or a large cut of pork belly, which is laid flat and heavily rubbed on the inside with salt, garlic, and black pepper, alongside quintessential Italian seasonings like rosemary, sage, and fennel seeds. The pork is then rolled back together and slow-roasted for several hours, allowing the interior to turn soft and tender while the skin develops a beautiful crackling.

Porchetta is a meal in itself, but goes even better in a sandwich. Start out with a thick Italian white bread, something that won't turn soggy when paired with the pork's rich meat juices and sauces — ciabatta or focaccia work perfectly here. To balance the richness, add something bright with a little zing. We think salsa verde is an excellent choice, since the lemon juice and vinegar bring a sharp acidity, while fresh parsley keeps the heavy roast from feeling cloying — it pairs perfectly with a Cuban interpretation of the recipe as well. Rather than piling on thick slabs of porchetta, slice it thinly, so it stays tender and folds nearly into the bread, and make sure every sandwich gets a few pieces of the crackling skin for a satisfying crunch. As for what else to put on the sandwich, draw inspiration from Bobby Flay's favorite Roman porchetta sandwich.

You should have no problem finding pre-rolled porchetta at the butcher, but why not save your money and make it at home instead? We promise it's not as intimidating as it looks. For the juiciest cut that's also easiest to roll and inexpensive, pick up a skin-on pork belly. Its exceptionally high fat content keeps the roast moist and makes it incredibly forgiving to work with, so even the least confident cooks won't have to worry about overcooking it.

Porchetta is the star of the sandwich, and it's the crackling skin that really steals the show, so getting it earth-shatteringly crispy is essential. Here, the secret to a crunchy crust is dryness, so before you get to work on seasoning, leave the pork uncovered in the fridge overnight to let the surface dry out. Many cooks also salt the meat generously before its rest, which draws out even more moisture. And before roasting, it's good practice to pat the skin completely dry, since any lingering moisture turns to steam and keeps the skin from crisping.

To keep the sandwich from turning into a soggy mess, it's essential to let your porchetta rest for around 15 to 30 minutes after roasting. It might be tempting to carve it right away and admire your hard work, but slicing too soon sends the flavorful juices flooding into the chopping board and then straight into the bread, which even the sturdiest ciabatta can't hold back. The logic is the same as with steak because resting gives the juices time to redistribute through the muscle, leaving the meat deliciously tender and flavorful.

Jumping across multiple tools and screens to complete one workflow is a clear sign that people are just becoming workers for the tools rather than using those tools to deliver their work better and faster. This is more relevant in the AI era. - Raja Shanmugam, TATA Consultancy Services

Leaders should investigate where employees bypass technology and address the underlying friction before it creates operational risks. - Judit Sharon, OnPage Corporation

If a sales or marketing team has to wait for an overnight update before responding to a customer or adjusting a campaign, the technology is setting the pace instead of supporting the work. Teams should be able to act on what is happening now, not yesterday’s picture. - Vibhor Kapoor, AdRoll

Stephen King’s name is synonymous with horror, but he’s written in multiple genres over the decades. From sci-fi thrillers like The Dead Zone to fantasy series like The Dark Tower, King's books are incredibly varied. While it might seem like every TV adaptation of King’s novels involves horror (to be fair, most do), there have actually been some adaptations of his non-horror works over the years. However, as with most Stephen King adaptations, the quality varies across the board.

Some of these non-horror adaptations are mostly faithful to the source material, while others are so different from the original work, they have almost nothing in common. A few are well-written, incredibly compelling, and look fantastic. Then, there are the others that might be entertaining, but suffer from writing and pacing issues. A few Stephen King non-horror works still haven’t been adapted or used to their full potential, so while this handful of shows is all that’s available now, hopefully, more adaptations will be made in the future.

Golden Years is likely a show few remember, as it aired only seven episodes on CBS in 1991. For those wondering when they missed the Stephen King novel of the same name — there wasn’t one. This adaptation actually came after King had written down the idea for it in a notebook, where it sat for years. He eventually decided to write it as a “novel for television,” according to an interview in The New York Times, which resulted in this miniseries.

While Golden Years was designed as a lead-in to a regular series, CBS opted not to pick it up. As a result, the TV show ended on a cliffhanger (though the home video version changed a few minutes of the last episode to give it a more hopeful ending). The series followed Harlan Williams, an elderly janitor who is exposed to chemicals after an explosion in a secret lab.

As a result, he begins aging in reverse, which gets the attention of those in charge of the lab. Soon, Harlan and his wife have to go on the run, where they meet Terry Spann, who has decided to help protect them. Of course, Harlan’s sudden youthful tendencies aren’t the only powers to develop; as time goes on, other strange things begin to happen. Starring Keith Szarabajka, Frances Sternhagen, R.D. Call, and Felicity Huffman, Golden Years is a decent little sci-fi thriller that mostly suffers from pacing issues and underdeveloped plot elements (and a lack of conclusion).

The second-longest-running Stephen King adaptation, Haven, is based on King’s The Colorado Kid only in the loosest sense. While the novel is a crime mystery involving the mysterious death of a stranger and a pair of older journalists sharing his tale with a newer, much younger journalist, Haven basically borrows only a few names, the location, and minor elements from the novel. The show still involves a dead man nicknamed The Colorado Kid, but it’s more of a procedural with supernatural elements.

Some Stephen King TV shows have fallen under the radar, among them a five-season sci-fi thriller that all fans of the King of Horror should watch.

When FBI Agent Audrey Parker arrives in the small town of Haven on a routine case, she soon discovers more is going on beneath its surface. The people of the town are afflicted with what they call The Troubles. These supernatural afflictions range from having what one draws come true to being a human bullet magnet. She soon quits the FBI and joins the local police, helping those who are Troubled, while also trying to figure out the mystery of her past.

It’s kind of like The X-Files but with a lower budget and less charm, and eventually the lore gets amazingly convoluted. However, Emily Rose as Park is compelling, while Eric Balfour as Duke Crocker is a blast. Under the Dome is another adaptation that varies considerably from its source material. However, in this case, Stephen King took to his website during its first season to reassure fans upset about the differences and to say he wholeheartedly approved of the changes thus far. He also pointed out that the novel covers eight days, while the TV show covers a far longer period, so some changes were necessary. Those changes worked, too, as the first season of the series was intriguing.

The series follows the town of Chester’s Mill, where, as one might guess, an indestructible, transparent dome appears one day, cutting the town off from the outside world. However, fans and critics both soured on the series when it returned for its second and third season. Unfortunately, the idea of a town stuck under a dome, told over multiple seasons, was spreading the premise a bit too thin. Under the Dome was reasonably decent as standard sci-fi fare, but it also leaned into its ridiculous elements a bit too much. Add in characters making increasingly irrational decisions, and things went far off course.

The longest-running King adaptation was The Dead Zone, which ran for six seasons. A seventh season was expected, but unfortunately, the series was canceled, partially due to financial concerns. That left the series hanging on a cliffhanger that answered some questions but raised many more. While it doesn’t have a Rotten Tomatoes score, it does carry a 7.3 on IMDb.

On Wednesday (Nov. 25), the distant ice giant Uranus will be at its best and brightest. Here's when, where and how to look for the seventh planet.

One of the hardest planets to see in the solar system — the distant ice giant Uranus — reaches its annual opposition on Wednesday (Nov. 25), just a day after the Beaver Supermoon. It's the best time to see the seventh planet from the sun, but you'll need to use stargazing binoculars or a good backyard telescope to find it.

Uranus is faint, slow-moving and rarely a target for casual stargazers. However, on Nov. 25, and, for a few weeks on either side, it will be at its closest point to Earth this year and will appear at its brightest, making it possible to spot.

Opposition occurs when Earth passes between Uranus and the sun; Uranus will be "opposite" the sun, hence, "opposition." The moment of opposition will happen at 6 p.m. ET on Nov. 25, according to EarthSky. Consequently, Uranus will rise in the east around sunset, reach its highest point in the sky around midnight and set in the west around sunrise.

You can find it in the constellation Taurus, close to the Pleiades open star cluster, also known as the Seven Sisters and M45. Uranus will be a few degrees southeast of the cluster, appearing as a faint point of light, about two-fifths of the distance between the Pleiades and Aldebaran, the brightest star in Taurus.

At magnitude +5.6, Uranus is technically visible to the naked eye under very dark skies. However, binoculars or a telescope will make it much easier to find.

Uranus as seen by NASA's Hubble Space Telescope.

At opposition, Uranus will be 18.4 astronomical units (AU) from Earth — about 1.7 billion miles (2.7 billion kilometers) away. Through a telescope, it will appear as a tiny blue-green disk. Its color comes from methane in its atmosphere, which absorbs red wavelengths of sunlight.

Named after Ouranos, the ancient Greek god of the sky and the heavens, according to the Planetary Society , Uranus is one of the least understood planets in the solar system. A day on Uranus lasts for about 17 hours, and the planet rotates sideways, according to NASA. It takes 84 Earth years to orbit the sun.

Uranus also has 13 faint rings and at least 29 known moons, the most recent of which was found in 2025 by the James Webb Space Telescope. It was named Uranus XXVIII and orbits between the moons Ophelia and Bianca. Many of Uranus' moons are named after characters from the works of William Shakespeare and Alexander Pope.

The distant planet has been visited only once, by NASA's Voyager 2 spacecraft on Jan. 24, 1986. However, contrary to what the probe found, scientists now think the planet emits its own internal heat. That could increase pressure on NASA to prepare a Uranus Orbiter and Probe (UOP), a mission concept the National Academy of Sciences recommended in 2022 as a high-priority planetary science mission for the next decade.

Uranus will next come to opposition on Nov. 30, 2027.

How to Deal With Holiday Arguments and Guilt Trips

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

After years or decades, problems and patterns can become deeply entrenched. Be a fly on the wall during a sample couples therapy session and witness the slow, deliberate work of changing those patterns for a more enduring love.

Self Tests are all about you. Are you outgoing or introverted? Are you a narcissist? Does perfectionism hold you back? Find out the answers to these questions and more with Psychology Today.

Posted November 24, 2026 | Reviewed by Michelle Quirk

Ideally, the holidays are a time of happiness, cheer, and positive relationship interactions. In practice, however, we often need to manage some bad with the good. There are usually a few “bones to pick” over the Thanksgiving turkey and “elephants in the room” along with the Christmas tree…

So, to maximize your holiday joy (and minimize your angst), I have compiled some strategies to deal with dysfunctional games, family patterns, arguments, and emotional manipulation. By the end, you should have a good idea why Aunt Ruth keeps giving you a guilt trip, how Uncle Stu keeps making you lose your cool, and what exactly you can do about all of it. Then, maybe, you can enjoy yourself, too!

To start, according to Eric Berne in his book Games People Play (1964), people generally try to manipulate us because they want something from us—but they don’t believe they can get it honestly or fairly. These negative beliefs (called existential positions) come in two forms. On one hand, they can believe that you’re not OK and try to bully you because they think you don’t deserve their fair treatment. On the other hand, they can believe that they’re not OK (i.e., I’m not okay) and try to guilt trip you into feeling that you owe them something for nothing.

Thus, people play games, argue, and manipulate when they believe they are not OK, other people are not OK, or both. This allows them to get what they want without giving back or risking being fair and trusting. Such manipulations can have secondary benefits for them, too, like being excused from standards or responsibilities (I'm not OK), or feeling morally superior (You're not OK). Unfortunately, those bad beliefs and manipulative games also cause many of our hurt feelings and dysfunctional relationship patterns in the process as well.

How these game-playing relatives and friends push your emotional buttons was explored by Niven, Totterdell, and Holman (2009) and defined as negative affect regulation strategies. Put simply, they use emotional manipulation tactics to make you feel bad, so you give them what they want. There are four general tactics they can employ to emotionally manipulate, but they all share one common theme—they reject you and hurt your feelings, while also putting their feelings first.

Beyond that, they might choose to emotionally bully you by being disrespectful and insulting or bragging and trying to make you feel bad by comparison. This would fall under the “You’re not OK” belief and allow them to feel superior at your expense. In contrast, they might decide to guilt-trip you by criticizing how you treat them or withdrawing until you feel bad and comply with their wishes. This would be part of an “I’m not OK” belief and allow them to be excused from standards and responsibilities—like doing something worthwhile for you back. In either case, unless you know how to defend yourself, you might be left trying to choke down negative emotions, while they munch on dessert!

Fortunately, there are things that you can do to defend yourself—and possibly even improve your relationship with that unruly relative, partner, or friend, too. To begin, though, you need to decide whether you want to deal with the manipulation indirectly by diverting attention and emotion away from the problem, or directly by engaging attention and emotion toward addressing the problem. In other words, can you make light of Cousin Bessie’s snotty comments and change the subject, or do you need to talk it out and come to a better arrangement?

To help you decide, consider some indirect and direct softening techniques suggested by Overall, Simpson, and Struthers (2013). They were initially devised for addressing problems with emotionally avoidant or argumentative romantic partners—and serve as a foundation for dealing with relationship problems in my book Attraction Psychology (Nicholson, 2022). Beyond that, I have found them helpful with family and friends as well, especially when they believe one of us is not “OK” and are emotionally hurtful about it.

Dealing with issues indirectly requires that you “soften” by temporarily downplaying the severity of the problem, until their bullying or guilt-tripping lessens. To do so, it helps to build positive rapport by communicating your care, acceptance, and regard—while also minimizing their emotional manipulation attempts with positive feelings and humor (i.e., kill them with kindness). From there, you can redirect them toward other positive aspects about your relationship with them and points of agreement. Essentially, this is a lot like dealing with a toddler having a tantrum—make light of the outburst and find a more pleasant topic to change their mind and improve their behavior.

Dealing with issues directly, however, requires that you “soften” by acknowledging some aspect of their point of view as a base of agreement—without surrendering to the negative emotions from their bullying or guilt trip. Pretend you’re a cool TV lawyer who says, “We can agree that there is a problem here, but it would help us both to keep working on a better solution.” From there, you can show optimism about solving the disagreement and having a good relationship with them. Where you are able, also acknowledge some of their positive efforts in the past toward connecting and compromising with you. This can help improve their emotional outlook and motivate them to find healthier ways to interact with you in the future as well.

Whatever strategy you choose, I hope you have Happy Holidays and a Good New Year, too!

Everything but the Plane: Here’s How One Brand Is Bringing Glamour Back to Travel

PS’s private terminals are the epitome of real luxury.

Imagine a trip to the airport with no lines. No lines to check in, to drop your bag, to be told your first-class ticket is the wrong first-class ticket for the first-class lounge. Now, add to that no interpersonal friction. There are no snappy desk workers, no turbo TSA employees, and no graveyard shift customs agents that give you grief.

Airlines can only solve so many issues. These are terminal problems. PS, a Los Angeles-based company operating its own luxury private terminals, is solving them. The goal is to get you as close to the charter experience as possible, all while still flying commercial. If you ask around or sit in the common areas and eavesdrop long enough, you’ll likely hear something along the lines of “I usually fly private, but when I don’t, this is what I use.”

The company has locations in Atlanta, Dallas-Fort Worth, Miami, and Los Angeles, and the next order of business is expansion to the airports its clients use most—business hubs, mostly. But it started at LAX in 2017, and that’s the perfect airport for something like this. The horseshoe design and the traffic it creates, the small terminals, and the paparazzi culture do not cater to the A-list and executive frequent fliers that use it as a home airport. PS offers a way around that with on-tarmac connection from your flight to its terminal or vice versa. A chauffeur pulls you straight off the jet bridge and into a luxury fleet vehicle; that partnership is currently with BMW. If you don’t have any checked luggage, that 7 Series can (for an additional price) drive you straight to your destination within 35 miles of the airport. It ain’t cheap: $1,300 per head one-way for the Salon (a shared bar area), $4,950 for a private suite (essentially an airport hotel room with butler and spa service, this price is all-inclusive), and $1,650 for that PS Direct drive-you-straight-home service. There’s the jet-set marketing—tagline: The “back door” to the airport—and meant-for-the-timeline branded cocktail napkins: “You’ve arrived.”

But once you accept that PS could not afford to excuse itself from the flex-driven airport exclusivity economy, you can really admire its hospitality-inspired finishes. Its raison d’être is to get you through the airport with as little friction as possible, but it stands out in its commitment to hospitality within those confines.

For example, interior design and artwork are curated. The centerpiece artworks of each location are done in collaboration with local artists, made to fit the location they are in. The crown jewel thus far is in Miami, where PS took over the old Pan Am headquarters and flight training academy. The midcentury building is a Miami landmark, with geometric thermal walls acting as the façades and this pinkish sandy and limestone-heavy concrete throughout. Argentinian-born ceramicist Nina Surel matched this with tilework water features and a ceramic mural at the center of the property. Design like this, small and mostly unnoticed in the grand scheme of things, is what turns your mind back to a golden age of travel. It’s not credit card points and moneymaking schemes—though actually, you can buy any of the non-centerpiece artworks—just beauty for the sake of beauty.

On top of that, the food and beverage program is considered and regional. The blue corn cornbread with honey and a ricotta spread that chef Kelsey Kirwin (chefs’ names are on the menus, an indication of the atmosphere) is making at the DFW location overtook my mother’s as my all-time favorite. I would consider the signature PS martini—with (guess what) caviar-stuffed olives—worthy of a place on any hot big-city cocktail menu. Servers don’t hover, they listen, and they’re backed by an extensive behind-the-scenes operation: Mention you prefer coffee, not bubbles, before a morning flight? For example, if you book a private suite, you can fly in, use it for a business meeting, and fly out. Or, your parents can accompany the family to departure, hang out in the suite, and squeeze every last second with the grandkids out of your visit. It is an airport experience actually designed around the ways we interact and what matters to us when we travel. That, more than the Champagne and caviar, is the definition of luxury.

UK warns AI-driven cyberattacks are exposing years of weak security

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The U.K. government’s cybersecurity unit says it has seen a “marked rise” in AI-driven cyberattacks in recent months, as hackers increasingly rely on autonomous hacking tools to identify and exploit simple weaknesses to break into systems and networks.

The U.K. National Cybersecurity Center (NCSC) said it has seen several cyber incidents since September involving autonomous hacking tools, such as penetration testing tools used by defenders to stress-test their cybersecurity defenses. These incidents have caused intrusions at organizations and resulted in downtime, the unit said in a blog post.

The NCSC says that while none of the vulnerabilities being exploited are new, the speed at which these AI tools are able to carry out simple hacks has changed.

The rise in AI-related cyberattacks coincides with the broader release earlier this year of open source autonomous penetration testing tools like Artex and Cairn, among others, which anyone can download and use at no cost. The U.S. Federal Bureau of Investigation recently warned that malicious hackers are using AI penetration testing tools to carry out cyberattacks at unprecedented speeds.

While cyberattacks and intrusions are on the rise, the message from London is that many of these attacks are preventable with basic cybersecurity defenses and best practices.

“Notably, the issues being exploited currently are not novel memory corruption vulnerabilities (or even known but unpatched vulnerabilities), but unmitigated weaknesses that are often the result of years of technical debt that organisations have accumulated,” the NCSC wrote.

The unit said that agentic hacking tools often look for exposed or internet-facing systems, then quickly run through lists of weak or easy-to-guess passwords or abuse API endpoints often used by developers to access data.

The NCSC said that AI “does not make established cyber security controls obsolete,” but rather that “building strong foundations has never mattered more.”

The unit added that AI has made it easier to find vulnerabilities and faster to exploit them, warning that weaknesses that have gone unnoticed for years can now be found in minutes.

Here's How Much Of A Flight Airline Pilots Actually Hand-Fly

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

guys_who_shoot | Shutterstock, Simple Flying

On a typical commercial flight, airline pilots only spend a small proportion of their time physically controlling the aircraft. Pilots normally hand-fly takeoff and part of the initial climb, while the autopilot handles most of the flight until approach. According to a 2026 report by the International Air Transport Association (IATA), commercial aircraft operate with automation engaged for approximately 90% of the time.

On a long-haul flight, a pilot may spend just a few minutes controlling a Boeing 787 or Airbus A350, although operating procedures and weather conditions can influence this substantially. Moreover, a pilot not physically moving the controls does not mean the aircraft is operating without human supervision. The crew remains responsible for managing the flight path, monitoring the automated systems, and taking over whenever necessary.

Airborne manual flying normally starts with takeoff, which remains a pilot-controlled maneuver on conventional commercial airplanes. Taxiing is generally manual as well, but is not included in the airborne flight time. The pilot maintains directional control, rotates, and establishes the initial climb. Although various systems can manage engine power, this should not be confused with the aircraft performing an automatic takeoff. The autopilot can only be engaged once the aircraft has reached its approved minimum engagement height.

After departure, the crew can generally engage the autopilot relatively early, sometimes only a few hundred feet above the ground. According to IATA's 2020 Aircraft Handling and Manual Flying Skills Report, many surveyed pilots reported engaging it around 400 or 500 feet above ground level, while others preferred to continue flying manually through 5,000 or even 10,000 feet. The exact altitude depends on the aircraft and airline.

Once engaged, the autopilot can handle most of the climb, cruise, and descent, following the route and altitude instructions managed by the crew. On arrival, the pilot may disconnect it early during a visual approach or keep it engaged until shortly before landing. The final flare and touchdown are normally performed manually, although suitably equipped aircraft can also carry out automatic landings under the appropriate conditions.

Estimates of how much time airline pilots spend hand-flying differ substantially, partly because there is no universal operating procedure. Back in 2011, airline captain Rory Kay, co-chair of a Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) advisory committee on pilot training, said that pilots were using automation "for all but approximately three minutes of a flight".

Another committee member, captain Bob Coffman, provided an even lower estimate for some regional airline operations. According to Coffman, pilots at commuter airlines might fly with the autopilot disengaged for approximately 80 seconds during a typical two-hour flight. Both estimates are historical rather than current industry averages.

Other pilots take a somewhat different approach. In an interview published by Johnny Jet, airline pilot Spencer Marker explained that most airline pilots spend ten minutes or less manually controlling their aircraft. However, he personally preferred to hand-fly departures until reaching 10,000 feet and disconnect the autopilot approximately five miles from the runway when weather and operating conditions allowed.

The length of the flight also makes a significant difference. Ten minutes represents almost 17% of a one-hour flight, but only 1.7% of a ten-hour service. On an ultra-long-haul operation, such as Qantas' nonstop Perth (PER) - London (LHR) route, the proportion can become even smaller.

From filling out paperwork to maintaining communication and constantly checking the instruments, the pilots are kept busy on the flight deck.

Although the autopilot physically controls the aircraft for much of a flight, it is not an independent system that simply receives a destination and makes every decision. Modern airliners use several connected systems, including the flight management system, flight director, autopilot, and automatic thrust controls. These each perform different functions, and the crew must ensure that the appropriate modes are selected and that the aircraft is following its intended flight path.

There is also an important difference between flying manually and flying without any assistance. A pilot can disconnect the autopilot while keeping the flight director engaged, which provides guidance on the primary flight display. Automatic thrust can also remain active while the pilot controls the aircraft's pitch and bank. Flying using basic instrument indications without flight director guidance, known as raw-data flying, demands additional proficiency.

During cruise, the pilots continue monitoring navigation, weather, fuel consumption, aircraft systems, and instructions from air traffic control. Changes to the assigned route or altitude must be correctly entered and verified. Automation also helps distribute workload between the pilot flying and pilot monitoring, especially during departures and approaches where both pilots may be managing several tasks simultaneously.

The time spent holding the controls therefore provides little indication of the crew's overall workload. Even with autopilot engaged, the pilots must decide whether the selected automation remains appropriate.

The pilot landing an ultra-long-haul A350 needs two consecutive hours of rest in the second half of the flight.

Airlines can take remarkably different approaches to manual flying. Some encourage pilots to use the highest available level of automation throughout most of the flight, while others explicitly allow manual flying whenever weather, air traffic, and cockpit workload permit. Manual flying opportunities therefore depend heavily on each airline's standard operating procedures (SOP).

IATA's 2020 survey, which received 5,650 responses overall, found that only 36% of the 5,241 pilots answering the relevant question said their airline allowed and supported manual flying without limitations. A separate question attracted 3,452 responses, of which approximately 37% admitted hesitating to disengage the autopilot and take manual control. Some respondents also feared criticism for hand-flying even where procedures permitted it.

Complex departures, congested airspace, and reduced visibility can increase workload, making automation the safer option. Some approaches and airspace procedures also require automation for precise navigation. An airline therefore needs to balance manual flying opportunities against the operational conditions of each individual flight.

IATA's survey also found differences during landing, with many pilots disconnecting the autopilot around 1,000 feet above the runway when conditions permitted. Others did so earlier or at the minimum altitude allowed by the relevant procedure. An approach flown with the autopilot engaged is not automatically an autoland; in most cases, pilots still take control before touchdown.

Modern airliners are incredibly automated, but pilots remain fully responsible for every decision during a flight. What do they actually do?

One particularly interesting difference concerns the type of flying performed by the crew. Short-haul pilots might operate several sectors on the same day, giving them multiple opportunities to practice takeoffs and landings. Long-haul pilots, on the other hand, can spend most of a working day on one flight, and augmented crews may share the workload during particularly long services. Consequently, each pilot may perform far fewer takeoffs and landings.

A 2017 study surveyed more than 1,500 commercial pilots about manual flying and airline procedures. Only 35.5% of long-haul pilots agreed that they had sufficient opportunities to hand-fly, compared with 79.9% of pilots operating short- and medium-haul flights. Pilots flying aircraft such as the Boeing 777, Airbus A380, and Airbus A330/A340 were among those reporting the fewest opportunities.

The same study also identified a clear difference between airline cultures. Among pilots working in supportive procedural environments, 68% felt they had sufficient opportunities to maintain their manual flying skills during normal operations. That figure fell to just 26% among pilots whose airlines imposed more restrictive procedures.

The difference is also not simply explainable by aircraft age or technology. United Airlines continues operating its Boeing 767 fleet even as newer widebodies enter service, and both generations rely extensively on automation. Accumulating flying hours on long sectors does not necessarily mean practicing basic aircraft handling.

While most landings follow a stable approach, almost all unstable approaches are followed by landing.

Concerns about reduced manual flying opportunities have been raised by aviation regulators for well over a decade. The Associated Press reported in 2011 that a draft FAA study had examined 46 accidents and major incidents, alongside hundreds of voluntary reports and observations from more than 9,000 flights. It found problems involving manual aircraft handling or automated flight controls in more than 60% of the accidents and 30% of the major incidents reviewed. These were findings from a selected sample, rather than percentages applying to all airline accidents.

The FAA subsequently issued Safety Alert for Operators 13002 in 2013, encouraging airlines to provide suitable opportunities for manual flying. A further safety alert in 2017 called for manual flying proficiency to be reinforced through both training and normal line operations. It recommended manual instrument departures and arrivals, upset recovery, and training across different automation modes.

The issue has not disappeared. In its 2026 flight path management report, IATA identified manual handling as an important safety concern and called for training and airline policies that develop both manual and automated flight path management skills. Full-flight simulators can reproduce unreliable airspeed, unusual attitudes, and other situations unsuitable for practice on commercial flights.

Research found that nearly 80% of surveyed pilots disagreed that simulator training alone was sufficient to maintain their manual flying skills. The aim is not to minimize autopilot use, but to preserve safe opportunities for pilots to control their aircraft in normal operations.

The US Air Force Bolted Jet Engines Onto Its Largest-Ever Bomber, But It Still Wasn't Enough To Save It

The Convair B-36 Peacemaker strategic bomber is one of the most ambitious bombers ever built. While the unusual pusher configuration of its six Pratt & Whitney R-4360 Wasp Major piston engines is perhaps its most distinctive feature, later variants also carried four General Electric J47 turbojets. These jets were added to improve take-off performance, climb rate, and high-speed dash capability, helping keep the enormous bomber operationally relevant.

This remained the case until newer all-jet bombers, particularly the B-52 Stratofortress, assumed the primary nuclear deterrence role. The United States Air Force procured 384 B-36s, each capable of carrying up to 86,000 lb (39,000 kg) of bombs. Although none remain airworthy today, four complete aircraft have been preserved in museums, where they provide a reminder of the largest piston-engined combat aircraft ever placed into operational service.

The Convair B-36 Peacemaker was the largest bomber built by the US, and it can be thought of as the most important transitional bomber ever built. The US Air Force Museum states that "responding to the U.S. Army Air Forces' requirement for a strategic bomber with intercontinental range, Consolidated Vultee (later Convair) designed the B-36 during World War II. The airplane made its maiden flight in August 1946, and in June 1948 the Strategic Air Command received its first operational B-36."

The museum also explains that "some B-36s served as photographic reconnaissance aircraft, and others were adapted to launch and retrieve specially modified RF-84F/K reconnaissance planes." Although conceived during the Second World War as an ultra-long-range conventional bomber, the B-36 entered service as the United States shifted towards a strategy centred on delivering nuclear weapons across intercontinental distances without relying on overseas bases.

This remains a central mission of the world's current strategic bombers (B-52, B-1B, B-2, B-21, Tu-160, and Tu-95MS). The Peacemaker's origins date to 1941, which is before the US even entered the Second World War. There was a fear in the US that the UK could fall to Germany, and if that happened, the US would lack a forward base for its bombers to raid Germany. As such, the requirements for the bomber included that it could fly intercontinentally from North America to Europe.

Designing and developing the bomber took time, and by the time it entered service in 1948, the Second World War had been over for three years. As things had panned out, the US had been able to use Britain against Germany, and had island-hopped to bring its B-29s within range of Japan.

However, the delays in designing the bomber also allowed engineers to update the design to carry the heavy nuclear bombs that weighed around 8,800 to 11,000 lbs (4,000 to 5,000 kg) and even the extremely heavy hydrogen bombs. The first experimental thermonuclear devices weighed tens of tonnes, while the first operational hydrogen bombs introduced in the early 1950s typically weighed around 30,000–42,000 lb (14,000–19,000 kg).

The Peacemaker was designed with the gigantic bomb bay doors needed to carry these bombs that no other aircraft could. When it entered service, it was able to carry around 86,000 lbs (39,000 kg) of payload, vastly more than any other bomber. With the establishment of the Strategic Air Command in 1946, the B-36 became the first true intercontinental nuclear bomber.

The world's geostrategic picture changed in 1949 as the Soviet Union exploded its first atomic bomb. Then, the US pivoted to requiring a bomber able to strike targets deep inside the USSR while using bases in the continental United States and be able to carry heavy first-generation nuclear bombs. This is something the Boeing B-29 Superfortress couldn't do, but it was very similar to what the B-36 was originally designed for.

Germany developed flying prototypes of longer range bombers that would be able to fly over and bomb New York City.

With the benefit of hindsight, the B-36 was always likely to be overtaken by the rapid pace of jet-powered aviation. It was conceived during the Second World War, but was ultimately built for a very different strategic environment. At first (late 1940s to early 1950s), the B-36 was unmatched.

No fighter could reliably intercept the Peacemaker at its high operating altitude (around 40,000 feet or 12,000 meters, later extending to 45,000 feet or 13,700 meters). This was before the USSR developed an integrated air defense network and a time when early Soviet jet fighters struggled to reach and engage such high-flying targets.

The Peacemaker's enormous range also allowed it to approach the USSR from multiple directions, further complicating interceptions. But the B-36 was becoming a relic of a bygone era. It was designed before jet-powered strategic bombers were practical. By the mid-1950s, new Soviet jet interceptors like the MiG-17 and then MiG-19 made it increasingly vulnerable.

This worsened with the development of increasingly capable radar-guided air defense systems and surface-to-air missile technology. The arrival of the all-jet B-52 Stratofortress further hastened the B-36's retirement by offering greater speed, improved survivability, and comparable intercontinental range. Perhaps somewhat similar, the arrival of the B-21 will inevitably increase pressure to retire the older, expensive, and less survivable B-2 Spirits.

By the time it entered service, the B-52 was in development, but was still years away. The Peacemaker was primarily powered by six enormous 28-cylinder Pratt & Whitney R-4360 Wasp Major piston engines arranged in a distinctive pusher configuration. However, these were too slow in the emerging jet age. In an effort to bridge the gap, the US Air Force invested heavily in modernizing the Peacemaker with four General Electric J47 turbojets.

The J47 turbojet engines were mounted in two twin-engine pods beneath the outwings and earned it the famous slogan "six turning and four burning." They were not intended for continuous cruise as they were incredibly fuel inefficient. Instead, they were used for heavy-weight takeoffs, improved climbing, increased dash speed in hostile territory, and reduced time spent over defended targets.

The USAF looked for other ways to improve the performance of the Peacemaker. It introduced the Featherweight modification programs, which removed defensive armament, ammunition, armor, and other equipment to improve altitude, range, and speed. The B-36 was given improved electronics, while the RB-36 strategic reconnaissance variant was also developed.

The Hercules has many tricks, one of which is external rocket pods, which enable it to get off the snow while operating on the poles.

The Air Force Museum states that "powered by six Pratt & Whitney R-4360 engines, the B-36J cruised at 230 mph [200 knots], but for additional bursts of speed its four General Electric J47s increased the maximum speed to 435 mph [378 knots]." Ultimately, the improvements addressed symptoms, but were unable to fix the underlying problem, which was that the B-36 just didn't belong in the jet age.

Relying on piston engines, the B-36 was too slow, typically cruising at 200 knots (378 knots at maximum speed). The B-47 Stratojet could cruise at 434 knots, and the B-52 at 456 knots. By the mid-1950s, increasingly capable Soviet jet interceptors such as the MiG-17 and MiG-19, together with radar-guided surface-to-air missile systems including the S-25 Berkut and later the S-75 Dvina, steadily eroded the B-36's survivability.

The Peacemaker faced other pressures. For example, the complex push-configuration radial engines frequently overheated, caught fire, and required changing a staggering 336 spark plugs per service. The massive size of the aircraft also demanded immense resources to maintain and operate.

The final B-36 Peacemaker was retired in 1959, just one year before a Soviet S-75 Dvina (SA-2) downed a Lockheed U-2 spy plane at an altitude of around 70,000 feet. The development contributed to the program that would give rise to the SR-71 as well as contributed to the abandoning of the XB-70 Valkyrie bomber.

Today, the Peacemaker is largely forgotten in popular memory. It is overshadowed by the earlier B-29 Superfortress, which entered service too late to make a difference in Germany but in time to play a pivotal role in ending the War in the Pacific, including dropping the nuclear bombs on Japan. The B-36 arrived too late for the Second World War, but it was also ill-prepared for the realities of the Cold War and the jet age.

It was quickly overshadowed by the early Cold War Boeing B-52 Stratofortress that entered service in the mid 1950s and continues in service today. These two successful bombers have had the effect of pushing the Peacemaker out of popular imagination. Unlike the B-29 and B-52, relatively few B-36s were preserved after retirement, with only four surviving in museums. None remain airworthy.

Final Fantasy 14's Evercold is Set to Be a Fascinating Social Experiment

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Stephanie Watel is a writer for DualShockers. She has 3 years of experience writing about video games, from news to lists to in-depth guides.

With well over a decade now under its belt since its fateful “rebirth” in 2013, Final Fantasy XIV is one of the biggest second chance success stories in the gaming industry, and has since cemented its coveted spot in the ever-exclusive genre of MMORPGs. While its battle gameplay remains a fixture of criticism, its player community only continues to grow and the Evercold expansion is set to bring about an avalanche of groundbreaking changes to alleviate many of those criticisms at long last (hopefully).

However, if you’ve been a part of FF14’s community long enough, then it’s easy enough to realize that the game’s biggest strength has never been rooted in its divisive battle content. Where Final Fantasy 14 truly shines and ironically puts a critical spotlight on other MMOs is its social scene. Through it all, the game has retained one of the most tight-knit global playerbases, and has been praised for its incredibly welcoming social atmosphere, to the degree that some players log in simply to hang out in the game’s most beloved hubs, go to venues for community events, and everything else that makes FFXIV one of the most engaging social games to be in outside of live service IPs and the handful of other established MMOs such as World of Warcraft (which is seeing its own astronomical resurgence with its release of WoW: Forever).

All that said, the insane amount of changes that are coming to Final Fantasy 14 with Evercold’s release in February 2027 are by and large systemic in scale. This means that the way players interact and how they socialize in the game will likely change, in ways that are both subtle and not so subtle. The ripple effect it will have is all but inevitable, and it remains to be seen how exactly it will all play out, not just for Evercold but for all expansions that follow it.

For years now and from one expansion to the next, the majority of Final Fantasy 14’s social circles have organically gravitated to the game’s biggest cities, turning them into bustling hubs of conversation and commerce that seldom ever sleep, no matter your timezone. By this I’m referring to New Gridania, Ul’dah, and Limsa Lominsa, the cities that first made their grand appearance in A Realm Reborn and have since remained the places that most players return to, regardless of what expansion they’re working through or whatever other type of content that’s keeping them busy.

Subsequent expansions have since debuted some truly vibrant city hubs, from the vast, enamoring architecture in Stormblood’s Kugane to the posh rooms of Shadowbringers’s Eulmore and later the futuristic storefronts of Dawntrail’s Solution 9. However, while these places see an initial surge in player counts that linger during their respective expansion’s patch cycle, as the content surrounding it wanes and periods of “content drought” come and go, nine times out of ten, players ultimately wind up back on the benches of Limsa Lominsa or the streets of Ul’dah to congregate with their fellow Warriors of Light.

While there’s no point blank reason for why this happens with every expansion, the easy answer is that all proverbial rivers always flow back to the cities that started it all. They’re where most of the game’s seasonal events take place, for instance, to allow “sprout” players to participate alongside years-long veterans. Free Companies, or FF14’s version of guilds, are often a mix of newcomers and seasoned players, and these cities are often where they mingle outside of their respective “FC House”.

There’s the commerce side of things too, as Market Boards, which serve as the main point of contact for selling items to other players in the game, are plentiful and often only a few steps away from an aetheryte crystal teleporter. On top of that, vendors and other NPCs who teach combat, crafting, and gathering classes are easy to come by in these places, all making for a reliable hub to take care of whatever you need on the day-to-day while hanging out with your friends.

However, this dynamic that’s remained in place all these years in FFXIV may experience a sort of social earthquake with Evercold’s arrival, as the game’s character UI is set to become almost entirely self-contained when it comes to progression and upgrades. The need to seek out vendors for everything is going to shrink dramatically (at least from what we’ve been told in the Fanfest keynotes so far), which takes a vital element for these active hubs out of the equation.

If players can take their tools of progression with them on the go instead of traveling to these sources, what will that mean for not only these cities but other hubs in the game that inevitably empty out in later patch cycles? Will places like Limsa Lominsa stand firm despite these changes or will they face winds of change that happen on a subconscious level? Most of all, what does that mean for late-expac hubs like Radz-at-Han and Solution 9? Will the dynamic of how hubs function in the game as a whole experience a generational shift?

This is by and far the biggest question Evercold faces, and it will be fascinating to watch that unfold in the initial months of the expansion’s content cycle.

While the social dynamic of FF14 is potentially about to undergo its biggest evolution since the game’s inception, there is one systemic update already underway that may help smooth over the impact that has. Director Naoki (“Yoshi-P”) Yoshida and his team have been in the process of integrating the game’s massive regional data centers, which house millions of players everywhere from North America to Europe, China, Japan, and Oceania.

Up to now, those data centers have helped mesh their respective servers to help give a sense of different global “neighborhoods” that players can feel at home in. However, as the game now has such a far reach, players have since proven that friendship has no boundaries, often teaming up with Warriors of Light in entirely different regions to do content or simply hang out.

To get to another data center, however, the process was tedious to the point that it would feel even more isolating than it should have. Normally, you’d have to log out of the game entirely and facilitate a data center transfer from the log-in menu, which can take up to a few minutes. Then, if you want to jump to yet another data center that’s not your own from there, you'd have to log back out, return to your home data center, then facilitate yet another transfer to get there. Exhausting, to say the least.

Just give us the name of the second Job already!

Finally, though, that archaic process is steadily becoming history as now the NA region has merged its data centers with those in the EU, which allows players to effortlessly jump between both hubs from any main aetheryte in the game’s biggest cities. No more having to spend precious time logging out and transferring over and over. Now it’s a simple click at the aetheryte and you’re there in seconds.

Not only is this extraordinarily more convenient, such a merge is helping to dissolve that imposed isolation and help all of these “neighborhoods” feel closer together. Players no longer have to feel like they have to resort to certain servers to get the experience they want, and ultimately this could (hopefully) lead to more content participation balance across the board. That was technically the initial goal of these integrations according to Yoshi-P, to help alleviate duty queue times, but we’ll have to wait for long-term results to really get the big picture.

While so much of the game’s systemic infrastructure is getting a staggering rework for Evercold onward, one particular social feature in the game that continues to get the proverbial rug pull, sadly, is housing.

Yoshi-P did announce during the Tokyo Fanfest keynote that housing in the game is getting a budget expansion for even more plots, though they’re still actively exploring how to implement that in a more effective way that doesn’t strain the digital space they have for it. Whether that’s creating “hidden” plot areas or more districts remains to be seen, and we’ll have to wait until after Evercold’s debut to find out the result.

While it’s always nice to have more room for players to settle and customize their home away from home, the hard truth is that FFXIV’s housing system remains an inconvenient mess with no indication of when we could potentially see the systemic change it so desperately needs. Over the years, the game has dealt with creating too much artificial scarcity for housing, turning it into far more of a privilege than it should be in a global MMORPG.

While it still should be earned through personal progression of the game, housing should be a guaranteed reward for your efforts. Forcing players to endure weeks or months of countless lottery drawings for a particular plot is equal parts exhausting and demeaning. On top of that, once you finally have a plot to put your name on, you can only keep it so long as your subscription remains active. Should you dare to not log in for more than a month or so, you face the prospect of a digital eviction, your purchased furnishings tossed to the curb, and a pity deposit back for your troubles.

It’s a glaring reality of how the game has chosen to approach housing from the get-go, and how those decisions have led it down a path that it can’t seem to escape or fix in an effective manner. Instead, it’s more plots to placate the masses, the option to manipulate the amount of interior space for an additional gil fee, and a long overdue unlock on the ability to “float” furnishings. These are nice additions, but I’ve long viewed FF14’s housing situation from the bigger picture.

Ultimately, I think it comes down to the desire to make housing genuinely feel like a neighborhood you can actively walk around in rather than making it entirely instanced like other games do. Trying to honor that immersion is commendable, but when it comes at the cost of depriving countless players of the opportunity to experience that based on a skewed luck system, then what is the point in the end?

All that said, I’m not without the hope that things could still change in the future for housing. Rather, it could very well be that it needs its own expansion to pull it off, just as the game’s done with its graphic overhaul in Dawntrail and now the gameplay overhaul in Evercold. Or perhaps, this is the way it truly needs to be in Final Fantasy 14 in terms of the game’s social dynamic. Maybe losing our digital neighborhoods to instanced housing isn’t the answer. Whatever it could turn out to be, to truly strengthen the game’s social sphere, housing needs to be something attainable for everyone, not a privileged few.

Final Fantasy XIV’s combat has been an ongoing point of discussion, criticism, praise, and everything in between. However, in the years of Dawntrail’s patch cycle, the criticism reached a fever pitch in regards to the game’s growing lack of job identity and how said jobs haven’t seen much of a systemic update at all since A Realm Reborn. It had gotten to the point that the future of FFXIV had reached a questionable stage, and that if these ongoing issues weren’t addressed then the MMO could see a tragic downturn sooner rather than later.

With that, Evercold is set to bring about a slew of long-awaited changes to battle content, pretty much from top to bottom. Virtually every aspect of combat in the game is set to get some degree of change, along with entirely new additions such as the already widely praised Gauntlet of Power. Meanwhile, existing content such as Alliance Raids are getting dissected and reworked to hopefully better fit modern playstyles where time is more precious and content needs to be more inherently beneficial to players.

These changes are yet another factor in the equation that will undoubtedly affect how players will team up and work together in a post-Evercold setting versus the traditional methods that have driven the game for years. Having more flexibility in playstyle and more diversity in the types of battle content that are available are definitely net positives for FF14, and it will be interesting to see how that subsequently helps the social aspect of the game evolve in the months and years to come.
