Coca-Cola Is the Most Boring Stock I Own. After 64 Years of Dividend Raises, I'll Never Sell.

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It's a blue chip dividend stock that you don't have to second-guess.

By Stefon Walters – Nov 26, 2026 at 7:39AM EST

10 stocks we like better than Coca-Cola ›

I consider myself adventurous, but I often prefer boring when it comes to the stocks I own. I have my fair share of growth stocks, for sure, but my staple "boring" stock is Coca-Cola (KO -0.03%).

Although Coca-Cola is up over 40% this year (as of Nov. 24), its main appeal has long been its dividend. It has increased its annual dividend for 64 consecutive years, making it one of the market's premier Dividend Kings ( companies with at least 50 consecutive years of dividend hikes). Considering that and its sustained success, it's a stock I'll never sell.

You can count on one hand the number of brands more well-known globally than Coca-Cola. Its products (especially its namesake soda) are available in virtually every part of the world, and its asset-light business model enables it to operate efficiently.

Usually, if a company wants distribution somewhere, it needs plants, warehouses, and delivery fleets in that market. In Coca-Cola's case, it relies on bottling and distribution partners to do so. Instead of owning factories and delivery fleets around the world, Coca-Cola sells concentrates and syrups to its partners and lets them do the heavy lifting.

Keeping its business asset-light lets Coca-Cola maintain high margins and an ultra-safe dividend. You don't get to 64 years of dividend increases without consistent, high-margin cash flows.

Not many companies fit the "recession-proof" narrative, but Coca-Cola is one of them. Even when the economy slows and money is tight, people still buy their favorite Coca-Cola brands. That's the beauty of its brand moat.

And despite being the undisputed leader in its space, Coca-Cola hasn't gotten complacent with its drink portfolio. It constantly refines its lineup to adjust to changing consumer preferences. Just six years ago, it had over 400 brands in its portfolio. Today, it has around half of that.

When you have an option for virtually every occasion, you keep customers in your ecosystem. It's how the company has sustained success for so long.

Coca-Cola is a premium business that's priced like one. At the time of this writing, it's trading at 28.9 times its earnings, which is higher than its average over the past few years and higher than the S&P 500's (^GSPC -0.25%)23.7 P/E ratio. The stock is still a buy, but after a 40% run-up this year, the upside may be more limited. Luckily, that doesn't affect its dividend.

Coca-Cola is always a good long-term investment, but with bear-market fears lingering, a short-term silver lining is that the stock typically outperforms the market during sell-offs. Past results don't guarantee future performance, but Coca-Cola has strong momentum at a time when the tech sector is dragging down the broader market.

A Mark Cuban-backed AI startup is helping families turn Thanksgiving conversations with their elderly relatives into lasting memories

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As 81.5 million Americans travel at least 50 miles from home for Thanksgiving, conversations around the dinner table could present a rare opportunity for people to learn more about their aging loved ones. A new class of AI startups wants to help them preserve those conversations forever.

Charlie Greene, the cofounder and CEO of Remento, first learned about the importance of recording memories when his father, Don Greene, died during the hijacking of United Airlines Flight 93 on 9/11.

A 10-year-old boy at the time of the terrorist attack, which resulted in his father’s plane crashing in Shanksville, Penn., the now 34-year-old Greene mostly has a collection of old home videos to remember him by.

So when his mother Claudette, 74, was diagnosed with stage-three lung cancer, he quickly acted to record her memories. With the intention of conducting an oral history interview, Greene searched “questions to ask a parent,” which Google morbidly autocompleted with “before they die.”

When he started asking her the questions like “How did you get to elementary school as a kid,” she lit up, surprised he was interested at all.

“The thing that blew me away about that experience was how unmorbid it felt,” he said.

Claudette is in remission, but her battle with cancer still left Greene “unhealthily fixated” on the idea of how to best preserve loved ones’ memories. In October 2023, he launched Remento, which sends older “storytellers” a weekly text or email reminder prompting them to respond to photos or questions chosen in advance by their family members, such as, “What was the hardest thing you had to overcome as a child?”

After gathering a maximum of a year’s worth of responses, the AI converts the raw transcript into a naturally flowing narrative that goes into a physical book complete with images and QR codes linking the text to the original recordings.

Greene told Fortune Thanksgiving presents a great opportunity for people to discover new memories or learn new twists on well worn stories from their relatives.

“One of the things I want for my daughter is the chance to sit next to her grandmother and hear her say ‘Believe it or not that’s me.’ Thanksgiving is a pretty good excuse to pull an old album off the shelf and make room for the two of them on the couch,” he said.

Last year, Greene secured a $300,000 investment from billionaire Mark Cuban during an episode of Shark Tank in exchange for a 10% stake in his company. He has raised a total of $4.3 million since launch.

Greene said he understands some people’s skepticism of AI, because “A lot of what AI is proposing to the world doesn’t feel actually that good to our lives right now.”

Widespread anxiety about AI replacing human workers as well as concerns over its environmental footprint have contributed to some people’s increasing skepticism toward the technology. A March study of more than 3,000 people by the nonprofit Jobs For the Future found that 38% of respondents said AI was doing more harm than good, while less than half said the technology was a net positive.

Yet Greene hasn’t gotten as much pushback about the company’s use of AI as he had expected because Remento is using the technology to provide its users with something deeply personal, he added.

“Investors want to invest in AI, because it’s an exciting place to be. But I think what we’re experiencing is people have problems and they want them solved, and they want consumer technology to be able to solve those problems,” he said.

Kindred Tales, a rival platform cofounded by Nick Hern and his wife Rebecca Hern, has seen a similar trend with their customers. The company has both AI and non-AI versions of its product.

While the non-AI version requires users to type out their responses to weekly prompts, the AI version helps by providing speech-to-text transcription and some light editing. It can also write a first draft of the interviewee’s story, if they want.

Before 2025, just over half of the company’s customers chose the AI version, but that number jumped to 80% after 2025.

“Customers increasingly see AI as a meaningful part of the storytelling experience, not just an extra,” Nick Hern told Fortune.

Part of the reason is that the company’s AI chatbot, called “Ali,” gives the interview process a conversational feel by teasing more out of users with follow-up questions crafted to their responses.

AI has helped Kern, who also works full time for Thomson Reuters, run the company as well. He said he has used the technology to help filter emails, as well as slowly replace freelancers that he previously hired to help him craft marketing and social content.

At a time when companies are spending billions to bring AI to every American, Greene, said, despite its flaws, AI has allowed Remento’s customers’, as well as himself, to record moments with family members that are invaluable.

“AI in our company is being used to get people to do things that they’ve always wanted to do but haven’t been able to, and to make that experience as seamless and humanlike as possible,” he said.

How Much Is Jensen Huang Worth to Nvidia? The Stock Market Just Gave a Clear Answer

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Jensen Huang’s return sent Nvidia’s stock soaring.

By Prosper Junior Bakiny – Nov 26, 2026 at 7:34AM EST

Jensen Huang's saga just took another unexpected turn. Nvidia's (NVDA -3.91%) board unanimously voted to reinstate him as CEO, less than a week after explicitly saying it wouldn't. The message the board is sending is clear. Despite Huang's extramarital affairs, including with an alleged Chinese spy and with one of his subordinates, he is extremely valuable to the company. Just how valuable? The market's reaction to Huang's reinstatement gives us a clue.

Jensen Huang. Image source: Getty Images.

It's no secret that Wall Street loves founder-led businesses, but why is that? There are several reasons. Let's discuss two of them. First, founders, who often own a substantial portion of the company, have a strong incentive to maximize shareholder value. Hired CEOs do too, to some extent. That's why equity-based compensation exists.

However, founders tend to have more skin in the game. They wouldn't just lose their job if their corporation failed, unlike many hired CEOs. They would lose a substantial portion of their wealth. Second, founders often have a much deeper knowledge of the business they created, its characteristics, risks, competitive advantages, etc. That may give them an edge in leading the business to greater heights. Before the announcement, the market didn't know whether it would take six weeks or six months, and it was hard to reliably assign probability estimates to each outcome. The market abhors uncertainty, so the news had a major calming effect for many investors. That doesn't mean that Nvidia is out of trouble.

The company still faces major investigations tied to Huang's scandals, and the man is scheduled to testify before Congress soon. Still, with Huang at the helm -- and even accounting for ongoing regulatory probes -- the stock looks attractive. The first sign of that is Nvidia's financial results. Nvidia clearly still has a large market to tap into, and with Huang back at the helm, it is as well-positioned as ever to do so. The data tells a different story: In 2024, EU firms used the euro for only 46 percent of their transactions outside the EU, down just 2 percentage points from 2016, compared to a steady 41 percent invoiced in dollars. Despite long-standing calls from the European Commission for companies to switch to euros, the dollar’s prominence partly reflects its dominance of energy markets; in 2024, EU firms used dollars for 87 percent of their petroleum imports.

Next, look at European financial investments. Since U.S. President Donald Trump embraced tariffs in 2025 and then threatened ttempted to seize Greenland in early 2026, there has been talk in some EU capitals that divesting Europe’s significant holdings of U.S. Treasurys could be a way to pressure Washington. EU-based investment funds and central banks must have missed the memo: Together, they made net purchases of U.S. Treasurys totaling $56 billion between January 2025 and this August. (The data excludes Belgium, Ireland, and Luxembourg, as these countries’ hefty holdings mostly reflect their role as financial hubs.)

Despite Paris’s role as Europe’s vocal champion of strategic autonomy, French investors accounted for 61 percent of these purchases—cementing France’s status as the top EU holder of U.S. Treasurys, with $363 billion in holdings as of August.

EU appetite for U.S. public debt stems from one simple fact: At just $7 trillion, the combined stock of German, French, and EU-issued sovereign bonds is five times smaller than the stock of U.S. Treasurys. Without a greater supply of euro-denominated safe assets, EU funds have little choice but to accumulate U.S. Treasurys. This increases Europe’s exposure to threats from the United States, such as the advice of former Federal Reserve Board of Governors member Stephen Miran to withhold part of the interest that Washington pays on foreign-owned Treasurys.

These days, all eyes are on artificial intelligence, and this sector highlights the extent to which calls for European de-risking are going unanswered in the tech field. In late October, Epoch AI ranked 64 models at or above the level of OpenAI’s GPT-5. Of these, 49 were American, 14 were Chinese, and just one was European—France’s Mistral Large 4. It’s no surprise, then, that American models are becoming the default choice for European firms. According to the German tech industry association Bitkom, 76 percent of German companies using AI rely on OpenAI’s ChatGPT, up from 70 percent last year. Not a single one of the surveyed firms uses Mistral.

U.S. dominance of Western AI is unlikely to end any time soon. Epoch researchers also found that between January and June 2026, U.S.-based firms bought 78 percent of all AI semiconductors powering data centers and supporting the training of new, more powerful models. EU-based companies purchased just 5 percent of these chips during the same period, paving the way for the trans-Atlantic AI gap to widen further.

Current EU investment metrics make it hard to imagine a more positive outcome for European ambitions in other frontier sectors, such as quantum computing and biotechnology. In the field of quantum technology, data from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology shows that only four EU countries (Finland, France, Germany, and the Netherlands) have funding levels worth noting. Yet together, they accounted for just around 10 percent of global financing in 2025.

Meanwhile, the United States captured nearly two-thirds of worldwide funding for quantum technology, while Britain and Canada—both much smaller economies than the EU—are also notable players. In medical biotech, the pattern is the same: U.S. firms secured 63 percent of global venture capital between January 2015 and June 2025, nine times the 7 percent raised by EU firms, according to the European Commission.

The abysmally low levels of funding for quantum computing and biotech suggest that current European concerns about dependence on the United States for AI are only the start of a long line of similar debates. Time and again, EU firms and consumers end up buying critical technology from elsewhere that they did not finance and develop at home. As the United States’ decision in June to cut off global access to Anthropic’s Fable 5 showed, this gives Washington a simple way to deprive Europe of critical technology.

Trade is the final area in which the EU is reliant on the United States. Consider EU exports. Despite pledges to diversify, European firms have become more dependent on the U.S. market over the past decade. U.S. firms bought 21 percent of the EU’s goods exports in 2025, up from 17 percent in 2015. While a rush to stockpile European goods before the start of U.S. tariffs can partly explain the spike in EU exports to the United States last year, it does not change the long-term trend. Nor is this Europe rerouting exports that China no longer buys; at 8 percent in 2025, China’s share of EU goods exports was unchanged from 2015.

Instead, two factors help explain why European exporters have become increasingly reliant on the U.S. market. The first is pharmaceuticals. Half of the 244 billion euro rise in EU exports to the United States between 2015 and 2025 can be attributed to medicines, such as the Irish-manufactured ingredients of Eli Lilly’s blockbuster obesity drug Mounjaro. In 2025, the United States absorbed 43 percent of EU exports of drugs and medical ingredients, up from 28 percent in 2015. Booming pharmaceutical exports are good news for Europe, but shipping nearly half of them to the United States gives Washington a clear target.

The second factor is that the EU’s pledge to diversify trade ties toward middle powers is materializing only slowly. The share of EU goods exports going to 10 major middle powers, including India, Indonesia, and Turkey, remained unchanged between 2015 and 2025, at only 15 percent.

In terms of imports, the EU’s reliance on the United States is growing, too—particularly in energy and intellectual property (IP). Increased dependence on U.S. energy following Europe’s shift away from Russian supplies is familiar by now: U.S. firms now supply almost two-thirds of EU imports of liquefied natural gas, up from zero in 2015. The lesser-known story has to do with IP. Contrary to Britain, Japan, and the United States, the EU is a net importer of IP, such as software, patents, or trademarks. U.S. firms supply 80 percent of these imports, and the trade is one-sided. Eurostat documents that at 213 billion euros in 2025, the bloc’s imports of U.S. IP have grown sevenfold over the past decade. Last year, the EU bought six times as much IP from the United States as vice versa.

Failed attempts to achieve strategic autonomy, de-risking, and diversification have a precedent. In 2019, Britain, France, and Germany created INSTEX, a barter mechanism intended to maintain European trade with Iran despite U.S. secondary sanctions. INSTEX handled just one transaction before closing in 2023.

Europe’s ambition to achieve economic security stands a better chance of succeeding if Brussels gets serious about creating a euro-denominated safe asset, investing heavily in frontier technology sectors, and doubling down on signing trade deals with middle powers. Delivering on these reforms would be a winning strategy—and not just a succession of buzzwords.

Amazon workers on food stamps have tripled despite its record revenue—and it’s just the latest evidence of the new economy of shrinking labor shares

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The U.S. workforce has made the type of history it likely wishes it didn’t. Working Americans are taking home the smallest percentage of economic output, 52.8%, since the Bureau of Labor Statistics began tracking the metric in 1947.

But as the share of wealth workers take home through wages—called labor share—is shrinking, corporate profits have exploded, with the S&P 500 index gaining 600% since the beginning of the century, while wages have increased just 12.5% over the same period, adjusted for inflation.

In other words: Corporations are raking in cash, but American workers are reaping less and less of the rewards.

The consequences associated with shrinking labor shares are now becoming tangible. A recent Government Accountability Office (GAO) report found that across the 11 states sampled, Amazon, the country’s largest company by revenue, has 12,346 workers on the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) and 11,338 relying on Medicaid. That was nearly triple the number of Amazon employees in need of federal assistance compared to 2020, when GAO conducted a similar report. During that same period, Amazon saw an increase in annual profits from $11.6 billion to $77.7 billion. Amazon’s 2025 revenue soared 12% year over year, from $638 billion to a record $717 billion.

Amazon spokesperson Rachael Lighty told Fortune the conclusion drawn from the GAO report is “wrong,” and that it is misleading to look at raw numbers rather than percentages.

“When you look at the actual facts, you see that Amazon pay is among the best in the industry, regular full-time employees have access to health care from their first day—at only $5 per week with $5 copays for employee-only coverage—and 74% of our regular full-time employees are enrolled in an Amazon health insurance plan, well above the 65% private-sector take-up rate for full-time workers,” she said in a statement. “We challenge other large retailers to provide the same Day 1 comprehensive health care benefits that we do.”

Walmart and FedEx saw similar increases in the number of workers taking federal assistance, as did ride-share and delivery companies.

Kathryn Larin, director for education, workforce, and income security issues at GAO, told Fortune the data illustrates that the Americans taking advantage of social safety net programs today are overwhelmingly in the workforce, with most working full-time. The income threshold for SNAP eligibility is about 130% of the poverty line, suggesting that despite many of these workers clocking in at their jobs, they still don’t have enough money to meet their basic needs.

“What this analysis really points to is the large number of people who have very low incomes and continue to have very low income,” Larin said. “I mean, these are families that are really barely able to make ends meet, and yet they are working, and they are working a lot.”

Diane Swonk, chief economist and managing director at KPMG, recently warned of the hidden consequences of a shrinking labor share, particularly that despite economic indicators suggesting the economy is stable, most Americans are facing an ongoing affordability crisis. KPMG found in February that since 1982, corporate profits as a share of U.S. GDP increased from 8% to 15.85%. During that same period, employee compensation as a share of U.S. GDP shrank from 66.6% to 61.9%.

“This chart from my recent Economic Compass still haunts me,” Swonk said in a social media post at the time. “A friend refers to it as the ‘revolution chart,’ which [is] disturbing but telling. Inequality fuels social and economic instability.”

This trend has been about 50 years in the making, according to Anna Stansbury, an assistant professor of work and organization studies at the MIT Sloan School of Management.

Fewer workers in the U.S. are represented by unions—20.1% of U.S. workers in 1983 compared to 10.0% in 2025—giving them fewer opportunities to bargain for salaries and benefits, she noted. But more so, Stansbury blames the fissuring of the workplace, or the breakdown of the employer-employee relationship. In the past, the typical employer-employee relationship would be that of direct employment: A worker for a company does their work at the company they are employed by. For example, a large bank like Bank of America used to employ a janitor to clean its offices.

But “in more and more cases, that’s not actually people’s experience of the workplace, particularly in lower middle-income jobs,” Stansbury told Fortune.

Instead, large workplaces like retailers and banks hire gig workers or subcontractors to complete jobs once done by direct employees: Companies hire a security provider, which employs a security guard to work outside that large company’s office. Delivery drivers are contract workers, not full-time employees.

As a result, companies don’t have to provide those workers equity or benefits. If a subcontractor violates labor law, the company contracting them is not liable. In the meantime, these large companies are not only saving money on benefits, but are also getting to argue that they are increasing efficiency by not spending resources on workers whose roles are not directly driving revenue, Stansbury explained. A bank should employ bankers, not janitors, to get the most bang for its buck, the thinking goes.

Brent Neiman, a professor of economics at the University of Chicago, said he believes AI has been the key driver of diminishing labor shares. In a New York Times op-ed, he argued the technology’s productivity promises pose the greatest threat to wages.

“Anyone who has used ChatGPT can see how much work now done by people could soon be done by technology,” he wrote.

Apollo chief economist Torsten Slok has already observed a correlation between AI adoption and lowered wages. In a white paper published in July, he and economist Sania Edlich found that among 321 occupations between 2015 and 2025, jobs with high exposure to AI saw a 6.7% decline in real wage growth after 2023 (following the wide release of ChatGPT), despite no employment changes, suggesting that while AI is not displacing jobs, it is associated with compressed wages. In other words, companies are seeing increases in productivity, but are using those increases as a cover to raise prices, while keeping wages stagnant.

Stansbury has a slightly different theory about AI. While the technology could begin to have an aggregate impact on the labor market, it’s still too early to say whether today’s shrinking labor share is part of a broader economic cycle, or if it’s a secular event, she said.

Unexpected inflation spikes, like what is currently happening, are usually associated with poorer real wages. If inflation is less volatile in the coming years and wage growth recovers, today’s trend of shrinking labor share could turn out to be cyclical, Stansbury said.

On the other hand, Stansbury noted, a tightening labor market should increase labor share, and the labor market is already relatively tight. If inflation stabilizes, employment remains narrow, and the labor share increases, it will be a sign of an economic cycle completing itself. A bigger problem will be if wage growth stays low even if inflation improves and the labor market stays tight.

“If those two things happen and the labor share continues to fall,” she said, “then it would suggest that there’s actually been a secular shift, a secular acceleration in the downward trend.”

Vibe coding security: a checklist before real users sign up

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 26 • Originally published at pantheontable.com • Fully Autonomous

Originally published at pantheontable.com/blog/vibe-coding-security-checklist

Deleting a leaked API key from your code does not make it safe. Copies stay in your Git history.

If strangers are now typing their details into an app you built by describing it, this checklist is for you. It takes the risks in the order that hurts users most, with what Andrej Karpathy, Pieter Levels, Patrick McKenzie, Jeff Atwood, Arvid Kahl, Simon Willison and Joel Spolsky said about each one, every record linked to its source.

Before you invite more people, close the risks that would hurt them first. Take every API key out of the code and revoke any that leaked, confirm payments on your server and leave card data with the processor, cap what one user can trigger, and prove you can restore a backup. Then have a fresh reviewer audit the whole app, because vibe coding security comes down to the checks the AI did not run for you.

You built the app by describing what you wanted, and now strangers are typing their details into it. Being scared of a leak at this point is reasonable, not paranoid. GitHub's own description of vibe coding separates the quick prototype, where little of the result gets read or tested, from the human-led kind, where you review, test and refine. Real users move you into the second kind, whether you planned it or not.

Checked fact: It separates full vibe coding (little reading or testing of the result, suited to quick experiments and prototypes) from human-led vibe coding (you review, test and refine).

GitHub, resources article 'What is vibe coding?', checked 2026-11-18

Checked fact: Risks it names when checks are skipped: hardcoded credentials, insecure endpoints, unvalidated input, messy structure and technical debt.

GitHub, resources article 'What is vibe coding?', checked 2026-11-18

"Ultimately, vibe coding full web apps today is kind of messy and not a good idea for anything of actual importance. But there are clear hints of greatness and I think the industry just needs a bit of time"

Andrej Karpathy, Vibe coding MenuGen (2025-04-27)

Karpathy wrote this in his essay on vibe coding MenuGen. The hints of greatness are real, and so is the mess you inherit once the thing matters to someone.

Read that list of risks as your checklist. The sections below take them in the order that hurts users most: keys, payments, abuse, AI features, data you cannot get back, and finally a review of the whole app.

An API key in your code works like a password for anyone who can see the code, and the bill or the breach lands on you. When you ask an AI tool to make a demo work, pasting the key straight into the file is the shortest path, so assume it may have happened and look. Your job is to move every key out of the code and keep the file that holds them out of every upload.

Checked fact: Anthropic's guidance: never put an API key directly in your code or configuration files, and add .env files to your ignore file so they are not uploaded.

Anthropic, Claude Help Center 'API key best practices'; GitHub Docs, 'About secret scanning'; Anthropic, Claude Code best practices, checked 2026-11-18

Checked fact: GitHub secret scanning looks for hardcoded API keys, passwords and tokens across the whole Git history of a repository, and public repositories get it automatically for free.

Anthropic, Claude Help Center 'API key best practices'; GitHub Docs, 'About secret scanning'; Anthropic, Claude Code best practices, checked 2026-11-18

Checked fact: In Claude Code a permission deny rule such as Read(./.env) stops the tool from reading the secrets file at all.

Anthropic, Claude Help Center 'API key best practices'; GitHub Docs, 'About secret scanning'; Anthropic, Claude Code best practices, checked 2026-11-18

Checked fact: A public repository is accessible to everyone on the internet; a private one only to its owner and people given access.

GitHub Docs and GitHub pricing page, checked 2026-11-18

Deleting the key from your code does not make it safe, because copies stay in the Git history. The order that works is revoke first, clean up later: kill the old key at the provider and issue a new one. GitHub's docs say that scrubbing a secret out of the history is slow and often unnecessary once the credential has been revoked.

Checked fact: If a key is compromised, delete it immediately in the Console; when a Claude API key appears in a public GitHub repository, GitHub notifies Anthropic and the key is deactivated automatically.

Principle: Audit Outbound Communication. Regularly monitor which applications are sending data from your machine to identify and remove suspicious software.

Andrej Karpathy, Digital hygiene (2025-03-17)

Karpathy's advice is to watch which programs send data off your machine and remove the suspicious ones. After a leak, turn the same habit on your app and your laptop.

Payments are where a vibe-coded shortcut costs real money. In the payment documentation we checked, the browser only starts the payment and your server finishes it with a key the browser never sees. A checkout that works in test mode can still skip the server step, so read that part of the code yourself or ask for it to be shown to you.

Checked fact: Flow of the widget: your page loads it with a client key and shows the payment methods, the customer pays, the browser returns to your success address, and your server confirms the payment with the secret key.

Toss Payments, developer center documentation (Korean), checked 2026-11-18

Checked fact: On return, your server should check that the amount matches the amount it saved before confirming.

Toss Payments, developer center documentation (Korean), checked 2026-11-18

"You never see, touch or store any of your customer's credit card data (only a token representing them) and that's how it should be because I wouldn't trust myself to know enough about security to store credit card data anyway."

Pieter Levels, Stripe Launches Beta In The Netherlands

Levels says this about a processor that hands back a token in place of the card. Pick that kind of setup and the most sensitive data never reaches your database.

Handing card data to a processor does not end the payment problem. A small shop with a cheap product and a fast checkout is exactly where stolen cards get tried, and the chargebacks come back to you.

What happened: Fights card testers abusing his $29.95 software. Before Stripe existed, McKenzie spent weeks fighting a gang of criminals who ran $29.95 purchases of his software to test stolen cards before draining victims' bank accounts. With little recourse as an entrepreneur, he eventually spent dozens of hours writing code to outsmart their scripts.

Lesson: A small online seller can become a fraud testing ground, so plan your own fraud detection rather than assume the payment network will protect you.

Patrick McKenzie, Credit Cards As A Legacy System (2022-12-02)

The processor keeps cards off your server; McKenzie's lesson is that it does not keep fraud off your store, so plan some detection of your own.

Principle: Accept non-zero fraud for conversion. McKenzie holds that merchants should trade off conversion rate against fraud rate rather than minimize fraud, since every anti-fraud step also reduces the customers a business can sell to.

Patrick McKenzie, The optimal amount of fraud is non-zero (2022-09-02)

This is the counterweight. Every check you add also turns away some honest buyers, so choose the few that stop the attacks you actually see.

So treat fraud control as a dial, not a wall. Start cheap: limit how many payment attempts one account or address can make in a short time, watch for bursts of small failed charges, and look at failed payments often during your first weeks. Tighten only where you see abuse, and watch your sign-ups when you do.

Principle: Design assuming users will be evil. Atwood holds that social software must be designed from day one assuming some users will be malicious, because naive designs like trackbacks eventually make the whole community suffer.

Jeff Atwood, The Day The Trackbacks Died (2025-06-04)

Trackbacks are his example of a feature built for good-faith users that the whole community paid for once others abused it.

What happened (2024): The Podscan Email Crisis. A single user set up alerts for extremely common words, triggering thousands of emails daily. This caused the email provider to block Arvid's entire domain, stopping even critical login and password reset emails.

Lesson: One user's unintentional actions can threaten the reputation and functionality of your entire business if safeguards aren't in place.

Arvid Kahl, From Overload To Opportunity (2024-10-18)

Kahl's user meant no harm, which is the point: you need limits for honest users too. The damage reached the login and password reset emails every other user depended on.

Podcast summary: Episode 405 | Minimum Viable Security, Moving on from AuditShark, and More Listener Questions. The episode on minimum viable security makes the same case for doing just enough, starting with the basics, and for throttling and isolating a public API. (the episode's official page)

Principle: Never mix untrusted text with tools. Willison holds that an LLM application must never combine untrusted input such as emails or scraped pages with access to tools or private data, because any attacker text can take control of the output.

Simon Willison, Imitation Intelligence, my keynote for PyCon US 2024

If your app reads emails, web pages or user uploads and the same model can also call tools or see private data, text written by an attacker can steer what it does.

If your app puts a model inside the product, such as a chatbot that searches user records or an assistant that sends messages, check each one against this rule. Where the model reads text you did not write, take away its tools and private data, or split the job so the part that reads the text cannot act. Test it yourself by pasting instructions into a message or a page the model will read, and see whether it obeys them.

"Don't rely on your host or anyone else to back up your important data. Do it yourself. If you aren't personally responsible for your own backups, they are effectively not happening."

Jeff Atwood, International Backup Awareness Day (2025-09-11)

A host's backup is a promise you have not checked. A copy you control is the one you can count on.

"The minimum bar for a reliable service is not that you have done a backup, but that you have done a restore ."

Joel Spolsky, Lets Stop Talking About Backups (2009-12-14)

A backup you have never restored from is a guess. Do one restore before you invite more users and see what comes back.

Principle: Run an AI security review before launch. Willison had a frontier AI model run a security evaluation of Datasette Apps before launch, and it found a real data exfiltration attack in his CSP allow-list design.

Simon Willison, Datasette Apps: Host custom HTML applications inside Datasette

The review caught a real attack that could pull data out through his CSP allow-list design, which is why it is worth running even when you believe the app is fine.

"Luckily for us I think the attack and defense capabilities of AI models seems to somewhat match? So as long as we keep typing 'do a security audit' regularly into our clanker we can keep it safe"

Pieter Levels, Claude Code Security Audit Every Vps

Where Willison describes one review before launch, Levels treats the audit as a habit to repeat. You need both: a gate before you open the doors and a routine after.

Checked fact: A review by a fresh agent that never saw the conversation (the /code-review command in Claude Code) is not swayed by the reasoning that produced the code.

What should I check first in an app built with AI?

Start with keys: make sure no API key sits in your code or in a public repository, and revoke any that did. Then check that payments are confirmed on your server, that one user cannot trigger unlimited emails or charges, and that you can restore a backup.

Revoke the key at the provider and create a new one before anything else. Removing it from the Git history is slow and often unnecessary once the old key no longer works, and GitHub secret scanning can check the whole history for others.

Is code written by AI less secure than code written by hand?

The risks are the familiar ones, such as hardcoded credentials, insecure endpoints and unvalidated input, but they are easier to miss when you did not write the code. Review and test the result yourself, and ask a fresh reviewer for evidence, not just a verdict.

Should my repository be public or private?

A public repository can be seen by everyone on the internet, so make it private unless it is meant to be open. Never upload keys either way.

Do I need to store credit card numbers to take payments?

No. A payment processor can hold the card and give you a token, and your server confirms the payment with a key the browser never sees, after checking that the amount matches.

Google's crawler docs now list pageburst, which renders pages for Google Ads and ignores robots.txt, plus more existing user agents to check in server logs.

Google identified pageburst as a user agent that renders pages to measure Google Ads quality and landing page safety and ignores robots.txt rules. Its two strings look like a browser’s and do not contain “Googlebot”; a bot rule created using “Googlebot” will not capture them.

Pageburst is one of 12 existing user agents Google added to its crawler documentation on Nov. 23, along with a corrected entry for APIs-Google. Google’s crawling documentation changelog explains:

“These crawlers and fetchers are not new; we’re documenting existing high-volume clients that are more likely to show up in server logs and that we’ve received questions about.”

The 12 belong to Google products other than Search, such as Google Ads, Gmail and Google Cloud.

Google’s special-case crawlers page, which lists crawlers used by specific Google products, says pageburst “renders web pages for Google Ads quality, landing-page safety, and page understanding.” The entry adds that “it’s unaffected by crawling preferences.”

The desktop string identifies as Chrome on Linux and the mobile one as Chrome for iOS on an iPad, and both include “compatible; pageburst.”

AdsBot, which checks web page ad quality for Google Ads, has its own robots.txt token. Pageburst’s entry lists no token and says the user agent “ignores robots.txt rules.”

The highlights show what to look for in server logs and where Google states its robots.txt policy.

Google’s documentation says user-agent strings can be faked. Special-case crawlers use the IP addresses defined in Google’s special-crawlers.json; these have a reverse-DNS match of rate-limited-proxy-***-***-***-***.google.com. The pageburst entry does not say what occurs when a website blocks the Google Ads check.

Besides pageburst, each of the five other newly documented special-case entries says the user agent “ignores robots.txt rules.”

Google’s user-triggered fetchers page covers the six newly documented fetchers and says that because a user requested the fetch, these fetchers “generally ignore robots.txt rules.” Three of those (AppEngine-Google, Google-Apps-Script and Google-Cloud-Tasks) send requests for code running within Google’s platform-based products. The remaining three are GoogleImageProxy, which is used to load image content into email applications such as Gmail, along with Google Web Preview and the PageSpeed Insights fetcher, both with strings ending in Chrome-Lighthouse.

Several of the newly listed user agents seem to use standard browser strings. For example, a string from GoogleImageProxy appears similar to Firefox 11 on Windows NT 5.1; the strings for Google Web Preview and PageSpeed Insights, and one of PlayStore-Google’s, were created using Chrome strings. A filter that only identifies “Googlebot” would miss all of these.

Google has moved APIs-Google, which makes requests to the webhook notification systems developers create within Google Cloud and Google Workspace APIs, from the list of special-case crawlers to the list of user-triggered fetchers. APIs-Google’s push requests, the changelog says, “come from the user-triggered fetcher IP ranges and ignore robots.txt rules.” Google added a missing semicolon after “APIs-Google” in its documented string and tells developers to modify any code that was previously using a hardcoded version of the earlier string.

Prior to the Nov. 23 update, Google had included robots.txt as a method to block requests from APIs-Google in its documentation. In an Oct. 21 capture, the robots.txt token was present on the special-case page. On Oct. 30, a capture of the APIs-Google page showed “Use robots.txt” as one of two methods to block requests from APIs-Google. Now the APIs-Google page says that it “ignores robots.txt rules”; for controlling the delivery of notifications, the page points to either the developer who created the notification system or Google Cloud’s abuse form for unexpected requests.

Pageburst renders pages for ads in Google and does not follow robots.txt. Check for “pageburst” in the log entries for your servers and CDNs, verify with Google’s procedures that the IP addresses are in its special-crawler ranges, and see if your pages were delivered to the verified requests, or if a challenge or a 403 was returned.

Roger Montti covered Google’s advice on Nov. 18 that general bot blocking may keep Googlebot from crawling content when moving a CDN; the same advice should be applied to other Google clients that do not have the name Googlebot.

Google’s special-case and user-triggered pages both say their lists aren’t exhaustive and cover the requestors “more likely to show up in log files.” The crawling documentation changelog, which is where Google logs additions like these, has an RSS feed.

Nepal: Predatory Lending Abuses Exacerbated by Conflict in the Gulf

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Loan shark victims demand justice after 38 days of protest in Kathmandu, Nepal, March 7, 2024. © 2024 Skanda Gautam/SOPA Images/Sipa via AP Photo Thousands of borrowers face exorbitant interest on fraudulent loan agreements taken out to fund labor migration and other needs.The Nepali government has criminalized abusive practices, but police and courts often side with loan sharks against distressed borrowers.Lost job opportunities due to war in the Gulf region have deepened the crisis facing many borrowers.

(Tokyo) – Authorities in Nepal should hold illegal moneylenders accountable for abuses against borrowers, including unlawful land dispossession, arbitrary eviction, threats and acts of violence, and sexual exploitation, Human Rights Watch said today. Conflicts in the Gulf region, where many Nepalis are migrant workers facing exorbitant recruitment fees, have made more people vulnerable to extortion by illegal lenders.

“Despite government commitments to protect borrowers, illegal practices are rife in Nepal’s informal credit sector, leaving those with the fewest resources dependent on moneylenders who can take everything they own,” said Meenakshi Ganguly, Asia deputy director at Human Rights Watch. “The government of Prime Minister Balendra Shah should carry out the government’s commitments to ensure that the law against fraudulent and abusive lending is consistently enforced.”

Human Rights Watch interviewed 18 borrowers, as well as local government officials, lawyers, and activists from six districts in the Terai region where informal money lending is rampant, reviewed government reports, and examined over 50 cases in Nawalparasi district.

Human Rights Watch found that many families are struggling with unmanageable debt particularly as migrant workers in the Gulf States during 2026 have faced reduced incomes or involuntary travel delays ahead of starting jobs due to the conflict between the US, Israel, and Iran. War-induced disruption amplified pre-existing abuses, including reliance on unauthorized individual agents and unreasonable recruitment fees. Human Rights Watch previously found that companies in Gulf states play a critical role in driving these illegal costs by refusing to cover costs and fees to recruiters that they know will be borne by workers.

Government investigations have shown that, under Nepal’s informal credit practices, a borrower signs or thumbprints an unsecured personal loan contract, known locally as kapali tamasuk, which can falsely list a principal amount three to ten times more than the cash received. These loans, known as meter byaj, in some cases compound at annual rates ranging from 36 percent to 60 percent or more, compared with less than 10 percent charged by banks.

As low-income households that had relied on remittances disrupted by the war have shifted their limited resources toward expenses for food, fuel, and healthcare, they have been increasingly unable to repay their loans, leading to a surge in defaults. In response, some moneylenders are forcing evictions or land transfers, including through violence, in the worst cases tying people into generational debt. Nepal authorities are not pushing for effective long term solutions beyond encouraging and facilitating debtors and creditors to enter pre-court settlements.

Following the outbreak of conflict in the Persian Gulf, in March 2026, the Nepal government suspended both institutional and individual labor permits to protect its citizens, including to Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, and Kuwait, with an estimated 2,000 Nepalis denied labor permits daily. Thousands were stopped from travelling at the international airport despite possessing valid medical clearances, verified visas, and binding employment contracts, while administrative delays also led foreign firms to cancel labor contracts. However, the authorities failed to step in to protect aspiring migrant laborers who still had to service their high interest meter byaj loans, leaving them exposed to violent enforcement by loan sharks.

“They did not just hit my father to get the money,” said one victim. “They beat him in the middle of the road during the day so that everyone else in the village would see what happens when you cannot pay the meter byaj. The local goons told us, ‘If you don’t sign over the land title today, your family will receive a corpse tomorrow.’”

While abuses, including violent attacks and forced evictions, appear to be increasing due to conflict related disruptions, many moneylenders have long wielded political and economic power over borrowers. Claiming that the justice system routinely favors the moneylenders, borrowers have staged three extended protests in Kathmandu over the last four years—the longest for 442 days—and three long marches. Each time, the government agreed to resolve debt issues but failed to honor its commitments to protect victims. Consequently, the borrowers have regularly protested at district and provincial levels as well.

Although government has criminalized such loans, borrowers and activists in Madhesh Province said that police have repeatedly refused to file reports when victims seek protection. Instead, they said, the police typically dismiss abuses and intimidation by moneylenders as “private civil disputes.”

“When the moneylender came to break our door, we ran to the police, but the officers told us to go home and settle personal transactional disputes ourselves,” said a Rautahat district migrant worker.

The police also back moneylenders when they use signed blank checks and banking laws to threaten defaulters with arrest, or use informal deeds as the basis to seize land and evict families. “The lender made me sign a blank check when he gave me a loan, and later on he filled it for 10 lakhs (US$6,500) and sent the police to threaten me with jail when the check bounced,” said a Siraha district farmer.

Borrowers also cite cases in which the authorities do not intervene even when lenders use patently unlawful loan agreements to obtain asset foreclosure orders. “The lender told me openly that he owns the courts and the administration,” said Shiv Shanker from Gaur, Rautahat district, who after facing abuses from a moneylender, is leading protests. “When the state shuts its doors on a poor farmer, death feels like the only place left to hide from this meter byaj.”

On May 5, 2023, the government criminalized informal loans as an “improper transaction,” and declared that forging loan contracts, threatening borrowers, or fabricating loan amounts, are punishable by up to seven years in prison. However, enforcement remains weak. Successive governments have established inquiry commissions to address the crisis, but over 65,000 complaints registered with inquiry commissions remain unresolved due to a lack of legal provisions and administrative bottlenecks.

These failures have contributed to deadly outcomes including suicide. Santidevi Mahato from Biratnagar died by suicide on June 21, following threats from a moneylender because she could not service her debt, people interviewed said. After borrowing 150,000 Nepali rupees (US$975) in October 2015, she had repaid 400,000 Nepali rupees (US$2,600) in installments. Yet, violating their agreement, the moneylender seized her land and threatened to evict her from her house.

Under international human rights law and standards, governments have the obligation to take steps to protect people from serious rights abuses caused by private actors, such as moneylenders, including through investigating and prosecuting serious crimes.

On July 17, the government led by Prime Minister Balendra Shah concluded a nine-point pact with protest leaders to eliminate predatory lending, after debt victims began a march to Kathmandu. The government should take immediate legal and policy steps to institutionalize its commitment, Human Rights Watch said. Nepal’s donors and international partners should assist the government to establish systematic debt relief, and grievance mechanisms that can support debt restructuring.

“The Balendra Shah government was elected with the promise of change, to overturn existing oppressive structures,” Ganguly said. “More closely regulating moneylenders and ending their chokehold on farmers and laborers is a crucial step in ensuring lasting social justice.”

Loan Sharks and Migrant Workers from Nepal

Nepal’s informal credit system is rooted in its feudal history, where local elites used predatory debt to trap peasants in tenancy and systems of generational servitude such as Kamaiya, Haliya, and Haruwa-Charuwa.

The present-day meter byaj system evolved around a spurt in labor migration during the decade-long civil war in Nepal, from 1996 to 2006, and rapidly spread after the 2007 political unrest in the Terai region. An estimated 7 million Nepalis work abroad, about half in India and a majority of the rest in Gulf countries, contributing a third of the national GDP. For many households, migration became one of the few viable ways to earn a living, while the absence of accessible and affordable institutional credit left them dependent on informal lenders to finance the costs of securing employment abroad.

Some local moneylenders and corrupt agents demanded exorbitant fees to help rural youth find jobs abroad, particularly in the Gulf States and Malaysia. Land or property was borrowers’ main collateral, but they often later discovered that their contracts were fraudulent, including falsified sums and forged documents. The schemes exploited bureaucratic complicity at government land offices to transfer property titles despite contracts being illegal, and borrowers lost their assets due to a lack of protection from the government.

In interviews, debt victims described devastating cycles in which migrant workers are forever bound to serving a predatory moneylender, while the authorities fail to intervene. Ramnath Musahar from Susta, Nawalparasi district, said the family borrowed to send two of his brothers to work in the Gulf States in 2015, but despite servicing the loan, their debt had grown enormously:

My brothers sent back Rs. 1,200,000 (US$8,000) from their wages, and we sold our plots [of land] to pay an extra Rs. 1,800,000 (US$11,000). But the lender went to the district court with a fake contract and claimed we still owe him Rs. 5,000,000 (US$33,000). The court looked only at the paper and our thumbprints; it did not look at our tears. The police came and threw our belongings into the dirt. We went from being independent farmers to beggars overnight.

Conflict Between the US, Israel, and Iran Exacerbates Debt Risks

Since the US and Israel began their assault on Iran on February 28, 2026, and Iran subsequently blocked and attacked ships traveling through the Strait of Hormuz and attacked sites across the Gulf States, the fallout is being felt far beyond the region.

In Nepal, UN agencies warned that “rising fuel prices, closure of shipping routes, and growing financial uncertainty are placing mounting pressure on economies, labor markets and vulnerable households.” According to the World Bank, Nepal’s real GDP growth could fall from 3.67 percent in 2024 to 2.3 percent in 2026 due to reduced tourism, higher transport costs, and supply chain snags. Rising inflation, a weakening currency leading to a rise in import costs, a spike in fuel costs, and stalled fertilizer supply have placed additional pressure on low-income households, and increased demands for overseas employment.

Hoping to seek jobs to support their families, many rural youths have taken loans from loan sharks, who have responded by raising rates. Many families were stuck servicing those loans, even as people were unable to travel because of conflict-related disruptions.

Workers already employed in the Gulf States are facing job losses, unpaid leave, wage reductions, and other disruptions that have reduced their ability to keep sending remittances. Compared with the same period the previous year, Gulf-bound labor approvals plummeted by 52 percent—from 228,165 down to 109,249—between the outbreak of the war in late February and mid-September, according to Department of Foreign Employment data. At least two Nepali workers have been killed due to the conflict, and 15 injured.

Many are returning to Nepal stripped of their anticipated income, and unable to service their loans. The authorities have failed to act as moneylenders crack down on defaulters, beating them, evicting them, and forcing land or asset transfers. The lenders are also seeking court-ordered asset foreclosures and even arrest warrants based on the loan contracts, including contracts that borrowers alleged contained fabricated loan amounts. As a Mahotari district borrower observed, “The law says meter byaj is a crime, but police demanded receipts I never got, so the thief walks free, and I am made the criminal.”

Women-led households, Dalits (considered an “untouchable” caste), and Indigenous ethnic communities have even fewer protections from violence such as beatings and sexual violence due to their caste, gender, and discrimination. One woman from Dhanusha district said she was raped by a moneylender under threat of eviction.

My husband was trapped in a labor camp abroad and couldn’t send money. The lender came to my house with the contract and said he would evict my children the next morning unless I “compromised” with him. He held our roof over my head like a weapon. I had to endure his abuse in silence because a poor woman in this village has no voice against a powerful man who holds her land paper.

Abuses by Moneylenders

While the conflict has exacerbated the crisis, debt-ridden families had already proliferated in Nepal, particularly in Terai districts. The authorities have failed to protect these families.

Chirai Chamar, a widowed Dalit worker in Paklihawa, Nawalparasi district, while struggling to gain control of her inherited family land, had borrowed 20,000 Nepali rupees (US$130). A moneylender offered to help with the legalities in exchange for a portion of that land. “A moneylender, from whom I had borrowed Rs. 20,000, came to me saying he will help me to acquire all the papers required to own my family land, and in return I need to give him two katthas (650 square meters) of land,” she said.

He then grabbed an additional four katthas (1336 square meters), paying Chamar a grossly undervalued sum of Rs. 150,000 (US$970). When rights activists intervened to reclaim Chamar’s land and restore her livelihood, local thugs threatened them and destroyed Chamar’s crops. Despite complaints, the police and the local officials failed to assist her.

Ashamaya (name changed), a resident of Thecho, Lalitpur, supported her family by running a small tea shop. However, when her father-in-law fell ill, she had to close the business to care for him. Left without income as the medical expenses mounted, she borrowed money from a moneylender, who charged an extortionate annual interest rate of 120 percent, which she could not pay. Under pressure, she took on another usurious loan simply to pay off the old one.

Although she settled that debt by selling a piece of land, she had to borrow again, this time to cover her husband’s medical costs, but she no longer had enough land for collateral. The moneylender coerced her into sex with multiple men after he forced her to sign a new contact for Rs. 2500,000 (UDS$16,167), amounting to five times her initial debt.

Seeking a Remedy

Debt victims have started to organize under various banners and groups, demanding that the government protect them from abusive loan sharks. In 2022, after hundreds walked barefoot for weeks from districts including Siraha, Dhanusha, Mahottari, Sarlahi, Kapilvastu, and Nawalparasi to stage protests in Kathmandu, the government at that time establisheda three-member Commission for the Investigation of Loan Sharking (CILS) in April 2023, to collect complaints, study structural issues, and resolve loan-sharking disputes.

By May 21, 2023, when the District Administration Offices stopped accepting these complaints, over 28,000 had been filed across 67 districts, with an alarming 21,552 cases concentrated in Madhesh Province. However, the commission only resolved and closed 5,115 loan disputes. Several victims reported abuses by district offices. “We went to the Chief District Officer expecting justice, but instead, the police and the lender ganged up on us, warned us that going to court would mean total land auction, and scared us into signing an agreement to pay half the inflated sum,” said a woman from Siraha.

As complaints persisted, including of violent encounters in which the police failed to intervene, the authorities largely resorted to symbolic agreements with the victims, followed by the repeated formation of new commissions. A commission was set up in March 2024, led by former high court judge Tej Bahadur Karki, and another in March 2025, led by Baburam Regmi. In November 2025, the Regmi Commission reported a total of 41,409 complaints registered in 43 districts. Of these, 6,393 cases had been resolved, while 35,016 complaints remain pending.

Each commission exposed the vast scale of the crisis but political commitments to break the stranglehold of moneylenders remain unfulfilled. Nawalparasi’s Deputy Chief District Officer Poshan Raj Bhandari told Human Rights Watch that, “The existing law will not be enough to resolve this problem; the government needs to think of a separate legal process to resolve this.”

When Prime Minister Balendra Shah’s government was elected in March 2026 with a large majority, victims had cause for hope. However, instead of the government ensuring a collective debt moratorium, victims say that moneylenders have convinced the police to issue a surge of arrest warrants against debtors.

In response, victims have organized new protests. Nigra Nabin, a leader of the protest movement, said that based on “past experiences” the victims “had three long-standing demands” including the nullification of all illegal agreements and the underlying loan contracts that support them; a new law to institutionalize these actions and establish special legal procedures; and a separate entity dedicated to ensuring justice for the victims.

On July 9, the anti-loan shark movement began an 11-day justice march from Janakpur to Kathmandu. On the sixth day of the march, Home Affairs Minister Sudhan Gurung traveled to Nijgadh to hold direct talks with the protesters. Following three days of negotiations, the government accepted the three core demands and signed an updated nine-point deal, leading the protesters to call off the march.

The victims are waiting. One of them, Sobita Devi Mahato, said: “If this is not resolved, the only resort I have is to go to the Home Ministry and hang myself there.”

K-beauty stocks rally as Korean brands top Amazon bestseller lists globally ahead of Black Friday

📅 2026-11-26  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

South Korean beauty stocks jumped Thursday as the country's skincare brands dominated Amazon's bestseller rankings ahead of Black Friday.

Shares of K-beauty giant APR climbed 3.77%, while cosmetic contracts manufacturers Kolmar Korea and Cosmax gained 5.46% and 2.91%, respectively.

APR shares, in particular, have rallied sharply this week, up 28%, defying a broader market sell-off in Seoul markets, with the benchmark Kospi index down 2.65% during the same period.

A cosmetic sector index tracked by Meritz Securities had gained 12% this week until Wednesday, according to a Thursday note from the brokerage.

The rally reflects renewed optimism over earnings growth following strong performances by K-beauty brands across major e-commerce platforms in the U.S. and Europe with Black Friday sales already underway, as well as increasingly attractive valuations following months of share price declines, according to Meritz.

K-beauty products topped Amazon's skincare bestseller rankings across six major markets — the U.S., the U.K., Germany, France, Spain and Italy — as of Nov. 25, according to data compiled by Meritz Securities. Other Korean beauty brands, including Biodance, Illiyoon and Anua, also ranked among the top 10 in the U.S., the data showed.

As of Thursday morning, Korean beauty products occupied several of the top spots in Amazon's U.S. beauty and personal care category, the e-commerce website showed.

Meritz Securities expects APR shares to maintain their positive momentum through the year-end, with a more substantial rebound in early 2027.

The brokerage named APR and Cosmax as its top picks in the cosmetics sector. Korean beauty brands have established an "overwhelming presence" in the bestseller rankings of major e-commerce platforms during Black Friday sales, prompting the market to reassess the growth's potential, the brokerage said.
