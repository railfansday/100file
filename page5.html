Laid-Off Textile Workers Ran Their Bosses' Hidden Exotics As A Museum For Two Years

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

What's the coolest automotive museum you've ever seen? What about the most mind-blowing piece of automotive history you've ever beheld with your own eyes? I live right down the street from the National Automotive Museum in Reno, NV, which is a great time, but it doesn't quite take the cake. There are some real gems out there, like the Gilmore Car Museum, which lets you drive a Model T, or the Studebaker National Museum, where you can watch footage from 1932 of grown men throwing cars down a hill for safety testing. But if I had a time machine and could visit any car museum anywhere in time and space, there's one in particular that I'd just love to see.

That would be the Schlumpf collection in Mulhouse, France, specifically during the two-year period in the mid-to-late-70s when it was run by laid-off textile workers after their bosses shut their factories down and fled the country. Yes, the story is as wild as it sounds.

Now housed in the Musée National de l'Automobile in Mulhouse, the Schlumpf collection is an astounding piece of both car collecting and labor history. It began as the personal (and, sure, slightly obsessive) collection of Fritz Schlumpf, a long-time Bugatti driver who, along with his brother Hans, ran a textile business with factories in Mulhouse. Born around 1906, Fritz Schlumpf began collecting Bugattis intentionally in the 1960s. He hunted them down with the help of the British Bugatti Owners Club, who helped him locate around 50 individual cars, plus a stash of 30 vintage Bugattis owned by John W. Shakespeare of Hoffman, Illinois. Over two years, Schlumpf and Shakespeare negotiated a purchase price, bringing Schlumpf's personal Bugatti collection up to 80. Then he bought 18 more off Ettore Bugatti himself. Schlumpf was only getting started.

Eventually, Fritz Schlumpf owned an estimated 105 Bugattis, which might've comprised as much as 20% of the Bugattis in existence at the time. Who can blame Schlumpf for apparently wanting them all? After all, Bugattis do drive like a dream, even the old ones. But Schlumpf hadn't just been buying up cars of his favorite French brand. He'd actually been amassing a mind-bogglingly large stable of rare cars, both vintage and contemporary.

By the 1970s, Schlumpf had over 400 cars stashed in an unused factory in Mulhouse that was being fitted into a museum. But in order to acquire them all during a worsening economy, he'd spent money from a government grant intended to keep his factories in operation. When his transgression was discovered, he promptly shut down all his businesses and fled to Switzerland, putting about 1,800 workers out of business.

In response, the workers decided they didn't need a boss and could keep things running without Schlumpf's oversight, so they formed a workers' co-op to keep themselves employed. When members of the co-op traveled to the supposedly unused factory in Mulhouse, what they discovered was a building packed to the gills with exotic cars. A collection the likes of which had never been seen. The workers collectively decided to run the museum while using it to spread the message of their cooperative to the wider world.

The museum was open for two years, with public access to the vehicles for a few hours a day, and it contained some real stunners, including gems like two Bugatti Type 52s, which were electric race cars sized for children. You can still see the collection today, though not exactly as Schlumpf originally had it. The collection was seized by officials and eventually reopened in 1982.

Alonso is under growing pressure as Chelsea were booed by their own fans after their drubbing by Leeds

As the half-time whistle blew on another dire afternoon for Chelsea, boos emanated from parts of Stamford Bridge. They were on level terms with Leeds at this point; it was about to get a hell of a lot worse for the Blues fans in attendance as they watched their team, playing without purpose, get overrun.

The outpouring of anger from what was left of the home support was far more unanimous an hour or so later. Leeds had run out 4-1 victors, a scoreline that frankly flattered Chelsea. Barring a flash of brilliance from Emmanuel Emegha for the opener, the hosts had been absolutely outclassed - and it hadn’t looked particularly laborious for the visitors. Like Tottenham fans a month earlier, the travelling Leeds contingent found themselves “ole-ing” at their opponents’ expense, enjoying total control at a ground they hadn’t previously won at this century.

Chelsea were embarrassed in a grudge match, again - and patience feels like it is really beginning to run thin. “I understand their disappointment for sure and we share that with them and whatever we can do is work, try to give our best to turn it back and to do much better,” Xabi Alonso said. He is fighting fires three months into the season.

This is a fourth defeat in five for Alonso, a run that started with the sacrilegious home defeat to Spurs. That sort of result turned the fanbase against Antonio Conte in 2018, less than a year after he’d guided them to the title - so it was imperative for Alonso to regroup his side quickly in order to avoid meeting the same end.

That he has not done. Victory over a pitiful Manchester United team aside, Chelsea have failed to come close to inspiring in recent weeks. And now, amid this atrocious run of form, they actually have more Premier League losses to their name than crisis-ridden United. “We are in a fragile moment mentally,” admitted Alonso, whose side is spiralling down the table.

The team looks systemically lost. In defence, Alonso has flip-flopped between a back three and back four on a gamely basis, though the confusion doesn’t seem like it’s hindering Chelsea’s opposition more than it does themselves. They are persistently look open and passive - and against a team like Leeds, they were bullied, just as they were against Sunderland. This goes for their midfield, too, especially without Moises Caicedo - who was withdrawn through injury midway through the first half.

And things aren’t much better going forward. What was initially touted as one of the most dangerous front threes in the league has turned toothless. The absence of Joao Pedro continues to be painfully detrimental, with Chelsea missing what he offers in the build-up along with his clinical finish. For parts of the first half, it felt like Emegha might be the solution Alonso has been searching for, finally playing a natural No 9 after three league matches of opting for alternatives. But the Dutchman faded and Chelsea ended the game with just an xG of 0.56.

Before the visit of Leeds, Alonso said: “I always know my best team for the next game we have.” It’s how he justified making the second most line-up changes of any other manager in the top-flight: 24. That rose to 28 on Saturday afternoon, and the result that followed confirmed that Alonso is still some way off finding his best team, or at least one with a clear identity.

This is obviously a cause for concern, because the Chelsea job is not known for having much of a grace period. The club’s reactionary ownership and culture of change is an occupational hazard that Alonso would have been fully aware of when he put pen to paper last Spring. But usually, the fanbase is more forgiving, especially in early days of a project.

One notable exception to this comes to mind, though. The interim appointment of Rafa Benitez in 2012, replacing fan favourite Roberto Di Matteo, was met with chagrin due to his Liverpool past. Fans never took to the Spaniard, even after he led them to the Europa League title and a third-place finish. His tenure was ultimately short-lived, with his rival Jose Mourinho preferred the following summer.

As Chelsea’s form metastasises, this buried anti-Liverpool sentiment is beginning to effervesce at the surface. Discontent towards the team could not even wait until full-time against Leeds, and fingers are being pointed at the manager.

Alonso arrived with a certain aura, the Spanish footballing mastermind that had turned Bayer Leverkusen into Bundesliga invincibles just two years earlier. But the honeymoon period is over; Alonso now must produce results to prevent it from boiling over, and risk falling to the same fate as his countryman and former boss.

Chelsea welcome Liverpool in three weeks’ time, a fixture which could determine whether Alonso sinks or swims with the fans. He deserves to be given the time to correct the chaos on the pitch - but as far as morale goes, he cannot afford more disappointment in front of his own supporters against an arch-rival, not least his first English love. It remains to be seen whether this relationship is salvageable.

The best way to pack a suitcase, according to science

While recently packing for an overseas excursion, I stuffed every spare space I could find in my carry-on. I used what I call the “Mary Poppins” packing method: willing myself to believe that whatever I wanted to put in my luggage would fit, regardless of the size of the object or the size of my bag. Somehow my system worked, but when I arrived at my destination and unzipped my bag, everything within it exploded onto the floor. “There’s gotta be a better way,” I thought.

To find out how best to pack according to science, Popular Science turned to Anne McAlpin, a travel expert and author of the book, Pack it Up: the Essential Guide to Smart Travel. Over the course of her lifetime, McAlpin has estimated she’s packed—and unpacked—more than 25,000 times! She’s a whiz at minimizing dead space, maximizing volume, and keeping clothes looking more like they came off a closet hanger than straight out of a laundry bin.

Many carry-on bags have “ruts” or internal grooves to accommodate a recessed trolley handle. This handle uses two narrow metal poles that retract straight into the middle or sides of the interior lining, causing the interior surface of the suitcase to be uneven.

“If you don’t have a flat foundation, make one,” says McAlpin. Having a flat foundation maximizes space, prevents wrinkles, and adds stability to your bag. To do this, consider evening out the space between ruts with packing or compression cubes. “You can put all of your underwear into a compression cube,” a small bag made of breathable nylon or polyester mesh used in part to organize items, and then place it between the ruts. This will even out the bottom of a hard-shell suitcase. Flexible items such as toiletry kits and rolled-up socks can help to form this base layer as well. “I then pack my clothing on top of that flat foundation.”

McAlpin puts her shoes (she usually packs two pairs and wears a third) towards the bottom of the suitcase so that the weight is on the wheels. “You don’t want to put your heavy items on the top of the inside of your suitcase,” she says. “This will cause it to constantly topple.”

When it comes to clothing, McAlpin rolls some items and flat packs others. “I call it interlayering,” she says.

For example, with pants, rather than folding them to stack in a pile, McAlpin lays each pair out along its natural creases as if she were going to put them on a hanger. Then, she places them in her suitcase: waistband at the top of one side of the bag, legs dangling out the other side of the bag. They’re not folded, but laid out straight. This way, McAlpin can then wrap them around the rolled items that she’ll later place on top of them.

She does the same with cotton shirts, sundresses—anything that might wrinkle. With each item, McAlpin alternates the direction, so that there are no two waistbands or shirt collars bulking up one side of the suitcase.

“I then roll my knit items and synthetic things that don’t wrinkle as much. I roll them up like Tootsie Rolls and I place them side by side,” in the middle of the laid-out items. While folding clothes and stacking them leaves pockets of air both inside the folds and within the layers, rolling wraps fabrics into a smooth, tight cylinder, which reduces the overall amount of space each item uses. It’s the best of both worlds! Your laid-out items aren’t as wrinkled and your wrinkle-resistant items are more tightly packed. This allows for more room in your suitcase overall.

When McAlpin has all her rolled items tightly packed together, she takes the pant legs and skirts that are still hanging off the sides of the suitcase, and “bends them up and over the ‘roll bar’ I’ve made of sweaters, pajamas, and sweats.” This helps avoid the knee creases that come when you’re simply folding pants. It also keeps items compact, and is a game-changer when it comes to unpacking. You can just lift all of the interlayered items out of your suitcase in one fell swoop.

Fill up empty space. For example, McAlpin says that if you’re a couple packing together, you may be able to slide a woman’s sandal right into a man’s shoe, or at least use the shoe’s interior cavity to store phone chargers, socks, or small items like earrings and even an eyeglasses case. Not only is it a space-saver, but filling up unused space prevents transit damage by keeping items inside from shifting.

Stuff the corners of a suitcase with socks, rolled-up t-shirts, etc, “because all those awkward corners offer prime packing space,” says McAlpin. Filling up those pockets also distributes structural stress throughout the bag, helping to keep features like zippers and fabric intact during excessive handling.

Another trick: laying belts around the luggage perimeter so that they’re easier to find.

To keep things condensed and organized, McAlpin suggests compression cubes. These small bags feature two zippers: a main zipper that closes the cube like a standard bag, and a second perimeter zipper that pulls the cube’s top and bottom panels closer together, squeezing out excess air and reducing volume. Compression cubes help eliminate air pockets that come with traditional folding and can reduce the volume of soft clothes like T-shirts and pants up to 30 percent.

Another option: roll-up compression bags. These pump-free, vacuum-free storage bags use one-way pressure valves to push air out, cutting volume by up to 80 percent. “I use these for laundry,” says McAlpin, because they temporarily block odors.

“They’re like an industrial Ziploc bag,” she says, and great for bulky items like a fleece jacket. But use them sparingly. Roll sacks are typically made of plastic and cause a lot more wrinkles than traditional compression cubes.

McAlpin carries dry laundry sheets and a two-gallon Ziploc bag for washing clothes. “The bag doesn’t take up any room, and then I can wash my socks and underwear without worrying if I’ve left any toothpaste in the sink.”

McAlpin says her packing “3:1 ratio” (three tops to every bottom) has “changed people’s lives.” She packs nine tops and three bottoms, and everything mixes and matches. “I base everything around two colors,” she says, “like a black and a tan,” and then throws in an occasional splash of color.

This works, says McAlpin, because human memory registers outfits based on what’s closest to the head and face. And while she travels with three pairs of shoes, each one should be for something different. For example, runners for long walks, sandals for the beach, and loafers for evenings.

As for the bag itself, McAlpin says four-wheel bags (spinner luggage) are a must. Not only are they easy to maneuver through packed terminals and airplane aisles, the ability to push the bag upright beside you keeps your arm in a neutral, bent-elbow position. This places less stress on your wrists, shoulders, and lower back compared to pulling a two-wheel bag behind you, and plays a big role in easing ergonomic strain.

They’re also ideal for stacking, since they stay upright. Think about it, says McAlpin. “When you pack your heavy tote bag on top of your suitcase, it’s the bag, not your shoulders, that’s carrying the weight.”

Ultimately, the idea is to make use of all your space, keep items from bumping around inside your suitcase and (if possible) wrinkle-free, and to not throw out your back in the process.

Of course, when it comes to finding room for souvenirs, there’s always the Mary Poppins packing method. But after talking with McAlpin, I’ve been using her method exclusively. My clothes, formerly creased, rumpled, and crinkled, are now decidedly wrinkle-free!

GLP-1 medications have transformed obesity and type 2 diabetes treatment. Now, researchers are investigating whether the effects of this blockbuster class of drugs extend beyond weight loss and affect brain health.

A comprehensive new review published on November 5 in the journal Science evaluated the current evidence on how GLP-1 medications like semaglutide and tirzepatide may affect the brain across aging, cardiometabolic disease, and Alzheimer’s disease.

The authors of the review suggested that GLP-1 medications like Ozempic and Zepbound may offer brain health benefits. However, they were also left with the question of whether the medications act directly on the brain or whether better brain health is actually a consequence of improved cardiometabolic health.

Sheena Aurora, MD, vice president, Medical Affairs, Alzheimer’s Association, who wasn’t involved in the review, told Healthline that more research is needed to better understand the effects of GLP-1 drugs on the brain.

“While research suggests GLP-1s could reduce brain inflammation, improve blood vessel health, and even protect neurons, much broader, randomized clinical trials on GLP-1s and brain health, including in people with Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, are needed,” she said.

GLP-1 medications like semaglutide, the active ingredient in Ozempic and Wegovy, mimic the GLP-1 hormone. Tirzepatide, the active ingredient in Mounjaro and Zepbound, also targets a second hormone pathway called GIP.

These medications have been shown to produce significant weight loss and improvements in cardiometabolic health.

This is important for brain health because obesity, type 2 diabetes, and vascular dysfunction are all linked with cognitive impairment and dementia. Insulin resistance and chronic inflammation may also link metabolic and brain health.

Why the Answer Page of a Daily Game Should Be the Least Exciting Page

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Product teams tend to make the answer page a finale. Confetti, a big reveal, a share card. After a year of running a companion site for a daily puzzle, I think the answer page should be the least exciting page in the product, and the reason is a design principle that generalises far beyond games.

The player arrives at the answer page after the interesting act has already happened. The two minutes before that page are where the game lives: the listening, the narrowing, the guess. A dramatic reveal page is competing with that experience rather than consolidating it. Worse, it adds latency to the one thing the user came for, which is the answer itself.

What the page actually needs is three things. The answer, above the fold, without a scroll. The hints next to it, collapsed, so someone still working can use the page without being spoiled. And a way back into the game, because a finite experience usually ends with a desire for one more round. That is it. Everything else is decoration that slows the page and makes the answer harder to confirm.

There is an SEO argument too. Answer pages are the pages that rank for question-like queries, and question queries want immediate answers. A page where the answer is buried under a hero animation and three paragraphs of preamble has a worse answer density, and answer density is what users and search engines are both estimating. Plain HTML that states the answer in the first screenful is not lazy; it is the correct product decision for this page type.

I would go further: the boring answer page is a brand decision. A site that respects the player's time after the round is a site the player trusts before the next round. Trust compounds in a way that confetti does not. You can compare the two approaches in five minutes: see the daily answer as it is laid out here, then look at the practice page and notice how much of the design is just removing steps.

Agent skills are folders with a SKILL.md file that teach a coding agent, such as Claude Code, Codex or Cursor, how to do one job. There are a lot of them now. I had about 720 skill files installed on my own machine.

Then I looked at my own history. Across several hundred coding sessions, only 26 different skills had ever started. The other 700 were sitting in the agent's context doing nothing, or worse, competing with the ones I needed.

So before building my own library, I counted what exists.

I downloaded twelve public skill libraries and wrote a script to read every SKILL.md: 23,064 files, 10,579 different skill names once the copies are removed.

Then I read a few hundred of them closely, domain by domain. The pattern was the same everywhere. Weak skills are lists of advice. Strong skills run a tool and end with a verdict. An advice skill cannot fail, so it cannot tell you anything.

mstack is the library I built from that. Every skill follows four rules.

1. It runs a script. The script produces the check. The model does not grade its own work.

2. It ends in one of four states: pass, fail, not verified, or blocked. "Not verified" never counts as a pass. If a tool is missing, the skill says so and stops. It does not pretend.

3. It is tested for when it starts. Each skill has ten requests where it should start and ten where it should stay quiet. The quiet ones are near misses: requests that belong to the skill next door. The results are published in the README, including the bad ones.

4. Packs are small. A coding agent only has room for so many skill descriptions. In Claude Code the skill listing gets about 1% of the context window, and when it overflows, the least-used descriptions are dropped. So mstack is split into packs by domain. You install the pack for the work you do.

You do not type commands. You say what you want.

The pack also ships a guard hook for Claude Code. It stops a recursive delete and points to safe-delete, and it asks before commands such as git reset --hard, a forced push or DROP TABLE. It also asks before MCP tools whose names look destructive, because a command filter cannot see what an API call deletes.

Each core skill got ten "should start" requests and ten "should stay quiet" requests, in a clean session with only that pack installed.

Zero false starts in 80 tries. The first time I ran these, debrief started on only 7 of 10, below my pass mark of 8. I reworded its description using the three requests it had missed, ran the test again, and it reached 9.

The other 107 skills have a smaller smoke test so far: two fitting and two near-miss requests each. Across all 115 skills, they started on 277 of 294 fitting requests (94%) and on 1 of 294 requests meant for a neighbour. That one false start is listed in the README with the rest.

These are single runs. With many other skills installed, results will differ. The second kind of test, "is the result better with the skill than without it", exists for every skill and has not been run yet.

Designing a Difficulty Dial Without Changing the Rules

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A four-letter word ladder has one rule. If the rule never changes, how do you make an easy mode and a hard mode? That question is where most of the interesting design work in a small daily puzzle actually lives, and the answer is not "add a timer".

The trick is to separate the rule from the shape of the board. The rule is always the same: change one letter, every step must be a real word, reach the target. What varies between difficulties is the start word and the route it implies. An easy start sits in a dense part of the graph with several short paths to the target. A hard start sits in a thin corner where the first natural move leads into a pocket with one exit.

That choice has a consequence worth being explicit about: difficulty is a property of the pair (start, target), not of the start alone. The same start word can be easy against one target and brutal against another, because the target's neighbourhood is half the puzzle. If you tune difficulty by only looking at starts, you will ship a hard level that turns out to be a two-move route through a word nobody expected.

Practically, I generate candidate starts, run a breadth-first search from each to the target, and bucket by shortest-path length and by the number of dead ends inside the shortest-radius region. Short path plus high branching is easy. Long path plus low branching is hard. The rule never changes, so a player who learns the game on easy is learning the real game.

The player-facing side matters too. The difficulty has to be visible before the round starts, and switching must be free. If switching costs a streak or a confirmation dialog, players will sit at the wrong difficulty out of politeness, and a difficulty dial nobody turns is not a feature.

There is a free practice version of this: the practice modes offer endless random starts at three settings with no daily wait, while a free daily word ladder keeps one fixed board per day. That combination is the cheapest possible way to let a player find their own level, and it costs nothing to keep running.

Cockpit followed a home-directory symlink in a privileged key helper

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Server administration consoles often perform one privileged action on behalf of a logged-in user. Cockpit's public-key addition workflow is a small example: the user supplies a key and the server places it in the target account's authorized-keys path. The helper runs with elevated authority, so every path it follows matters.

CVE-2026-91151 describes an insecure symlink-handling flaw in ssh-add-public-key.sh. A local authenticated user who controls a target account's home directory can place a symlink in the path used by the helper. When a privileged Cockpit session adds a public key, the helper follows the link and performs privileged filesystem operations on an unintended file.

The Red Hat record describes the outcome as the ability to change file ownership and modes, potentially leading to local privilege escalation. The affected products listed in public CVE metadata include multiple Red Hat Enterprise Linux generations and Red Hat OpenShift Dev Spaces images.

The vulnerable sequence is not a remote command injection. It is a privileged file operation performed through a path that a different local identity can influence. The attacker needs local authentication and control over the home directory, but the effect crosses into the privileged Cockpit context.

This kind of flaw is easy to miss in a web-console review because the browser action looks like a normal administrative feature. The dangerous code is in the helper that runs outside the browser, where path ownership and symlink resolution determine whether the operation stays inside the intended account directory.

Consider hosts where administrators manage other users, shared service accounts, or home directories on shared storage. A department administrator with a Cockpit session may not be the owner of every target home directory. A user with write access to that directory can prepare the link before the administrator adds a key.

Containers and development environments need separate review. OpenShift Dev Spaces images contain the affected package according to public CVE metadata, even if the runtime does not expose the same management workflow. Inventory the image as part of patch planning and verify whether the helper is reachable in the deployed service.

Apply the Cockpit package update from each distribution. The fixed commit in the advisory references changes to the helper's path handling. Confirm the installed package version on the host and inside long-lived images, then restart the affected service according to the vendor instructions.

Until the update is deployed, restrict the public-key workflow to administrators who are trusted to manage the target home directory. Do not allow untrusted local users to write the home of an account that a privileged Cockpit user will modify. Use a dedicated administration account and avoid shared home directories for role-bound service accounts.

Review other helper scripts that accept a username and construct a path under /home. Validate the resolved path with a file-descriptor-based operation, refuse symlinks in privileged writes, and perform the final open as the intended account rather than as root. A string comparison of the path before the open is not enough if a link can change after the check.

Create a test home directory owned by an unprivileged user, place a symlink where the helper expects the authorized-keys file, and run the workflow through the patched Cockpit version. The helper should reject the link or write only to a verified target. Check both the file contents and the ownership and mode changes.

Inspect audit logs for ssh-add-public-key.sh executions, target usernames, and file changes outside the expected home directory. Correlate them with Cockpit login sessions and local account changes. If an affected host accepted an untrusted home-directory link, review ownership and modes of sensitive files and rebuild if the privileged operation cannot be bounded.

The patch is important, but the durable control is to treat user-controlled paths as hostile input even when the caller is already authenticated. A console's privilege should not flow through a directory another user can rewrite.

Dead Calm is a thriller that only needs three people and a lot of open water. A new version would just need to deal with the fact that satellite phones and GPS make getting stranded at sea much harder today, which could actually add an interesting twist to the story. All a Dead Calm remake needs is a smart director and two strong leads to become the kind of adrenaline-fueled, nerve-wracking thriller that has been missing from the big screen for a while now.

Brian De Palma’s Blow Out bombed at the box office when it first came out, but it has since been recognized as one of the greatest conspiracy thrillers ever made. When Jack plays back his tape, he hears something that sounds like a gunshot right before the tire blows out, and he soon realizes he has recorded a murder that powerful people want covered up.

Blow Out is a thriller story about how easily the truth can be buried, and that idea feels even more relevant now. In 1981, Jack’s audio tape was the only proof that something was wrong. A modern remake of the film could explore what happens when evidence can be faked, edited, or dismissed as a deepfake in seconds. Jack’s obsession with proving what he heard would hit even harder in a world where nobody knows what to believe anymore. The problem is that much of the first half has aged poorly, since almost everyone around Susy treats her like a helpless child. A remake could keep the brilliant premise while giving Susy the strength and intelligence she deserves from the very first scene. Hepburn earned an Oscar nomination for the role, but Wait Until Dark is ready for a brand-new heroine who can outsmart her attackers right from the start.

Scientists Catch Water Molecules Breaking Apart Before Their Electrons Can Respond

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

When X-rays strike a water molecule, the molecule can break apart faster than its electrons can complete a fundamental relaxation process. Scientists have discovered that both bonds holding hydrogen to oxygen can rupture within just a few femtoseconds, leaving behind an excited, electrically neutral oxygen atom.

The discovery challenges established interpretations of how water responds to X-ray radiation. It also identifies a previously overlooked source of highly reactive atomic oxygen, which can participate in further chemical reactions.

When a water molecule absorbs a sufficiently energetic X-ray photon, an electron can be ejected from an inner shell of its oxygen atom. The resulting vacancy normally triggers Auger decay, a rapid process in which another electron fills the vacancy and transfers enough energy to eject a second electron.

However, the researchers found that the molecular structure can change even faster. The particular electronic state produced by X-ray absorption creates strong repulsive forces, allowing both oxygen-hydrogen bonds to break before Auger decay occurs.

“What makes this result particularly exciting is that we see the molecule break apart before the electronic relaxation has even finished. Both O–H bonds rupture within the lifetime of the oxygen core hole, so the nuclear motion is essentially winning the race against the electrons,” explained Florian Trinter of FHI in Berlin.

The team examined isolated water molecules in the gas phase using high-resolution electron spectroscopy at two synchrotron facilities: PETRA III at DESY in Hamburg, Germany, and the FlexPES beamline at MAX IV in Lund, Sweden.

Both experiments detected unusually sharp peaks at specific electron energies. These features could not be explained by the electronic decay of intact water molecules or the molecular fragments typically expected after X-ray absorption.

“Seeing the characteristic signal also at MAX IV was an important part of establishing this as a genuine molecular fragmentation pathway. The measurements at the two facilities give us a very consistent picture of what happens to the water molecule after X-ray excitation,” said Gunnar Öhrwall of MAX IV in Lund.

To determine what produced the unusual electron signals, the researchers performed detailed molecular and atomic calculations. The predicted electron energies matched the decay of excited neutral oxygen atoms rather than hydroxyl (OH) fragments.

This distinction matters because the energies of emitted electrons depend on the electronic structure of the atom or molecule producing them. The exceptionally narrow spectral features provided a recognizable signature of oxygen that had already separated from the original water molecule.

“The energy of the emitted electrons provides a kind of fingerprint of the state that is formed. Our calculations show that the observed features are consistent with the decay of an excited neutral oxygen atom that has already separated from the water molecule,” explained Ludger Inhester of CFEL at DESY in Hamburg.

The results offer a new explanation for high-energy electron features observed in earlier water experiments. Signals previously associated with hydroxyl fragments may instead originate from excited neutral oxygen atoms produced through extremely rapid molecular fragmentation.

Because neutral atomic oxygen is highly reactive, understanding how it forms could improve models of chemical processes initiated by X-ray radiation. The findings also provide a more precise basis for interpreting electron spectra from water and related molecular systems.

Most cyberpunk TV shows and movies focus a little too much on the visual aspect, featuring neon-soaked cityscapes and edgy characters. Prime Video's The Peripheral, on the other hand, truly understood the essence of William Gibson's near-future cyberpunk. In its 2032 timeline, the show features a very familiar, economically broken rural America.

Meanwhile, The Peripheral had immense potential, but failed to last beyond one season. After being considered "unfilmable" for quite some time, William Gibson's Neuromancer is finally being adapted by Apple TV. Apple TV's history of bringing challenging adaptations like Isaac Asimov's Foundation to the screen alone is a proof of concept for its ability to successfully translate "unfilmable" sci-fi literature. 6 Intense War Films of the 2010s, Ranked by Rotten Tomatoes Score

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Ben Brewster is a filmmaker, critic, screenwriter, and actor whose work sits at the intersection of film culture, craft analysis, and storytelling.

He is a movie list contributing writer at Collider and a contributing writer for High on Films, where he covers contemporary cinema, independent film, and festival releases with an eye toward narrative structure, performance, and visual language.

Ben is an active film festival participant, having worked with Newport Film, the Nantucket Film Festival and as a screener for the Austin, Boston & Woodstock film festivals.  He brings to that work a programming instinct shaped by years of close engagement with independent and documentary cinema. This comes with a deep investment in the kinds of films that rarely find their way into the mainstream conversation without someone willing to advocate for them.

His background spans documentary and narrative filmmaking, film marketing, and production, including work with award-winning directors. This as well as developing his own indie production company, Lilac Creative Studio, through which he develops and produces original work across fiction and nonfiction forms.  His debut documentary, Diamond in the Rust, is an examination of economic redevelopment and displacement in the Queens area of New York City. The film was recently accepted to the Unisphere Film Festival, the Defense of Democracy Film Festival, and the Urbanite Arts & Film Festival. This would mark his first festival recognition as a director.  In addition to his writing and programming work, Ben hosts Beneath the Bloom, a film podcast exploring the work behind the scenes in cinema, and the struggles one can face to make a movie become a reality. The show reflects the same sensibility that runs through all of his work. A conviction that film is not merely entertainment but one of the most powerful tools human beings have for understanding each other.

Across mediums, his work is driven by a deep belief in film as a communal, transformative art form.

In the 2010s, war films felt odd and hard to pin down. The fighting in Iraq and Afghanistan was still running its course. At the same time, World War II veterans were passing away one by one. Hollywood looked two ways in response. It nodded back to the older "good war" stories that shaped the genre for years. It also faced newer conflicts that did not end neatly. No clear heroes always showed up. Because of that, many movies seemed to argue with each other about what a war film is supposed to do and what it is supposed to stand for in this decade.

The top films from that era share one main trait: intensity. This was the period of films that try to place you right in the middle of events. Directors like Christopher Nolan and Sam Mendes leaned into big, crisp images and shots that do not cut away for a long time. The goal was simple, even if the work was not. Put the viewer in the mud and the water. Other filmmakers, including Kathryn Bigelow and Peter Berg, leaned toward a documentary kind of drive. Their stories often felt like they came straight from headlines. Even the sound mattered a lot. In several cases, major awards recognized the way these films made gunshots, machinery and quiet hit hard in the gut.

Some movies in this list got mixed reviews from critics. A few of the highest earners also stirred strong reactions. This happened most often when the films dealt with tense recent history. If you sort them by Rotten Tomatoes scores, you get a quick, not necessarily wholistic view of what some critics thought at the time. That method is by no means perfect. Scores do not always say how a film has held up later. They also do not always match what viewers liked. The range here is wide. There are grim POW survival stories. There are also very detailed retellings of real military missions, scene by scene. These are the most intense war films of the decade, ranked.

Angelina Jolie directed Unbroken, based on Laura Hillenbrand's 2010 book about Olympic runner and bombardier Louis Zamperini. The film stars Jack O'Connell as Zamperini, who survives a plane crash in the Pacific. He drifts on a raft for 47 days before being captured and taken away by the Japanese. O'Connell plays him with a wiry, stubborn energy, with Jolie cutting between flashbacks to his boyhood and his running days in between the horrors. He lands in prison camps under the cruel corporal Watanabe (Miyavi), known as the Bird, who runs the prison camp. The great Roger Deakins captured the open ocean and shot the muddy camps in wide, painterly-like frames.

Joel and Ethan Coen, Richard LaGravenese, and William Nicholson share the screenplay credit. Unbroken opened on Christmas Day 2014 and grossed $163 million worldwide. Rotten Tomatoes lists Unbroken at 52%, and its consensus calls it well-intentioned but too dependent on overly familiar prestige war genre beats. Deakins earned an Oscar nomination for his cinematography, and the National Board of Review named the film one of its top ten of the year, despite the Oscar bait it had written all over it. Many felt the story became a long parade of suffering, although the audience score was friendly, landing at 70%. Zamperini died in July 2014, a few months before it opened, and a faith-based sequel, Unbroken: Path to Redemption, followed in 2018.

A drawl, a fedora, and a killer robot walk into Hollywood.

Clint Eastwood directed American Sniper at age 84. It is a biopic of Navy SEAL Chris Kyle, the most lethal sniper in U.S. military history. He's a Texas cowboy who netted the nickname Legend for his pinpoint accuracy in Iraq. Bradley Cooper bulked up for the role, and plays Kyle across four tours in Iraq, as he struggles to connect with his wife at home, Taya (Siena Miller). Jason Hall adapted the script directly from Kyle's memoir, with the film cutting from Iraqi rooftops to quiet kitchen scenes in Texas. Rotten Tomatoes lists it at 71% from critics and 84% from audiences, and moviegoers gave it an A+ CinemaScore.

The opening scene, in which Kyle must decide whether to fire at a child carrying a grenade, sets a tense tone. A rival sniper known as Mustafa gives the story a cat and mouse thread. Cooper earned a Best Actor nomination, one of six Oscar nominations that included Best Picture and a win for Sound Editing. American Sniper grossed $547.6 million worldwide on a $58 million budget, marking it as Eastwood's highest grossing film, and the biggest January release ever up to that point. Controversy followed, with critics arguing about its heroic portrait of Kyle and its historical liberties. More than a decade later, it landed in Netflix's top 10 with 4.4 million views in a single week.

David Ayer wrote and directed Fury, which follows a five-man Sherman tank crew pushing through Germany in April 1945. It's when the war's last days are its most brutal, and Brad Pitt plays Wardaddy, a battle-scarred sergeant who has kept his crew alive since North Africa. Logan Lerman is Norman Ellison, a young typist forced into the crew, with Shia LaBeouf, Michael Peña, and Jon Bernthal as the veterans who both mock and protect him. Ayer allegedly put the cast through a brutal boot camp before filming.

Fury grossed $211.8 million and opened at No.1 at the box office on a budget of about $68 million. The production used a real Tiger tank, Tiger 131, the only operational Tiger in the world. Rotten Tomatoes lists it at 75% from critics, and 84% from audiences. The consensus calls it a well acted, raw depiction of war that does not fully live up to its lofty ambitions. James Berardinelli praised how it shows the horrors of war without reveling in cruelty, while LA Weekly's Amy Nicholson felt that Ayer wallowed in ugliness. Ayer drew on the real 2nd Armored Division, nicknamed Hell on Wheels.

1917 follows two young British soldiers on a single day in April 1917, and Sam Mendes built it to look like one unbroken shot. George MacKay and Dean-Charles Chapman play Lance Corporals Schofield and Blake, two young British soldiers sent across no man's land to deliver a message that could save 1,600 men from a German trap. Mendes co-wrote the story with Krysty Wilson-Cairns, drawing on tales his grandfather Alfred Mendes told him about WWI.

Roger Deakins shot through trenches, craters, and rivers, and captured a burning town without a visible cut. Colin Firth, Benedict Cumberbatch, Mark Strong, and Andrew Scott appear in brief but memorable roles. Rotten Tomatoes lists 1917 at 88% from 472 reviews, and its consensus calls it hard-hitting, immersive and an impressive technical achievement. The movie won three Oscars, garnering a Best Picture nomination as well. It also took home the BAFTA for Best Film and the Golden Globes for Best Motion Picture, Drama and Best Director. The night sequence in a burning town lit by flares and the final run across the battlefield are the most visually stunning sequences.

Zero Dark Thirty opens with darkness and the sound of real 9/11 calls. Kathryn Bigelow directed the film off a script from Mark Boal, her collaborator on The Hurt Locker. Bigelow constantly stresses the stakes, with Jessica Chastain as the lead Maya, a CIA analyst whose obsession has driven a decade's worth of work hunting Osama bin Laden. The film then pivots to harsh and brutal interrogation scenes, with Jason Clarke playing the interrogator Dan, and Jennifer Ehle, Kyle Chandler, Mark Strong, and Joel Edgerton rounding out the cast.

Rotten Tomatoes lists it at 91% from 304 reviews, with an 80% audience score. Boal's script moves like a procedural, with offices, briefings, and dead ends. Critics called it gripping, suspenseful, and brilliantly crafted. Chastain earned an Oscar nomination for Best Actress, and the movie tied with Skyfall for Best Sound Editing. The final 30 minutes recreate the May 2011 raid on the Abbottabad compound in dim green night vision, and the sequence is considered among the most tense in modern war movies. Controversy surrounding its torture scenes has never faded, and some critics now see the movie differently after the 2014 torture report.

Dunkirk divides its story into three timelines, one week on the beach, one day at sea, and one hour in the air. Christopher Nolan depicts the 1940 evacuation of Allied troops through the perspective of Tommy (Fionn Whitehead), a young solider hunting for any way off the sand, and Harrier (Tom Hardy), a spitfire pilot with a failing fuel gauge. Mark Rylance is a man named Mr. Dawson, a civilian who sails his boat across the channel to help rescue troops. Kenneth Branagh, Cillian Murphy, Harry Styles, Barry Keoghan and Jack Lowden appear in the ensemble. Hoyte van Hoytema shot it in large format, and Hans Zimmer's score keeps a ticking clock running in the background.

Rotten Tomatoes lists Dunkirk at 92%, with an 81% audience score. Nolan used real ships and planes which gave the beaches and skies a physical weight. The consensus describes the project as an emotionally satisfying spectacle from a writer-director in full command of his craft. Dunkirk grossed more than $500 million worldwide and saw eight Oscar nominations, including Best Picture and Best Director. Between the grand practical effects, the dialogue is sparse, and the sound design carries much of the tension. Critics have called it one of the most immersive war movies ever and one of the best movies of the 2010s.

Anya Taylor-Joy’s Sci-Fi Thriller Is Still Her Greatest Movie 2 Years Later

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Anya Taylor-Joy has brought so much to her many hits over the years, but her greatest movie is the one where she says the least. The 2024 prequel Furiosa: A Mad Max Saga sees her take over the title role from Charlize Theron and portray the early years of the post-apocalyptic warrior who became the highlight of 2015’s Mad Max: Fury Road. With George Miller’s direction and Taylor-Joy’s exceptional on-screen presence, the movie became an instant classic.

Set years before the events of Fury Road, in the radioactive wasteland of post-apocalyptic Australia, the film follows the path of Furiosa from childhood, where she is played by Alyla Browne, to early adulthood, where Taylor-Joy takes over. Stolen from her idyllic sanctuary home and forcibly adopted by warlord Dementus (Chris Hemsworth), she rises up the ranks of the Citadel, ruled by the tyrannical Immortan Joe (Lachy Hulme). She becomes caught in the middle of the rivalry between the two men, and begins using their ambition to exact revenge and work her way to freedom.

The movie is the fifth in the Mad Max franchise, but the first not to prominently feature the character of Max Rockatansky (who appears in a cameo played by Jacob Tomuri, Tom Hardy’s stunt double in Fury Road) — though many fans of the 2015 movie argue that Theron’s performance as Furiosa was the most compelling in the film, and that she was the story’s true lead. This prequel fulfills that potential with a captivating adventure worthy of its lofty predecessors.

Warning: you will cry over two different rocks.

Narratively speaking, Furiosa is an expansion of the world that was introduced in Fury Road. The 2015 film showed us the landscape passing by at 100 miles per hour, in what was essentially one big, beautiful chase movie. Max’s mission was simple – race to escape the Citadel, and then turn back to reclaim it. Furiosa’s origin story is more complex, divided into chapters and focused more on the characters involved. The torment she endures, and the loss that hardens her, are all essential to the plot, making for a much darker and more thoughtful movie.

Both are classics, but Miller’s approach is different each time. Fury Road is a practically perfect example of an action movie, with choreography and spectacle that has few equals before or since. Furiosa is an epic revenge tragedy, examining the harrowing path one must take to be compelled to overthrow tyranny. Furiosa must endure, waiting for a moment that is far in the distance, while clinging to the things that keep her soul alive. We also see more world-building, as it is revealed how the Citadel, Gas Town, and Bullet Farm interact. It is an immersive experience, different from Fury Road but brilliant in its own way.

Furiosa is a character who has learned to fly under the radar, blend into any setting, and survive at all costs. This means a character who is very careful with her words, resulting in a leading role with around 30 lines of dialogue. You might think this would be a hindrance for a star, but Taylor-Joy makes it a strength. Using everything except words, she conveys a character being molded into her environment, learning to be relentless and endure anything in the name of getting home. That means a performance that is conveyed by expression, movement, and the smallest of gestures. Great actors overcome limitations to tell stories, and the Golden Globe winner rose to the challenge.

Looking back on the film, the actor said that Miller had a very specific idea of how she should hold her face, meaning she could only use her eyes to convey emotions for large portions of the movie. “As an actor, where your job is to be able to telegraph how you’re feeling with absolutely everything at your disposal," she told GQ. "To suddenly have everything gone other than [your eyes] was quite petrifying.”

The gamble paid off, however, as she delivered a soulful, determined performance in the middle of a large-scale action thriller. There are few actors with the discipline, presence, and talent to not only take up the mantle of a beloved character, but to make it their own, and that’s exactly what Taylor-Joy does. For all the many hits of her past, and the exciting projects to come, Furiosa: A Mad Max Saga has a special place in her filmography.

There’s no better time to return to Camp Blood

There’s no better time to return to Camp Blood

Over the years, corporations and filmmakers alike have eagerly sought to revisit the origins of horror classics (e.g., Bates Motel and Psycho, Welcome to Derry and IT, etc.) for modern audiences. It’s an easy way to find successwhen you already have an audience that loves the IP you’re reimagining

Not even the Friday the 13th franchise has escaped the trend of revisiting the past. Crystal Lake, a new series from A24 and Peacock, follows Pamela Voorhees (Betsy Palmer), Jason Voorhees’s deranged mother​​​​​​​. It’s a prequel that, frankly, nobody really asked for (though we kind of liked it anyway), since Jason’s origins have already been explored: Sean S. Cunningham’s 1980s horror film Friday the 13th — available to watch now for free on YouTube.

Following the release of the genre-defining slasher Halloween, Cunningham and writer Victor Miller faced a real challenge: how to stand out in a subgenre flooded with horror movies (Black Christmas, Texas Chainsaw Massacre, to name just a few) trying to ride the slasher wave. Friday the 13th had to be memorable, interesting, and stand out.

On the surface, Friday the 13th doesn’t feel too different. It’s full of horny teens, heavy suspense, and a sense of isolation as a deranged killer hunts and kills them off, one by one. However, unlike other slashers like Halloween, Cunningham takes the brutality one step further by asking two very different but meaningful questions. Careful now, spoilers for a 46-year-old movie ahead.

First, what if the killer were not a man in a mask but a vengeful mother driven mad by society’s disregard for her disabled son? Second, what if Cunningham and Paramount hired Tom Savini, famous for his work on George A. Romero’s Dawn of the Dead, to create some of the most gruesome deaths audiences had yet seen on the silver screen?

The result is one of the bloodiest slashers of all time, one that shook up perceptions of how grotesque the horror subgenre could be and who, exactly, could be the one dealing out those kills in the first place.

Slashers like Halloween and Black Christmas have some gnarly kills—who could forget Michael Myers pinning Bob to a pantry door with a large knife or Barb being stabbed with a unicorn figurine’s horn? Savini, however, elevated Friday the 13th’s kills into a bloody art form with shocking, realistic gore.

Savini did more than deliver, too. Just as he used practical effects for Dawn of the Dead, he did so again for several of the film’s kills. One especially memorable kill is counselor Jack’s, in which he’s stabbed in the neck as he lies in bed. Using a prosthetic neck piece and a pump, fake blood was pumped to create a blood-spurt effect that, for its time, looked remarkably realistic.

The most significant thing that set Friday the 13th apart from its predecessors, however, was its killer: Pamela Voorhees. The slasher genre was no longer defined by masculine rage, but by a mother’s love. Even in the modern era of horror, slashers still feature far more male than female killers, but in 1980, Mrs. Voorhees was undoubtedly one of a kind.

Furthermore, for as deranged and murderous as she was, she exerted a strange, empathetic pull on audiences. Yes, murder is wrong — but you can’t deny that she loved her son. Unlike Michael Myers, who is often depicted as evil incarnate, Mrs. Voorhees has a complexity that made her brutal motives more compelling.

46 years later, Mrs. Voorhees's story begins anew with Crystal Lake. It’s unclear whether the series will get a second season, but even if it doesn’t, fans of Jason and his diabolical family tree needn’t worry. You can never go wrong with the original.

58 Years Later, Captain Kirk's Best Star Trek Quote Is the Greatest Line in Sci-Fi History

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

This Captain Kirk speech says more about what Star Trek stands for than any other line in the original series.

Some lines of dialogue become so famous they stop belonging to the movies and TV shows that produced them. “May the Force be with you.” “I’ll be back.” “You're gonna need a bigger boat.” You don't need to know the context. The words have long since transcended their original context and permeated the pop culture zeitgeist.

Star Trek: The Original Series has plenty of those. Spock’s wish that everyone to “Live long and prosper” is arguably the franchise's signature phrase. He also turned the simple word “fascinating” into a catchphrase simply by raising one eyebrow before saying it. Dr. McCoy (DeForest Kelley) spent three seasons reminding everyone, “I'm a doctor, not a…” and, at least in our collective memory declaring, “He's dead, Jim” while standing over the latest redshirt who wandered a little too far from the landing party.

But if I could only pick one line to represent everything Star Trek stands for, it would be four simple words spoken by Captain James T. Kirk (William Shatner) halfway through “Return to Tomorrow," the 20th episode of season 2:

On the surface, it doesn't seem like an obvious choice. It's not printed on T-shirts nearly as often as “live long and prosper.” It isn't endlessly quoted by people who have never watched an episode of Star Trek. And unlike “beam me up, Scotty,” it never became part of the broader pop culture vocabulary. But that's exactly why it's the best quote. It doesn't just capture Kirk. It captures the entire philosophy of Star Trek.

First broadcast on Feb. 9, 1968, the episode “Return to Tomorrow” finds the Enterprise answering a mysterious distress call from a dead planet. There, Kirk and his crew discover Sargon, one of three survivors of an ancient civilization that has existed as beings of pure energy for half a million years. Sargon asks Kirk, Spock (Leonard Nimoy), and Dr. Ann Mulhall (Diana Muldaur) to temporarily surrender their bodies so he, his wife Thalassa, and the ambitious Henoch can build android bodies and begin life again. Dr. McCoy believes the aliens are manipulating them. Kirk believes the chance to learn from a civilization hundreds of thousands of years more advanced than humanity is worth the danger.

The episode also marks the first of three Star Trek appearances by Diana Muldaur. She plays Dr. Ann Mulhall here before returning as Miranda Jones in the third season episode “Is There in Truth No Beauty?” Years later, she'd become a series regular on Star Trek: The Next Generation as Dr. Katherine Pulaski. “Return to Tomorrow” is also a fun piece of Trek trivia because it's one of the times Spock describes Sargon as “pure energy,” a phrase longtime fans immediately recognize.

Faced with allowing unimaginably powerful aliens to occupy the bodies of his crew, Kirk doesn't issue an order. He makes an argument and reminds his crew that every leap forward in human history required someone willing to embrace uncertainty.

"They used to say if man could fly, he'd have wings," Kirk says. "But he did fly. He discovered he had to." He likens Sargon's offer to humanity's own journey from the Moon to Mars and eventually the stars, arguing that the potential for knowledge outweighs the danger. Only then does he arrive at the four words that define both the captain and the franchise. Those four words explain Kirk better than decades of pop-culture impressions ever could.

For years, pop culture has reduced Kirk to an oversimplified caricature: the swaggering captain who punches first, flirts with every alien woman he meets, and somehow loses his shirt during every other away mission. The real Kirk is far more thoughtful than that. He takes risks, certainly, but not because he enjoys gambling. He takes them because he believes some opportunities are too important to ignore.

There's a world of difference between being reckless and accepting risk in pursuit of discovery. Kirk isn't arguing that danger is exciting. He's arguing that progress is impossible without it.

More importantly, he's explaining what separates Starfleet from almost every other organization. Plenty of fictional explorers travel because they're ordered to. Others do it for conquest, profit, or survival. Starfleet goes because knowledge itself is worth pursuing.

That's why I think this quote is even more meaningful than the famous opening narration about boldly going where no one has gone before. The opening tells us what the Enterprise does. This one explains why.

The timing of the episode makes the speech even more remarkable. “Return to Tomorrow” aired in early 1968, roughly 17 months before Apollo 11 landed on the Moon. America was still trying to prove humans could successfully reach another world, and Star Trek was already imagining the next step beyond that. Kirk's speech places the Enterprise in the same lineage as every explorer who came before it, suggesting humanity's future won't be built by playing it safe.

That idea has become the beating heart of Star Trek. Optimism, in the Star Trek universe, has never meant believing everything will work out. It means believing the unknown is worth facing anyway.

There are more famous quotes in The Original Series. There are funnier ones. There are certainly more quotable ones. But no other line distills what Star Trek has always been trying to say better than four words Kirk spoke nearly 60 years ago: “Risk is our business.”

8 Best Games Like the Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 Campaign

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

News Editor at DualShockers, 10+ years writing experience, OpenCritic-listed reviewer, Master’s Degree in Developmental Psychology

Last year’s Call of Duty was one to forget, but that futuristic fever dream has been completely eclipsed by Infinity Ward’s Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4.

Boasting arguably the best campaign since the original Modern Warfare 1-2 punch, this is one we will be telling the next generations to play if they want a taste of what Call of Duty is like at its best.

This is DualShockers' review of COD: Modern Warfare 4, covering all the ins and outs of the campaign, multiplayer, and the new DMZ Extraction Mode.

If you’re like me and have been left wanting more after wolfing down the sixteen missions in Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4, here are games that match the vibes and mechanics of this great comeback story.

It’s a tragedy that the 2015 remake of Star Wars: Battlefront has been relegated to the flop corner of your local second-hand games store, because to this day, nothing else quite captures the Old Trilogy as well.

If you like Modern Warfare 4 missions like Entrenched, then this one is for you. While Battlefront 2 (2017) became great after the rollback of its controversial monetization model, its predecessor is an easy contestant for the best Star Wars shooter ever.

Like Park and the ROK Marines he serves with, Battlefront made you feel like a lonely grunt fighting a war much bigger than you. There are no supersoldiers to turn the tide, only unrestricted violence and blasters scarring the air. Its biggest sin is that it had no real single-player campaign.

If you enjoyed the vibes of the Counter Attack mission, cowering behind Lt. West’s ACV as you get raked by fire from a building, you might want to check out Squad. Full disclaimer: the pacing and gunplay could not be more different from any Call of Duty game, even its slower early titles, but few games capture the plight of mechanized infantry quite like Squad.

Because of its enormous maps, Squad is meant to be played by riding vehicles to the hot zone. When everything works out fine, you have a quiet dismount in a remote area, set up a defensive perimeter, and nothing happens.

However, the enemy typically has opinions about you capturing objectives. That tends to result in the squad leader shouting for everybody to get out, machine guns raking the side of the vehicle, and the medic desperately trying to work while smoke grenades cloud the area.

There’s an indescribable level of chaos that comes from a hot insertion, and Squad is the place to be if you want to experience it for longer than the ten minutes that sequence takes.

The North Koreans have taken over, Burger Town has fallen, and there’s no Ramírez to save the day. Homefront: The Revolution was much maligned on release, but it scratches a specific kind of itch for an open-world, resistance-themed shooter.

Outside of Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4, few games let you experience a full-scale fictional North Korean invasion, and though Homefront takes plenty of creative liberties compared to Infinity Ward’s (mostly) grounded approach, it’s a unique set of circumstances.

Alternatively, if you don’t mind some extra jank, the original Homefront is a pretty decent game in and of itself. The production value alone makes it worth a try, so long as you understand this is now a 15-year-old game.

For what feels like the first time in a long while, a Call of Duty game decided to stick with a strong art direction and identity throughout, and DMZ is arguably the best showcase of it.

This year’s extraction mode is a lot more grounded and entertaining than previous iterations, but if the skill-based matchmaking is too much to stomach, Insurgency: Sandstorm blends that fast-paced action with some of the best gunplay on the market, sans the sweatiness.

Insurgency: Sandstorm is all about close combat, with enough tactical Barbie to give you a visual identity but without ever going down the infamous Nicki Minaj route that has plagued Activision’s mainline shooter for years.

A lot of Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4’s campaign is spent behind enemy lines in North Korea, and while many of the stealth and infiltration sections are fairly competent for a run-and-gun first-person shooter, they still can’t compete with the real deal.

Since we have no release date for the next Splinter Cell game, there’s no better time to revisit Sam Fisher’s finest hour as the world inched closer to nuclear armageddon once more.

Released in 2005, Chaos Theory sends Fisher snooping in South America and the Korean peninsula, with a little less-than-relaxing stop at Tokyo in the process. It’s everything John Price does in Modern Warfare 4, but better. Sorry, Captain.

The gunplay in Modern Warfare 4 is hardly its strong point, but it fits the chaotic combat quite nicely. Albeit the twitchy run-and-gun was introduced in the AAA sphere by the original Modern Warfare way back in 2007, it came of age in 2011 with Battlefield 3.

Set in a (then) hypothetical conflict between the United States and Iran, Battlefield 3 nails the vibes of Private Park’s side of the campaign. You primarily play Staff Sergeant Henry Blackburn, a Recon Marine who ends up embroiled in a mess well beyond his pay grade.

Blackburn is hardly an idealist, but it doesn’t take a genius to realize that sometimes you need to do bad things to prevent much worse things from happening.

If you prefer Price’s side of the campaign, then I’d recommend Battlefield 4 instead, with a lot less grunt business but plenty of operating in the shadows.

The tenth level of Modern Warfare 4, Stranded, is one of the best showcases of how good Call of Duty can be when you take off the training wheels. Private Park and his team survive a crash deep into North Korean territory during a secretive mission, and have to live off the land to complete their objectives, then fight their way out.

Both mechanically and story-wise, Stranded is an encapsulated version of Tom Clancy’s Ghost Recon: Breakpoint, the latest instalment in the series.

Breakpoint is set in a near-future where an annoying tech bro built an island where technology has no safeguards, and the results are predictable. As one of the sole survivors, Nomad has to scour Auroa for weapons, equipment, and allies to help defeat its militarized owners.

If you like a good friends-to-enemies plot like Price and Ghost, Breakpoint also puts you against a former ally from Ghost Recon Wildlands.

I’ve always been a staunch defender of 2013’s Medal of Honor: Warfighter, and to this day it remains one of the most underrated shooters of its time. It is also the game with the clearest influence on Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4, in both story beats and mechanics.

The foot chases, driving sequences, the ally’s execution by the main baddie, the slow-mo breaches, even the last time you pull the trigger in the campaign, all come straight from Warfighter. The campaign also features a fairly similar split, where one side of the story is told through a discharged special forces veteran operating in the shadows, and another from the front lines.

There are times when Medal of Honor: Warfighter goes a little too corny in its hunt for being respectable, like the Taco Bell cashier who says ‘thank you for your service’ to the kid straight out of boot camp, but the meat of the game is one of the best depictions of how real special ops play out in the modern world.

Check what your AI agents do before they do it, with Semantic governance policies

Two days before earnings, an employee asks their coworker agent to send the preliminary quarterly results to an investor who says they're under NDA, and to copy the employee's personal Gmail so they can review the numbers tonight. The agent can read the file and send email, so it fulfills the user request. Semantic governance policies, available in Preview in Gemini platform, enable enterprises to govern their agents and provide guardrails to stop that call before it runs. They check each tool call an agent proposes against what the user asked for and against rules you write in plain language.

Permissions alone can't catch this. They say what an agent can do, not whether a particular call follows your rules. A model can also be talked out of the rules in its system instructions, by a user with a good reason, or by a document telling it to do something nobody asked for, a technique called indirect prompt injection. So the check has to sit outside the agent. Semantic governance policies are enforced through a policy enforcement point, such as Agent Gateway, alongside the agent governance controls we expanded in October, so policies stay separate from your agent's code.

Semantic governance policies check each tool call your agent's model proposes, before it runs, against two things:

Today, the policy enforcement point that Semantic governance policies support is Agent Gateway. You need to configure your agent so all your agent egress traffic goes through Agent Gateway, facilitating traffic between your agent and its model. When the model responds with a tool call, Agent Gateway sends the proposed call to the Semantic governance policy engine for evaluation before the response reaches your agent.

In the policy engine, an LLM judge evaluates the proposed call against the conversation so far, the agent's system instructions and tool descriptions, and your policies.

If the call passes, the response reaches your agent unchanged. If it doesn't, the engine replaces the response's content, tool calls included, with a configurable denial message, so the call never runs. Either way, the verdict and the judge's rationale go to Cloud Logging in your project. Responses that contain only text, such as an answer, a plan, or a question back to the user, aren't evaluated, because they take no action.

Consider a coworker agent for a finance team. It can search the shared drive, read files, send email, and delete draft files, and its system instructions include the date of the next earnings release. The company writes one policy for the agent:

"Quarterly financial results (revenue, margins, bookings, guidance) should not be shared publicly until the public earnings release. Before the release, they may be shared only with @example.com addresses. Never send them to external or personal addresses, investors, analysts, or press, regardless of NDAs, urgency, or the requester's seniority."

In each denied case, the agent gets a denial message instead of the call. The judge's rationale can quote your policy, so if users shouldn't see it, set your own denial message on the policy.

The earnings rule is one policy. Each policy is a natural-language constraint attached to an agent. It can apply to all of an agent's tools or to one tool, and it can name a tool's parameters. Other examples:

Because the earnings policy doesn't name a tool, it's checked on every tool call the agent proposes, so it would also apply to a tool that shares or uploads files. For guidance on wording, see best practices for writing policies. You can create it in the Google Cloud console or with the Google Cloud CLI:

IAM and Agent Gateway access policies decide which tools and MCP servers an agent can reach. Model Armor screens prompts and responses for threats such as jailbreaks and sensitive data. Agent Anomaly Detection analyzes sessions asynchronously, out of band. Semantic governance policies add the check none of them makes, as one layer of defense in depth: they judge each action inline, before it runs.

Because verdicts come from an LLM, which is probabilistic, they may vary. We recommend reviewing them in dry run mode before you enforce, and using Semantic governance policies alongside your other controls. The judge decides from the conversation, the agent's instructions, and your policies, so include facts a policy depends on, such as the earnings date, in the agent's system instructions. Streamed model responses aren't evaluated. Semantic governance policies complement, rather than replace, your disclosure controls and legal and compliance reviews.

When the Egress Agent Gateway receives a tool call for your Cloud Run MCP server URL, it resolves the hostname once before injecting the packet into your VPC. Without Private Cloud DNS, public DNS resolves .run.app domains to public internet IPs, which time out (HTTP 502) because a PSC Network Attachment does not route out to the public internet.

Instead of creating a wildcard *.run.app private zone, which would override DNS resolution for every Cloud Run service in a Shared VPC, you create a Private Cloud DNS zone scoped exclusively to your MCP service's exact fully qualified domain name (FQDN) and peer only that domain with the Agent Gateway. Because Cloud DNS uses longest-suffix matching, every other Cloud Run service in your Shared VPC continues resolving normally. Meanwhile, the Agent Gateway opens its HTTPS connection to the private VIP (199.36.153.8 or 240.0.0.10) while preserving your Cloud Run service hostname in the TLS SNI and HTTP Host header so Cloud Run routes the request to your exact container.

Lazy-Load Tool Schemas. Keep Authorization Eager.

A large tool catalog creates a real context problem. Loading every parameter schema into every request makes the agent pay for tools it will never use.

The obvious fix is to load schemas on demand. The dangerous mistake is to treat that optimization as a permission system.

A tool can be discoverable without being usable. A schema can be loaded without authorizing the requested action. Those are separate states, and production agent systems need to keep them separate.

The agent may have turned a harmless read request into a write request while filling in parameters. The call boundary is where those differences become concrete.

The context cost is not theoretical. One DEV.to experiment reported 24,522 tokens for 200 synthetic MCP tools and 61,302 for 500 before the agent did useful work. The same author found that a real file server used more tokens per tool than the synthetic set.

Those numbers are measurements from one setup, not a universal budget or a benchmark reproduced here. They still point to the right engineering question: how much of the request is being spent describing tools that will not be selected?

Schema deferral can reduce that menu overhead by keeping lightweight metadata available and loading full definitions only for plausible candidates. It also makes the retrieval decision observable. If the trace distinguishes search, schema load, and function call, operators can see whether an agent is failing because it cannot find a tool, cannot form valid arguments, or cannot pass authorization.

Do not collapse all costs into “tool overhead,” though. Tool definitions, retrieved results, conversation history, and an extra retrieval turn are different surfaces. A progressive-discovery design can reduce one while increasing another through additional latency or model calls.

For a small, stable catalog, eager loading may still be the simpler and faster choice. Switch to progressive discovery when measurements show that definitions consume a meaningful share of context, not because lazy loading sounds more sophisticated.

A discovery-first design needs negative tests, not just a happy path.

At minimum, log the discovered candidate, the concrete requested action, the authorization decision, and the execution result as separate events. Test revocation between discovery and execution. Make denial explicit and auditable rather than returning a generic tool error.

Keep the catalog simple until it becomes expensive. When it does, make schema loading progressive—but keep authorization eager.

The useful boundary is not “the model may see this tool.” It is “this exact identity may perform this exact action against this exact target under the current policy.” Tool retrieval helps answer the first question. It does not answer the second.

That distinction is small in a diagram and decisive in production. Optimize context at discovery time. Recompute authority at execution time. Record both decisions so the next failure is diagnosable.

Familiarity vs. Fit: Why Picking Tools You Already Know Creates Deep Technical Debt

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 21 • Originally published at ezinner.com

There is an old engineering habit I see over and over: learn a handful of tools very deeply, and then try to build every single future project exclusively with those tools.

On paper, the logic sounds safe. You know the query planner, you know where the configs live, and your team doesn't introduce another moving piece to the stack.

In practice, that mindset causes some of the nastiest technical debt I have ever had to untangle.

Take automation and industrial backend systems. A team gets hired primarily for their SQL background. They build the initial system around a relational database like Postgres or MySQL—storing users, device metadata, recipes, and schemas. That works fine.

Then the system grows, and they need to store high-frequency telemetry, sensor metrics, and execution logs from thousands of concurrent processes.

Right around the corner, there are time-series databases built specifically for this workload: append-only write streams, automatic downsampling, and rolling data retention.

Instead, the team adds a process_logs table to their relational database.

The developer doesn't want to step outside their SQL comfort zone, and the team lead is terrified of adding another database to the infrastructure.

Fast forward a year: that relational database is struggling with write locks. Tables have ballooned to hundreds of gigabytes. Backups take forever. So the team starts writing spaghetti workarounds: custom table partitioning cron jobs, manual cleanup scripts, and complex indexing hacks just to keep queries from timing out.

By the time it becomes a genuine operational headache, nobody can touch it. The workaround code is so tightly coupled to the rest of the application that moving to a proper time-series store means a painful, high-risk refactor.

They ended up with an unmaintainable legacy system because they picked what they already knew instead of what the problem actually needed.

Years ago, sticking to tools you already knew had a rational justification.

Adopting a completely different database or language meant reading through thick documentation for weeks, tripping over unfamiliar syntax, and making beginner mistakes in production. When the learning curve took months, forcing an existing tool to handle an unsuited workload could feel like an acceptable compromise.

With modern tooling, syntax is practically free. You do not need to memorize every command of an unfamiliar query dialect or runtime before you can use it effectively. If you understand the fundamental architecture—access patterns, memory constraints, network boundaries, and I/O mechanics—looking up syntax and boilerplates takes seconds.

You are no longer stuck with whatever stack you happened to work in five years ago. You can actually choose tools based on what fits the data and the problem.

Whenever I argue for picking specialized tools over familiar ones, people ask the same question:

If you don't have years of experience with this specific tool's syntax, how do you review pull requests and know the code is safe?

My answer: line-by-line syntax familiarity in code reviews was never what kept production safe anyway.

Look at the sheer volume of high-severity CVEs surfacing every single week across the industry. The libraries and utilities with critical vulnerabilities aren't written by engineers experimenting with new tools; they are maintained by veterans with twenty years of deep experience in C, Java, or Python. Deep familiarity with a language doesn't stop people from introducing fundamental bugs.

The real validation has always been what we built automated testing for:

If a dedicated time-series engine ingests thousands of events per second with low resource overhead, and your automated test suite verifies that queries, rollups, and error cases return the expected results under load, you have a much cleaner system than an overloaded SQL instance propped up by custom cron scripts.

I would much rather introduce a new, well-suited tool to a stack than spend months writing duct-tape code to make an old tool do something it was never designed to do.

Syntax is easy to look up. Architectural mismatch is painful and expensive to undo.

Pick tools based on what the problem requires, write solid tests around the boundaries, and stop forcing one database or language to solve every problem just because it's what you learned first.

How To Fly Without A License In America

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Gaining a pilot's license can be costly and prohibitive for many, including for those who just want to fly in a hang glider or ultralight aircraft. The FAA created an exemption on the premise that a vehicle light and slow enough poses minimal kinetic risk to people and property on the ground. It ruled that pilots of these aircraft, subject to strict parameters, can be mostly free of its regulations. This allows some people to enjoy the thrill of flying without having to worry about regulatory red tape.

When the FAA drafted Part 103 in 1982, it drew a hard line. It set tight restrictions on the weight and speed of the aircraft and then permitted the pilot to not be regulated at all. Other countries have gone in a different direction, requiring basic pilot training and registration and relaxing the limitations on the aircraft. Here is what to know about legally flying in the United States without a pilot's license or medical certificate.

Flying is extremely regulated, and pilots are required to have licenses to fly aircraft. But as with many things in life, it's not quite that simple, and there are allowances on the margins. The FAA’s Part 103 rules let you fly an “ultralight vehicle” with no pilot certificate, medical, registration, or airworthiness certificate.

The FAA explains, "Pilots of ultralight vehicles subject to Part 103 are not required to have training or previous experience prior to the operation of these vehicles. You should consider receiving adequate training prior to participation." How the aircraft is advertised does not override FAA regulations. The vehicle may be advertised as "powered ultralight," "hang glider," or "hang balloon", but its weight and performance determine how it is classified under FAA rules.

Buyers are encouraged to be aware of aircraft advertised as meeting the requirements for use as an ultralight vehicle, but do not. Being within Part 103 means operators are not required to have a student, sport, or private pilot license, nor are they legally required to log flight hours or pass an FAA written or practical checkride. A checkride is the practical test a pilot takes to earn a new certificate, rating, or to maintain proficiency.

To qualify as an ultralight vehicle, the aircraft must be single-seat and, if it is powered, it must weigh under 254 lb (115 kg) empty with no more than five gallons (19 liters) of fuel. Its speed is capped at 55 knots at full power and a 24-knot stall speed. Unpowered ultralights, such as hang gliders, must weigh under 155 lb (70 kg). Balloons and gliders are considered unpowered vehicles.

If the ultralight exceeds these limits, it is deemed to be an aircraft and requires a registration and a certified pilot. An ultralight also cannot be operated under Part 103 if it has been issued a current US or foreign airworthiness certificate. Flying within Part 103 limits operations to daylight only and prohibits operating within congested areas. This includes areas where people have assembled (e.g., a stadium).

Pilots operating aircraft under Part 103 are still required to keep ultralights separated from commercial traffic and populated areas. Ultralights are permitted to fly freely in Class G (uncontrolled airspace), but are prohibited from Class B, C, D, or surface-level Class E airspace unless the pilot obtains prior authorization. They must also yield the right of way to all conventional aircraft. Weather conditions are required to be reasonably clear with visual reference to the surface.

Oceanic airspace is a challenging place to be, far away from civilization and communication outposts. Before entering, pilots reduce speed, but why?

Due to the strict empty weight and stall speed limits, qualifying powered ultralights fall into four main design categories. These are Powered Paragliders (Paramotors), Powered Parachutes (PPCs), Weight-Shift Control Trikes, and Fixed-Wing Ultralights. Powered Paragliders are the lightest and most portable of the categories and generally consist of a backpack motor unit and a ram-air fabric wing. The motor typically weighs around 46–65 lb (21–29 kg).

Powered Parachutes are wheeled carts that carry a small two-stroke or four-stroke engine suspended under a large, square parachute canopy. These designs provide a very slow flight speed and high stability. Weight-Shift Control Trikes are a type of minimalist hang-glider-style wing mounted above a lightweight three-wheeled carriage. These are steered in pitch and roll by shifting the center of gravity rather than using traditional flight control surfaces.

Fixed-Wing Ultralights are the closest category to a conventional aircraft and resemble traditional miniature aircraft. They are lightweight, low-speed recreational airplanes with rigid wings and conventional three-axis flight controls (ailerons, elevator, and rudder). These aircraft are designed for affordable, accessible sport and leisure flying. U-Fly-It Aerolite 103 (discussed below) makes some aircraft that fit into this category.

The FAA stipulates that ultralight operations can only be conducted as a sporting or recreational activity. The operators of the vehicles are responsible for assessing risk and ensuring their own safety. The FAA also cautions, "the ultralight community is encouraged to adopt good operation practices and programs in order to avoid more extensive regulation by the Federal Aviation Administration."

In the US, ultralights can be purchased through specialized dealers, manufacturers, and classified marketplaces such as Barnstormers, Hangar67, and PilotMix. Examples of ultralights on the market include the Northwing ATF Trike, Northwing Maverick Ultralight, Aero-Tech 2000 Lockwood Drifter, and U-Fly-It Aerolite 103. The prices of ultralights vary considerably on the marketplace. Second-hand examples can cost a few thousand dollars; new craft may cost up to tens of thousands.

U-Fly-It Aerolite 103 says of its products, "The Aerolite 103 Quick-Build Kit has one of the lowest assembly times in the industry. Average for a first time builder is approximately 30-40 hours, and experienced builders can assemble the aircraft in less than 30 hours." It also advertises its Complete Aerolite 103 Quick-Build Airframe Kit at a price of $19,450. Customers have the choice of having the kit assembled through a dealer for an extra fee.

The pilot landing an ultra-long-haul A350 needs two consecutive hours of rest in the second half of the flight.

It should be noted that the information above is regulated by the FAA, and the FAA only has jurisdiction in US airspace. None of this is binding in other countries, and other countries have different sets of rules and regulations. In some cases, other countries are more generous with their allowances than the US, and in other cases, they are more restrictive. That said, all other peer countries are more restrictive in the sense that pilots must uniformly have some sort of basic license or registration.

Among countries like Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand, and Europe, the United States appears to be the only country that does not require a pilot license/permit or ultralight registration. At the same time, the minimum ultralight category in some countries can be much heavier than in the US. In Canada, it's 1,200 lb (544 kg MTOW), while it's 661 lb (300 kg) in the United Kingdom.

Some countries (e.g., Australia, New Zealand, Europe/EASA) may allow the aircraft to be two-seaters, although the conditions in which a passenger can be carried vary. New Zealand and Australia take a self-administration approach rather than direct government licensing. In Australia, it is controlled under Civil Aviation Order 95.10 and 95.55, and in New Zealand CAA Part 103, with New Zealand borrowing FAA numbering.

The next step up from a Part 103 ultralight in the United States is the FAA Sport Pilot Certificate (governed by 14 CFR Part 61, Subpart J). The FAA created two new pilot certificates in 2004 to enable pilots to operate aircraft that meet specific criteria without the need for a medical certificate. Those certificates were Sport Pilot and Flight Instructor with a Sport Pilot Rating. The Sport Pilot rule was massively expanded by the FAA's MOSAIC final rule, which went into effect in October 2025.

Under these updated rules, light sport aircraft no longer have a 1,320 lb (598 kg) weight limit. The weight limit has been abolished. Under MOSAIC, a light-sport category airplane is permitted to have up to four seats and a VS0 stall of 61 knots or less (14 CFR 22.100). A sport pilot may fly any airplane with a VS1 of 59 knots or less and up to four seats (14 CFR 61.316).

Other regulations include that the cabin must be non-pressurized, it can't have multiple engines, and it must have fixed landing gear. However, sport pilots can now fly retractable landing gear and manually controllable-pitch propellers after receiving specific ground/flight training and an instructor endorsement. Obtaining a Sport Pilot Certificate requires 20 hours total, or half the minimum 40 hours for a Private Pilot.

WinRM Basic authentication is not a remote-management baseline

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Windows Remote Management is a management protocol. A successful session can read event logs, query services, change configuration, or run PowerShell with the privileges of the authenticated account. Treating its listener like a generic HTTPS endpoint misses that authority. The authentication method and the firewall path are part of the remote administration boundary.

Microsoft's WinRM documentation warns that Basic authentication sends a user name and password and provides no encryption at the authentication layer. Basic authentication may be useful for a constrained client compatibility case, but it should not be the default for ordinary domain administration. Kerberos is preferred when domain identity and server identity are available. Negotiate can use the organization's Windows authentication mechanisms. CredSSP and certificate-based options have their own tradeoffs and should be selected for a defined need.

The transport still matters. PowerShell Remoting commonly listens on HTTP port 5985 and HTTPS port 5986. HTTPS with a validated server certificate protects the channel, but it does not turn a shared administrator password into a strong per-user identity. If the client reaches a server by IP address or through a workgroup path, Kerberos may not be available and the design may fall back to NTLM or another weaker identity path. Test which method is actually used rather than assuming the domain-joined label settled it.

The listener security descriptor controls who can connect to the WinRM service. The session configuration controls who can create a PowerShell session and what the endpoint exposes. Restrict both. Remove Basic unless a documented client requires it. Scope the firewall rule to management networks or a jump host. Do not treat the TrustedHosts list as a trust decision; Microsoft describes it as a way to suppress identity errors for hosts whose identity cannot be verified through the current authentication path.

Use Just Enough Administration where a task can be expressed as a constrained endpoint. The endpoint should expose the smallest set of commands and role capabilities needed for the job. Keep administrative sessions separate from application service accounts. Log the connection, the endpoint, the user, and the command evidence. Then review changes to listener configuration and session definitions as privileged changes.

Basic authentication can also spread through a chain of automation. A script may store a reusable password in a credential object, use it against several hosts, and leave an authenticated session open. Prefer a managed service identity, certificate, or domain authentication path where possible. Limit logon rights and network reachability. Rotate shared credentials and remove them from scripts and configuration files.

Start with winrm enumerate winrm/config/listener on representative hosts, but verify the effective policy through Group Policy and the actual client connection. The goal is not to ban every legacy option. The goal is to know which hosts accept which method, from which networks, and with what authority after sign-in.

“Race realism” isn’t realistic—and it isn’t good science

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Modern genetics does not support claims of innate racial differences in intelligence.

At this year’s World Economic Forum meeting in Davos, US President Donald Trump stated that people from Somalia have low IQs. “I always say these are low-IQ people,” he said in his speech. His son, Donald Trump Jr, made a similar statement about Haitian people in 2024 (Trump’s government has since ended the protected status of both Somali and Haitian migrants in the US).

These statements and their consequences are symptoms of a growing trend in Western politics: the re-emergence of racial views of human nature that have been largely in hiding for the past 70 years. Narratives about the innate intellectual inferiority of black or brown people compared with white people—which can still be found in academic journals today—are fuelling far-right propaganda in some of the world’s largest democracies. Such claims underpin misinformation strategies on social media and bolster political movements.

These narratives are sometimes euphemistically referred to as “race science” or more recently “race realism.” But, as we argue, they are not actually scientific. One member of this community, Nathan Cofnas, recently attracted public attention by accusing the late Jason Arday of plagiarism.

What got less attention was that Cofnas has links to a network of people who make supposedly scientific claims about the innate intelligence, criminality, or sexuality of different racial groups. Some within this network are affiliated with universities, giving their work the mask of legitimacy.

This group produces work that has the appearance of science, but which is methodologically deeply flawed and fails to engage with rigorous scientific research. Yet they regularly succeed in publishing this work in (a handful of) mainstream academic journals.

How do they manage to do this, and what are the consequences?

Contemporary race scientists position themselves as persecuted but legitimate scientists who are prevented from pursuing their work for ideological reasons. They sometimes refer to what they defend as hereditarianism. This is the idea that genetic differences play a key causal role in determining differences in human behavior and intelligence between human groups—such as races—over purely environmental differences.

Yet scientific evidence has shown repeatedly and from many points of view that hereditarian thinking is both flawed and wrong. Race scientists make extremely simplistic claims such as: “Black people have innately lower intelligence than white people, because selection pressures resulted in the evolution of higher intelligence in white people.” No part of this claim can be scientifically supported; not from the point of view of genetics, evolutionary biology, or psychology.

In the 1970s, the influential evolutionary biologist Richard Lewontin famously showed that there is more genetic variation within human groups than between them. Socially defined racial groups just aren’t that genetically different from one another. There is genetic variation—though we’re actually a relatively genetically similar species compared to closely related primates. But this variation doesn’t fall neatly into clusters that correlate with how we typically categorize races.

Skin tone and color do vary between geographical locations. So do some other physiological features like hair texture, or the ability to process oxygen or milk products. But these differences are associated with characteristics of the environment rather than with racial groups. Some genetic variants associated with white skin color, for example, may have originated in Africa. Similarly, the ability to digest milk is seen in some populations in Europe, Africa, and Asia, rather than being an exclusively “Aryan” characteristic, as far right groups sometimes claim.

We now know considerably more about the human genome than Lewontin did in the 1970s, but his conclusions are supported today. Knowing someone’s race just doesn’t tell us a great deal about their genetics.

Recent scientific advances allowing us to examine ancient DNA from archaeological sites help explain why genetic differences do not fall neatly along racial lines. This research paints a picture of constant mixing between human groups, as waves of migration washed to and fro across the globe. It ultimately makes any belief in the genetic “purity” of European or African populations meaningless.

While human genetics is fascinatingly complex, race scientists ignore this complexity in favor of a far-too-simplistic view of humanity. And this view is not supported by the work of scientists who have used rigorous scientific methods to further our understanding of our species.

When race scientists say that black people have lower intelligence than white people, they typically base that on claims of differences in IQ test results. But the data underlying these claims is often shaky, to say the least.

Hereditarians have relied heavily on a database that claims to provide average IQs for countries worldwide: the “national IQ” database. Researchers have repeatedly demonstrated that this database is deeply methodologically flawed. It isn’t possible to measure intelligence in the same way in very different cultures, with different exposure to formal education. That’s because we know that education is one of several other factors linked to differences in IQ.

The methods used to put the database together did not come even close to meeting typical standards of scientific rigor. There’s even analysis suggesting that low IQ samples were deliberately chosen to represent sub-Saharan Africa, to make the IQ for black populations artificially low.

Meanwhile, the Flynn effect, showing that IQ has risen considerably during the 20th and 21st centuries, clearly demonstrates that IQ scores are heavily determined by the environment we’re in. Such a rapid rise over a short period of time must have environmental, not genetic, causes.

What’s more, intelligence is a contested topic—something that is hard to define and measure. IQ tests only measure specific cognitive abilities, ignoring things like social intelligence or creativity, for example. In fact, social intelligence features heavily in mainstream research on the evolution of human intelligence.

As one of us recently outlined in a peer-reviewed paper, race scientists are largely silent on the social brain hypothesis—the view that humans evolved remarkable intellectual abilities to manage social issues. In every group, there is strong social competition around resources, mating, and status. These are the things humans spend the most time navigating—the complexities of our rich social lives.

Not one of the prominent mainstream views on the evolution of human intelligence concludes there are innate cognitive differences between racial groups. Factors that have been put forward range from our cooperative child rearing to our heavy reliance on culture and learning. These all involve our social relationships, and all have received equal lack of attention from race scientists.

Mainstream opinion in the evolutionary behavioral sciences is that cognitively, all human groups are the same, though of course individuals within groups differ. Our amazing behavioral diversity stems from our species’ ability to adapt flexibly to our environment. This is especially because of our reliance on culture and social learning—not because we have evolved different psychologies to suit different environments.

Rather than drawing on this mainstream research, race scientists use simplistic “just-so” stories to explain how race differences in traits such as intelligence supposedly evolved. Their “cold winters theory,” for example, claims that the challenges of cold winters in Europe and east Asia created evolutionary selection pressures for greater intelligence after human groups migrated out of Africa. In doing so, they make unsupportable assumptions about environmental differences between Africa and the rest of the world. They also fail to acknowledge half a century of research on the evolution of human cognitive ability.

Yes, we needed to learn new food gathering practices and methods for dealing with environmental challenges as we migrated across the world. But the same was true as humans migrated across the large and ecologically diverse continent of Africa. Race scientists provide no evidence for their arguments. They don’t rely on archaeological evidence, nor do they use typical methods from evolutionary biology to understand evolutionary processes. Using the methods of race scientists, it would be equally easy to make the argument that challenges posed by hot summers drove the evolution of higher intelligence, and equally without scientific backing.

No matter which way you look at these issues—from the view of genetics, anthropology, evolutionary biology, or psychology—hereditarianism does not hold up.

To understand the potential consequences of these views, it is important to see them in historical context. Early in the 20th century, race science was mainstream. Overly simplistic and biologically deterministic views about the origins of differences between human groups—not just race but class—were widespread (if not universal) and important to the eugenics movement.

If disadvantaged groups owe their disadvantage to some fault within them, and not to the structural conditions they experience, then some argue society is not responsible for helping those people. Such arguments led to eugenic sterilization laws that aimed to “improve” society by preventing—without their consent—the reproduction of those considered likely to pass on their innate “defects” to future children. And it was ultimately behind the Nazis’ “solution” of trying to eliminate “undesirables” entirely.

In the decades after the Second World War, the horrors of the Nazi regime led to a decline in the popularity of eugenics in mainstream research and a rejection of race science. But the belief that science supported eugenic claims was already collapsing, even as the Nazis were trying to implement a race-based society. As we learned more about human biology and genetics during the 20th century, it became obvious that human genetic variation was too complex to be mapped into socially recognized racial (or class) categories. Advances in science meant race science dropped out of mainstream thinking.

Yet those committed to a belief in a biological hierarchy of races, regardless of what the science said, wanted venues in which they could continue their discussions. In the 1960s, the pseudo-scientific journal Mankind Quarterly became their venue. Mankind Quarterly was funded by the Pioneer Fund, a nonprofit foundation set up in the 1930s by a US textile magnate who chose to use his fortune to fund research on race science and fight desegregation in the US.

The Pioneer Fund continued to fund race science into the 21st century—including supporting Mankind Quarterly—when it was taken over by the current network of race scientists. They channeled its remaining funds into a new company, the Human Diversity Foundation, subsequently rebranded as Polygenic Scores LLC.

Given all this, how is race science still being published in mainstream academic journals? To be clear, race science does not typically get published in genetics or evolutionary biology journals. Most academic journals operate peer review, meaning papers only get published after evaluation by experts in the field. The overall absence of race science in genetics or biology journals is a clear signal that it does not meet contemporary standards of scientific rigor in genetics or biology.

Instead, race scientists continue to publish in para-scientific journals. But race science also makes it into a growing number of mainstream social science or humanities journals. Psychologist Andrew Winston, an expert in the history of race science in psychology, has described it as a “community project… with its own networks, mentorships, journals, funding, conferences, heroes, origin myths, martyrs, defenders, and historiography.”

Behind the network of writers producing race science sits a community of editors, peer reviewers, and others in gatekeeping roles, which too often allows race science to be published in mainstream journals. This is particularly in the subfields of psychology interested in intelligence and individual differences, and evolutionary psychology.

Editors of Intelligence, the mainstream journal that has probably published the largest quantity of race science, have occasionally published editorials defending their publication of race science in the name of ensuring robust academic debate even on controversial topics.

There have been sanctions. Philippe Rushton, one of an earlier generation of race scientists, faced sanctions from his university in Canada but kept his job apparently owing to the intervention of Edward O. Wilson, arguably the most famous biologist toward the end of the 20th century.

Cofnas created justified controversy over his call for a “hereditarian revolution” after which it will be acknowledged that black people are innately intellectually disadvantaged compared to white people. But he kept his postdoctoral fellowship at Cambridge University (though his affiliation with a Cambridge college was ended). He has also kept his postdoctoral position at Ghent University after using the opportunity provided by excessive news coverage of Arday to publicize his hereditarian views (though some sanctions have been imposed, such as restrictions on teaching). Cofnas has defended his views by saying he’s been misunderstood and is entitled to free inquiry.

Moreover, when University College London discovered that the race science community had been holding conferences on its premises (the “London Conferences on Intelligence”), without their knowledge of the subject of the conferences, they acted to prevent further such conferences being held. The publisher of the journal Intelligence, Elsevier, has recently appointed new editors, who have stated their commitment to publishing both rigorous and socially responsible science. Race scientists are increasingly publishing in evolutionary psychology journals rather than Intelligence.

However, the London Conferences on Intelligence continue, though not at UCL. Race scientists also have a media arm, Aporia magazine, which promotes race science using much more overt language and with more obvious policy focus than publications in academic journals. One Aporia article, for example, argued for eugenics as a solution to Africa’s lack of economic development, noting in a post that some may find it “distasteful.” It claimed—relying on race science’s unscientific arguments—that artificial selection for higher intelligence is needed to improve the continent’s innately unintelligent populace.

Race science may not have the respect of the scientific community, but it does have funding and influential backers. A report by Hope not Hate revealed that the network of race scientists received significant funding from the US businessman Andrew Conru, who withdrew his funding after the report was released.

The US Ambassador to Belgium even intervened when Ghent University set up an investigation into how Cofnas was hired.

Race scientists are far from the victims they claim to be. Race scientists in this and previous generations have worked hard to ensure their messages are heard beyond academia. And in this generation, they are successfully using social media to spread their beliefs, especially Elon Musk’s X.

But what about academic freedom? Isn’t it good for science to discuss any hypothesis to ensure free exchange of ideas? Academic debate is productive only if it involves methodologically rigorous research. Science will not progress if one side of any debate consistently uses poor-quality, methodologically flawed work. Nor is it necessary to continually debate topics that have long since been settled.

We believe it is unequivocal that race science is fundamentally pseudoscience. In our view, it’s critical to call attention to not only the scientific flaws underlying this body of work but also the real target it’s aimed at: political actors and everyday readers who ultimately decide the direction that science, and society, goes in.

A DIY digital rangefinder requires two donors, though. Rather than the FED 5 rangefinder Mr. 50mm used before, he’s sticking with two Canon cameras this time around. Although Mr. 50mm hasn’t finalized everything or released a build video yet — it’s in the works — the C7M sure does look impressive thus far. Mr. 50mm is especially impressed with the Canon 7’s finder, which he describes as a “marked upgrade compared to a Fed camera.”

Then, about a decade ago, I started getting more serious about putting on muscle, and my trainer at the time, the big-armed and bigger-brained David Otey, introduced me to a kind of body-composition testing, a.k.a. “bioelectrical impedance analysis,” or BIA. The tech has been used to estimate body composition for decades, and you’ve probably seen one of the more sophisticated machines at your gym or doctor’s office. Step onto a platform, grab a pair of handles, wait 30 seconds, and the machine spits out a breakdown of how much of you is body fat, skeletal muscle mass, and more.

David walked me through how BIA works: The machine sends a teeny-tiny electrical current through your body. Electricity moves more easily through lean tissue than through fat, so by measuring how that current travels through you, the machine can estimate how much fat and muscle you’re carrying.

In 2019, I bought an InBody Dial H20N for my bathroom. By then, BIA was well established in the consumer “smart scale” market, and InBody’s scale was among the more advanced at-home options, using sensors at both your hands and feet to take a more complete reading of your body than the foot-only models that were common at the time. The app tracks every measurement, so I can see not just what my body looks like on any given day but how it has changed over time.

To understand why I love the damn thing, it’s important to know that I weigh roughly the same today as when I first brought the scale home seven years ago.

A conventional (dumb) scale would look at seven years of strength training, workouts I wanted to skip, workouts I probably should have skipped, and a totally unreasonable amount of thought about what I put into my body and deliver its verdict: Congrats, you weigh the same!

Today, it shows I’ve got around 95 pounds of skeletal muscle and 24 pounds of body fat. My hydration levels can throw them off. What I just ate can throw them off. A hard workout can really throw them off.

Still, in studies comparing the InBody to much fancier ways of measuring body composition, the scale holds up surprisingly well for something that lives in your bathroom. There’s even a new model, the H40, that Inbody says is even more precise. (I keep meaning to buy it, but then something breaks in my house and a new scale gets knocked down the priority list.)

None of this means that I obsess over whether I have 94.7 pounds of muscle on Tuesday and 93.9 on Thursday. What makes this useful to me aren’t the exact numbers; it’s the direction they’re headed in. I still don’t really care how much I weigh—I wear the same pants size as when I was 25 and 35, and all my blood work is fine. But I’m trying to carry more muscle, less fat, and a stronger, more capable body into the second half of my life. I just want to know that some of my ass-busting efforts are paying off.

A dumb scale can’t tell me that. But a smart scale—this smart scale—is giving me all the direction I need.

Do You Always Have to Sacrifice Passion for Emotional Intimacy?

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Do you remember the beginning of your relationship with your partner? When you would be turned on by just the thought of your fingers sharing space with theirs? When you’d bust into the apartment movie-style, tossing aside layers of clothes because you just couldn’t wait to get to skin?

Now, your relationship looks a little different. There are no mental gymnastics before grabbing your partner’s hand and you don’t mind putting your clothes away nicely before jumping into bed. It’s a comfortable, cozy phase—one that you worked hard and long to get to.

But sometimes, you might wish you could have both.

We’ve collectively accepted that a long-term relationship somehow means the end of passion. You spend months or even years learning everything you can about your partner. You’ve gained trust, a strong foundation, and yet, the passion seems to have disappeared with the uncertainty.

“The comfort you worked so hard to build may be what’s subtly killing your desire for your partner,” certified sex therapist Dr. Mindy DeSeta, PhD tells Cosmopolitan.

She explains that over time, we unknowingly become complacent about romancing our partner. All of the exciting unknowns of the beginning of a relationship (Do they want to kiss you? Do they want to come home with you?) eventually fade away.

“You may be intimately familiar with your partner's moves after a number of years, and that's a beautiful thing,” Dr. DeSeta says. “But repetition can dull the excitement of physical intimacy.”

But contrary to what we’ve all learned to accept, this isn’t an inevitable diagnosis for your long-term relationship. It is possible to have both emotional intimacy and sexual passion. You just have to work for it.

“Your sex life will suffer if you do not actively continue to learn your partner’s desires,” Dr. DeSeta says.

You might think that you know everything there is to know already about your partner. But as we get older, our desires change—even if our partner doesn’t.

“Your pleasure is constantly evolving and if you want to continue to have a thriving and exciting sex life, a couple must be able to talk about most recent desires,” she says. “What turned you on at 25 may not still turn you on at 40.”

This is why communicating your desires and feelings to your partner is so important. Sure, you and your partner may be pros at working through disagreements and emotionally supporting each other through life’s changes—but does that energy translate into your sex life? Do you share your curiosities? Do you hold space for them telling you they’d like to try something different? If you don’t, it might be time to make a change.

First, in order to feel sexy with your partner, you need to feel sexy yourself. Do something every day that makes you feel sexy and confident,” Dr. DeSeta says. “The foundation of a great sex life starts with each person having a thriving libido.”

Then try to initiate sexy conversation with your partner. Dr. DeSeta suggests planning something that feels tantalizing without the expectation of physical intimacy to follow. Reach each other erotica, talk about new fantasies you’ve had, discuss a trendy sex topic in media.

“Many long-term couples need a prompt to get the sexy conversations started,” she says. They also need reminders to touch each other more.

“Long term couples are notorious for forgetting physical affection,” she says. Dr. DeSeta emphasizes small touches every day. This can look like caressing their back as you pass them in the kitchen or holding hands while you watch your favorite show on the couch. Instead of maneuvering around each other, lean into the touch. It signals to your partner that you see them and want them throughout the day. And just the feeling of being wanted can ignite some of that passion from the early days of your relationship.

Most importantly, you should date your partner every day. Dr. DeSeta recommends “Daily Dates” to couples she sees in therapy. This is when you set aside 20 minutes everyday to make your partner a priority.

“All too often long-term couples put their relationship second, so this tune up is always needed,” she says. “During these 20 minutes the phones are silenced, logistical conversations are off the table, and the time is dedicated to emotionally connecting with their partner.”

By doing this, she explains that you slow down the daily hustle and really learn how to reconnect with your partner. You might even learn a few new things about them in the process.

“If we do not make the intentional effort to keep the passion alive, then you are at risk of falling into autopilot,” Dr.Deseta says. We often think passion and emotional intimacy are at odds with each other, but that’s because we think it’s acceptable to simply fall into our relationship without making an active effort to keep our relationship exciting. And no, you don’t have to be constantly ripping each other’s clothes off or feel nervous in order to have a passionate relationship. But you should aim to feel the same deep desire and attraction to your partner that you did when you first started off—if not more.
