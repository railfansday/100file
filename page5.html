Amazon CEO Andy Jassy feared being ‘a lousy dad’—so he did this

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

When Amazon CEO Andy Jassy became a parent for the first time 26 years ago, he didn’t want to choose between being a good worker and being a good father.

That left him with only one option.

“I realized that I had to change the way I worked,” Jassy recently said during an appearance at Amazon’s Bring Your Parents to Work Day. “[Otherwise,] I was either going to have to leave Amazon, or I was going to be a lousy dad.”

Jassy was used to working grueling hours—often staying at the office late into the night—and he wasn’t sure if he could do his job effectively on a tighter schedule.

“I didn’t know how I would do my job without [working all the time],” he said. “But like everything else, if you think about it and you rework it and you find ways to be more efficient and you make trade-offs, you can do it.”

One step was to ensure that, whenever he was at the office, he was using his time efficiently.

“I did spend a lot of time in the hallway talking to people, and I wasn’t always prioritizing the things that mattered most,” Jassy said. “It forced me to get more efficient in what I did.”

He also established rules to help him prioritize family: being home for dinner every day, having weekly breakfasts with each of his children, and maintaining a standing Tuesday date night with his wife.

The challenge Jassy faced will likely be familiar to any parent struggling to balance career ambitions with responsibilities at home. And it is increasingly common among men, a growing number of whom say they are interested in being parents or caregivers. 

In one recent survey, over two-thirds of men said they consider caregiving to be as important as paid work and that both parents should contribute equally—yet only 40% of men who are caregivers said they shared the responsibility equally with their partners.

In the same survey, nearly two-thirds of male caregivers said work obligations interfered with their family time. A third reported having “no control” over their schedules, and only 40% said they had a hybrid or remote work arrangement. (Amazon issued a five-day RTO policy in 2024.)

Meanwhile, the study found that male caregivers are increasingly missing work or cutting back on their work hours. The percentage of men who care for adults or children with extra needs who reported missing work rose from 64% in 2019 to 76% in 2026.  

And, as Fast Company reported recently, men in some states are filing at least as many claims for parental leave as mothers.

For fathers who want to keep their career ambitions and still be equal parents, Jassy said there may be no one-size-fits-all solution.

“There are times you have to be flexible with what’s happening in your life and at work,” he said. “But it’s doable if you want to do it.”

Ayumu Imazu Talks ‘musubi’ & The Belief that Music ‘Brings People Together’: Billboard Japan Monthly Interview

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

He chatted with Billboard Japan about the making of the song & what he envisions for the Budokan and beyond.

Singer/songwriter Ayumu Imazu spoke to Billboard Japan for its Monthly Feature interview series spotlighting artists and releases on its radar.

Imazu’s latest single “musubi,” released digitally on Oct. 2, has become the biggest hit of his career. “musubi” is a love song portraying the bonds between people through the motif of the “red string of fate.” A demo posted to TikTok in late August, when only a single verse and chorus existed, spread widely and led to an “emergency release.” The track swept the charts across streaming services immediately after it dropped and earned the ​26-year-old artist his first-ever No. 1 on Billboard Japan’s Streaming Songs chart (dated Oct. 14, 2026), marking the biggest breakthrough of his career to date.

2026 has also been a milestone year for him — it marks the fifth anniversary of his major-label debut, he announced a partnership with SKY-HI-led BMSG in February, and in May he released CLASSIC, his first album in four years. After pouring all of himself into CLASSIC, “musubi” emerged as Imazu circled back to the foundation of his music, which is about “bringing people together.” He chatted with Billboard Japan about the making of the song, the inspiration he’s drawn from his new environment at BMSG, and what he envisions for the Budokan and beyond.

“musubi” has been generating a huge response. When did you start working on it?

Around two months ago, in mid-August. I finished the first verse and chorus at the end of August and posted it on TikTok maybe two days after that.

The title is symbolic. “musubi” is a love song about the bonds that tie people together. Where did the idea come from?

In May​,​ I released CLASSIC, my first album in four years, and most of it was new songs I co-wrote with overseas producers. I put so much into making it that I felt pretty empty in a way afterward. I was gradually taking things in and building my energy back up while touring, and as I considered what to make next, I vaguely thought it’d be nice to do something about bringing people together, because I feel that’s the biggest purpose of making music. I started making it with that as the big goal.

Bringing people together through music — is that one of the motivations you’ve always held onto?

Maybe not always, but with the way the times are going… right now I feel like everyone’s own worlds of the things they like are scattered all over the place. It’s becoming harder for everyone to get excited about a single piece of music or to share the same feelings. That’s why I wanted to make a song like “musubi.”

You announced your partnership with BMSG this February, and in April you also shared that you’d gotten married. Do you feel those changes in your working environment and life stage have influenced you?

I wasn’t thinking about those things in particular. More than anything, I really wanted to make something that carried on from the album. I put out CLASSIC after forming the partnership with BMSG, wanting to show the fullest extent of myself all over again, like, “This is Ayumu Imazu.” So doing the same thing didn’t feel right. My next focus naturally turned to what’s at the core of my music, the theme of connecting people.

CLASSIC was an album built around powerful, polished dance numbers that were quintessential Ayumu Imazu. At the same time, vocal-driven songs that showcase the beauty of the Japanese language, like “Anata To Itai” and “musubi,” are essential to any discussion of your discography. How do you think about balancing or blending the two?

When I was first starting out and trying to show people who Ayumu Imazu is as an artist, I was really conflicted between going with dance tracks and the fact that I also love ballads. But my strongest feeling was that I didn’t want to choose (just one), so after putting out all kinds of songs, I now feel the dance tracks are very Ayumu Imazu, and I also really feel that slow-tempo songs like “musubi” are me, too. The range of musical styles is broad, but I don’t really feel like I’m moving away from the foundation of my own feelings and personality.

Does your actual production process change depending on the style of the song?

It does. With dance tracks​,​ I usually start with the beat, and with ballad-type songs like this one, I usually work out the chords on acoustic guitar or piano, write the melody to a certain point, and then program it.

What did you focus on most in terms of your vocals?

The demo was already out there and everyone had gotten used to hearing it, so when I recorded the final take, I had to either make it sound similar or surpass it. I’d gotten used to hearing it myself, especially from just before the chorus through the first half of the chorus, so that was where I struggled most, and probably the part I was most particular about.

Where do you feel you managed to “surpass” it?

If anything, I think it has a different kind of appeal. The demo had its own appeal as a demo, that relaxed quality of casually singing at home. The released version has more feeling put into it, I guess… it really comes across as emotional. Personally, I really feel that difference.

When you shared the demo, you posted on TikTok that you were “still deciding on a title.”

I read through lots of comments and ideas. A lot of people said they “want to use it at their wedding,” so when I was finishing the full version, I thought, “Maybe I could make it a bit more like a wedding song.” It was a great reference and really helped me out.

Tell us about the changes in your environment, too. BMSG, the company you partnered with, brings together artists brimming with individuality under the slogan “​​Never extinguish talent.” What kind of inspiration and influence have you gotten in that environment?

At first​,​ I was completely wrapped up in my own stuff, but I recently took part in BMSG FES for the first time and got to see the other BMSG artists perform, and I was really inspired. Personally, it was my first time riding a trolley float, so that was a great experience too. [Laughs] Also, it reminded me that it truly is a music-first company, and since what everyone expresses is pretty different, I thought it was really great that every group and every artist puts their own colors front and center without being bound by any set format.

BMSG also has a vision called “TYOISM,” about reaching the world from Tokyo. With you joining — someone who moved to the U.S. at 14 and is still based there and in Japan — it seems likely to spark some new chemistry.

I think I have a pretty good feel for how things are done abroad, so when it comes to opening doors to global challenges, I definitely want to do that together with BMSG. Mr. Hidaka (SKY-HI) and I had talked about that kind of thing from the start.

Now that five years have passed since your 2021 major-label debut, how do you see the phase you’re in as an artist?

Right now might be a phase where I just make music really freely, without overthinking it. Last year or so, I was thinking a lot about things like, “I want to release this kind of song and become this kind of artist,” but lately I feel like I’ve thought it all through, so it’s more like I’m going with the flow.

What artists have you been listening to a lot lately?

I’ve had Remi Wolf’s album (Mud) on heavy rotation lately. I really love indie rock-type stuff, too.

You have a headlining show set at Nippon Budokan in January 2027. Tell us how you’re feeling going in.

The Budokan is truly a stage I’ve set as a goal, so I think it’s going to be a really flashy show. [Laughs] More people than ever are listening to my music, and I’ve got a concert booked at my biggest venue yet, so I’m super happy and feel like all I have to do is keep doing what I’ve been doing. I want to keep creating spaces where everyone can come together as one and enjoy music at bigger and bigger venues.

—This interview by Takuto Ueda first appeared on Billboard Japan

In the Codename MDASH pilot, GitHub and Microsoft turned thousands of raw AI findings into 99 checked, private reports with suggested fixes for six widely used open source projects. Maintainers have already fixed more than half.

AI has made finding potential vulnerabilities fast and cheap. Fixing them still takes human judgment, and in open source, that work falls to maintainers. Many are already flooded with AI-generated security findings, including duplicates and reports that aren’t vulnerabilities at all. Another raw scan doesn’t help them. Anyone pointing a new model at a project should ask its maintainers first and come with fixes, not just findings.

Maintainers are under intense pressure from this flood of reports. As Christian Grobmeier, a maintainer of Log4j, has put it, the AI defending open source has to be better than the AI attacking it.

AI shouldn’t add to maintainers’ workload. So, with maintainers, GitHub and Microsoft ran a pilot together: could AI help maintainers ship fixes instead of handing them more findings?

GitHub and Microsoft are using Codename MDASH, Microsoft’s multi-model agentic scanning harness, to find and fix security vulnerabilities in widely used open source software, strengthening the foundation that much of the world’s code is built on.

Our pilot had two goals: send only findings that matter, and never send a maintainer an unverified finding. Here’s how it worked:

Reports and fixes stayed on GitHub, where maintainers already work.

The result: Codename MDASH, using Microsoft’s MAI Cyber model, flagged thousands of possible issues across the six projects. After AI triage and hand review by seven Microsoft FORGE Lab researchers, maintainers received 99 private reports. As of November 15, they had fixed 55, and 14 of those fixes were already in a release.

America's hyperscale bit barns draw as much power as entire states

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Harvard study claims emissions attributable to datacenters are two to three times higher than in 2018

Just 403 hyperscale datacenters consumed an estimated 1.8 percent of US electricity in 2024-25, and their carbon dioxide emissions were roughly two to three times an earlier forecast for 2018.

Research led by the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health and the Harvard Data Science Initiative looked specifically at hyperscale facilities, the largest class of server farms that typically support cloud computing and AI processing.

The published study, "Assessing the carbon emissions and energy consumption of US hyperscale data centres," estimates their combined electricity use approached that of a single state such as Indiana, Michigan, or Tennessee under the highest-load scenario.

The researchers identified 403 hyperscale facilities by using a larger datacenter dataset, satellite imagery, and building footprints on OpenStreetMap.

Rather than reading electricity meters, the researchers worked out consumption from each site's electrical capacity, the hours in a year, and assumptions about how heavily the facility was running.

Across four operating scenarios, ranging from low-intensity use to AI-heavy workloads, electricity draw was believed to total 68-99 terawatt-hours (TWh). Each of those states consumed approximately 100 TWh during the study period.

The researchers also calculated carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions using eGRID2023 plant-level generation and emissions data from the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). Emissions ranged from 21 million to 31 million metric tons of CO2 across the scenarios, with a central estimate of 25.7 million. That compares with 10.5 million for 2018.

The study covered May 2024 to April 2025, so electricity demand and carbon emissions are likely to be even higher now, the researchers say.

"What we're seeing is already striking, but it's only a snapshot of a rapidly expanding industry," said Gianluca Guidi, lead author on the paper and postdoctoral fellow in the Department of Biostatistics at Harvard.

"The datacenter boom hasn't slowed down since our study period ended in April 2025. New facilities have been built, existing campuses have expanded, and demand for AI computing continues to grow. That means the electricity use and carbon footprint we're reporting could already be substantially larger now in 2026."

Harvard's report also considers the mix of fuels being used to generate the electricity that powers all those hyperscale data houses. The mix varies considerably between regional grids, so where a hyperscale facility is built makes a substantial difference to its attributed emissions.

Across the sites studied, roughly 54 percent of the electricity attributed to their consumption came from fossil fuels, mainly natural gas and coal. The electricity attributed to these facilities nevertheless had a lower average carbon intensity than the US grid overall.

The researchers acknowledge limitations, including that there wasn't enough available data to study all hyperscale sites in the US, and that the data did not allow them to differentiate between power used for general-purpose compute versus AI-specialized operations at individual campuses. ®

‘Both utopians and the doomers are going to be disappointed’: Fed advisor Marc Andreessen on AI going from boom to bust

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Marc Andreessen got the last word at the Federal Reserve’s big AI conference in San Francisco on Monday, and he used it to tell the central bank that nearly everyone in the room was going to be let down.

“My line has been both utopians and the doomers are going to be disappointed,” the venture capitalist said at the San Francisco Fed, “specifically disappointed by the scope and magnitude of the real-world impact, and specifically the economic impact.”

Andreessen co-leads the Productivity and Jobs Task Force, which Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh set up to tell the central bank what super intelligence—“SI,” in the White House’s preferred term—will do to growth, prices and jobs. On Monday, Andreessen previewed what he’ll contribute to the group’s report. Without using the name, he described one of the oldest problems in economics: William Baumol’s “cost disease,” the reason flat-screen TVs get cheaper every year while hospital bills, tuition and rent go the other way. His case is that AI won’t cure it, and could even make it worse.

The Fed hasn’t quite laid out that outcome.

The task force is advisory, and Andreessen has no vote on interest rates. He co-leads it with Stanford economist Charles Jones and Microsoft executive Asha Sharma, one of five outside panels the Fed named in June to rethink how the central bank works. Its charge, per the Fed, is to assess the economic impact of “new general-purpose technologies, including super intelligence, to inform the Federal Reserve’s policy judgments.” The panels report to the rate-setting committee with recommendations due by year-end, though Warsh said in October that “some, but not all” would turn their homework in on time. The Fed’s task force page lists no ethics or recusal terms for the panels. Andreessen’s firm, Andreessen Horowitz, raised more than $15 billion in new funds in January, as venture firms raced to keep up with the AI boom.

Andreessen is also Silicon Valley’s best-known accelerationist. His 2023 “Techno-Optimist Manifesto” declared, “We believe in accelerationism,” which he defined as “the conscious and deliberate propulsion of technological development.” It listed @BasedBeffJezos, the pseudonymous account behind the “effective accelerationism,” or e/acc, movement, first among its “patron saints,” and it named the enemy: “Our enemy is stagnation.” When OpenAI’s board fired Sam Altman a month later, Andreessen posted simply “E/acc!” This is the man who told the Fed that stagnation might win out over an AI-powered industrial revolution on Monday.

Warsh opened the day by laying out three “illustrative scenarios” Fed board staff have begun to evaluate. In one, AI lifts productivity, the gains are broadly shared and the economy stays close to full employment. In another, growth still runs above its long-term average, but automation pushes unemployment higher. In the third, AI “could disappoint after significant capital investment and stock market gains,” stocks fall, the economy slows and it eventually recovers “without the productivity gains.”

All three turn on whether AI arrives in force. Andreessen’s version is duller: most of the economy just doesn’t let it in.

He splits the economy in two. The “fast sectors,” like consumer electronics, media and commodity goods, take up new technology quickly, and “what those sectors all have in common is they have rapidly falling prices.” The “slow sectors”—health care, education, housing, and law and government—“are not legally allowed to have rapid change, like literally not.”

The machine “cannot get an MD, cannot prescribe medication,” he said. “The machine cannot get admitted to the bar.” The slow sectors, in his telling, pair restricted supply with subsidized demand: you can’t easily build new housing or open new hospitals, and when voters revolt over the cost, Washington steps in with student loans and housing subsidies. “So, of course, prices can only go up.”

Then he followed it to its end. As prices crash in the fast sectors, they shrink as a share of GDP, and the slow sectors take over. Health care, he said, “is already a fifth of US GDP and I don’t see why it doesn’t go to a fourth, a third and then half and then ultimately 80%.” (The government’s count is 18% in 2024, or $5.3 trillion, per CMS actuaries.)

“You might interpret this as good news because it means the AI revolution is not going to, you know, destroy all the jobs or kill everybody,” he said. “You could also view this as very negative, which is productivity growth just simply may not grow very much. Economic growth just simply may not grow very much. We may just continue to stagnate.”

Andreessen called himself “extremely optimistic,” saying that AI gives the country “a magic formula” and comparing it to being “handed this elixir to boost productivity growth.” But policy is a problem, he suggested. The decisions about what to do with AI, he said, “are going to matter actually way more than what the technology is capable of,” because without them “the technology will simply not get rolled out.”

That landed in a room where, three hours earlier, Warsh had brought up “secular stagnation,” the 1930s worry that economists returned to after the financial crisis, and asked whether superintelligence “dramatically” changes that picture.

Baumol, the late New York University and Princeton economist, worked this out in the 1960s, starting with the economics of the performing arts. A string quartet needs the same four musicians and the same playing time it did in Mozart’s day, but those musicians have to be paid wages set by an economy where factory workers keep getting more productive. So live music, and later education and medicine, keep getting more expensive relative to everything else, and keep taking a bigger share of spending.

In his 2012 book, The Cost Disease, Baumol projected that if health costs kept growing at their recent pace, they would rise from 15% of the average person’s income in 2005 to 62% by 2105. He was an optimist about it. “No matter how painful rising medical and educational bills may be, society can afford them,” he wrote, because productivity elsewhere makes everyone richer. The real threat, he added, was “foolish public policy.”

For Baumol, the disease was built into the work: you can’t speed up a string quartet. For Andreessen, it’s built into the law. That matters for the Fed, because an agency that sets interest rates can’t fix zoning boards or medical licensing.

The economist who has done the most to bring Baumol into the AI debate was in the audience. Jones, who is on leave at Anthropic, stayed quiet because the company is preparing to go public, as Andreessen remarked to the crowd. In a 2017 paper for the National Bureau of Economic Research, Jones, Philippe Aghion and Benjamin Jones argued that “growth may be constrained not by what we are good at but rather by what is essential and yet hard to improve,” crediting the idea to Baumol.

San Francisco Fed President Mary Daly asked a panel of executives where their “weak link” was, and Erik Wexler, CEO of the nonprofit hospital system Providence, gave the slow-sector answer. “We’re part of the weak link,” he said. Providence operates in seven states, and as each writes its own AI rules, “it’s almost like sometimes being in seven different countries.”

Earlier on the same panel, though, Wexler complicated Andreessen’s picture. AI tools that listen in on appointments and write up the notes are saving some physicians “two to four hours of time a day,” he said, and doctors have called it “life-changing.” In 35 years in the industry, he said, he’d never heard them say that about anything else. So the hours are being saved inside a slow sector. The open question is whether they ever show up in the price of a hospital visit.

Andreessen’s forecast cuts against the case Warsh made before he got the job. In a Wall Street Journal op-ed late last year, Warsh wrote that AI “will be a significant disinflationary force, increasing productivity and bolstering American competitiveness,” as Axios reported. In speeches and writing, he argued AI-driven productivity could justify lower interest rates, an echo of Alan Greenspan’s Fed holding off on hikes in the late 1990s.

As chair, he has hedged. “My judgment is we have seen an improvement in productivity; we can’t measure it precisely,” he told reporters on Oct. 28, adding that “most of that change in productivity precedes everything we’re talking about—super intelligence.” The Fed held rates at 3.75% to 4% that day, and a reporter told him most of his colleagues “seem to be on board with raising rates in December.” Warsh declined to prejudge the meeting.

On Monday, Warsh didn’t push back on Andreessen. He restated the argument in Fed terms, as product prices versus services prices, and moved on. He closed the day “cautiously optimistic,” calling it “a hinge point for the US economy,” and said, “I’d rather have our problems than anyone else’s in the world.”

Andreessen has been making this case for years. In 2017 he warned that the slow sectors were “basically just going to eat the economy,” and in March 2023 he wrote that “AI is simply already illegal across most of the economy.” Back then the argument was a rebuttal to the job-loss doomers. On Monday, in front of the Fed chair, it came with the other half of the bargain: if the jobs are safe, the productivity boom may be stuck too.

For companies, the slow sectors are a line item. Workers now pay an average of $7,471 a year toward employer family health coverage, up 9% from 2025, according to KFF’s annual survey. That’s the part of the economy that, by Andreessen’s account, AI isn’t legally allowed to touch.

For the Fed, the question is what kind of disinflation AI brings, if any. Earlier in the day, Warsh asked a panel of economists whether AI might do to services what the China shock did to goods—“productizing services”—and push prices down. Stanford’s Nick Bloom said falling token costs made him “kind of bullish.” Stanford President Jonathan Levin gave Baumol’s answer without the name: health care and education are “the parts of the economy where the disinflationary pressures from automation have not hit,” where “the costs have continued to rise with wages.” Whether that changes depends on what people want, he said, “and there’s also regulation.”

If Andreessen is right, AI’s price cuts land mostly where prices were already falling, and services, where inflation has been hardest to bring down, stay out of reach.

Models are now good enough to work for hours or even days on their own. You don’t have to watch over every thing they do, but you still need to be able to check in, no matter where you are. An agent can’t live only inside one application. The work should continue even If you close a window or switch devices.

In GitHub Copilot you can start an agent session in one place and pick it up in another. This session continuity runs on the Agent Host Protocol (AHP), an open standard built first in VS Code and adopted across GitHub. Here’s how it works, what we learned building it, and how you can use it to bring your agents into your own apps and workflows.

GitHub Copilot was first built in VS Code as an extension that bundled its own harness. This gave you access to your agents in VS Code, but it tied their lifetime to the editor. Extensions are attached to a single VS Code window. Close that window, and the agent is gone. Update VS Code, and every agent stops as the application restarts. Connect to a remote machine, and you get an entirely new extension host commit-locked against the version of the VS Code workbench you have.

At the same time, the Copilot app was being developed, alongside the existing GitHub mobile apps for iOS and Android. Each product was doing its own distinct integration with cloud-based agents to work towards delivering session continuity.

Each of these integrations solved the problem for one product, but developers also still want to be able to pull their agents into their own workflows–whether that’s an editor, a terminal, a phone, or a tool they built themselves–and they can’t do that if every client needs its own one-off integration. That’s why developers needed a protocol: a standard, independently versioned language across versions of VS Code, the GitHub Copilot app, mobile apps, and any other client that might host an agent. A shared protocol means every client sees the same agents, history, and state without bespoke integrations, and you aren’t tied to one app’s idea of what an agent session is.

The protocol needed to allow multiple clients to interact with the same set of agents seamlessly. Start a session in the cloud from your phone, for example, then continue it from your laptop. It should tolerate network interruptions, reconnections, and concurrent client mutations. And it needed to be full-featured enough for you to do real work from each of your clients.

At the same time, the protocol needed to stay out of agent behavior. Unlike the domains that produced the Language Server Protocol and Debug Adapter Protocol, the world of agents is evolving quickly. That’s why AHP avoids encoding how agents work, and instead focuses on how you want to interact with them.

You also need the protocol to be simple enough to implement on your own client or server. While the full feature set would necessarily be extensive, the core protocol needed to be minimal.

The result of these requirements is the Agent Host Protocol (AHP). It is oriented around URI-identified “channels”, which are the coarsest unit of functionality in the protocol. Every server must implement the root, session, and chat channels, but can also implement more channels that provide terminal support, MCP additions, or OTel reporting, for example.

Within each channel is a reducer-based state object, which drives most of the logic for clients. The server is the arbiter of the state, and each client sends a sequence of actions that either mutate the state or trigger side effects, like sending a chat message. This provides a few very nice properties. Reconnection and replay in poor network conditions are easy: a client sends all queued actions, presents the right ‘loading’ state, and waits for the server to reconcile and acknowledge the actions before confirming them. Clients and servers are straightforward to debug. And reducer-based state also plays nicely with popular UI frameworks like React.

Adopting the Agent Host Protocol in VS Code required building a new harness and agentic stack underneath it. The Copilot Chat extension, which used to provide all of VS Code’s AI and agentic capabilities, had been growing for over three years, reaching over 400,000 lines of TypeScript, not counting tests. But, as a VS Code extension, it was structured around the lifecycle of a VS Code window and depended entirely on the VS Code extension API. Neither that lifetime nor those APIs would be available in a standalone agent host process.

As we started building the Agent Host Protocol in early 2026, the Copilot SDK was also being developed, and it made sense to leverage it as the foundation for the new agentic stack. This was a big transition. It’s easy to build a harness implementation that says hello and can edit files—we were using the agent host to develop the agent host after the first couple weeks—but it’s another thing to build one that covers the long tail of features developers had come to expect from Copilot Chat.

GitHub has had Copilot cloud agents for a couple of years, allowing developers to run tasks in the cloud from any device. While VS Code was quietly developing the Agent Host Protocol, GitHub was looking to expand that capability to coordinate sessions running on your own devices via the Copilot app, or in cloud sandboxes. This was exactly the N:M problem that AHP was built to solve, so adopting the same technology made perfect sense.

From the outset, the GitHub Agent Host had different requirements than VS Code’s implementation. VS Code runs the host locally and can drop its host on other compute over SSH, containers, WSL, or Dev Tunnels, and it is actually included as part of the VS Code Server bundle. The GitHub Agent Host needed to run in the cloud and across a much wider variety of infrastructure, environments, and security constraints.

Those differing requirements are why they’re separate implementations written in different languages: VS Code’s in TypeScript and GitHub’s in Rust. For now, having two agent hosts has been a good acid-test for the protocol itself. How well does it work when two entirely different teams and sets of agents implement the same protocol independently?

Pretty well, it turns out. Like MCP, the Agent Host Protocol provides escape hatches via custom methods and _metadata on objects that consumers can use for out-of-band signaling. After several months heads-down on our respective implementations, our first task as we worked toward unification was inventorying the ways each implementation had extended the protocol. Many of these extensions reasonably stay GitHub-specific, such as the GitHub Agent Host using metadata to link to project boards. In other cases, variance was a sign of a gap in the protocol; early versions, for example, could not accurately represent shell-type tool output that wasn’t backed by a PTY. And some were bad assumptions, like VS Code’s client assuming a particular structure for the session URIs it received from the agent host. A protocol with only one implementation isn’t truly portable; as we taught our clients and servers to talk to each other, AHP became portable in practice, not just in theory.

Today, the Agent Host Protocol provides that same continuity across all VS Code and GitHub surfaces. Agent sessions running locally in the GitHub Copilot app, in cloud sandboxes, and in VS Code all share the same underlying protocol. On Nov. 17, 1967, NASA's Surveyor 6 spacecraft made the first liftoff from the lunar surface.

Surveyor 6 was one of several robotic lunar missions NASA sent to scout the moon before sending humans there. It took television pictures of the surface and learned about the composition of lunar soil. Then came the big liftoff.

First Surveyor 6 launched from the surface and hopped to another position about 8 feet (2 meters) away. Next, Surveyor 6 took pictures of its original landing site. Flight controllers on Earth could see the landing pad prints of Surveyor 6. They also saw an imprint from the rocket exhaust when Surveyor 6 lifted off.

Surveyor 6 showed that spacecraft could land on the moon, and lift off again. Less than two years later, humans did the same thing during Apollo 11.

NASA's Surveyor 6 spacecraft launched to the moon on Nov. 7, 1967 and made its first landing on the lunar surface on Nov. 10 of that year. It's primary mission was to make a soft landing on the moon in the Sinus Medii of the moon.

The probe took nearly 30,000 photos of the moon during its 16 days on the lunar surface, with the lander shutting down due to the onset of lunar night on Nov. 26. Surveyor 6 was the sixth and penultimate moon mission of NASA's Surveyor lunar program, so a lot of what it did (launching and landing on the moon) had been done before.

But a lunar liftoff? That was something new.

"On November 17, as an engineering test to see if its rockets could be restarted on the Moon, Surveyor’s engines fired for 2.5 seconds, lifting the spacecraft 12 feet high and moving it 8 feet west of its original landing site, in the first powered liftoff from another celestial body," NASA wrote in a mission overview.

The engineering test proved that a spacecraft could land on the moon, and then lift off and take flight once more. That capability was a core requirement for NASA's Apollo astronaut moon landings that followed, since astronauts needed a way to return home after visiting the moon.

But there was critical science Surveyor 6 was able to do with the lunar hop.

"This maneuver provided excellent views of the surface disturbances produced by the initial landing and the effects of firing rocket engines close to the lunar surface," scientists with the Lunar and Planetary Institute wrote in a summary. "Photography obtained after the hop contributed to the soil mechanics investigation."

Surveyor 6 also had a bar magnet on its footpad that allowed scientists to test how magnetic the lunar regolith was at the landing site.

Not Ribeye: Bobby Flay Recommends This Steak Cut For The Best Tacos

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

When taco night rolls around, you'll want to head to the butcher and grab some good meat. If you're making carne asada, your instinct might be to pick the ribeye. While it might be the gold standard of steak, tacos and ribeye are an inherent mismatch. Tacos are one of the most democratic foods out there, while ribeye is made for deep pockets and expensive tastes. Even Food Network's Bobby Flay recommends skipping it. Instead, he swaps the pricey cut for the seriously underrated skirt steak.

Ribeye is America's favorite steak thanks to its rich marbling and tender texture, but you'll rarely find it in taquerias. Beyond being pricey, ribeye's assets are its downfall in tacos. In an interview with Food and Wine, Flay revealed the reason why he often chooses to skip ribeye altogether: "[Ribeye] can be inconsistent because the fat doesn't always melt even when cooked properly." This is a real problem in tacos. Encased in a taco, you don't have the option to trim around a rubbery pocket of fat or leave it on the plate. Even if you trim the fat before adding the steak to your tacos, it's so unevenly distributed that you risk ending up with wildly inconsistent slices.

Unlike ribeye, skirt is cut from the plate primal, a hard-working area near the diaphragm. Rather than being streaked with generous ribbons of fat, it's comprised of long, coarse muscle fibers, which give it a chewier, more fibrous bite along with a bold, beef-forward flavor. Its thin profile may not win it fans at white tablecloth restaurants, but it's exactly why taquerias can't get enough of it. All that surface area compared to its thickness means skirt steak cooks quickly, soaks up marinades fast, and slices evenly, which is everything you want from taco meat.

There's no cut of beef more well-suited to carne asada than skirt. Literally translating to "grilled meat," the beef is usually marinated in a citrus and garlic blend, with the acidic ingredients helping to tenderize the surface of the meat. After being left to soak up the flavor for a couple of hours, it's then seared or grilled over an open flame or charcoal to impart a smoky, charred flavor (an air fryer also works if you can't grill). Because skirt is such a thin cut, it doesn't enjoy spending too much time over high heat; it only needs around two to four minutes per side. Skirt can be prone to drying out, so it would be wise to avoid cooking past a medium doneness.

How you cut skirt is just as important as how you cook it. Compared to other cuts, skirt has particularly long, dense muscle fibers, and if you slice them with the grain, you'll leave them intact, resulting in chewy, rubbery bites. Slicing against the grain shortens these fibers, turning them into more satisfying, bite-sized strands. To identify the grain, look closely at the surface of the meat to spot the direction of the muscle fibers running through the beef.

Although skirt is beloved by taquerias, that doesn't make it suited to all beef tacos. One popular style where we would skip skirt is birria, the slow-cooked, tender beef marinated in a chili-and-spice adobo served with a flavorful broth for dipping. The whole concept of birria revolves around slow-braising, requiring tough, collagen-rich cuts like chuck or beef cheek, which break down into tender, shreddable meat over several hours. While skirt does have some connective tissue, it's simply too thin and lean to survive hours of prolonged heat.

Posted November 17, 2026 | Reviewed by Abigail Fagan

Recently, I was scrolling through the Billboard Year-End Hot 100 singles of 1980. I was 14 years old that year, making it the absolute peak of my "musical reminiscence bump," the developmental window when our brains permanently wire the music of our youth to our deepest emotions.

While perusing the list, I realized there's one song that always stops me dead in my tracks and hits surprisingly hard whenever it catches me off guard in a random place, like a supermarket checkout line. That song is Elton John's "Little Jeannie."

Lyrically, this song doesn't say anything profoundly moving until near the end. And the refrain "I want you to be my acrobat, I want you to be my lover" always seems like a throwaway line. On paper, the song could easily be categorized as a seriously schmaltzy, mid-tempo early '80s pop song.

Nevertheless, every time I hear the opening percussion and cascading drum intro, I get verklempt. During the 20-second, lyric-free introduction that might sound like "Muzak-y" elevator music to some, my emotions swell and I'm filled with a deep sense of, for lack of a better word, "wistfulness" that doesn't make sense to me logically.

Curious if I was alone in having such a poignant response to a somewhat cheesy pop song, I looked at the YouTube comments to see if other listeners echoed my sentiment.

Notably, for many of the "Little Jeannie" versions on YouTube, all of which are sung in English by Elton John, the comment sections are dominated by foreign-language responses (lots of Portuguese!). According to Google Translate, these comments convey that even among non-English speakers or ESL listeners, "Little Jeannie" hits hard in bittersweet ways that seem to catch people off guard.

Why does a song that isn't inherently sad make someone cry? And how can a listener feel nostalgia for an era they didn't live through? As it turns out, the answers lie in a fascinating intersection of behavioral psychology, imagination, and neuroscience.

A new study published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS) on November 16, 2026, investigated the sociocultural dimensions of music-evoked memories. The researchers played 30-second clips of billboard-topping hits to 223 college students, utilizing songs from their own teenage years (the 2010s) and their parents' teenage years (the 1980s and 1990s).

Using natural language processing to analyze the memories these songs evoked, the researchers found that certain songs reliably generated strikingly similar memory scenarios among strangers from comparable backgrounds, driven by culturally learned "scripts."

To find out where that emotion was coming from, the researchers stripped away the music and gave a separate group of participants just a song's lyrics to read. Without music accompanying the words to a song, the shared sociocultural dimensions of people's autobiographical memories and associations diminished.

Based on this observation, the researchers concluded that the lyrics alone don't drive our intense reactions to pop music. Instead, the emotional scripts are embedded in the musical sound and genre itself.

When a YouTube commenter notes that "Little Jeannie" isn't a sad song but makes their eyes well up with tears, it isn't necessarily the lyrics driving that reaction. It's the specific acoustic architecture created by an old Fender Rhodes electric piano, alto saxophone, and flugelhorn, all wrapped in layered vocal harmonies. This unique musical alchemy tugs directly at the heartstrings, completely bypassing the analytical mind where words reside.

How does neuroscience explain the enigma of a young listener feeling "ridiculously melancholic" when hearing a song released decades before they were born?

One possible explanation: While we experience a primary surge of memory attachment for the music of our own adolescence, we also develop a secondary attachment to the music of our parents and caregivers from earlier generations. As the authors explain in their recent PNAS paper:

"Interestingly, however, music-evoked autobiographical memories (MEAMs) are characterized by a potentially unique phenomenon known as 'cascading reminiscence bumps,' in which they exhibit a secondary reminiscence bump associated with music that was popular before listeners were born."

As children, we're often captive audiences in the backseat of a car or the family living room. Long before music was siloed into individual earbuds via streaming apps, "hard copy" formats like vinyl records, CDs, and mixtapes filled these shared spaces. Played aloud on common speakers, we passively absorbed our parents' adolescent soundtracks by osmosis.

Yet, even as modern listening habits shift toward isolated headphones, this intergenerational phenomenon persists. This new (2026) study suggests that these cultural "scripts" are so deeply wired into us that even if a younger participant had never heard a specific older song before, their brain can use the song's genre to automatically retrieve a feeling that fits the cultural vibe.

Because these acoustic architectures activate shared neural representations, the emotional script transcends both the generation and the delivery method.

Another recent study by Princeton's Elizabeth Margulis and colleagues, published in Nature Communications on August 18, 2026, used fMRI to explore the neural representations of imagined narratives that participants conjured up while listening to unfamiliar music.

During the experiment, researchers scanned people's brains while they listened to completely instrumental tracks. The scans revealed that across different test subjects, the music produced highly organized, shared patterns in the brain's default mode network (DMN). As the authors explain:

"This work demonstrates that music elicits detectable, modality-general representations, a kind of higher-order 'meaning', integrating both narrative imagery and its associated emotional profile, specific to particular excerpts."

Interestingly, Margulis's team found that the DMN doesn't need semantic language to engage in profound meaning-making. This helps to explain why it's almost impossible to pinpoint why certain music evokes shared imagined narratives; these emotions bubble up outside the realm of words (i.e., lexical semantics).

According to the latest 2026 research, music has the power to tap into a cultural frequency that bypasses our critical minds, reminding us that oftentimes, profound meaning is found in things we feel deeply but can't articulate.

For the last 60 years or so, we've been warned about stress and told to do everything we can to avoid it.

We've been taught how to meditate our way out of stress, down-regulate from it, "hack our cortisol," reduce our adrenaline, manage our vagus nerve, calm ourselves, numb ourselves, distract ourselves, and halt our stress responses in as many ways as we can.

We've also been taught to view many of the difficulties in our lives as always-negative exterior forces, or stressors, that act upon us without our knowledge or permission. Stress has been presented to us as a dangerously unwanted thing with no value whatsoever.

Thankfully, newer research into stressors and our stress response has found the opposite to be true.

In part 1 of this series, I focused on the weasel word of stress, because our stress response is an emotional response. In my work, I help people understand the emotions that form the foundation of our stress response. We learn how to name these emotions with precision and work directly with them to access the essential intelligence they contribute to us.

Three of the main emotions I see in our stress response are: anxiety (the emotion of motivation and task completion), anger (which helps us identify and uphold what we value), and panic (which steps forward when our lives are in danger).

Each of these emotions helps us meet stressors with intelligence and focus. When we know which emotions are supporting us, and we know how to work with those emotions (instead of against them), stressful situations can teach us who we are, what's important to us, and how we can meet life's challenges with focus, skills, and resilience.

But if we don't know how to identify or work with our emotions, we may feel overwhelmed as our bodies increase our activation to help us meet the necessary stressors we face. We may also learn two unhelpful lessons:

If we believe that stressors are foreign and that our emotions are problems, we'll create unnecessary suffering, not just in response to the stressors but because our skills are missing in action.

Psychological researcher Kelly McGonigal has been focusing on stress for many years, and in her 2016 book, The Upside of Stress: Why Stress Is Good for You, and How to Get Good at It, she shares information from decades of stress research. Her book is full of surprising facts that turn our understanding of stress on its ear. For instance:

If I told you that the price of tiger chow has gone up by 40 percent, you might have little to no reaction (assuming you aren't a tiger keeper). This is a big price increase, but because it has no meaning to you and does not impact any part of your life, your stress response will be quiet—as it should be; there's nothing for you to do about this, and your emotions don't need to be involved.

But if I told you that your share of the cost for your child's monthly doctor's visits has gone up by 40 percent, it's likely that your stress response would engage. Your emotions, most notably anxiety and anger, might intensify to bring you the energy and focus you need to work the problem. Your stress response would help you know that this situation is important, that it's worth your time and focus, and that you need its increased energy to help you make new plans to support your child's well-being.

Sadly, most stress management approaches would have you try to calm yourself or suppress your emotions (or merely observe them), as if the activation you feel is a problem in and of itself. But this old, tired idea is not only wrong; it's counterproductive, because the strength of your stress response is a key to your success and even mastery of the situation.

Of all the surprising things in stress research, this may be the most delightful: The strength of your stress response—your emotional intensity, your hyper-focus, your "nerves"—is directly connected to your ability to thrive. And this continues to be true even when you're well-acquainted with a stressor and no longer see it as surprising or shocking.

Researchers studied new and seasoned skydivers, certain that they would see a reduced stress response (reduced cortisol, adrenaline, and heart rate, less "stress") as the skydivers became accustomed to throwing themselves out of planes. Strangely, there wasn't any reduction in the markers of stress in the seasoned skydivers; in fact, the most skilled skydivers continued to exhibit very high stress responses, often higher than people jumping out of planes for the first time.

What they did find was that the seasoned skydivers had reframed their stress responses as excitement, readiness, stimulation, and even zest for life. This reframing helped them treat their activation as a necessary and fun part of skydiving, even though they might have experienced the exact same stress response as overwhelming the first time they leapt out of a plane.

This seemingly paradoxical relationship holds true for other high-performers such as athletes, surgeons, artists, actors, and musicians. A higher stress response does not predict failure; it is intimately connected to skill and mastery.

So, the key is not to avoid stressors or your stress response, and it's not to become an endlessly chilled-out and calm person.

The key to developing your resilience alongside your skills is to learn how to reframe your natural responses to stressors and welcome the stimulation, focus, and preparedness they provide.

Clouds block satellite cameras, so an AI learned how to redraw the ground beneath them

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

GeoCore-9B learned how to recreate the ground that is hidden beneath cloud cover in satellite photos.

A satellite picture of a flooded town is only worth something if the camera can see the town. On a cloudy day it can’t, and the picture shows cloud where the streets should be. A new AI model fills those streets back in.

It was built to know where on Earth it is looking and how much ground each pixel covers.

Jeonghyeok Do and Munchurl Kim of KAIST in South Korea built the model, called GeoCore-9B. Their paper is a preprint that has been accepted for presentation at NeurIPS 2026, an artificial intelligence conference. It has not yet been published in a journal.

The ground the model draws under a cloud is a reconstruction, not a photograph. Do and Kim make that point themselves, and they warn that realistic fake satellite imagery could be used to spread disinformation about disasters or geopolitical events.

Ordinary satellite pictures work like a camera. They record sunlight bouncing off the ground, so thick cloud or nighttime leaves them with nothing to record.

That matters most during floods and wildfires, when the weather is often bad and the ground is exactly what people need to see.

A radar satellite sends radio waves down and measures the echoes that come back. The method is called synthetic aperture radar, and it works at night and through cloud.

But its images don’t look like photographs, because brightness and texture in them mean different things. Do and Kim wanted one model for both problems.

Earlier attempts started with an AI built to generate ordinary photographs and then taught it about satellites. Do and Kim wrote that this builds in biases.

Ordinary photos are taken from eye level with a subject in the middle, while a satellite looks straight down at a scene with no center.

So they trained their AI model from scratch on satellite views alone, adjusting about 9 billion internal settings, known as parameters, as it learned.

The training set was 10 million satellite images, each paired with a sentence describing it. The note for a port mentioned ships and warehouses; the note for farmland mentioned a road between fields.

Each image also carried its latitude, longitude, and how much ground one pixel covers. A building looks very different when each pixel covers about 3 feet (1 meter) than when each covers about 105 feet (32 meters).

Without that number, a small building and a large industrial plant can have the same shape.

A model this large, trained from nothing, learns slowly and tends to produce scenes with no coherent layout. So Do and Kim brought in a second AI, already trained to recognize satellite imagery, as a teacher.

During training, the new model was scored on how well its internal picture of a scene agreed with the teacher’s picture of how roads connect and where field boundaries lie.

The teacher is switched off once training ends, so it adds no work when the model is in use. Without it, the team found, the model’s images had fragmented textures and distorted structures. With it, every score they tracked improved.

The team also tested what the model had absorbed about geography. Given only a latitude and longitude, with no text at all, it drew dense skyscrapers for the coordinates of New York City and ice fields for Antarctica. It drew sand dunes for the Sahara and forest for the Amazon.

The team checked 500 of these coordinate-only images generated by the model against all 10.5 million real world photos that were used as training images.

They found none that closely matched the real-world satellite images, suggesting that the AI was not simply copying existing photographs. However, they wrote that the test can’t rule out every form of memorization used by the AI model.

To put the model to work, Do and Kim froze it and trained a small set of extra settings for each task. This was far cheaper than retraining it.

On a public test set of cloudy and clear image pairs, GeoCore-9B outscored every program built specifically for cloud removal on two of three standard measures. Those were cover brightness and color accuracy, and how closely the structure matches the clear image.

On the third, how natural the image looks, it scored about the same as the best specialist. The roads and field boundaries in its reconstructions stayed where the clear images put them.

On a second public dataset, the model turned radar images into photo-like images. It scored best on the two measures of realism and fine detail, and it roughly matched the best specialist on structural similarity.

“By training the AI on where each satellite image was taken and the actual size of the terrain it shows, we made it possible to use a single model for multiple tasks such as cloud removal and image conversion,” said Kim, a professor in KAIST’s School of Electrical Engineering.

He added that he expects the work to make weather-limited satellite data more usable for assessing disasters and monitoring changes in forests and cities.

The model only produces ordinary color images. It can read a radar image but can’t generate one. In addition, it doesn’t handle the multispectral images, with light bands beyond visible color, that some Earth-observation satellites record.

One piece, the component that compresses each image before the model works on it, came from ordinary photography. On the cloud-removal images, it kept the main features but lost some faint local detail.

By their own account, the experiments can’t say how much of the gain came from the model’s size, its training data, or the teacher.

So far each task has been tested on a single public dataset. How the model performs on other satellites and regions is unknown, and that is the test Do and Kim plan next.

Do and Kim also want the model to predict changes on the ground: flooding, building damage, wildfire spread, and urban growth. The team plans to explore surveillance and reconnaissance as well.

The model’s code and weights are online, so other groups can run those tests.

The full study has been posted as a preprint on arXiv and accepted for presentation at NeurIPS 2026. It has not been published in a journal.

This 30-Mile-Long Canyonlands Art Gallery Might Be Utah’s Best Backpacking Trip

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

When you think of ancient rock art, what comes to mind? Handprints? Stick figures? Maybe a long-limbed man holding something? For the most part, you’d be right. The petroglyphs and pictographs of the southwest are beautiful, but they aren’t known for their intricate details.

But there’s one canyon in Utah that blows those expectations out of the water.

Hidden deep inside Canyonlands National Park, Salt Creek Canyon holds some of the most impressive pictographs I’ve ever seen. Enormous human figures, elaborate patterns, and colorful paintings that have survived for hundreds of years. It’s an open-air art museum, and one that’s slowly weathering away.

And the museum doesn’t stop at rock art. There are Ancestral Puebloan granaries, settlers’ abandoned ranch buildings, and reminders of the conservationists who helped protect this landscape.

A walk through Salt Creek Canyon is a walk through history. Over two days and more than 30 miles, it became one of my biggest backpacking surprises of the year.

Trail: Salt Creek Trail + Angel Arch Detour Start: Cathedral Butte / Salt Creek Canyon Trailhead Finish: Cave Spring / Salt Creek Gate Distance: 30.78 miles (my GPS; mapped distance may differ) Elevation gain: 1,054 feet (my GPS) Type: Point-to-point, vehicle shuttle required Permits: Required for overnight trips Highlight: The All American Man pictograph

The trail begins high in the contested Bears Ears National Monument, in the shadow of Cathedral Butte. Typically, this is the sort of view you’d have to earn after hours of climbing. Instead, you start at the highest point, looking down into a canyon that stretches toward the horizon.

It’s mostly downhill from here, but don’t mistake that for easy. Thirty miles with a loaded backpack is still 30 miles, especially when much of it is through deep sand.

About two miles in, we crossed into Canyonlands National Park. My Gaia GPS map was littered with waypoints and “Don’t miss!” markers, the first of which were Kirk Spring and Kirk’s Cabin.

In an otherwise dusty landscape of sagebrush and juniper, a little oasis appeared. Reeds, cottonwoods, and bright green vegetation surrounded a small spring. We filtered some water and continued toward the cabin, an 1890s-era log home with a fieldstone chimney.

Outside the weathered log structure sat the wheels and axles of an old wagon. Inside, bear traps and antlers hung on the walls. It looked like something out of a history museum, only sandier, dustier, and considerably more authentic.

This was our first introduction to one of Salt Creek’s defining qualities: Layers of human history occupy nearly every corner of the canyon.

Long before ranchers arrived, Indigenous people—Ancestral Puebloans and Fremont, then Ute, Paiute, and Diné—lived, hunted, and farmed here. Later came European explorers, conservationists, and eventually backpackers like us. Different people, different centuries, but all drawn to the same remarkable place.

One of the biggest reasons was water. Unlike much of Canyonlands, Salt Creek supports a surprisingly lush corridor of vegetation, even in a dry year. We encountered several places to filter water, although sources became scarcer farther down the canyon. (Water here is seasonal and never guaranteed, so come prepared to carry plenty.)

Around mile six, a small spur trail led us beneath a sandstone overhang. Tucked into the alcove were several stone structures, likely granaries used by Ancestral Puebloan people to store their harvests. Just below them, dark-green vines spilled across the sand. They looked like squash plants, still thriving in the shadow of structures built centuries ago.

I’d come to Salt Creek expecting incredible rock art, but nothing prepared me for what we found around mile eight: The All American Man.

High inside a sandstone alcove stands a human-like figure, nearly life-size, painted in striking blue, white, and red. He appears to wear white boots, an elaborate headdress, and something resembling an American flag across his body.

For decades, the figure’s unusual appearance led some to question whether it was even ancient. Radiocarbon testing eventually confirmed its authenticity, suggesting it dates to around the 14th century. Standing below it, I found the whole thing almost impossible to believe. (Do yourself a favor: If you plan on hiking Salt Creek, don’t look up pictures of the All American Man beforehand. Let it surprise you.)

Not far beyond, we encountered another extraordinary panel near Four Faces Spring. Four enormous, intricately patterned faces stared out from beneath a sandstone alcove. Remnants of stone structures sat beneath them.

Who were these figures? Were they people, spirits, or something else entirely?

Their exact meaning remains uncertain, but the artwork survives, part of a cultural history that remains important to Indigenous communities today.

The farther we walked, the more the canyon changed.

Towering sandstone walls closed in around us. Willows, reeds, and cottonwoods transformed the dusty trail into something resembling a jungle. Small streams appeared beneath the vegetation.

Unfortunately, so did the mosquitoes. I’ve spent a lot of time hiking through the desert, but I’ve never encountered so many of the little suckers.

After miles of hiking, a soft rain finally brought some relief. A rainbow appeared against the red canyon walls just as we approached SC4, our reserved campsite for the evening.

At roughly mile 12, SC4 is the last of the four designated Salt Creek campsites when traveling north. It wasn’t quite halfway through our route, which meant we had an even longer second day ahead.

The next morning, we packed up and headed toward another of the canyon’s great attractions. Several miles off the main trail, at the end of a remote side canyon, stood Angel Arch.

Arches National Park may be famous for its sandstone formations, but Angel Arch can hold its own against just about anything you’ll find there.

Its massive sandstone opening rises roughly 135 feet high, with an unusual formation beside it resembling an angel leaning forward with folded wings. It’s an incredible sight, made even more impressive by the effort required to reach it. We were the only people there. In fact, we were the only hikers on the entire trail: We hadn’t seen a single other person or group. The detour added several miles to an already long day, but I’d do it again without hesitation.

Back on the main route, one more archaeological treasure awaited us: the Flying Carpet Panel. Like the All American Man and Four Faces, the artwork was enormous, with figures and intricate designs spread across the sandstone.

Then came the final miles. The beautiful canyon trail eventually gave way to a sandy four-wheel-drive road, where every step seemed to require twice the effort. After nearly 30 miles, walking through deep sand was just about the last thing my legs wanted to do.

Eventually, we reached a gate, a paved road, and the end of our journey.

As spectacular as the scenery was, what I’ll remember most about Salt Creek isn’t the canyon itself. It’s the people who called it home.

For centuries, Ancestral Puebloan communities farmed along the creek, stored food in cliffside granaries, and created artwork that still watches over the canyon today. Walking past these places, I couldn’t help thinking about how many people had traveled this same canyon before us. How many had looked up at those same cliffs, collected water from those same springs, or sheltered beneath those sandstone overhangs?

We spent two days here, carrying everything we needed on our backs. For others, this canyon was home. For a brief moment, we got to be part of its story too.

Voyager 1 reaches 1 light-day from Earth

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

At 5:16 a.m. EST on Wednesday, Nov. 18, Voyager 1 will become the first spacecraft to reach a distance of 1 light-day from Earth. A command radioed from home will need 24 hours to arrive, and a reply another 24 to come back. NASA is asking the public to celebrate by sending the spacecraft a signal on the evening of Tuesday, Nov. 17.

NASA launched the twin Voyager spacecraft in 1977 to tour the giant planets: Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. Voyager 1 flew past Jupiter and Saturn, while Voyager 2 went on to Uranus and Neptune and is still the only spacecraft to visit either one. Both then kept going. They’re now the only probes ever to study interstellar space directly, the region beyond the Sun’s magnetic field.

Each spacecraft runs on a radioisotope thermoelectric generator, the same kind of power source that drives NASA’s Curiosity and Perseverance rovers on Mars. Plutonium-238, the fuel source, gives off heat as it naturally decays, and the generators turn that heat into electricity. But because the output fades by about 4 watts every year, engineers have spent years shutting off components and instruments to stretch the spacecraft’s lives. This year, a risky maneuver nicknamed the “Big Bang” turned off several devices in one coordinated move to buy the spacecraft and their remaining instruments several more years of operation. Today, Voyager 1 only runs two of its original 10 instruments, and Voyager 2 runs three.

Voyager 1 is closing in on 1 light-day from Earth (about 16.1 billion miles [25.9 billion kilometers]) — farther than any spacecraft has ever gone. Its twin is about 2 billion miles (3.2 billion km) closer to Earth, near enough that a signal takes about 20 hours to reach Voyager 2.

Voyager 1 lifted off Sept. 5, 1977, two weeks after Voyager 2 but on a shorter trajectory as it was traveling to Jupiter. After visiting Saturn in 1980, it drifted away from the solar system’s planets for good. A decade later, it captured the famous photograph of Earth known as the Pale Blue Dot. In August 2012, it became the first spacecraft to enter interstellar space, and Voyager 2 followed in 2018. Voyager 2 won’t reach the light-day mark until November 2035. You can see both missions’ exact locations on NASA’s mission status page.

A light-day is a distance measuring how far light travels in 24 hours. At about 186,000 miles per second (299,792 km per second), that adds up to roughly 16.1 billion miles (25.9 billion km). For scale, the Moon is just 1.3 light-seconds away, the Sun about 8.3 light-minutes, and Neptune a bit over 4 light-hours. Proxima Centauri, the nearest star beyond the Sun, lies more than 1,500 light-days away.

At a speed of nearly 38,000 mph (61,000 km/h), Voyager 1 is anything but slow. Still, it needed more than 49 years to cover the distance light crosses in a single day.

Astronomy usually asks readers to dim their lights. But tomorrow night is an exception: To celebrate Voyager 1’s accomplishment, NASA invites everyone to flash Voyager’s call sign, V-G-R, skyward in Morse code on Tuesday evening. Use NASA’s automated flasher on your smartphone or tablet. Or grab a plain flashlight and do it manually: V: dot-dot-dot-dash; G: dash-dash-dot; R: dot-dash-dot. Dots are quick blinks of the light, and dashes are long flashes of about a second. Pause between letters.

How Each Music Listening Platform Is Treating AI Music (Updating)

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

From Bandcamp's outright ban of AI music to Spotify's "AI Personas" approach, here's how all the music platforms are dealing with the flood of AI-generated content.

One of the most pressing questions in the music industry right now is how streaming services should be treating the deluge of AI-generated music on their platforms. Should AI music be demonetized, or even removed from platforms? Should it be labeled or flagged so that users know what they are listening to? Or, for those optimistic about AI’s creative possibilities, should it all just be left alone?

It’s hard to know the exact scale of AI music on streaming services today, but what is clear is that there’s a lot of it — and the amount of it is growing fast. Deezer reports the count of daily AI track uploads at over 50% at “peak level” in June 2026, and Apple Music told Billboard in April that “more than a third” of monthly uploads were 100% AI-generated. Qobuz also noted in January that 34% of new uploads each day are AI-generated. These metrics, however, may still represent an undercount of all the material that uses AI in some part of the creative process, given partially AI generated music is much more challenging to detect. Plus, it’s impossible to determine when AI is used to write lyrics or melodies that are then recorded by human musicians, unless a user offered up their prompting history.

When considering the challenges of actually implementing regulations on streaming services, the question of what to do about AI music becomes incredibly complex. There’s a risk of false positives with AI detection tools, but requiring self-disclosure from rights holders will likely lead to outright deception at worst, or innocent omission at best, given how prevalent AI elements can be at any point in the recording process.

Still, lines are being drawn, and until some sort of consensus emerges, those lines are different at different digital service providers. To learn more about what each music listening platform is doing about AI-generated music, Billboard has compiled an updating list of each services’ policies as they articulate them.

SoundCloud bans all AI-generated content on its platform that impersonates human artists or otherwise clones their voices, and it requires that AI-generated or manipulated content complies with the same platform requirements as any other music uploaded to SoundCloud. The platform itself has said it does not use any music content to train generative AI models and would not do so any time in the future without prior consent.

In an open letter in November 2026, SoundCloud CEO Eliah Seton wrote that the company will also be adding protections against users who use AI to spam the platform with slop, and it will beef up its defenses against streaming manipulation, a common byproduct of AI. Those who violate its policies will be blocked from the platform.

SoundCloud, however, has no issue with AI music on its platform in general. Unlike other listening platforms, SoundCloud has leaned into partnering with compliant, licensed AI music companies, like ElevenLabs and Moises, to provide a direct pipeline for AI users to share their work online.

Spotify is attempting to tackle the negative uses of AI music, while leaving the door open for users to enjoy the songs if they want to. Last year, the service announced policies against spam, impersonation and streaming fraud — all of which are not issues specific to AI, necessarily, but that can be exacerbated by the technology. In April 2026, Spotify provided a way for creators using AI to disclose exactly how they did it in the credits, including whether AI was used in just one guitar lick or in the entire production.

The service also announced verification badges for human creators and is rolling out a service called Artist Profile Protection which allows participants to essentially lock their artist pages from getting random uploads added to them, a persistent issue on Spotify and other streaming services.

The company’s boldest step yet came in August when the Swedish streamer announced the creation of a new label that will be applied to AI-generated artists and begin rolling out in mid-September. Music from artists bearing the label, called “AI Persona,” will not be included in personalized recommendations on the platform.

Apple Music is working towards visible labels for AI-generated music on the platform. The system, dubbed Made With AI, was announced in August and is set to roll out by the end of 2026. The platform noted that any song that is tagged as being “materially generated using AI” by content providers, like record labels and distributors, will receive these labels.

The move builds on Apple Music’s March announcement, requiring content providers to disclose and tag where AI is being used in the creative process, whether that’s in an upload’s artwork, recording, composition or music video. The system relies on disclosure, rather than an automated AI detection and flagging system, even though Apple Music head Oliver Schusser has told Billboard the company has a detector the team uses internally to monitor the rise of AI content.

YouTube requires that anyone uploading generative AI content, including music, discloses when AI has been used. Then, the platform adds a label to that content for viewer transparency. Beyond that, YouTube has not formed other policies around AI music specifically. (Google, YouTube’s parent company, has also been pursuing an AI music-making model of its own, Lyria 3, after acquiring ProducerAI earlier this year.)

Amazon Music has systems in place to prevent impersonation, deception and spam, all of which are commonly found in AI music uploads, but beyond that the company does not have any policies related to AI music. In a statement, Amazon said, “The broader music community is defining what labeling standards should look like, and we’re part of those conversations. As those discussions continue, we’ll continue to explore ways to help customers make informed choices about what they’re listening to.”

In June, Tidal, which had previously stayed silent on the issue of AI-generated music, came out with some of the strictest rules against the content type in the industry. This included the announcement that Tidal will not monetize AI-generated content and is making distributors responsible for identifying AI-generated music before uploading it. It will also try to identify AI content itself and will administer labels for AI music, which began in mid-July, so that users know the origins of what music they are hearing. This labeling system will start with fully AI generated music before being expanded to identify and label “substantially AI-generated” music as technology improves and “AI-detection methods become more reliable.”

“The responsibility to identify and tag AI-generated content should not rest with Tidal alone,” the company said in a press release. “We expect — and will begin to enforce — that content distributors identify AI-generated content before it reaches our platform.”

In January, Qobuz announced that it is “taking a human-first stand on AI-generated music,” and vowed that its editorial curation — which includes playlists, articles and reviews created by human experts — would remain “100% human,” and that it would work to ensure its “Discover” page “filter[s] out AI-generated tracks.” The service also said it would identify fraudulent streams and exclude them from the royalty pool and that it was “in the process of developing detection and monitoring systems to identify AI-generated content and fraudulent streaming patterns.”

Additionally, Qobuz now has contractual clauses prohibiting delivery of 100% AI-generated music, and it will integrate “dedicated DDEX fields for artists and labels to report AI-generated content.”

Deezer has been the most outspoken of the streaming services about AI music thus far. In January 2025, the French service began reporting the number of AI songs it was detecting on its service. Back then, it was reported that 10% of daily uploads were fully AI-generated, according to their in-house detector. Now, as of July, it was reported as more than 50% at “peak level in June 2026.”

The company has announced a number of policies designed to combat the negative uses of AI music. It uses an AI detector to label fully AI tracks and excludes that music from algorithmic or editorial recommendation. Plus, as of July, Deezer started removing any AI tracks that it suspects have been used in streaming fraud scams or that haven’t been streamed for six months or more. (Deezer has said previously that up to 85% of the streams on fully AI tracks were fraudulent).

In January, Bandcamp announced a total ban on AI-generated songs on its platform, whether that AI use is in all or a “substantial part” of the creative process. This was a first for online music platforms, but it came a few months after iHeartRadio announced a similar ban on “play[ing] AI music that features synthetic vocalists pretending to be human,” on its radio stations nationwide. Bandcamp also noted that it does not allow the use of any AI tools “to impersonate other artists or styles.”

I’m A Professional Cook &amp; My Secret To Perfectly Smooth Gravy Is This $4 Pantry Staple

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Gravy is one of the most problematic dishes on the Thanksgiving table. Every year, home cooks struggle and sweat over gravy that’s too thin, too greasy, or—worst of all—lumpy. All while your relatives watch and the turkey gets cold.

Making the gravy the day before is a surefire way to avoid the last-minute panic. But on holidays when prepping ahead just isn’t in the cards, one simple hack can solve your gravy woes: a $4 pantry staple that will thicken up any gravy in mere minutes—without any lumps.

The secret to perfectly smooth gravy is Wondra flour. Known to chefs and home cooks alike for its bright blue cylindrical can, Wondra is wheat flour that has been pre-gelatinized, dried, and finely milled so it dissolves more easily and thickens sauces faster than regular flour. This means you can whisk it directly into any gravy (no matter the temperature!) without worry about clumping—and it will thicken that gravy almost immediately.

If you’re making gravy from scratch with no other thickener besides Wondra, I recommend starting with 1 tablespoon for every cup of stock and/or drippings. You can do this by heating the drippings or stock in a pot on the stove, then just whisking in the Wondra. It should thicken within a few minutes. If your drippings don’t have a ton of fat, I recommend whisking in a little butter too, to help with the flavor.

If you’re using Wondra to troubleshoot a gravy that’s too thin, I recommend whisking in a few teaspoons at a time. The Wondra should do its thickening magic within a minute or two after it goes in, and then you can decide whether you need more.

While Wondra flour is great for thickening gravies and sauces, it’s far from a one-trick pony. I’ve used it to coat white fish (such as tilapia) before pan-searing to give it a beautifully thin, crispy crust—and help prevent the fish from sticking to the pan. It’s also a common ingredient in batters for fried foods, since its lower protein content helps form a lighter, crispier crust—and it’s also a magical way to get a flakier pastry dough. So even if you only make gravy once a year, that blue canister won’t go to waste.

As decision model use skyrockets, is customization the next frontier?

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In October, more than one in four requests on Vercel’s AI model router went to decision models, not LLMs — an enormous share of work for a kind of computerized intelligence that was invented only a month earlier.

Decision models are LLMs that don’t produce output tokens; instead, they’re tuned to use their general intelligence to quickly and cheaply decide between preset options, while registering its level of confidence in the choice. They’re useful in production environments, where fast decisions can be more important than the reasoning provided by a slower and more expensive frontier model.

TypeSafe became a $7.5 billion company after releasing its Jev family of models, which continues to dominate usage (at least where we can see metrics from services like OpenRouter, which handled more than 1.5 billion queries to TypeSafe’s models last week).

However, it’s easy to make a decision model, and dozens were released in the wake of Jev. One Amazon engineer made one in a weekend, and soon every hyperscaler had its own option. On Tuesday, Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella said that his company is rolling out its decision model, Microsoft-Decision-1.1, for wide use on Azure immediately.

The action is happening on both ends of the ecosystem: Sqwish, a U.K.-based AI startup behind a decision model called kettei fat, announced a $3 million pre-seed round today. Co-founders Ushnish Sengupta and Federica Freddi, who met as Cambridge grad students, had been developing small LLMs when Jev arrived on the scene, and they realized their architectures could be repurposed as a powerful decision model.

Data appears to be the key to a successful model in this category — TypeSafe CEO Diogo Almeida told TechCrunch that the hardest part of building Jev was finely calibrating its judgement using statistically-adjusted synthetic data. Sqiwsh, too, has been working with synthetic data, but its unique offering is built-in post-training tools that use a customer’s own data to improve the model’s judgement.

“What makes us not a commodity, I think, is the fact that we are offering our customers the same power…they can keep uploading their production data, and just the same way that we trained our models, their models can also keep getting better at the tasks that they care about,” CEO Ushnish Sengupta told TechCrunch.

Attempting to rank decision models is difficult; Sqwish’s “kettei fat” model has performed well on some benchmarks, but most builders of the technology say that the real proof is in the pudding: It’s generally simple for developers to sub in one model for another and see how it does in a specific context.

“The benchmark is more of an invitation to try the model out, and then the continual tuning is the reason why they should stick to us,” Sengupta said.

The Microsoft-Decision-1.1 model is also the result of the company carefully balancing across cost, latency, intelligence and calibration. Achint Srivastava, the engineering VP who led the project, agreed that too much focus on benchmarks is a mistake.

“What we see is that ‘benchmaxed’ models fall flat under real enterprise usage very quickly,” Srivastava wrote in an email. “Our testing underscores just how challenging it is to deliver accuracy, reliability, speed and cost efficiency in a single model. Microsoft-Decision-1.1 and Jev are among the few that perform strongly across all four dimensions, even when evaluated on ‘held out’ or unseen benchmarks reflective of real use cases.”

For startups like Sqwish, competition with hyperscalers and frontier labs is an opportunity. Freddi predicts that future systems will see so-called System One intelligence, like decision models, working in concert with System Two intelligence, like frontier LLMs.

“Even in that future, System One is maybe a $10 billion, $50 billion [opportunity], and OpenAI is doing this IPO with you know $1 trillion, $2 trillion market value in mind,” she told TechCrunch. “This is all we care about; whereas for them, this is just a side project.”

Data center slowdown would raise risks for utility investments

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Let Utility Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

An estimated 191 GW of planned data center capacity faces new risk after the midterm elections, with exposure concentrated in Texas, Ohio and Georgia, according to a report from Orennia.

Utilities could be left holding the bill for billions in infrastructure spending committed in anticipation of artificial intelligence data centers that are delayed, downsized or canceled, industry observers and experts told Utility Dive.

Opposition to data center construction has crossed traditional party lines, while Democratic gains in the midterms are adding pressure on earlier-stage investments across key markets. Together, these dynamics raise the stakes for project approvals and utility cost recovery, they say.

“From a financing perspective, prolonged uncertainty will increase the cost of capital, reduce available leverage and make investors more hesitant to commit significant development dollars before power is secured,” said Andrejka Bernatova, managing partner of investment firm Dynamix Capital Partners, which invests in energy infrastructure.

Even in regions that theoretically have enough capacity to accommodate new large loads, projects may struggle to secure a credible path to power on the timelines they need.

“Interconnection, permitting, and financing all matter, but they are part of the same execution question of whether the team can get reliable power to the site and actually deliver,” Bernatova said. “A customer commitment or a large announced capacity number does not answer that by itself.”

Ultimately, Bernatova expects investors to favor markets that offer certainty around power availability and timelines, potentially eroding the competitive advantage of markets like Texas, which until recently attracted substantial data center investment.

Over the summer, Gov. Greg Abbott, R, announced a pause on new data center interconnections pending an audit of the queue. In the midterms, he lost his bid for reelection to Democrat Gina Hinojosa, who has pledged to maintain pressure on the industry after taking office next year.

Nearly all of the 46 municipal ballot measures tracked by Bloomberg that addressed data center development resulted in restrictions, moratoriums or bans.

That unfavorability extends to a substantial share of the development pipeline. An estimated 191 GW of planned data center capacity faces new risk, with exposure concentrated in Texas, Ohio and Georgia, where incoming Democratic governors campaigned on pausing or restricting construction, according to a report from energy analytics firm Orennia that was shared with Utility Dive.

Texas accounts for nearly half of the pre-construction in development in the U.S., so a statewide approval freeze would have an outsized impact on the national buildout, but the “impact of delayed or unrealized data center build-out on power prices varies widely across technologies and markets,” Orennia said.

If 20% of projected data center demand in the Electric Reliability Council of Texas region fails to materialize, solar and wind generators could see their average wholesale revenue fall 38% and 37%, respectively, according to the report. Implied heat rates, a measure of the economics of gas-fired generation relative to fuel costs, could decline 39%. In the same scenario, two-hour battery storage arbitrage spreads could fall below new-build break-even requirements.

In PJM, delaying half of the planned data center buildout by two years could reduce four-hour battery storage arbitrage spreads by 16% and lower solar and wind capture prices and implied heat rates by about 12% through 2030, the modeling suggests.

The election results are unlikely to produce sweeping federal AI legislation but could lead to congressional hearings and greater transparency requirements, according to D.C.-based research and advisory firm Capstone. Its analysts expect much of the policy debate to take place at state utility commissions and local siting boards.

Despite a proliferation of large-load tariffs over the past year, safeguards may still leave customers exposed in the event of a slowdown, some say.

“Existing tariffs and regulatory frameworks likely won’t fully protect customers because they were designed for a different century,” Louisa Eberle, principal at the Regulatory Assistance Project, warned in a written statement.

Eberle also called for reforms to utility business practices “to turn this data center surge into updated infrastructure, cleaner energy and a better deal for average customers.”

The push for lower electricity bills is prompting policymakers to look more closely at how utilities use existing grid infrastructure.

Richard Caperton, vice president of policy at SPAN, a maker of smart electrical panels, anticipates greater emphasis on data center projects that can be served without major new grid investments.

“I’m really thrilled to see governors like Abigail Spanberger [in Virginia] campaign on affordability and then embrace utilization as the solution,” he said.

In April, Gov. Spanberger signed legislation requiring Dominion Energy and Appalachian Power to report how effectively they use existing grid infrastructure. The State Corporation Commission can use those metrics to establish improvement targets and evaluate utility spending.

“Winning candidates who emphasized lowering electricity prices are already looking for ways to deliver on their promises quickly,” Caperton said. “I expect that will lead to more policies and programs that boost grid utilization being ratified and implemented in early 2027. How those policies affect data center development and utility investments will vary by state.”

Stephen Rose, CEO of infrastructure software provider Render Networks, said he expects to see a “pause and necessary shakeout” in data center development.

“Fully permitted projects with real capital will keep moving,” Rose said. “The speculative end takes the hit: developers land-banking without firm power, and the contractors who staffed up for projects that won't break ground.”

Rose cited AEP Ohio's large-load tariff, approved in July 2025, which requires new data centers above 25 MW to pay for at least 85% of requested capacity for up to 12 years, with exit fees and financial guarantees. The utility’s data-center pipeline subsequently fell by more than half from more than 30 GW to about 13.

Rose expressed concern, however, that a broad slowdown wouldn’t affect hyperscalers as much as it would the businesses and people that build data centers. He recalled delays in Louisiana's Broadband Equity, Access and Deployment program in 2025, where service providers bore the costs of equipment and crews while waiting for approvals.

“When timelines stalled, the hit didn't land on big tech balance sheets. It landed on local drilling, civil and fiber contractors,” Rose said. “A data center slowdown hits the same people first, and Texas risks the same fallout.”

Permitting hurdles could make data center developers more selective about where they build and under what terms.

Andy Roehr, managing director in the tax advisory firm Baker Tilly’s energy and infrastructure practice, sees developers as more likely to redirect capital than abandon projects – though that could lead to a winnowing of queues in some jurisdictions.

“A developer is typically evaluating three, four or five sites at any given moment, so when permitting, regulation or local opposition makes one location difficult to read, the project is far more likely to be delayed or redirected than canceled,” Roehr said.

Roehr said states and municipalities competing for projects that can no longer move forward elsewhere could find themselves in a “bidding war.”

"The most interesting thing I am seeing is that these moratoria are creating a developer’s market since they have the assets, tax revenues and jobs that communities want or need,” he continued.

Some developers may shift to “less optimal sites where projects can get done at an effective price point and time-to-market, but the regulatory and legislative environment is less hostile,” he said.

Changes in expected demand could also lead to repricing across the supply chain, from gas turbines and processor racks to construction costs.

Another uncertainty is the possibility of federal permitting reform, which could make it easier to build certain kinds of energy projects.

The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, which advanced in the Senate last week, faces a difficult path to passage following the midterm elections, analysts say.

Democrats are split over the proposal, with some expressing concerns over changes to environmental protections in the bill and urging party leaders to wait until next year when the Democrats will control both the House and Senate to pursue their priorities.

Roehr said in his view, the BAAJA takes an “all of the above” approach and would be as meaningful to solar as to nuclear or natural gas. But what the industry needs most, he said, is stability.

“Developers, investors and host communities need certainty to keep committing capital and local resources to these projects,” Roehr said. “These are 20-, 40-, even 60-year investments, and if the environment keeps whipsawing then jobs don’t appear, tax payments don’t appear and capital moves on — there will be no winners.”

Physicists at the Large Hadron Collider have detected a rare, 1-in-10-billion particle interaction that opens a view into the 'zeptoverse,' the smallest distance scale ever measured.

Using the Large Hadron Collider (LHC), the world's largest particle accelerator, scientists have gotten the first glimpse of the "zeptoverse" — the universe as it behaves within less than a hundred-trillionth of a wavelength of visible light, the smallest scale that researchers have explored to date.

The new findings may one day help to answer long-standing cosmic mysteries, such as why there is far more matter than antimatter seen in the universe, and what makes up the dark matter that constitutes about 85% of all matter in the universe, the researchers announced Nov. 3 at a seminar at CERN (the European Organization for Nuclear Research), the organization that operates the LHC. The study, available as a preprint, has not yet been peer reviewed.

At the LHC, located near Geneva at the border of France and Switzerland, researchers were investigating elusive particles called beauty quarks. These subatomic particles are cousins of the standard "up" and "down" quarks that make up the cores of all atoms in the universe.

When beauty quarks are created — in this case, when the LHC smashes atoms together at incredibly high speeds — they live for just one and a half trillionths of a second before they decay. There are a number of ways beauty quarks can break down; researchers hope that analyzing rare forms of these decays might reveal that they might not unfold as expected.

Such outcomes could then shed light on new kinds of physics found only at very tiny scales of zeptometers, or trillionths of a nanometer. (For comparison, a wavelength of visible light ranges from about 380 to 750 nanometers.)

An artist's visualization of a beauty particle decaying into two muons.

By exploring this zeptoverse, scientists hope to discover new particles that have escaped detection until now.

"We're confident there must be new particles to discover, and these could help explain mysteries like dark matter and the existence of matter in the universe," study co-author Harry Cliff, a particle physicist at the University of Cambridge, told Live Science.

"Why haven't we seen these particles yet? One answer, and perhaps the most likely answer, is these new particles only become manifest at very, very short-distance scales. So by probing into the zepto scale, we give ourselves the best chance possible of finding these new particles that could explain these mysteries."

We're now recording in a single year what took 10 years to record in the past.

Now, after hunting for more than four decades, physicists have found the first evidence of the rarest kind of beauty-quark decay seen yet. "Only one in 10 billion beauty particles decay this way," Cliff said.

In this form of decay, technically known as the B0 to mu+ mu− decay, a beauty quark collides with an oppositely charged beauty antiquark. They annihilate each other, generating a pair of smaller particles ‪—‬ a muon and an antimuon. Previous research suggested that this requires a complicated set of interactions, making this kind of decay extraordinarily rare.

An experiment at the LHC called LHCb was specifically designed to study beauty quarks. In 2024, a major upgrade to LHCb made it possible to distinguish different types of particles. Using machine learning techniques, the researchers suppressed huge amounts of background noise, which helped them detect the first evidence of the B0 to mu+ mu− decay.

"We're now recording in a single year what took 10 years to record in the past," Cliff said. "And so we have this massive sample of beauty decays, and that is ultimately what allowed us to see this decay for the first time."

The new results suggest that the way this rare decay happens matches the predictions of the Standard Model of particle physics, the current best description of how the basic building blocks of the universe behave. As such, the researchers have not yet discovered signs of new particles or new physics beyond the Standard Model.

"For me, I think the most exciting thing about this result is the fact that we are probing these unimaginably short distance scales," Cliff said. "Seeing the evidence for this decay begins a new area of research where we can start to make precision measurements that will give us the best chance of finding new physics in the future."

The researchers would now like to upgrade LHCb to fully exploit the improvements to the LHC's luminosity that are currently planned for the 2030s.

"However, there is some doubt over that now because the U.K. recently withdrew funding from the project," Cliff said. "So we are really trying to figure out if we'll be able to continue this type of research."

In an ideal world, "we'll collect many hundreds of these decays in the future, make precise measurements and probe deep into the zeptoverse," Cliff said.
