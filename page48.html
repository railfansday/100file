SAG-AFTRA Boss Says AI Takedowns Matter More Than Damages To Performers

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In an era of deepfakes, performers whose likenesses are misused by AI mostly want that content taken down rather than to go after damages, according to Duncan Crabtree-Ireland, CEO of SAG-AFTRA.

“I would say the vast majority, 90-plus percent of the time, that’s what it’s about,” Crabtree-Ireland said at Web Summit in Lisbon. “It’s not someone wants to get into litigation and make money. They want it to stop because they don’t agree with it, they don’t want their image used that way. It’s wrong.”

The SAG-AFTRA topper was speaking at a Deadline-moderated session at Web Summit entitled ‘Hollywood’s Identity Crisis’. He recalled his own experience of having his likeness misused while his members were on strike in 2023, before foregrounding the proposed No Fakes Act, which would give people greater control over their own voice and likeness. Related Stories News Web Summit Cancels Day Two As Storm Charles Hits Lisbon News Hollywood Unions Wonder Whether Next California Governor Will Share Newsom's Commitment To Expanding Film Tax Credits

“People deserve the right to have a say in what words come out of their mouth and what they appear to be doing online. A system like the No Fakes Act would provide that. And to quell any fears, it’s really not about suing people and making a lot of money. What it’s really about is getting platforms to take stuff down when your image, likeness and voice is being used without your permission.” Watch on Deadline

AI was the main talking point at Web Summit, and Crabtree-Ireland was asked if he detected a shift in his members’ attitudes toward the technology. “I think that there’s been a sense that if we put the right basic rules in place, that there is a way to coexist with AI,” he said. ”There’s been an understanding and an acceptance of the fact that no one can stop it, no one can block it.”

He added that a portion of SAG AFTRA membership remains staunch AI refuseniks. “Some of our members would like us to block it. I know, I hear from them every day and they’re angry that we aren’t. But the reality is, if I were estimating, I’d say that’s something like 10%, maybe 15% [of members].”

There is also the portion of SAG-AFTRA that wants to use AI to augment their work. “Some of our members really want to work with AI tools for augmentation, but none of them want to be replaced by AI because they love the act of creation, that’s why they do this. What you want to do is have the chance to do it, and yes, have tools that help you expand your creativity. That’s the sweet spot.

“Frankly, we could settle on that and then send the rest of the AI resources toward curing more diseases or some other things like that.”

Crabtree-Ireland was speaking on Web Summit’s enormous main stage, where, earlier in the week, AI-generated performer Tilly Norwood had been a guest. The SAG-AFTRA boss was at pains to highlight the difference between fully synthetic characters and AI replicas of real performers.

“It’s important that we distinguish between humans and algorithmic outputs because an AI character, an AI asset, a generative AI-created character is not a person. It doesn’t have the life of a person; it doesn’t have the experience of a person. One of the bigger problems we have with AI in general is we are anthropomorphizing AI models and robots and blurring the lines between humanity and non-human, artificially created entities.”

Canada is threatening to sue Stellantis over Brampton plant sale

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

Industry Minister Mélanie Joly said Stellantis has 30 days to address a notice of default tied to a federal funding agreement

Canada warned it may take Stellantis $STLA to court over the automaker's plan to sell its shuttered factory in Brampton, Ontario, giving it 30 days to resolve Ottawa's objections. Industry Minister Mélanie Joly told a Toronto news conference on Thursday that Ottawa had formally notified Stellantis it is in default.

"We sent a notice of default and, basically, they have 30 days — or else, of course, we will sue them," Joly told reporters, according to CNBC.

Joly said Stellantis broke the contractual promises it made to Ottawa and to Brampton's workforce when it revealed it had struck a deal to sell the facility to Roshel, which makes armored vehicles. She said the company is "on the hook" for several hundred million dollars.

Stellantis, the parent of Jeep, Dodge, Fiat, Chrysler and Peugeot, announced on Monday a deal to sell the plant to Roshel, though it did not disclose the price. The company said it recognizes what it owes the facility's employees and is ready to negotiate a settlement for union-represented workers in talks with Unifor, Canada's largest private sector union.

Stellantis Canada CEO Trevor Longley said on Thursday that the automaker will give back C$24 million in subsidies it received from Ottawa, according to CNBC. That figure falls short of what the government estimates Stellantis owes.

"We recognize the disappointment and uncertainty surrounding the future of the facility. While our decision to sell the plant represents a change from the original plans for Brampton, the agreement we have advanced with a Canadian manufacturer provides a path to return the facility to productive industrial use," Longley said, according to CNBC.

A Stellantis spokesperson was not available for comment on Friday morning, according to CNBC.

Prime Minister Mark Carney has called the situation at the plant “unacceptable” and a “betrayal” of its workers, according to CNBC.

Stellantis had dropped its plan to build the Jeep Compass at Brampton, moving future output to an Illinois factory as U.S. auto tariffs and other pressures in the market weighed on the company, according to CNBC. The U.S. and Canada are in a trade dispute, and President Donald Trump has urged carmakers to shift their vehicle manufacturing to the United States.

10 Classic Rock Albums To Listen to Before Kicking the Bucket

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

​​​​​​Joining the choir invisible. Shuffling off this mortal coil. Ceasing to be. Biting the dust. Wearing wooden pajamas. Vacating the earthly meat prison. No matter how it's said, it all means the same thing: death. Death is the great equalizer, the one thing that is inevitable regardless of one's race, religion, income or social status. Every day brings us closer to hopping the twig.

But hey, you're still on this side of the grass, right? Unless you're a well-read zombie, but that seems unlikely (but not impossible... discuss). So you have a choice to make: sit idly by, paralyzed by the thought you may have had your last Slurpee; or make the most of the time you have left. And if — hopefully — you've selected the latter, before kicking the bucket you've got things you want — nay, need — to do, see, and hear. Thankfully, your friends here at Collider have compiled a list that speaks to the latter: 10 rock albums you need to listen to before going belly up, ones that are not only awesome but offer guidance as you prepare for the inevitable. We're dying, literally, to share those with you now.

There comes a point in everyone's life where it's time to drop the charades and get real. That means raw, emotional honesty with others and yourself, and there is no better album that wears emotional honesty on every track like Fleetwood Mac's 1977 classic Rumours. The story behind the album is legendary at this point, recorded as the interpersonal drama among the group was akin to a soap opera, with separations, outright spite and heartbreak, making the atmosphere in the studio, shall we say, tense. A lesser band would have buried that under upbeat pop/rock songs, recording in separate sessions if needed. Not Fleetwood Mac: they owned that energy and put it all on vinyl, creating a timeless masterpiece that is relatable, real, and, in the case of "Don't Stop," even hopeful.

Getting real with ourselves potentially opens up a proverbial Pandora's Box of questions about life, death, time, money, or even one's mental health. While it may not provide any answers to these questions, Pink Floyd, with their iconic album Dark Side of the Moon, sure as all hell wax philosophical about those same themes. Like Rumours, the album has an emotional honesty that resonates only with the fluid cohesion that comes from being a concept album. As a result, Dark Side of the Moon is less a record than it is an experience, one that should be made before going up to the "Great Gig in the Sky."

IDFA Artistic Director Isabel Arrate Fernandez On Festival’s Competition Lineups, “Huge” Interest In Elizabeth Holmes Doc, And AI In Documentaries

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The 39th edition of IDFA – the world’s biggest nonfiction film festival – is underway in Amsterdam, gathering the documentary field’s top talents, Oscar seekers and emerging filmmakers with dynamic work.

“It’s hectic, all the last-minute things,” artistic director Isabel Arrate Fernandez told Deadline just before the kickoff. “From the lineup competitions to also the industry program we have, there are many exciting things going on.”

The opening night slot Friday evening goes to White Tiger Column, a world premiere directed by Robert Mentov, Dwight Jantzi, and Bryan Dickie that’s set within the resistance movement opposing Myanmar’s military-dominated regime. The festival calls it “a striking example of documentary cinema… The film offers a moving portrait of a courageous generation.”

White Tiger Column is programmed in International Competition, along with films from Morocco, Madagascar, Burkina Faso, Brazil, France, Germany, the Republic of Georgia, and other countries. An Incomplete Day in the History of Georgia 10.06.93, directed by Salomé Jashi, examines a critical moment for Georgia in 1993 when the country, recently freed from Soviet domination, debated measures to replace the Russian ruble. Considering Vladimir Putin’s attempt to reassert Russian dominance over much of the old USSR – including Ukraine – it’s a film with immediate relevance. Watch on Deadline

A baker’s dozen of features is programmed in the festival’s Envision section, which platforms films with bold stylistic choices. “The way we look at both of our competitions,” Arrate Fernandez notes, “they are of equal value… In Envision, we are really looking at filmmakers that are challenging documentary, are challenging the form.” Arrate Fernandez adds, “If we don’t have that, then how does a genre, how does cinema evolve?”

One of the Envision titles, Ragazzi al fiume, examines the experience of three young Afghan immigrants to Switzerland. The IDFA program notes the documentary directed by Pablo Briones “gradually evolves into an observational portrait of isolation, loss, and the longing for a peaceful future.”

Fellow Envision Competition documentary The Black Box Is Orange focuses on the Israeli town of Shlomi that “was built on land belonging to the destroyed Palestinian village of Al-Bassa, near the Lebanese border,” according to the IDFA program. “Beginning with the Nakba mass displacement of Palestinians in 1948, its residents were forced out and citizens of the newly established state of Israel were settled there. All traces of the previous inhabitants were to be destroyed, including all Palestinian archives.”

That film is directed by Yuula Benivolski, an Israeli Jewish filmmaker born in Canada and raised in the village of Shlomi. (Two years ago, she posted a video on YouTube discussing her decision to renounce her Israeli citizenship. “It wasn’t a decision I made lightly… Yes, I did it in response to Israel’s horrific assault on Gaza,” she explained, before adding, “Whether Israel realizes it or not, all this death harms its own people as well.”).

More than 250 films are programmed across the festival’s sections. Among them are some of the strongest contenders for Oscars, including To Hold a Mountain, the Sundance winner directed by Biljana Tutorov and Petar Glomazić; Once Upon a Time in Harlem, directed by David Greaves and based upon material shot by his father, William Greaves (this festival is separately screening a large selection of William Greaves’s work); Musk, directed by Alex Gibney; Closure, directed by Michał Marczak, and You Can See Everything, the box office sensation about Elizabeth Holmes that’s directed by Nathan Fielder and Lance Oppenheim. Do they know the imprisoned Theranos founder in the Netherlands? Maybe so, maybe not, but enthusiasm for the film has certainly crossed the Atlantic.

Interest in You Can See Everything “has been huge,” Arrate Fernandez affirms. “The festival screenings sold out very quickly… It’s definitely generated some attention, to put it mildly.”

IDFA has become an increasingly important stop for filmmakers in Oscar contention, in part because of the plethora of Academy documentary branch voters who are expected to attend. (“It’s difficult to count them, I can tell you,” Arrate Fernandez notes. “Let’s see.”). Last year’s winner in IDFA’s International Competition, A Fox Under a Pink Moon, has become a leading contender for Oscar consideration this year. (That film’s protagonist and co-director, the young artist Soraya Akhlaghi, designed this year’s IDFA’s poster and contributed artwork used in the festival trailer. Watch it below).

Arrate Fernandez mentions other films in the 2026 IDFA lineup making a strong bid for Oscar recognition.

“The winner of Cannes, Rehearsals for a Revolution; Traces, a Ukrainian film talking about sexual assault as a war crime against women; Closure, NAZA… there are many interesting films there that I hope will make it,” she says. “Even though they are not the films with the bigger budgets and everything for their campaigning, I really hope they get far.”

14th, another top Oscar contender that will screen at IDFA, is among the documentaries that make use of AI – in the case of Ava DuVernay’s film, to animate vintage photographs. For the first time ever, the artistic director tells us, IDFA asked filmmakers to complete a form indicating if they had incorporated AI into their film’s production.

“Last year, we noticed that sometimes we were wondering while watching films and now we have this, we can go back and see what they filled out just to understand,” Arrate Fernandez comments. “But also, it’s part of the conversations that are taking place with regards to how to use [AI], what is acceptable? There are so many questions, so I think that really [speaks] to conversations that are being held within the industry.”

To that end, IDFA will be hosting two panel discussions on AI, part of a robust lineup of industry talks. A panel titled “Fighting Back – Cinema as an act of resistance?” will feature Oscar winner Laura Poitras, who is bringing They’re Here to IDFA, a short film she directed with Rachel Lauren Mueller that examines the fatal ICE surge in Minneapolis. Fellow panelists include Sofía Bordenave, director of Forest Up in the Mountain, and Mohanad Yaqubi, director of Revolutionaries Never Die. Fernandez is slated to moderate a conversation with this year’s IDFA Guest of Honor, filmmaker Tatiana Huezo.

This is the second year of Arrate Fernandez’s leadership as artistic director. Last year, she faced questions after IDFA revealed it would not invite to the festival what it considered to be Israeli state-supported organizations, such as DocAviv. On its website, IDFA spells out its position under a section titled “Principles and guidelines” governing the 2026 festival.

“For films, makers, and organizations from countries where freedom of expression is under pressure or human rights are violated, it is essential to assess and investigate their independence carefully, alongside the artistic selection process,” IDFA writes. “In this way, we aim to prevent governments and organizations involved in and/or suspected of serious human rights violations from gaining a platform at IDFA.”

The IDFA policy statement continues, “Films, makers, and organizations are never excluded on the basis of their origin and/or nationality, but rather on the basis of any possible relationship with regimes or organizations held internationally accountable for serious human rights violations.”

Arrate Fernandez tells Deadline, “We also really looked at what we did last year, how we’re implementing things, what are our internal processes. And basically, for… a film work or a film project, everything just went through the regular route of being evaluated by our different teams of programmers on the creative criteria. And the moment that films make it through the different phases and end up on a short list, then in cases of countries… that are known for human rights violations, we check the independence of this film or of this project in the sense of also going about checking credits, who are financiers. At the same time, it’s not a blanket approach in the sense of, ‘Oh, this financier, it’s out.’ We also look at the film, so we still have our case-by-case approach.”

She continued, “Then in terms of accreditations… we have the similar line in the sense of having this case-by-case approach.”

American Israeli filmmaker Rachel Leah Jones (Advocate) will be presenting her new project, Podium (You Have Three Minutes), at IDFA Forum, the event’s pitching, co-financing and co-production market for nonfiction and new media projects. Logline: “What does resistance sound like when delivered from the parliamentary podium? Since Israel’s founding, Palestinian lawmakers have taken the mic to appeal, demand, denounce, and warn — articulating a powerful counter-history of the state itself.”

Avi Mograbi, described in the IDFA program as “one of Israel’s most distinguished filmmakers,” heads to the festival for the Dutch premiere of his short documentary Wild Asparagus (Halayon), which bowed at the Venice Film Festival. In Collapse, Israeli director Anat Even returns to Kibbutz Nir Oz, where she lost her home and friends in the Hamas sneak attack of October 7, 2023. She films what remains there and also attempts to enter Gaza, documenting “a gathering of settlers who plan to build in Gaza and proclaim this by chanting, ‘Occupy, expel, settle,’” the festival program reads. “An introspective and revealing film essay on loss, grief, revenge, dehumanization and indifference.” (The program description includes this note: “This film contains content on potentially sensitive topics.”).

The production company for Collapse is listed as France.

“You won’t find films that have as a production country Israel” in this year’s festival, Arrate Fernandez indicates, “but there are Israeli filmmakers. The thing is also that we don’t ask people for their nationality… We have several films that are taking place in Israel and others that are taking place in Palestine or in the Occupied Territories.”

Just about every corner of the globe is reflected in the festival lineup. IDFA runs through November 22, a chance for documentary filmmakers and lovers of the form to gather in person in one of the world’s great cities.

Says Arrate Fernandez, “In a world where storytelling is being formatted by decision makers, AI, algorithm, numbers, I think it’s important to treasure these spaces.”

On Sunday evening, Mauricio Pochettino got the call he had been waiting for.

He and long-time assistant Jesus Perez had frequently asked about Manchester United over the last decade. They would often contact friends in the game who were connected to Old Trafford, looking to find out what was happening, whether there could be an opening.

Pochettino came very close to taking one of those openings on at least three occasions: 2018, 2021 and 2024. Now, elements finally have aligned.

It is a call that others at Old Trafford have long waited for, too. Back when they had a much publicised dinner in 2016, Sir Alex Ferguson essentially told Pochettino he should be the United manager. The Argentine was enthralled at that meeting by stories from one of the game’s great glory eras, and a sense of magic he had felt just standing in Old Trafford had been deepened.

Pochettino had been especially taken by Ferguson’s account of rebuilding United from the 1980s, initially drawing comparisons with what he was doing at Tottenham Hotspur. Now, he’s set to take on that exact same job, if one that has become so much bigger than in 2016. It is arguably even bigger than in 1986.

Pochettino, however, is fired by the idea of being the man to finally fix United. It has become one of the great managerial quests. The United hierarchy have ultimately gone for him because of that personality, and his standing. They don’t want a coach who hasn’t worked at this level. They want someone ready. Both the Glazers and Sir Jim Ratcliffe can sense that, since the two sides of the United ownership have known Pochettino for years.

If this is an appointment that always felt destined, though, that does raise one big question. Is this the right moment for it? Is it like Jose Mourinho, in that it came too late? Is it typical of modern United in that sense? Is it the same old “magical thinking” of hoping a famous name saves them, when the game has changed and the actual problems are now so structural?

Pochettino probably should have gone to United in 2016 or 2018, when he was at a peak in terms of being one of the most exciting coaches in football. The club did consider a move in the summer of 2016, but sources say an exit clause was allowed to expire. How different things could have been.

Old Trafford insiders are now talking about the value of “a name”, but the hierarchy did pass on Pochettino in 2024, when they initially considered sacking Erik ten Hag before ultimately deciding to stick with the Dutchman. His interview wasn’t seen as convincing as that of Thomas Tuchel or Roberto De Zerbi. Those who know him also say he is closer to being “a manager” now, in terms of a wider outlook, rather than the on-the-grass coach that so drove Spurs.

The very fact United had to negotiate to pry him from the United States national team, without wishing to be disrespectful, also illustrates how his stock has recently dropped. Some close to Pochettino would nevertheless put that down to necessary choices at certain moments. Taking charge of the hosts of the biggest World Cup ever, that had so much pomp around it, was too enticing. Before that, he probably should have pushed to go to United or Real Madrid in 2018 but he was convinced he could do something special with Spurs. He’d put too much into it.

And that does point to one of the other great twists of fate in modern football, where Pochettino’s profile could be completely different. His Tottenham team were genuinely one of the best in Europe over 2016-18, and one of the great Premier League teams. To achieve 86 points in 2016-17, on what was then only the sixth highest wage bill in English football, was genuine alchemy. It was immense overperformance, fostered by a side that could just overrun you.

We also now know that the wage bill of the eventual champions for that season, Chelsea, had in part come from signings that had been secured by payments against the rules. The punishment from that guilty verdict still looks weak. There is a strong argument that Chelsea should have been stripped of the 2015 and 2017 titles. Pochettino himself said as much in a wide-ranging interview with The Independent and other media in September: “I’ve said that, that we should win one title, one Premier League. We should have been awarded it… for sure, we deserve to be champions for that year.”

Had that justice been done, Pochettino would not just be a “name” who created an exciting Spurs once. He would be an English champion, and one responsible for one of the most historic titles given the much wealthier competition. A great institution would have won just their third title, and first in 56 years.

Both he and Spurs could have had their futures transformed, as well as their place in history. It would have been a modern equivalent of what Ferguson himself did with Aberdeen.

Now, he wants to follow Ferguson in a different way. This is the job he has been waiting for.

A certain apathy towards the US job was displayed in that September interview when he spoke about how his contract didn’t necessarily mean he’d stay the full term.

For all of that, and how their World Cup campaign ended in the chaos of Donald Trump’s disruptive interference in the Folarin Balogun case, there were very impressive spells when the US really looked like a classic Pochettino team. The way they ripped into Paraguay was reminiscent of Spurs 2016-18, right up to some of the flourishes. And that was in a much more limited and restricted environment than a club team.

This is the other thing about just taking the job, too. Regardless of United’s current problems, and perceptions of where Pochettino is in his career, he is energised by this. He has that bite. As one figure who knows him well says, “he has got the IP, he has got the brand, he has got the experience”.

Having already worked for Chelsea and Paris Saint-Germain, he’s been at big clubs with demanding owners. He has the mettle for that. And he now has the big job he’s always wanted.

Inside the $3.25 Million Pasadena Glass Pavilion That’s Still Studied in Architecture Classes Today

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A three-bedroom midcentury modern home designed by Case Study architect Craig Ellwood has just come on the market in Pasadena, CA, for $3.25 million. It features a notable cantilevered glass pavilion.

Built in 1965, the Kubly House was originally constructed for less than $45,000. The 2,227-square-foot home has since been thoughtfully updated.

“What makes this house special is its architecture and design, and how true it has remained to the original,” says Chris Suarez, who is co-listing the property with Kimberly Benz of Suarez + Benz at Compass. “Nothing was significantly altered. Everything done was intended to restore the house to its original condition. The footprint, the floors, the glass—everything has remained true to the original design.”

Located in the Linda Vista neighborhood, the home has an iconic redwood, cantilevered glass pavilion that opens to a single-level floor plan. The private half-acre lot includes a pool and spa surrounded by lush landscaping with eucalyptus and oak trees.

With exposed post-and-beam construction, clean horizontal lines, and expansive walls of glass, the home was designed to give the illusion of floating above its natural surroundings. The deck was recently restored using specially milled wood.

“It was sourced from northern Washington and brought down here,” Suarez notes. “Everything was done painstakingly to avoid altering the house’s integrity, architecture, and design. That being said, significant work has been done, including updates to the roof, infrastructure, air conditioning, and electrical systems. All the major systems have been updated, but you wouldn’t necessarily know it because the work was done so carefully.”

Originally commissioned by ArtCenter College of Design alumni and educators Don and Sally Kubly, the home represents their understanding of design and the culture of the college community. It is still studied in classes there.

“Don was ArtCenter’s first president in Pasadena,” Suarez explains. “There’s also a connection through Craig Ellwood, who designed ArtCenter’s main campus building. The main campus is an incredible steel-and-glass building designed by Ellwood. Don played a significant role in moving the school from Los Angeles to Pasadena, where he served as president for many years. He’s very well known within that community, and this house is significant to the college’s history and culture.”

Based on available architectural records, the Kubly House is said to be Ellwood’s only documented residential project in Pasadena.

After remaining in the Kubly family for nearly six decades, the home changed hands for $3.6 million in 2022. Since then, the sellers have undertaken a preservation-minded restoration with Carl Bronson of Bronstruction, updating major systems to contemporary standards.

“A house that helped usher in much midcentury modernism was the Glass House, designed by Philip Johnson around 1948 or 1949,” Benz notes. “That design was definitely something Ellwood loved. You can clearly see that house’s influence here, which is important because it reveals some of the influences behind Ellwood’s work.”

Benz adds that the home’s design was also influenced by Ellwood’s peers, including midcentury designer George Nelson.

“A furniture plan developed for the house clearly referenced Mies van der Rohe and George Nelson, both important figures in midcentury modern design,” she says. “Even the deck and the steps leading to the house resemble a George Nelson bench. There’s actually a bench built into the concrete wall near the pool that resembles a George Nelson design. I don’t know whether Nelson designed or made it, but the influence is certainly there.”

The house is special because many of the original details have remained intact, she adds.

“You can see the influence of the people Ellwood admired throughout the house,” Benz says. “That significance is what makes this house important when you look at the midcentury modernist movement. All these examples are visible in the house and remain there.”

Some of the built-in furniture has a distinctive style, too.

“There’s a stunning floating credenza that looks like a George Nelson piece,” she notes. “We think it might be one of his designs. It’s in the living room. There’s also a built-in console that might be a George Nelson design as well. So, some of the furniture is actually built into the architecture.”

While some pieces are originals and others are reproductions, Benz says, “the most important thing was following the original plan. The history of this house is everything.”

When staging the home, she says they were “fortunate to discover an original interior design study among the blueprints, plans, and documents that came with the house. We were able to look at that study, and it became a guide and a road map for staging the property. Sourcing the furniture was a challenge because we had to find the specific pieces.”

The city of Pasadena has also identified the property as eligible for individual listing on the National Register of Historic Places.

A hopeless thriller is one that keeps you grabbed by the neck but still ends in a painful manner. Someone in a thriller says, “We just need to find the truth,” and I immediately start worrying about them. Because finding the truth is one thing. Surviving what it does to your life is another. I've watched characters sacrifice everything to uncover a secret, only to discover that knowing the answer doesn't give them the slightest power to change it. It's infuriating. It's heartbreaking. And when a show earns that feeling, I cannot look away.

I think helplessness becomes truly frightening when you can understand every decision that leads somebody there. You wouldn't necessarily have behaved differently. You might have trusted the same person, followed the same evidence, or convinced yourself that one more sacrifice would finally put things right. These six thrillers are merciless about that very human tendency.

The Killing makes the investigation painfully personal, particularly for Rosie's parents, Stan (Brent Sexton) and Mitch (Michelle Forbes).

What gets under my skin is how little the truth can actually repair. Mitch can barely function inside the home where her daughter should still be living. Stan's grief keeps twisting into anger, leaving him vulnerable to decisions that could destroy what's left of his family. Meanwhile, Linden's inability to abandon the case damages her relationship with her own son. Everybody wants justice, but nobody can promise that justice will make living any easier. I find that distinction absolutely devastating.

Paul Spector (Jamie Dornan) has a wife, children, and a job as a grief counselor. He also stalks and murders women in Belfast. Dornan's most disturbing moments aren't necessarily the violent ones. Sometimes Paul is simply behaving affectionately toward his children, and I want him as far away from them as possible. The data is stark, but as the doc’s title suggests, Satour and Perkins are interested in more than just listing these figures. Watch on Deadline

“The statistics tell one story. We tell another,” the film’s opening frame reads.

Structured as a six-part anthology, Facing The Numbers explores these disparities through the real-life stories of First Nations people using a bold mix of dance, poetry, and spoken word.

Satour and Perkins wrote and directed the film together. Perkins also served as the DoP. Satour produced alongside Ramona Telecican. The film debuted earlier this year at the Melbourne Film Festival before screening across the Aussie circuit and premiering at the Sydney Opera House. The doc will screen on NITV in Australia on November 15 and will later be repackaged for distribution on social platforms.

Below, Satour and Perkins speak with Deadline about how they developed the anthology film and their bold plans for its release.

DEADLINE: This film has a very unique structure. How did you develop this approach to the material?

TYSON PERKINS: This kind of subject matter is often portrayed in a very academic way on film. It tends to take the form of talking-head interviews and fairly basic re-creations, which don’t get close to the emotional point of view—or the emotional suffering—that many victims of police brutality go through. We started with that as our aim, and this little, unusual thing came out of it.

DEADLINE: I read this started as a short project and then expanded. Is that right?

PERKINS: It was as much about treating the subject matter in a fresh way as it was about using a fresh format. For us, that format was short-form and shareable online. It came as a response to the Black Lives Matter movement. We were watching what was happening in America from Australia and the artistic responses that came out of it—not the news coverage, but the things that appeared on our Instagram feeds. It prompted Kieran and me to think about Australia. Some of our statistics on incarceration, youth incarceration, male suicide, and deaths in custody are among the worst in the world. We wanted to take some of those subjects and statistics and frame them through the short-form, viral storytelling many African American artists in the U.S. were using. By “viral,” we didn’t necessarily mean that it had to go viral; we meant something short, shareable, and to the point.

As happens with films, once we delved into the individual subjects and the stories, they expanded. We realised there was a way to tell the story of this country—its institutional racism, its history of oppression and its ongoing oppression—through these personal, first-hand stories. We thought we had six separate subject films, but when we put them together, they formed a truthful picture of what Aboriginal people face in this country.

DEADLINE: You incorporate several different art forms in the film. There’s dance, spoken word. But it feels seamless. How did you get it to work?

KIERAN SATOUR: One of the amazing things about Facing the Numbers is that so many First Nations artists came together to tell this story. The spoken word, poetry, and dancing feel seamless because everyone is telling the same story. We’re telling multiple separate stories, but they represent a collective Aboriginal experience. Tyson and I were lucky to work with all these incredible creatives.

DEADLINE: How have people been responding to the film, especially in Australia?

SATOUR: People have been responding really well. We premiered at the Melbourne International Film Festival and had a great turnout. About 75% of tickets were sold for both screenings. We had standing ovations, and the Letterboxd reviews came in almost immediately after the first screening. Nothing was under four stars, and the reviews were glowing. We screened at the Darwin International Film Festival. My sister lives in Darwin. She’s also a co-founder of the production company and an executive producer on the film. She told me that people have been stopping her in Darwin to talk about Facing the Numbers because they’d been at the screening. It’s been amazing.

DEADLINE: Where did you find your participants?

SATOUR: During the global movement after George Floyd’s murder, people were sharing their stories online, and we found some of these stories through social media. Geoffrey Johnson is one example. Otherwise, we were doing research and development, trying to find stories that spoke to this collective experience. It came down to meetings and phone calls.

PERKINS: It’s important to mention that it would not be difficult to find thousands of stories like these. Thousands of Aboriginal people have direct descendants who were taken during the Stolen Generations or sent to schools like Kinchela Boys’ Home. Probably every Aboriginal family knows someone who has been a victim of police brutality, whether in their own family or a family they know. The stories we chose are told by extremely courageous people. It takes strength to repeat your story on camera, allow it to be filmed, and make it public. But one of the key messages of the film is that these experiences are extraordinarily common—in people’s lives and statistically.

DEADLINE: How did you build this production? I can’t imagine the Australian public funding system was jumping to invest in something like this. Or maybe I’m wrong.

SATOUR: It was funded through a digital-series, online-production fund at Screen Australia, our national public screen-funding body. That fund doesn’t even exist anymore. The project was never meant to be a feature film. We made the anthology into a feature partly because that helped us unlock extra funding and become eligible for a significant tax offset. Production went through a few phases. At the end of the first phase, we had two strong rough cuts, which we used to secure more funding. We received a little more from the national broadcasting funding body, then a broadcaster attached through a pre-sale, and we brought in philanthropic support. It was a struggle, but we got there. A film like this doesn’t get made without the goodwill of philanthropists, but also without all the work everyone put into those first two rough cuts. They were strong, and I think the project sold itself from there.

DEADLINE: How will it be distributed? I know you’re screening at the Sydney Opera House, which is wonderful. Do you have plans for international screenings? In the UK and U.S., people involved in solidarity movements would be intrigued to hear from you and see a film like this.

SATOUR: Totally. In terms of craft, we were inspired by global Black filmmakers. Steve McQueen’s Small Axe was a huge reference for us, as was Spike Lee. We both admire the American director Khalil Joseph. We’re connecting with global Black and brown movements because we’re facing similar issues everywhere: over-policing, incarceration, and child removal. We’re still waiting to find out where the international premiere will be. In Australia, we’re doing a film-festival run, and then the film will be broadcast on NITV, the National Indigenous Television Network, which sits under SBS, our multicultural public broadcaster. The digital release will follow once the holdbacks for the national TV broadcast are over. The films will also be released online through a microsite.

A key partner is Common Ground, an Aboriginal-run and Aboriginal-led organisation focused on education. They’re running our impact campaign. The organisation’s founder, Rona Glynn-McDonald, is Warwick Thornton’s daughter, so filmmaking is in her blood. We’re excited to get these stories out digitally and reach younger people who may not watch television or attend film festivals, and to connect with an audience that isn’t already converted.

PERKINS: For me, the online release is what I’m most excited about. In film and television now, you see people who have built audiences on YouTube or Instagram bringing those audiences into more traditional film and TV. The ideal outcome would be for some of the storytellers to experience justice—whether for a family member’s death or, in Uncle Widdy’s case, by having more of the public learn what happened to him at Kinchela Boys’ Home. Not many people know that thousands of children went through that school or what happened to them. The survivors are trying to reclaim the land where the school stood. Once the films have been shared and completed their festival runs, I hope some of those outcomes can begin to happen. I’m really excited about that.

Three central banks have completed tests of a cross-border foreign exchange settlement system connecting traditional payment infrastructure with distributed ledger technology.

In a joint statement on Friday, the Bank of England, Monetary Authority of Singapore, and Bank of Thailand said they conducted the experiments using a prototype developed by Partior, which coordinated payment-versus-payment (PvP) settlement and multilateral net FX transactions across their test environments.

The three central banks are working under Project Meridian FX APAC, announced last November. The project encompassed multiple currencies, technologies, settlement infrastructures, and time zones, with the Reserve Bank of Australia and Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas also participating as observers, the banks said.

According to the statement, Partio's system matched settlement instructions, coordinated the reservation of funds, monitored payment readiness, and initiated settlement.

The experiment also examined multilateral netting, which allows banks to offset payments against one another to reduce the liquidity needed for settlement.

However, the accompanying policy report identified questions around operational resilience, settlement finality, governance, liability, and cross-border data sharing. It also warned that additional functions, including holding funds or calculating net positions, could change an operator's regulatory responsibilities.

Tom Mutton, the BOE's executive director of payments, innovation and fintech, said the project provided "practical evidence of how synchronized settlement could help reduce risk and improve efficiency in cross-border FX transactions."

The BOE plans to launch a live synchronization interface in 2028. Findings from Project Meridian FX APAC will inform further work on synchronized settlement.
