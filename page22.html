Just over half of the country’s nonprofit hospitals have charity care and community benefit investments that fall short of their tax savings, according to the latest of the Lown Institute’s analyses on more than 3,000 hospitals’ tax filings.

In 2023, the most recent year in which Internal Revenue Service Form 990s were available for review, the think tank identified $38.8 billion of industry-wide tax exemptions, or $12.8 million per hospital and 3.8% of total expenses on average. About $37.1 billion was collectively spent by the same hospitals on various community benefits, as reported in the IRS forms matched to other filings such as Medicare cost reports.

While the total exemption handily outpaces the benefits investments by Lown’s measure, the group underscored that these "fair share” deficits or surpluses also vary widely between facilities, and that some of the industry’s biggest names are on the wrong side of the divide.

Here, Mayo Clinic Hospital led the pack with a $366 million deficit, according to Lown’s analysis, followed by Tisch Hospital (-$294 million deficit), Stanford Hospital (-$255 million deficit), New York-Presbyterian Hospital (-$248 million deficit) and Massachusetts General Hospital (-$219 million deficit).

"Some of America's wealthiest hospitals fall hundreds of millions short of giving back what they receive from their communities, leaving those with fewer resources to pick up the slack," Vikas Saini, M.D., president of the Lown Institute, said in a release on the new analysis, released Tuesday.

Among those leading the latter group were North Shore University Hospital ($167 fair share surplus), Rochester General Hospital ($146 million surplus) and Englewood Hospital ($142 million surplus). Lown’s analysis includes a searchable hospital-by-hospital breakdown of the tax exemptions and community investment totals.

When splitting the 52% of deficit hospitals apart from the surplus hospitals, the former group logged about $15.5 billion above their reported community benefits—a deficit Lown said could wipe the medical debt of 12 million Americans. Fair share surplus hospitals outpaced their exemptions by $13.8 billion.

Total tax exemptions were also “very evenly distributed” among the hospitals, Lown said. The 30 hospitals, or about 1% of all hospitals, with the largest tax exemptions collectively avoided $6.8 billion, or about 18% of total exemptions. The 607 hospitals, or about 20%, accounted for three-quarters of the country’s hospital tax exemptions.

Lown’s measure of nonprofit hospitals’ tax breaks includes exemptions from federal and state corporate income taxes, state and local sales taxes, property taxes, tax-exempt bonds, tax-exempt charitable donations and federal unemployment tax exemptions. Not included are other benefits provided to many nonprofits, like Medicaid disproportionate share payments, 340B discounts or profits from indirect tax arbitrage.

Its tally of community benefit includes spending on patient financial assistance, subsidized care, payments to other nonprofits to improve health or various community health or social determinants of health investments.

Of note, it excludes Medicaid shortfall—the difference between hospitals’ costs and revenues when treating Medicaid patients—and spending on professional education and research. The hospital industry typically points to these billions of dollars of exclusions when discussing their tax breaks, with Medicaid shortfall alone accounting for nearly half of their preferred community benefit spending totals. Lown noted in its methodology that these accounting losses are often offset by other discounts and supplemental benefits and don’t always have a direct impact on community health, among other objections.

Lown said the disparities between hospitals and the outsized benefits being delivered by some are a call to amend, not eliminate, nonprofit status exemptions. The think tank accompanied its analysis with calls to increase reporting transparency and set spending targets for hospitals tied to variables such as size, exemption value, financial health and prior spending.

“Tax exemptions aren't a gift—they're an expectation, and regulators need to make sure all hospitals hold up their end of the bargain," Saini said.

Lown acknowledged the impending financial pressure hospitals will face with upcoming H.R. 1 policy changes, and said such changes should be accompanied by “short-term” financial protections.

Still, the group isn’t alone in drawing scrutiny to the tax breaks. Lawmakers and policy advisors have pointed to, and questioned the justification for, nonprofits’ exemptions when questioning health system leaders on collections policies or high prices.

Academics and researchers have also called for greater transparency or other overhauls of how community benefits are reported or calculated. Of note, a research article published earlier this month in Health Affairs reviewing hospital filings between 2013 and 2022 outlined a disconnect between different hospitals’ listed charity care eligibility thresholds and their relative spending, which more closely aligned with community need than their stated policy.

Modern physics rests on two frameworks: general relativity and quantum mechanics. General relativity describes very large things, like stars; quantum mechanics describes very small things, such as atoms. Both are astonishingly good at predicting how the universe behaves, but they’re mathematically incompatible. Finding a theory that unites them—“quantum gravity”—is one of the field’s biggest goals. Black holes are a natural testing ground, because they’re one of the few areas where both relativity and quantum mechanics matter—they pack immense mass into a very small space.

One of the most useful tools for studying quantum gravity is called holographic duality. The real universe has three dimensions of space and one of time, which relativity combines into four-dimensional spacetime. But physicists studying quantum gravity often work with simpler model universes that they can understand in more detail.

The basic idea of holographic duality is that some of these theoretical universes can be described in two ways: as a complete universe with gravity and spacetime, and in quantum terms, with no gravity at all, “written” on the universe’s outer boundary. Just as a real hologram encodes a 3D image on a 2D plane, the quantum model encodes all the information of the universe in one dimension fewer (a universe with four dimensions of space, for instance, can be fully described by a quantum theory with three). This lets physicists turn hard questions about gravity into questions about quantum physics, which are sometimes easier to answer.

The first problem Claude solved concerns what happens when you heat up one of these theoretical universes. At low temperatures, the universe fills up with a gas of particles. Above a certain temperature, a black hole forms instead. In 1983, Stephen Hawking and Don Page showed that this switch is abrupt: it’s a phase transition, similar to how water suddenly boils at 100°C. But Hawking and Page’s calculation relies on general relativity, which holds only when the universe is vast, compared with the tiny scale at which that description breaks down. As the theoretical universe shrinks toward that smaller scale, spacetime loses any definite shape and becomes blurry, and nobody knows how to do the calculation on the gravity side of the duality. On the quantum side, though, the same situation can be calculated, at least in principle.

In 1999, physicists found that there was a phase transition in that regime as well. But they didn’t know whether it was abrupt, like Hawking and Page’s, or smooth. In 2003, a group of physicists set out the calculation that would settle the question, but it was extremely complex and was never completed. Claude did it by running a small research group of copies of itself, in which it worked out almost every piece of the calculation independently and checked its math against known results. It showed that the phase transition is abrupt. The same sudden switch appears at both extremes, suggesting that black hole formation isn’t just a feature of relativity but is actually built into the underlying quantum theory.

The second problem concerns a simplified theoretical universe with two dimensions of space instead of three. This universe is straightforward enough to study in detail, but still has black holes and other mysteries similar to those of our own universe. Even in this simpler universe, physicists don’t know how to create a full theory of quantum gravity, so they approach it from the other direction: rather than building a single, correct theory, they try to rule out wrong ones by showing that they are internally inconsistent. This method is called bootstrapping.

Since 2009, physicists have used a version of this method to set a ceiling on how heavy the lightest black hole in such a universe could be. Any theory that breaks the ceiling is ruled out, and each lowering of the ceiling narrows the search. Results from numerical calculations hinted at where the best possible ceiling lay, but they couldn’t prove it, and the best proven ceiling was well short of what the numerical results suggested.

Claude Fable 5.2 noticed a pattern in the numerical results that physicists had missed and borrowed a method from elsewhere in math and physics to calculate the precise ceiling for the simplest version of this technique and prove that it could not be lowered. It doesn’t identify the correct theory, but it may point physicists in the right direction.

Neither result describes our own universe directly, and neither has practical applications, but both contribute to a long-running theoretical effort to understand how quantum mechanics and relativity fit together.

In the rest of this post, we explain in more detail what each problem was and what Claude did to solve them.

The agents ran in the background, with no special setup, for about six weeks.

The agents solved the two problems in this post. One was the Hawking-Page phase transition, the question of when a hot gas turns into a black hole. Weeks after one of the agents reached a solution, a physicist, who had independently used Claude to tackle the same problem, shared his result, and a colleague realized that Hongbin’s agent had reached the same answer earlier. The two solutions used different methods, so they were a useful check on each other.

The modular bootstrap result came from several agents. The one that was assigned the problem spotted the pattern in the numerical results when surveying the bootstrap literature, and another one—which was working on a mathematically related problem about how tightly spheres can be packed into a high-dimensional space—turned that pattern into a method. It found that this method reproduced a sphere-packing result recently proved by an OpenAI model. In September, the results were formally verified in Lean, a computerized proof checker. Claude then wrote the paper with minimal guidance, and a physicist checked every main result.

To understand the first problem, we need to start with something much more familiar: a walk through New York City.

Imagine that you’re heading from Penn Station to the High Line entrance on West 30th Street and 10th Avenue, and there are two equally good routes. You could walk west along West 33rd Street and turn south, or head south first and then walk west along West 30th Street. Either way, you have to pick one. In the quantum world, though, that’s not true: a particle can take both routes simultaneously.

This is most clearly demonstrated in the famous double-slit experiment. In this test, you fire particles, one at a time, at a barrier cut with two narrow slits, and record where, on a screen behind it, each one lands. Each particle lands at a particular point on the screen, as a particle should. But as you fire more of them, they build up a pattern of stripes that would only be possible if each one had passed through both slits, like a wave.

To predict what a quantum particle will do more generally, physicists have to add up every path it could possibly take, known as its possible histories. Not every history counts equally; some contribute far more to the sum than others. It’s an extravagant and mysterious way to describe how a single particle behaves, but it’s also one of the most successful ideas in physics.

This approach, known as the “sum over histories,” is well established for particles and anything made of them. But what about gravity? Einstein showed that gravity is a consequence of curved spacetime: objects with mass bend space and time around them, and we feel that bending as gravity. In a quantum theory of gravity, therefore, what gets summed over is the shape of spacetime itself, rather than just the paths of particles moving through it. To make predictions in a quantum theory of gravity, we would have to add up every shape that the universe could possibly take, and nobody yet knows how to do that. Much of modern quantum gravity research is an attempt to work it out.

Hawking and Page found a case where this sum is simple enough to handle but still interesting. To explain Hawking and Page’s work, we need to imagine a different kind of universe, called anti-de Sitter spacetime. Imagine you’re standing in a giant bowl. No matter how hard you throw an object, it will always roll back to the place you threw it from.

Our universe isn’t like that. The difference comes down to a quantity called the cosmological constant, which describes the energy of empty space. In our universe, this energy is positive, which makes space expand ever faster. In anti-de Sitter spacetime, the energy is negative, so everything is pulled back to where it started. Hawking and Page chose this universe for their calculation because of how it behaves when it heats up: the bowl keeps heat in so it can stay at a steady temperature.

At any given temperature, two histories matter far more than all the others. Here, a “history” means an entire version of the universe: a particular shape of spacetime, along with everything in it. In one, the bowl is filled with an ordinary gas of particles—including gravitons, the particles believed to carry gravity. In the other, it contains a black hole. Whichever contributes more to the sum is, in effect, what we would observe—it “wins.” At low temperatures, the gas history wins, and the universe is full of gas; at high temperatures, the black-hole history wins, and the universe contains a black hole. At one particular temperature—which physicists now call the Hawking-Page temperature—the universe switches abruptly from one to the other. This is the phase transition.

It’s a rare case where we can see two very different shapes of spacetime competing in the sum over histories, and can say which one wins.

This is where the holographic principle enters the story. In the 1970s, Jacob Bekenstein and Hawking found that the amount of information a black hole can hold is proportional to its surface area, not to its volume. That’s very different from ordinary matter, where every bit of volume holds roughly the same amount of information.

In the early 1990s, Gerard ’t Hooft and Leonard Susskind proposed that this idea applies to gravity in general—that everything inside a region of space can be fully described by the information on its boundary, just as a flat hologram can display a three-dimensional image. In 1997, Juan Maldacena formalized the idea further. Gravity inside the anti-de Sitter bowl, he argued, is equivalent to an ordinary quantum theory of particles, with no gravity at all, existing on the rim of the bowl (the universe’s outer boundary). These are the gravity side and the quantum side of the duality we described in the introduction—they’re two languages for describing the same underlying reality.

In the case Claude studied, the quantum theory on the rim is called N=4 super Yang-Mills theory. It’s related to the theories that describe how particles interact in the real universe, but it has extra symmetries that make it much easier for physicists to work with. (Claude has worked with this theory before, calculating how particles interact within it to a new level of precision.) Since the gravity side predicts two histories competing with each other, the obvious question is: does N=4 super Yang-Mills theory (the quantum side) show the same thing?

For now, physicists can analyze this theory in detail only when its particles barely interact with each other, a condition known as weak coupling. This is the opposite end of the spectrum from where Hawking and Page’s gravitational calculation works. Weak coupling on the quantum side corresponds to the small, blurry universe on the gravity side. So there was no guarantee that any of Hawking and Page’s findings would hold there.

Remarkably, they do. Bo Sundborg in 1999, and after him Ofer Aharony, Joseph Marsano, Shiraz Minwalla, Kyriakos Papadodimas, and Mark Van Raamsdonk, showed that even at weak coupling, the theory on the rim goes through a transition as it heats up. This is its counterpart of the switch from gas to black hole inside the bowl. But it was still unknown whether this transition is abrupt, like Hawking and Page’s, or smooth and gradual.

When particles barely interact, physicists have to calculate how they behave one step at a time. They start with a simplified version of the theory in which particles don’t interact at all. Then they add a series of corrections, each more precise and harder to compute than the last. Physicists call these steps loops. In 2003, Aharony and his colleagues realized that whether the phase transition was an abrupt jump, as Hawking and Page predicted, or gradual, hinged on a single number that appeared only three loops in. Computing that number involves many thousands of terms, known as diagrams—far too many to work through by hand. In 2005, the same group completed the calculation for a simpler cousin of the theory and found an abrupt transition. But the version for N=4 super Yang-Mills, more than 10 times larger, was never completed.

The scale of the computational problem, while daunting, wasn’t the only obstacle to completing the calculation. There was a conceptual issue, as well: naively adding up all the parts of the calculation gives an infinite answer, and those infinities have to be tamed carefully to extract a meaningful result.

To solve the problem, Claude ran a small research group of agents, all copies of itself. Almost every piece of the calculation was done twice, by agents acting independently. For extra consistency checks, parts of the calculation could also be compared to previous results in the literature.

The result Claude found is an abrupt jump as the temperature rises, exactly analogous to what Hawking and Page had found using the sum over spacetime histories. The emerging picture is very simple: the transition is abrupt at both weak and strong coupling—the blurry and the smooth ends, in the gravity description. The most natural possibility is that it stays abrupt everywhere in between.

This is a beautiful outcome. It shows that a calculation using the sum over histories in gravity qualitatively matches the behavior of an ordinary quantum theory with no gravity at all.

Many open questions remain. The abrupt transition Claude found is very much like the one we see whenever we boil water. One interesting feature of abrupt transitions is that the losing side of the competition can survive for a while: for instance, water can be superheated above 100°C without boiling, as you could see for yourself if you were to put a cup of extremely pure water in the microwave. (But please don’t do this; it’s dangerous, and Anthropic would rather not be responsible for an epidemic of scalded readers.)

Something similar happens here. Above the Hawking-Page temperature, the hot gas of gravitons and other particles can survive for a long time in a superheated state. But at a higher temperature it can no longer survive: this is another transition, the Hagedorn transition, which is a characteristic signature of string theory. On the gravity side, that transition has many interesting properties of its own, and it would be valuable to check in detail whether they also appear in N=4 super Yang-Mills theory.

Another open question concerns the other possible histories of spacetime. Hawking and Page considered three: one in which their model universe is filled with gas, one that contains a large black hole, and one containing a smaller black hole that never comes to dominate the sum over histories. In principle, there are many more possible histories. As far as anyone knows, none of them ever dominates. But given certain changes to the starting conditions—if the universe is set spinning, for instance, or given a fixed amount of energy or charge—then some of these histories do take over. Working out exactly which ones win, and when, is an interesting problem for the future.

As we saw earlier, one useful model universe has just two dimensions of space and one of time: it’s called “3D gravity” because it describes gravity in three spacetime dimensions. According to the principle of holographic duality, 3D gravity inside the anti-de Sitter bowl is equivalent to an ordinary quantum theory living on the bowl’s rim.

As a model, 3D gravity is much simpler than the real thing, but it still allows for black holes and many of the same mysteries. It provides a useful laboratory for studying the sum over spacetime histories. It’s also a good place to test the idea that spacetime isn’t a basic ingredient of the universe, but emerges from something deeper. In general relativity, black holes look smooth. But in quantum gravity, if you zoom in to the smallest scales—the Planck length, around 10-35 meters, where quantum effects on gravity become impossible to ignore—black holes are made of an enormous number of tiny, individual building blocks. Physicists believe the same is true in our own, four-dimensional universe, but the 3D version is much easier to analyze with holographic duality.

Claude’s results build on a method called the bootstrap, a general strategy for solving quantum theories. Counterintuitively, the strategy doesn’t try to solve a theory directly. For 3D gravity, that isn’t even possible, because nobody knows how to define the theory precisely. Instead, the bootstrap rules theories out by showing that they contradict themselves. Physicists scan over all possible quantum theories and try to eliminate them one by one.

Remarkably, in many cases this method makes it possible to pinpoint a single quantum theory and calculate precisely how it behaves. The requirements of relativity and quantum mechanics are so demanding, and so delicately balanced, that together they rule out every alternative. In 2012, the bootstrap was used to solve a quantum field theory called the 3D critical Ising model, a simple model of magnetism that also describes water at the point where liquid and vapor become indistinguishable. Since then, it has been applied to many different quantum systems.

In 2009, Simeon Hellerman introduced a technique called the modular bootstrap, which applies the bootstrap method to 3D gravity. Its goal is to rule out inconsistent theories of quantum gravity in three spacetime dimensions. By combining it with other bootstrap methods, physicists hope to eventually eliminate every inconsistent theory. Whatever is left must be consistent. There could be no consistent theories, or just one, or whole families of them. Any of these outcomes would be interesting, because it would help us understand what spacetime looks like at the Planck scale in this simplified universe, and give us hints about the Planck scale in our own.

Here’s how the modular bootstrap works. 3D gravity in anti-de Sitter spacetime has a quantity called the central charge (c), which roughly counts how many microscopic building blocks the universe is made of. When general relativity holds, c is very large, because large black holes contain an enormous number of building blocks. A second quantity, M₁, is the mass of the lightest black hole. To run the bootstrap, physicists scan over every possible theory, with every possible value of c and M₁, and look for inconsistencies.

To understand how that search works, we need to go back to the sum over spacetime histories. In 3D gravity, that means adding up different 3D shapes of spacetime. They can be simple, like a ball, or more complicated, like the donut and two-holed donut in the figure below. The spacetime is the inside of the shape—the dough inside the donut. Its surface, like the rim of the bowl in the earlier section, is where the quantum theory is “written,” in one fewer dimension.

The modular bootstrap starts with a spacetime shaped like an ordinary, one-holed donut, or torus. There are two different ways to interpret this shape, depending on which direction we call “time” and which we call “space.” In the first, the long direction around the hole is time and the other, shorter loop, around the tube, is space. In the second, they’re swapped: the long loop is space and the shorter loop is time.

In calculations like these, the length of the time loop sets the temperature: the shorter the loop, the hotter the universe. So swapping the two loops trades a cold universe for a hot one. The two interpretations correspond to the two competing histories that Hawking and Page studied. In 3D gravity there are no gravitons, but there is a transition similar to the one that Claude analyzed in N=4 super Yang-Mills theory.

Now we come to the crucial step. In relativity, space and time are on an equal footing. So it can’t matter which loop we label space and which we label time. As far as relativity is concerned, the two interpretations are equivalent, and the physics must be the same for every possible experiment.

Quantum mechanics, however, treats time differently from space. To calculate anything, you pick a direction to call time and track how the system evolves along it. So, when we calculate observable quantities in quantum gravity, the two interpretations of the donut lead to calculations that look very different. But in relativity, it doesn’t matter which loop is called time, so the two calculations have to give exactly the same answer.

If we knew the theory of 3D quantum gravity, we could do both calculations and confirm that they match. We don’t, but with the bootstrap method, that isn’t a problem. Physicists scan over the possible values of c (the number of building blocks) and M₁ (the mass of the lightest black hole). For each pair of values, they try to prove that the theory would be inconsistent—that the two calculations on the donut couldn’t possibly give the same answer. This involves searching for a special mathematical object called a bootstrap functional. If physicists find one, then the theory is inconsistent, and those values are ruled out.

The hope, supported by the bootstrap’s successes elsewhere, is that combining this approach with other bootstrap methods will eventually rule out most possible theories of 3D quantum gravity. The theories left standing would be the only ones consistent with both gravity and quantum mechanics. Physicists could then use them to explore some of the biggest questions in quantum gravity: is there only one theory? Does it rely on string theory, or are there alternatives? What are black holes made of at the microscopic level, and how do we sum over spacetime histories?

This strategy can work in principle, but it’s very difficult in practice, because bootstrap functionals are hard to find. What matters for quantum gravity is what happens when c is large—but the larger c gets, the harder the search becomes. In the original paper on the modular bootstrap, Hellerman proved that in any consistent theory of 3D gravity, M₁ can be no larger than c/6, up to small corrections. Theories with a heavier lightest black hole can’t possibly be consistent with both gravity and quantum mechanics.

The goal of the modular bootstrap is to push this ceiling as low as possible, pinning down how heavy the lightest black hole can be. Physicists write the ceiling as M₁ ≤ c/Λ. A lower ceiling means a larger number Λ. The results below, including Claude’s, concern the simplest and most-studied version of the method, which ignores how the black holes spin.

Over the past 15 years, physicists have worked to lower the ceiling and pin down the theory as tightly as possible. There are two ways to do this. One is analytical—building a bootstrap functional by hand, with pen-and-paper mathematics. The other is numerical—using computers to search for functionals and optimize them. The results of these numerical calculations suggested that the modular bootstrap could push the ceiling down to about c/9.1. But these calculations get harder as c grows, so they couldn’t settle the question. On the analytical side, Tom Hartman, Dalimil Mazáč, and Leonardo Rastelli proved a ceiling of c/8.5, but because this was higher than the numerical results had suggested, this clearly wasn’t the best the modular bootstrap could do. Since then, just how tightly the modular bootstrap can constrain quantum gravity has remained an open question.

Claude set about solving the problem by running numerical experiments and reproducing results from the literature. In doing so, it noticed a pattern that physicists had so far missed, involving the way the numerical ceiling shifted slightly as c grew. Based on that pattern, Claude developed a hypothesis about the behavior of the bootstrap functional, using a mathematical tool called a spectral curve. Spectral curves are well known in other areas of mathematics and physics, but had never been applied to this problem. Claude found the spectral curve that governs the modular bootstrap at large values of c. It then used it to prove that the lightest black hole in 3D quantum gravity can be no heavier than c/Λ, where Λ is given by an exact formula and is approximately 9.1266. It also proved that this ceiling is optimal: it’s the lowest this version of the modular bootstrap can ever go. Claude delivered the solution across four research notes, which it later merged into a single paper.

This is one of the first times the bootstrap has been used to derive an exact result in the regime of quantum gravity where black holes matter. It also shows that this version of the method can’t, on its own, push the ceiling down to c/12, the mass at which black holes first appear; that will take other ingredients. Although it doesn’t pin down the theory of quantum gravity in three dimensions by itself, it provides a starting point and helps to narrow the search.

These two results show Claude doing two very different kinds of research. The first is a known calculation that physicists had set aside for two decades, because the work was too long and error-prone to complete by hand. The second required a new idea: spotting a pattern others had missed and bringing in a mathematical tool from another area of physics. Both were carried out largely autonomously. Neither result settles the question of quantum gravity, or even comes close to doing so, but each pushes physics forward in a longstanding area of research.

Unlocking Stable On-Policy RL on TPU with Bitwise-Zero Train–Inference Mismatch

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models is notoriously sensitive to numerical instability: even tiny discrepancies between rollout generation and gradient updates can destabilize training or stall convergence. In LLM RL, the model serves as the policy, π(action | previous tokens), and true on-policy learning requires every action in an update to be generated and evaluated by that exact same policy. While a synchronous loop ensures each rollout batch is scored using the same weights that generated it, a critical numerical requirement remains: the sampler and trainer must evaluate that policy identically.

In practice, production RL stacks run sampling and training through separate, specialized execution paths. The sampler chooses each action token during incremental decoding and records its behavior logprob. The trainer evaluates the same token in batched forward-backward passes to compute its current logprob and gradients. Under the same weights, these differing execution paths can still assign different logprobs to the same token and context. The training schedule is on-policy, but the numerical execution has introduced a hidden off-policy gap that compounds over multi-turn trajectories.

Our zero train–inference mismatch (Zero-TIM) implementation, developed in Tunix and verified at multi-host scale across 64 TPU v5p chips (DP8×TP8), closes this numerical gap. At the same weights, the sampler and trainer assign bitwise-identical logprobs to the same action and token history—bringing state-of-the-art, true on-policy stability to TPU RL training.

A reduction is a tree of floating-point additions. Floating-point addition is not associative, so changing the tree changes where rounding occurs. The effect is easier to expose in bfloat16, which retains less precision than float32.

The same issue appears in model reductions: matrix products accumulate partial sums, normalization computes statistics, and tensor-parallel shards combine partial outputs. In our original stack, sampler decode and trainer forward used different token-array shapes and forward implementations. JAX specializes computations to their input shapes, and the training forward also runs inside a differentiated program.

Across these paths, reduction order and intermediate precision were not consistently aligned. Floating-point rounding turned those arithmetic differences into different logprob bits. The weights and model equations were identical; the arithmetic was not.

The contract is simple: across sampler decode, serving prefill, and trainer forward, all three paths must produce bitwise-identical logprobs for every sampled action—comparing raw floating-point bytes under the same rollout weights, token history, and sampling settings, with zero rounding or tolerance.

Why three paths instead of two? In a decoupled RL stack, a direct sampler–trainer disagreement shows that a numerical gap exists, but not where it enters. Introducing serving prefill as a middle checkpoint turns alignment into a systematic two-step diagnostic that any RL stack can adopt:

Sampler decode vs. prefill verifies that the serving engine agrees with itself between incremental token generation and full-sequence re-scoring, while prefill vs. trainer verifies that the serving and training engines agree across compilation and differentiation boundaries.

Achieving Zero-TIM does not require merging the sampler and trainer into a single monolithic engine. Instead, we use a generalizable four-part recipe that bridges separate serving and training pipelines across multi-host TPU meshes: locking within-shard kernel accumulation, ordering cross-shard reductions, standardizing the logprob scoring tail, and wrapping serving kernels with custom differentiation rules.

Inside each tensor-parallel rank, batch-invariant Pallas kernels control accumulation order and intermediate precision. Projection kernels traverse fixed contraction blocks in order, keeping the reduction blocking independent of token-row count. Partial products accumulate in float32, with explicit output casts.

For row-parallel projections, each tensor-parallel rank produces a partial output. We separate communication from addition: ppermute transports the partials, then every rank adds them in the same source-rank order.

The reduction order is explicit in the program rather than selected by a collective implementation.

Alignment must extend beyond model logits to the probability of the sampled action. Decode, prefill, and trainer apply matching sampling transforms, use the same fixed Pallas log-softmax construction, and select the recorded action token.

Prefill and trainer score the action generated during rollout rather than choosing another action. We also preserve sampled token IDs across environment turns so that every path evaluates the same token history.

Training places that forward inside a differentiated JAX program. We pass --xla_allow_excess_precision=false through XLA_FLAGS to the compiler process, constraining excess intermediate precision in the operations surrounding the Pallas kernels.

Long trajectories are evaluated in fixed-size chunks, carrying the paged KV cache forward. All action logprobs feed the complete train-batch RL loss. The reverse pass propagates gradients through those cache connections and combines parameter-gradient contributions before the optimizer update.

Recent open-source efforts—including vLLM with TorchTitan and RadixArk/LMSys Miles—have demonstrated how batch-invariant kernels and bitwise numerical alignment prevent off-policy drift during policy optimization.

Our work delivers an open-source, modular JAX/XLA and Pallas implementation of this contract for TPU in Tunix. Agents are sessions, not requests: size GKE node pools for AI agent sessions

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Your company wants to move its coding agents from developer laptops to a shared GKE cluster, for persistence, scale, and governance. Each of those agent sessions stays open all day but works only part of it, and how you run them decides how much of your node bill pays for waiting.

By the end of this article, you will know why an agent session needs a different runtime than a web request, how Agent Substrate and AX run sessions on GKE, and how to identify cost optimization opportunities in your GKE node pools. This article assumes you know Kubernetes Pods and node pools. If not, check out the GKE overview.

A web request lasts milliseconds. The server answers it, keeps nothing, and serves the next one, so Kubernetes scales a web app by its traffic: more requests, more Pods.

An agent session works another way. A coding agent's workspace can stay open for the developer's whole workday: it keeps its memory and its files between prompts, and it works only after a prompt arrives.

How much of that day does the agent really work? Much less than it waits. In a study of GitHub Copilot agent traces, Banruo Liu and colleagues found that in a typical session with several prompts, the agent waits for the user about 80% of the time. A turn, one prompt and the agent's whole response to it, takes 396.3 seconds of agent work on average.

Put those numbers on one developer's day. With 2 prompts an hour over an 8 hour workday, which is our assumption, the agent works 22% of the day and waits for the other 78%. If each session holds a machine, you pay for that 78%.

Teams usually pick one of two answers, and both work well for web apps.

The first is to give each session its own Pod. The session keeps its state, but its Pod holds 1 vCPU and 1 GiB all day. For example, a c3-standard-4 node fits 3 such Pods after GKE's reservations, so in our cost model 100 coding agents need 34 nodes and about USD 1,288 a month, for agents that work 22% of the time.

The second is to scale the Pod to zero between prompts. The node is free, and files on a persistent volume survive, but the process and its memory do not, so the next prompt starts a new agent process.

Based on that, you want a third option: keep the session, and free the machine while the session waits.

Agent Substrate and AX are built for that: the developer's session stays open, and the sandbox that serves it gives its machine back while the session waits. Together they cover the parts of an agentic platform that are the same for every team: the control plane, the runtime, and the sandbox. Agent Substrate answers where and how an agent runs. AX answers what the agent is.

Following the CNCF donation of gVisor, Agent Substrate, and OpenRL in November 2026, you have the flexibility to run this open source, agentic platform across any environment. Agent Substrate 1.0 is generally available on GKE.

One distinction helps from here on. The session is the multi turn interaction between the developer and the agent, with its conversation history. The compute that serves it is an isolated sandbox: an actor in Agent Substrate, or a Task in AX. Parking acts on the sandbox, and the session stays open.

Agent Substrate answers where and how an agent runs. It separates the stateful agent from the compute it runs on. An actor is the sandbox for one agent, and it holds the agent's running processes, files, and identity. A worker is a reusable sandbox drawn from a warm pool of Kubernetes Pods, and one worker hosts several actors.

An agent runs code that a model wrote, so anything in its environment can leak through a prompt injection or a bad command, API keys included. Gateways control the actor's inbound and outbound traffic and add credentials on the way out, based on the actor's identity, so the agent never holds the keys.

SuspendActor snapshots an idle actor to object storage and frees its worker, and the next request restores the actor on any warm worker. Using this approach, we can achieve higher density by multiplexing more agents to run on the same amount of compute.

AX answers what the agent is and what it knows. You declare a Task, which says what runs (image, command, and limits), and a Workspace, which says what the agent knows (repositories and files). AX runs each Task as an actor. ax suspend parks the task and keeps its /workspace. Today AX asks for a volume snapshot, so the files come back on resume and the agent's processes start fresh (runner guide).

AX passes suspend, resume, and delete to Agent Substrate. The AX roadmap adds budgets, policies, and gateways, which limit what an agent may do, and a record of what each task did.

Two details decide the bill before you size anything.

First, you decide when a sandbox parks. Agent Substrate does not suspend an idle actor by itself (#424), so your agent harness, or the service in front of it, calls the suspend API after an idle timeout. Until then, an idle actor keeps its worker. When idle actors hold every worker, a request for another actor waits up to 5 seconds by default, then fails with HTTP 503 (Agent Substrate docs).

Second, you decide how much CPU each sandbox is promised. An actor is sized by its CPU and memory limits, and each worker books those limits against the capacity you declare for its pool (API guide). Our cost model sets that capacity to what the node really has, so every actor keeps its full limit. You can declare more capacity than the node has, and then you overcommit CPU at the pool instead of in each Pod.

Our cost model prices parking for the same 100 coding agents, at Google Cloud list prices in us-central1.

With a 2 minute idle timeout, each session holds a worker 29% of its open time. A warm pool sized for bursts (the 99th percentile of sessions holding a worker at once, if sessions act independently, plus 15% headroom) needs 15 nodes. Add one node that runs around the clock for the Agent Substrate control plane and its PostgreSQL database, and a job that scales the pool to zero after hours:

Parking saves 40% of the node bill. The pool is full about once in 3,500 turns, so a prompt rarely meets that 503. In an hour at 2.5 prompts instead of 2, the same 15 nodes are full for 3% of turns, so size the pool for your busiest hour.

Keep in mind that parking pays most when your agents wait the most. At 2 prompts an hour, the saving is 40%. At 4 prompts an hour, the agent works 44% of the hour, the pool grows to 27 nodes, and the saving is 4%. Around 4.5 prompts an hour, one Pod per session becomes the cheaper option, so measure your prompt rate first.

Also, the pool size is yours to set. The autoscaling option in the GKE install guide follows worker Pods, not suspended actors, so scale the pool to zero after hours with a scheduled job: a pool that runs all 730 hours of the month costs about twice the one Pod per session bill.

Finally, the fleet size matters. The Agent Substrate control plane costs the same for 10 sessions or 1,000, so parking pays from about 37 coding sessions, or 13 for an assumed chat assistant that works about a minute an hour.

Overcommit changes the comparison. If your Pods request less CPU than their limit, Kubernetes places them by their requests. A Pod that requests 0.25 vCPU with a 1 vCPU limit packs 12 to a node, so the same 100 coding agents fit on 9 nodes, fewer than our parked pool. Agent Substrate can overcommit too, through the capacity you declare on the worker pool, and our cost model does not price that case.

Overcommit has the same cost on both sides: agents share CPU and slow down when their neighbors are busy, during builds and tests. Compare like with like before you decide to park: Pods and actors with the same CPU guarantee.

Back to the shared cluster. Agents are sessions, not requests: size the warm pool for the sessions that hold a worker, not the sessions that are open.

We would park long lived sessions that wait most of the hour, and keep one Pod per session when developers prompt more than about 4 times an hour. Size the warm pool for working time plus a short idle timeout at your busiest hour, and compare with your current Pods at the same CPU guarantee before you park.

The New Year is just around the corner, and it is a common time for people to revisit self-improvement-related goals. If you want to reduce or stop drinking alcohol, you may be wondering if switching to non-alcoholic drinks could be helpful. As it turns out, for some people, non-alcoholic drinks could be a helpful substitute for alcoholic beverages, but there are also reasons to be cautious about this overall approach.

Before reading this post, if you are not already familiar with non-alcoholic drinks, take a look at my recent post, “Is there alcohol in non-alcoholic drinks?” The short answer is that the amount of alcohol depends on where you live. Generally, non-alcoholic drinks contain much lower alcohol by volume (ABV) than traditional alcoholic drinks, but they may still contain some alcohol. If you are trying to stop drinking alcohol completely, it would be important to check the rules where you live to make sure your chosen non-alcoholic drink has 0% ABV.

There are many reasons why someone may want to stop or reduce drinking alcoholic drinks, which I will not go over here. More relevant to this topic are the reasons why someone would want to consume non-alcoholic drinks, which are made to look and taste like alcoholic beverages, rather than traditional beverages that do not resemble alcoholic beverages (e.g., soft drinks, juices, water).

One reason for the popularity of non-alcoholic drinks is that someone may enjoy the taste of alcoholic drinks, like beer and wine, and wish to continue to drink similarly flavoured beverages (without the risks associated with consuming alcohol). Another reason people may choose non-alcoholic drinks is that they find it helps them continue to participate in social activities and routines that typically involve alcohol. For instance, when interviewing adults who drink alcohol heavily and have used non-alcoholic drinks to reduce drinking, some interviewees described that ordering a non-alcoholic beverage at a bar, club, or other social gathering allowed them to participate in the outing and blend in with their peers who were drinking alcohol.1 Further, unlike drinking alternatives like soft drinks, they describe that the experience of drinking a non-alcoholic drink remains symbolic as an "adult treat" given its look and presentation.

People may take different approaches to substitute with non-alcoholic drinks depending on their specific needs. Some people may completely substitute their alcoholic drinks for non-alcoholic drinks. Alternatively, people may replace alcoholic drinks in specific contexts, such as primarily substituting their home-based alcoholic drinks versus outing-based alcoholic drinks with non-alcoholic drinks.1 When replacing alcoholic drinks one typically consumes, some may replace them completely with non-alcoholic drinks while others may alternate between drink types (sometimes referred to as “zebra striping”).

While people describe using non-alcoholic drinks to reduce their alcohol use, an important question remains about whether these drinks can actually help you stop or reduce drinking. As it turns out, the qualities that make non-alcoholic drinks an attractive substitute for alcoholic drinks may also get in the way of successfully reducing one’s drinking. Aspects of drinking (beyond alcohol itself) can serve as powerful reminders—referred to as cues—of alcohol. Specifically, qualities of the drink like its taste, container, and where it is consumed can, in fact, lead to a strong urge to drink alcohol itself. By definition, non-alcoholic beverages will not lead to intoxication. Once consumed, these drinks may strengthen the desire to become intoxicated (rather than quench that desire), and lead someone to return to drinking alcohol.

Initial studies have suggested that non-alcoholic drinks may help people reduce their drinking. When adults who drink were provided non-alcoholic drinks free of charge over a 12-week period, they reported greater reductions in their alcoholic drink consumption compared to adults who were not provided these drinks.2 This reduction in alcoholic drinks continued a month after these non-alcoholic drinks were provided. A follow-up study suggested, however, that the reduced alcohol drinking was not observed amongst people with higher symptoms of alcohol use disorder.3 Additional research is needed to understand if non-alcoholic drinks may be a helpful way to reduce or stop drinking among people with an alcohol use disorder.

Ultimately, there would be a net health and mental health benefit if someone reduces the overall number of alcoholic drinks by substituting non-alcoholic drinks. For instance, if someone switches from drinking four alcoholic drinks per week to two alcoholic drinks plus two non-alcoholic drinks, they would be reducing their exposure to harmful aspects of alcohol. However, depending on an individual’s circumstances, the chosen strategy for which drinks to substitute can affect how much they benefit overall. For instance, if a person finds they have more difficulty moderating their drinking when out with friends (versus at home), having a strategy to rely on non-alcoholic drinks when out with friends may be more beneficial than substituting their alcohol drinks consumed at home.

While research is still in its early stages, non-alcoholic drinks show promise in helping people reduce their drinking. For some people, substituting non-alcoholic drinks may provide the benefits of drinking alcoholic drinks without the negatives; however, it is important to make sure the substitute approach does not become an add-on approach. In cases where non-alcoholic drinks are too similar to alcoholic drinks that it leads you to drink alcohol anyway, then the substitution approach is not successful. Therefore, if you are thinking about giving this strategy a try, it is important to keep track of your drinking carefully to determine if it is actually helping you to reduce the number of alcoholic drinks you consume.

When a test runner, Docker build, or dependency step emits thousands of repeated status lines, ANSI progress-bar redraws, or ConnectionResetError retries, the actual traceback gets buried under megabytes of repetitive scrollback.

Ad-hoc grep -v or tail -n 200 pipes introduce two dangerous bugs: they slice away surrounding context needed for root-cause triage, and naive shell pipes can swallow the upstream command's non-zero exit code, turning a broken build falsely green.

A practical stream compressor reads line-by-line from stdin instead of buffering gigabytes in RAM, normalizes noisy ANSI carriage-return frames, and collapses consecutive or repeated pattern lines into a compact count.

Built purely on the Python standard library, logshrink shrinks verbose CI and build output by 70–90% while letting every panic stack, exception traceback, and failure boundary pass through unmodified.

Sandisk Is Splitting Its Stock 8-for-1. Here’s the Number I’d Check Before Buying.

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sandisk’s profit margin matters more to the investment case than its new share count.

A stock split always generates buzz. So, when Sandisk (SNDK -7.58%) recently announced an 8-for-1 stock split, investors cheered.

Yet while stock splits can make headlines, investors may want to focus on another figure -- its gross margin -- rather than its new, lower stock price. Here's why.

First, let's talk about stock splits. While they may seem exotic, they don't actually change anything fundamental about the business. The best analogy is culinary: If you take a whole, uncut pizza and cut it into eight slices, you've done nothing to change how much food you have. You've simply divided it up.

Stock splits work the same way. Fundamentals, like revenue, net income, and free cash flow, do not change when a company splits its shares. When Sandisk shares split on Nov. 20, no one receives anything for free; they will simply see what they already own split according to the 8-for-1 principle.

For example, say you own 10 shares of Sandisk today. As of Nov. 13, each share trades for around $858, for a total market value of $8,580. After the split, someone who owned 10 shares will own 80 shares, and each share will drop to $107.25 (assuming the price stays steady between now and then). The total market value of 80 shares at $107.25 per share? $8,580, the same as before.

Granted, the price of Sandisk shares will fluctuate between now and Nov. 20, but the principle remains the same. The market value of shares going into Nov. 20 will still equal the market value after the split; the only thing that will change is the share price.

Rather than focusing on the stock split, I think investors should take a closer look at Sandisk's gross margins. Gross margin is the profit remaining after deducting the cost of making a product.

Imagine Sandisk sells a memory chip for $100. If it took $15 to physically assemble the product, its gross margin on that product would be 85%. Bear in mind that the company doesn't get to keep the full $85 – it still has administrative and research costs, not to mention taxes.

However, gross margins are useful because they strip away these costs, allowing investors to see how profitable a business is without them. Sandisk's gross margins exploded since 2024, increasing from near 10% to around 85%.

SNDK Gross Profit Margin (Quarterly) data by YCharts

However, the big question is whether the company can maintain these incredible margins, or whether those margins will come crashing down.

For Sandisk, its gross margins are driven more by the price customers are willing to pay than by the input costs for its products. Therefore, it may be helpful to think of Sandisk's business like a hotel. The same rooms are far more profitable at $400 per night than at $150 per night, with many of the hotel's costs remaining steady.

Similarly, the supply-and-demand dynamics of the memory market have an enormous effect on Sandisk's gross margins. If memory demand were to pull back, perhaps as a result of slowing AI infrastructure investment, Sandisk's gross margins could tumble. On the other hand, if the company's long-term memory contracts prove durable, Sandisk's gross margins may remain resilient.

Stock splits alone do not make a stock an attractive investment. The split changes the unit size; profits determine the investment's appeal.

As a result, Sandisk must maintain its impressive gross margins in future quarters in order to justify its appeal. The stock already trades at a price-to-sales (P/S) ratio of 4.6. That's down significantly from its peak of over 25 in June, but it remains far above its 2025 average multiple of around 1.7.

The market has already priced a major step into Sandisk's profitability. If that fall doesn't materialize, the stock could be primed for a move higher. That's why I'm intrigued by Sandisk's gross margins, not its upcoming stock split.

Keeping you occupied just long enough is the plan, so you don't end up calling the bank for a second opinion.

Dedicated scammers are known to stay on the line as the victims login to the accounts to transfer funds, buy gift cards or make a visit to the nearby ATM to withdraw cash. Some even go as far as to school you on what to tell bank employees when questioned about that odd transaction. When the person "helping" you protect your savings is deterring you from speaking to anyone else, that's a pretty obvious sign that something is off.

One possible way in could be a compromised bank account. A creative scammer could inform you about open accounts under your name or even scare you with an arrest warrant. The plan is to make you feel that following their instructions is the only way out, so hanging up doesn't feel like a plausible option.

Sometimes, the whole script includes more than just one scam artist. Back in August 2026, the FBI reported that scammers posing as bank employees told their victims that their information had been used to open an account to purchase illegal firearms. The scammers then transferred the victims to someone posing as an FBI agent, but disconnected after the victims were encouraged to verify the number.

Shortly after, they received a call displaying the FBI's Boston office number, which had been spoofed. These "FBI agents" could then ask the victims to continue the conversations using an encrypted messaging app. The FBI didn't specify how many people were targeted by this scheme.

But the threatening villain persona is not the only way these scammers are out to get you. Some of them act professional, stay patient and even go a step further to sound sympathetic. Now, when these friendly voices start to discourage you from reaching out to the organization directly or getting a second opinion, that should get your alarm bells ringing. After all, con artists don't want all of their efforts to result in failure.

Once the small talk is done, call handlers may switch to a dictating tone in order to get the outcome they want. This is a clear warning sign. When the caller demands you stay connected or instructs you to keep the conversation private, be sure to trust your instincts and end the call.

When they ask you to move funds to a "safer" account, it's time to hang up. Any request for passwords or even one-time verification codes should also be met with a call termination. Since scammers are often savvy enough to spoof authentic phone numbers, caller ID isn't all that reliable either.

Once you hang up or end that text conversation, reach out to the organization, but only using its official app or the number on your bank card or statement. Steer clear of contact numbers that the caller gives you, and trusting the customer-service numbers from sponsored search results isn't safe either. If it's a bit late to heed this advice and you've already shared sensitive information or worse transferred money to the scammer, contact your bank or payment provider right away.

If you own a supported Samsung Galaxy phone, try out spam filtering under Phone > Settings > Caller ID and spam protection, and Google Pixel owners can set up Call Screen using the Phone app's settings on supported devices in eligible regions. Sharing personal narratives—whether through storytelling, group discussions, or expressive writing—has emerged as a valuable psychosocial intervention in oncology. Storytelling is the communicative act of patients recounting their illness experiences through words, images, or even digital media, often revealing deeply personal emotions and insights. Across cancer types and patient populations, narrative-sharing interventions are increasingly recognized for fostering emotional expression and psychological adaptation to illness. Peer-reviewed research spanning children, adolescents, adults, and the elderly, indicates that storytelling bestows a range of benefits. These benefits include improvements in psychological well-being, coping and resilience, treatment adherence, social support, and even physiological or clinical outcomes.

Peer-reviewed research overwhelmingly supports the therapeutic value of storytelling and personal narratives for cancer patients of all ages and stages. Through sharing their experiences—whether in a support group, with a counselor, via digital media, or in a private journal—patients often achieve better psychological well-being, characterized by reduced anxiety, stress, and even depression. Storytelling fosters adaptation and coping, empowering patients with resilience and practical strategies to face cancer’s challenges. It also enhances social connectedness, diminishing isolation and strengthening support networks among those who “get it.” Notably, narrative interventions can encourage patients to stay engaged in treatment and healthy behaviors, potentially improving adherence and follow-through in care. While more research is needed, emerging evidence points to physiological benefits—improved symptom control, functional status, and overall quality of life—arising from the mind-body interplay of expressive storytelling. Taken together, these studies support the belief that the simple act of sharing one’s story is far more than talk or writing on a page; it is a meaningful therapeutic process that can help cancer patients heal psychologically, cope more effectively, build community, and perhaps even influence their health trajectories for the better. Narrative practices in oncology—from diagnosis through survivorship or end-of-life care—represent a holistic approach that honors patients’ voices while delivering measurable benefits across multiple dimensions of well-being.

When U.S. President Donald Trump touches down in Shenzhen, China, this week for a three-way summit with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin, he will be embarking on the highest-level diplomacy he has ever undertaken at his weakest point in power.

The problem is not merely the devastating midterm election losses for his Republican Party, in which he urged supporters to turn out and cast their votes as if he were on the ballot. In recent weeks, Trump has displayed startling new levels of incoherence and evasion of responsibility for a sitting president. This seemed to reach a crescendo last week with two national crises for which Trump had no suitable answers.

The first of these crises brought news that Jensen Huang, the head of Nvidia, at once one of the world’s most valuable and strategically important companies, had had an affair with a suspected Chinese intelligence asset. Trump pleaded ignorance, denying well-sourced reports that he had been briefed about the matter as long ago as May, including by his national security advisor and secretary of state, Marco Rubio. The only thoughts the president seemed able to summon reflected credulity about Huang’s claim that he had never suspected the woman could have been a spy and personal sympathy for a man who had, until his sudden resignation from Nvidia last week, belonged to a club that means more to Trump than any other: the exclusive circle of the world’s richest and most powerful men. Perhaps for that reason, on Monday, Trump told reporters he “would not mind” if Huang was reinstated.

For all of Trump’s envy of powerful authoritarians, if the situations were reversed and Huang were a Chinese national leading a company as important as Nvidia, it is impossible to imagine Xi treating a man under such suspicions this way, including welcoming him on a flight to his country’s No. 1 competitor and adversary. But Trump’s need for this kind of validation is an ongoing matter and not something he has learned a lesson from. Witness his decision to invite the world’s richest man, Elon Musk, to join him on this trip, if not in the meetings with his fellow heads of state.

For a U.S. president heading off to a summit such as this, the week’s second crisis may have been even worse. The image the United States presented to the world in the wake of a breakdown in the nationwide air traffic control system on Friday was one of utter dishevelment and incompetence. It put the spotlight on two of arguably the least qualified members of a cabinet resolutely hostile to meritocracy: Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy, a former congressman and Fox News personality, and Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin, a former congressman whose formal education culminated with an associate’s degree in plumbing and construction.

With thousands of flights canceled and a country desperate for answers and reassurance, they bumbled their way through an almost incoherent news conference that offered no real explanations. Instead, they cast blame on others, including Congress and past administrations, while exalting Trump’s leadership as if they were members of a personality cult and the public should be too. During the first 36 hours of the crisis, Trump for his part chose to lie low, making no statements about the matter, apparently fearing that doing so would mean taking responsibility for something bad that happened on his watch, which he has always been deeply allergic to.

These displays of unaccountable behavior and cluelessness are bad enough for the United States in their own right, but Americans should be worried about their implications for the upcoming summit. Even in ordinary times, Trump’s narcissism and emotional neediness make diplomacy like this dangerous because he craves fellowship and approval from men such as Putin and Xi, whom he all but openly idolizes. But with last week’s crises and domestic scandals sprouting in many directions as Democrats prepare to take over Congress, Trump is liable to make large and unwise concessions to his imagined peers in order to secure political coups that he hopes can restore prestige and momentum to his badly weakened presidency. The cliché holds that weakened or trapped animals are liable to strike out to defend themselves. With Trump, the danger is the opposite—given his self-centered view of the world, where his own ego gratification is paramount, he could give away the store.

What could that mean? Here, one feels that Trump is so easily flattered that the sky is almost the limit. He has reportedly already forsworn raising the Nvidia crisis with Xi as well as Russia’s ongoing war of aggression against Ukraine. If true, such self-imposed restrictions themselves emphasize the weakness of the U.S. president’s hand and preemptively vitiate the utility of such an unusual gathering.

What does this leave on the table for discussion? The Trump administration has said it hopes to make progress on limiting nuclear weapons armament at a time when the United States and Russia still have enormous legacy arsenals whose maintenance and modernization are costly and China is belatedly racing to catch up. Trump officials have also said they hope to engage leaders of the world’s other two big powers on the United States’ stop-and-go war against Iran, which by now has turned into a quagmire.

But what could the latter possibly yield? That Trump has already displayed weakness and enormous gullibility on Iran in his dealings with both powers does not bode well. Russia has supplied intelligence and munitions support to Iran throughout the war, enabling that country to target U.S. military bases throughout the Middle East with devastating effect. Consistent with his general enthrallment to Putin, Trump has publicly downplayed the importance of this, making clear that maintaining warm relations with Moscow is his priority. China, if more cautious than Russia, has also hewed to a line that has enhanced the Iranian state’s position. It has done this by continuing to purchase as much Iranian oil as it can while also reportedly supplying electronic components essential to that country’s missile and drone production. Genuine progress toward administration goals on Iran would require concrete commitments from both powers to stop what they are currently doing. Trump appears unlikely to demand this, and even if he did, neither is likely to follow through.

Putin has already mesmerized Trump into treating Ukraine as if it were the faulty party in its defensive war against Russia. That conflict may not officially be on the agenda when the three leaders sit down together on Thursday, but the specific danger in Shenzhen is that Putin will offer Trump a vague, unverifiable promise to limit support for Iran—something that Trump can present as a win—in exchange for Trump’s deeper complicity in ending the Ukraine war on Russian terms. It would be a trade of something real and irreversible for something hollow and unenforceable. Because of Trump’s unique weakness for glad-handing and grandiosity, it may take little more than that. A more lucid and competent U.S. president would never allow such confusion between the two crises.

What might Xi seek, and how do Trump’s growing vulnerabilities play into this? Beijing’s biggest goal is progress toward exerting control over Taiwan, if not taking over the island altogether by the eventual end of Xi’s longtime hold on power. Toward this end, Trump has already gifted Xi in important ways without receiving anything in return. At the level of official rhetoric, Trump has weakened America’s traditional support for a peaceful resolution of the dispute between Beijing and Taipei over the island’s future. In fact, he has come close to saying the United States would have no business defending Taiwan in case a war broke out between the two sides.

This by itself is an immense gift, and it would be enhanced further if Trump delayed arms sales to Taiwan to ingratiate himself with Xi. Already, the U.S. military posture in East Asia has been dramatically weakened by the prolonged deployment of aircraft carrier battle groups near Iran and by heavy depletion of advanced weapons systems during the war with that country. Trump has also weakened U.S. alliances in Asia through tariff demands and unpredictable behavior. By nature, Xi seems much more cautious than Putin. But sensing Trump’s weakness and need for some kind of foreign-policy achievement, however illusory, he could try to induce concessions through displays of strongman camaraderie and vague pledges of cooperation in areas of U.S. need.

If we cannot know in advance what these meetings will produce, we should know one thing already. Trump will emerge from them making great claims about how well he gets along with the world’s two other most powerful heads of state. In fact, nothing more might publicly come out of the meetings beyond this. But the world should be wary even of any quiet understandings that Trump, at this moment of extreme weakness and vulnerability, believes he has reached. The likelihood is that he is being played due to his personal and political need, and if the details are unknowable, the costs could be great.

All the Award-Winning Photos From Nature Photographer of the Year 2026

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

All the Award-Winning Photos From Nature Photographer of the Year 2026

The Nature Photographer of the Year 2026 winners have been unveiled, showcasing the world’s best nature, wildlife, and landscape photography.

This year’s competition received 27,108 entries from photographers in nearly 100 countries, a record-breaking number for the longtime photo contest.

Finnish photographer Hannu Ahonen bested stiff competition to earn the title of Nature Photographer of the Year. Ahonen’s award-winning photo, “The One that Left and the One that Stayed,” is an extraordinary image of a water lily leaf positioned alongside its icy duplicate.

“It is not often that a photograph of a plant is crowned the overall winner of a major photography competition, but the jury could not overlook this fascinating image by Hannu Ahonen,” says jury chair Theo Bosboom. “First and foremost, the photograph captures a unique situation: a water lily leaf with an almost perfect black-and-white replica of it lying next to it in the water.”

Ahonen’s winning photo tells a compelling and unusual story. The living water lily leaf is positioned on the left, while the ice that formed on top of it and slipped off sits delicately to the side, blown away slightly by the breeze. Even the veins from the leaf are visible in the icy shell.

Nature Photographer of the Year adds that the photo reveals the “marvelous beauty of nature” while embodying its transient character. The sheet of ice will soon melt away, joining the water that keeps the lily alive in the first place. It’s a symbolic photo at a time when climate change is impacting all life on Earth, especially its ice.

“The winter ice had arrived, but it remained snowless for a long time. Some of the pond’s lily pad leaves began to crumble on top of the ice. The wind carried a few of them away, revealing delicate, frosty ornaments left behind. I eagerly captured this natural phenomenon, something I had never witnessed before,” Ahonen explains.

Ahonen has an inspiring story. He has ADHD and lives in a wilderness home in Finland with no carbon footprint. Rarely venturing more than a couple of miles from his home, he spends much of his time behind the camera in his yard, using photography as a spiritual escape and creative outlet.

Alongside the overall winner, Ahonen, the competition’s jury also selected winners across nearly a dozen categories. There are also special prizes for youth photographers and an accomplished portfolio. Ahonen took the top spot in the Plants and Fungi category, so that category is not included below.

French photographer Lucas Mugnier-Lavorel won this year’s Birds category for his environmental portrait of a Magellanic woodpecker in Patagonia.

“Environmental portraits of wildlife can make us feel closer to the animals despite being farther away from them, when done right. This image of a Magellanic woodpecker in its gorgeous habitat was a perfect example. It stood out from thousands of submissions because of its understated sense of place, beauty, simplicity, and timing,” says judge Steffen Foerster.

“Overall, it was a very successful reminder of how the portrayal of nature in its literal state can still be the most powerful of photographic expressions,” Foerster concludes.

British photographer Paul Goldstein’s foggy photo of a giraffe earned the top prize in the Mammals category.

“Quiet images can be easy to overlook in the fiercely competitive mammal category, one of the largest in NPOTY. This ethereal image of a giraffe peeking out of the mist revealed its strengths gradually. Initially a little disorienting, it offered just enough of a visual puzzle to keep the viewer engaged until the image resolved,” Foerster explains.

Judges tapped Marcus Kuan’s macro photo of a surprisingly rich and diverse little ecosystem alongside a pitcher plant in the Other Animals category. The Singaporean photographer impressed the panel with his composition and lighting.

“The lighting, colors, and unexpected gathering of species immediately caught our attention. Kuan beautifully captured a world where crustaceans, amphibians, and carnivorous plants cross paths,” explains jury member Louis Guillot.

“As judges, we were drawn to the possibilities unfolding within this single frame. Could the pitchers be digesting invertebrates? Might the frog be exploring a potential egg-laying site, while the crab continues its daily search for food among the plants?” Guillot continues.

Photographer J Fritz Rumpf from the United States won this year’s Landscape category with a silhouetted photo of Iguazú Falls in Argentina.

“What immediately caught our attention was the incredible light and atmosphere in this photo. The longer we looked, the more fascinating it became. There is so much to discover: the last light of day illuminating the mist rising from Iguazú Falls, the silhouettes of trees and rocks, the birds circling above, the sunlight streaming through an opening in the rock at the center, and the subtle waterfall on the left,” says jury member Ilse Koks.

Another American photographer, Matt Pérez, won the Underwater category with a striking photo of a turtle beneath a collection of newt eggs.

“Pérez’s photograph immediately drew us in. As a jury, we wanted to linger over it, exploring the details and wondering what we were seeing,” remarks Guillot. “Newt egg masses decorate the submerged branches like baubles on a Christmas tree, framing the scene and guiding our eyes towards the pond turtle with its piercing yellow eyes.”

Guillot adds that the scene looks “otherworldly,” which is a very apt description.

Chinese photographer Xuzhong Liu’s abstract drone shot of a winding landscape ticks all the right boxes. It has interesting patterns, striking colors, and a very pleasing composition.

Liu captured the image in a wetland area in China and says the scene strongly reminded him of peach blossoms he had previously photographed—hence the title, “Peach Blossoms in the Snow.”

“This photograph has everything that can make a drone shot interesting and fascinating. First and foremost, there are beautiful shapes and subtle colours, but there is also a sense of alienation. What am I actually looking at, and what is the scale of the landscape in the photograph?” asks the chair of the judging panel, Theo Bosboom.

Czech photographer Petr Bambousek’s winning photo depicts a sobering scene of a Malayan sun bear resting inside a garbage incinerator.

“The winning image stood out for its striking depiction of the impact human activity has on local wildlife,” explains jury member Lana Tannir. “The sleeping Malayan sun bear inside a garbage incinerator creates a powerful visual contrast, showing how habitat loss and human waste increasingly force wild animals into close contact with human environments.”

Tannir says Bambousek’s photo is “unexpected and unsettling.”

Hungarian photographer László Tóth won the Black and White category for his striking, high-contrast, abstract photo.

“This photo really stood out to us. At first, we were not quite sure what we were looking at. It sparked some wonderful discussions among the jury and made us look even closer,” says Ilse Koks.

As for what the photo actually depicts, it’s a thin layer of snow on top of a frozen lake, shaped by the wind.

This photo might be familiar to PetaPixel readers. Norwegian photographer Audun Rikardsen recently won an award for a similar underwater portrait of a polar bear peering down through a hole in the ice.

“Just when we thought we had seen about every kind of polar bear image there is, along comes this shot taken from inside a hole in the ice looking up at the bear,” says Steffen Foerster. “Once you realize what is happening, you start thinking about how this shot could possibly have been taken. Then it dawns on you that it seems almost impossible, no matter how.”

This special category, “De Lage Landen” (which translates to “The Low Countries”), celebrates photos captured in the low-lying region of northwestern Europe, including the Netherlands, where Nature Photographer of the Year is based. This year’s winning photo is by Hungarian photographer Enikő Kálovics.

“The winning image stood out for its ability to transform a familiar landscape into something dreamlike and unexpected,” says Lana Tannir. “The combination of the tiny lacewings, the autumn forest, and the purplish night sky creates a delicate, atmospheric interpretation of the nature of ‘De Lage Landen.'”

American photographer Parham Pourahmad, who was also recently celebrated in the 2027 North American Nature Photography Association (NANPA) Showcase, won this year’s Youth category with a lovely monochromatic photo of a coyote beneath a large tree.

“In ‘Tree of Life’, Pourahmad captured a striking scene that could comfortably hold its own in the adult categories,” says Guillot. “Black and white was an excellent choice, defining the coyote’s silhouette and revealing the majestic oak’s intricate network of branches. Even within this wider scene, the coyote’s downward gaze is clear. Pourahmad explained that it was searching for rodent prey, a small detail that brings the photograph to life.”

Pourahmad has also been named Youth Photographer of the Year.

The Fred Hazelhoff Award celebrates a portfolio of nature photos, rather than a single image. This year’s portfolio winner is German photographer Simone Baumeister.

“In the portfolio category, the bar is set considerably higher for participating photographers than in the individual categories. Whilst a single fantastic photograph is sufficient there, in the portfolio category you need a whole series of outstanding images that not only complement one another but also fit together well in terms of style and theme. Simone Baumeister has succeeded brilliantly in this with her portfolio on eagle owls in the city,” says jury chair Theo Bosboom.

Baumeister has been fascinated by owls for as long as she has been interested in nature photography. The eagle owl’s story is a positive one, in contrast to the plight of many species across the globe. The species, once on the brink of extinction in Germany, has made an incredible recovery. Its resurgence is thanks largely to major conservation efforts and extensive reintroduction programs.

“It was perched in twilight on a church, right below the street lamps, moving cars, and a string of people passing by, with music blaring from a pub right on the church square. Not a soul took notice of the impressive bird with a wingspan of 1.7 meters flying up the steeple, sitting enthroned right within everybody’s view,” the photographer says of the first time she saw an eagle owl in a city.

Ever since then, she has been consistently excited by eagle owls and keen to photograph them in many different places. Observing and photographing them in various cities inevitably morphed into a dedicated project.

FBI says Artex AI open-source hacking tool used to breach networks in ‘under three hours’

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The FBI is warning that hackers are relying on autonomous AI hacking tools to launch cyberattacks across the U.S. and globally. The campaigns include a widespread hack of customers of American IT company ConnectWise and across South Korea’s financial industry.

In its latest flash cybersecurity advisory, the FBI says hackers have targeted several industries in recent months, including financial services, government, tech, defense, phone and internet companies, and law firms, using open-source tools like Artex to carry out autonomous-driven cyberattacks.

Artex is one of several open-source AI-powered penetration testing platforms, a category of software built to probe a network’s defenses. Hackers can connect Artex to hosted frontier large language models (LLMs) like those from Anthropic and OpenAI, as well as to open-source models and non-U.S. models. The FBI says Artex intrusions can be “noisy,” meaning they generate reams of traffic and data that may trigger a targeted company’s defensive alerts. But the tools allow hackers to move at speeds that makes it far more harder for human defenders to keep up.

In some cases, Artex-linked attacks can move from targeting a system to active compromise “in under three hours.”

The FBI’s warning comes as AI-powered hacks are escalating, particularly by finding easy-to-find security flaws and breaking into companies with relative ease.

Oftentimes, Artex will rely on rapidly scanning a victim’s network to look for signs of weakness, and then exploit simple security bugs, such as weak protections and exposed or stolen passwords to gain a foothold. From there, Artex allows the hacker to spread across the network for databases, mailboxes, and other sensitive information.

The FBI’s advisory noted that Artex has been used in several cyberattacks since the tool’s first public debut in July. Artex was used to hack a number of South Korean financial institutions, blamed in large part on weak cybersecurity defenses and lacking protections, such as multi-factor authentication. The attacks allowed hackers to steal the personal data of at least 68,000 people.

The FBI confirmed earlier findings from Microsoft that hackers also used Artex to break into IT management giant ConnectWise and gain access to the Microsoft 365 cloud environments of at least 2,250 of its customers.

The hackers, who Microsoft says are likely Chinese, used the tool to steal data from affected ConnectWise customers.

A spokesperson for ConnectWise did not respond to a request for comment about the FBI’s claim.

Cybersecurity company Barracuda also disclosed that it was breached as part of an Artex-linked attack by financially motivated criminals, in which the tool found an exposed credential belonging to a third-party service that Barracuda had not revoked when it ended work with the vendor. That access allowed the hackers to access stores of credentials used by Barracuda to collect data from its customers’ systems. Barracuda said it couldn’t rule out access, as its logs were not detailed enough, and chose to “assume the worst” and changed all of its customer credentials.

“Defenders who rely on spotting malicious activity and then responding to it will often arrive after the attacker has finished,” wrote Barracuda’s chief information security officer Arve Kjoelen.

Barracuda did not return a request for comment about how many customers are affected by the breach.

After Artex was used in cyberattacks, the tool’s developer made the software closed-source to prevent further attacks. But the FBI says the previous open-source version continues to circulate and remains “widely accessible” for use in cyberattacks.

It’s not only companies and targeted victims that have been compromised as a result of weakened security exploited by AI tools; the tools themselves have been exposed as well, potentially allowing outsiders to access powerful hacking capabilities. Last week, researchers found hundreds of publicly exposed Artex systems online, which had been placed on the internet without a password, likely by mistake or misconfiguration — including the Artex system that was used to target South Korea.

Nokia, Nscale, Skeleton, Telia and Verda have formed a group to bid for one of the EU’s planned AI gigafactories. Called AI Gigafactory Northern, it would spread one facility across several Nordic and Baltic sites, the partners said in a joint release on Monday.

Everything You Need to Know About Korean Scalp Scalers

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

If you’ve ever been to a Korean head spa, you know it’s less of a hair wash and more of an entire reset. Before anyone touches a shampoo bottle, there’s usually a close-up look at your scalp on a magnifying camera, followed by a treatment that loosens all the oil, dead skin, and product reside that a regular wash leaves behind. It’s the step that makes your hair feel impossibly light afterward, and it’s called scaling.

You don’t need a spa appointment to get the same effect, though. Scalp scalers bring that deep clean home, whether that’s an acid-based treatment you leave on before shampooing or a salt scrub that lathers up in the shower. They’re especially useful if you lean on dry shampoo, work out often, or have fine hair that goes flat a day after washing.

Below, experts explain what a scalp scaler actually does, who should use one and how to keep from overdoing it, plus the formulas worth trying.

The name actually comes from the dentist’s office. “In Korean, ‘scaling’ is the same word we use for dental cleaning,” says Dr. Ellie Byun, a doctor of Korean medicine and licensed acupuncturist. “Plaque builds up on your teeth even when you brush, and in the same way, oil and dead skin build up on your scalp even when you shampoo.” For many Korean women, it’s a routine they’ve had for years. “I’ve been using scalp scalers since high school,” says Kristin Lim is the founder of Base-K.

What sets a scaler apart from a regular wash is time. “A clarifying shampoo only cleanses during the short time it’s on your scalp,” says Dr. Byun. “A scaler stays on for several minutes and chemically dissolves dead skin and oil with acids and enzymes, the same principle as an exfoliating toner for your face.” That said, plenty of Korean scalers, including several below, use salt granules instead of acids, or a mix of both. “A scalp scaler focuses on loosening dead skin and buildup, using acids, physical exfoliants or a combination of both,” says Lim. If you go the gritty route, keep it gentle. “A scrub physically buffs the surface with grains, and if you use it too often, it can make the scalp barrier sensitive,” says Dr. Byun.

“It’s especially good for oily scalps, flaking caused by buildup, fine hair that goes flat quickly, people who use a lot of dry shampoo and styling products, and anyone who sweats a lot,” says Byun. Flaking is where it gets tricky, though. “It can also help loosen surface flakes, but persistent flaking and itching deserve a closer look,” says Lim. “Dandruff and other scalp conditions may need targeted treatment rather than more exfoliation.”

The first thing most people notice is how light their hair feels afterward. “Removing that residue can leave the scalp feeling cleaner and the hair feeling lighter,” says Lim. With regular use, it can help with oil and flaking, too. “In a four-week study of a salicylic acid formula, researchers examined the scalp with a magnification camera and found that flaking, itching, redness, and oiliness all decreased,” says Dr. Byun. A calmer scalp may also mean better hair over time. “Studies show that when scalp health declines, hair quality declines too, and when the scalp recovers, hair quality recovers with it,” she says.

The biggest risk is doing too much. “Over-exfoliating weakens the scalp barrier and can cause irritation, dryness, and even a rebound increase in oil,” says Dr. Byun. It’s not the right fit for every scalp, either. “Individuals with sensitive or irritated scalps should approach exfoliating treatments cautiously,” says Joe Cho, team lead of media relations at Medicube. Even a mild label doesn’t change that. “Even a gentle formula may not suit a scalp that’s actively inflamed,” says Lim. If something feels off, don’t push through it. “If you notice sudden heavy shedding, pain, or lesions, see a professional,” says Dr. Byun.

“Some scalers go onto a dry or damp scalp before shampooing, while others are used on wet hair as part of the wash,” says Lim. Either way, the product needs to reach your scalp, not just your hair. “Part your hair in sections about an inch apart and apply it directly to the scalp along each part,” says Byun. “Then massage with your fingertips, never your nails, for two to three minutes, pressing slowly on the crown and the base of the skull.” Then rinse it out on time. “Leaving it on longer or scrubbing harder won’t improve the result,” says Lim.

Less is more with frequency, too. “I recommend once a week for oily scalps, and once every two weeks for normal or dry scalps,” says Byun. Your scalp will tell you when you’ve gone too far. “Signs you’re overdoing it include tightness, stinging, redness, or tenderness, more flaking instead of less, new itching, or a scalp that feels oilier than before,” she says. Lim also recommends sticking to one exfoliant at a time and avoiding combining several scalp exfoliants in the same wash.

Afterward, think of your scalp the way you’d think about your face after an exfoliating toner. “Korean care follows a simple idea: clear first, then nourish,” Dr. Byun says. “Use a mild, low-pH shampoo, then a lightweight scalp serum with niacinamide, panthenol, or centella, and apply conditioner only from the mid-lengths to the ends.”

Not directly. “While scalp health plays an important role in overall hair care, there’s currently limited clinical evidence that scalp exfoliation alone can directly stimulate hair growth or prevent shedding,” says Cho. Dr. Byun sees it as groundwork. “A scaler doesn’t grow hair on its own, but it does have a real, research-backed role,” she says. “So I see scaling as the first step in creating an environment where healthy hair can grow.” Lim is just as clear: “Promising that a scaler alone will regrow hair or stop shedding would be over-promising.”

Usually, scalp scales are safe for colored hair as long as you keep it where it belongs. “It’s generally fine on color-treated hair as long as you apply it to the scalp, not the hair itself,” says Byun.
