NASA is reviving discussions to rescue the Hubble Space Telescope, which is operating well but is on track to reenter Earth's atmosphere about six years from now.

The agency issued a request for information (RFI) on Oct. 29 for "in-space servicing" capabilities, which could include boosting Hubble's orbit (which is naturally degrading due to atmospheric drag), or even a mission to "enhance its capabilities" in some way.

Hubble, which launched in April 1990 in partnership with the European Space Agency, was serviced five times by astronauts on space shuttle missions, the most recent being in 2009. The observatory's groundbreaking achievements include helping to show that the expansion of the universe is accelerating — a discovery that won the Nobel Prize — as well as a swath of finds about planets, galaxies, stars and other parts of the universe.

Hubble also regularly teams up with other telescopes for science work, and its observations have led to the publication of at least 23,000 scientific papers, NASA figures suggest. And, despite its age, Hubble remains in high demand. "Astronomers request about seven times as much observing time as is available each year," the agency stated in September.

Hubble is also widely appreciated by the public. People have long enjoyed and celebrated its iconic images, such as the Eagle Nebula's "Pillars of Creation," a gorgeous shot that has been revisited several times over the decades. As such, this is not the first time in recent years that discussions have opened about keeping the famed observatory running for a while longer.

In 2022, for example, billionaire tech entrepreneur Jared Isaacman proposed using SpaceX hardware to mount a Hubble-servicing mission. (Isaacman also purchased and commanded two crewed SpaceX missions to Earth orbit, which flew in September 2021 and September 2024.) NASA declined, however, citing possible risks to the healthy observatory.

But things have changed, apparently. Isaacman, who in December 2025 became NASA administrator, said in an Oct. 30 agency statement that Hubble's science return means the agency should "be looking at every reasonable opportunity to keep it delivering for as long as possible" — especially if doing so would be a cost savings compared with launching another mission.

In an Oct. 30 post on X, Isaacman added that it's "still worth exploring the options" and noted that NASA had previously considered "a far lower-cost solution" to keep the observatory flying. "That was then. Still worth the effort — Hubble deserves it," he added.

The new RFI also allows for alternative scenarios, however, which could include a "safe, controlled reentry into Earth's atmosphere or orbital disposal at the end of Hubble's mission."

Hubble will naturally enter the atmosphere in 2033 or 2034 due to orbital drag, so any attempt to service it would have to be in 2032, the agency stated.

Earlier this year, NASA worked with Katalyst Space Technologies on a $30 million mission that aimed to boost the orbit of the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory, which is also falling back to Earth due to orbital drag.

That long-shot rescue mission — which was developed quickly to race against accelerated solar activity, which ballooned Earth's atmosphere and increased drag — launched successfully this summer. But control issues prevented Katalyst's LINK spacecraft from meeting up with Swift. Swift is expected to reenter Earth's atmosphere tomorrow (Nov. 4) after 22 years of operations — far past its two-year design lifetime.

What My Job Is Like as a Fact Checker at Bon Appétit

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It takes a village to publish a magazine—and website and app and Youtube channel and more. Phew! Today we’re introducing you to Bon Appétit’s research director Ryan Harrington.

For the non-journalists out there: What does research and fact-checking actually mean?

At Bon Appétit, every story or recipe goes through multiple edits. After that, it’s eligible for research review. At that stage, there are a few major things me and my team looks out for:

Information accuracy: This includes checking the spelling of names and brands, making sure descriptions of restaurant menus and dishes are up to date, reviewing the credibility of the sources we point to, verifying portrayals of quoted individuals, and anything else to make sure that when you read a Bon Appétit story, you know it’s true.

Cultural sensitivity: For a long time, food media has been dominated by a certain privileged perspective. This perpetuated a system that sort of relived the Age of Explorers, with people “discovering” new foods and pigeonholing or exotifying other cultures. A big part of research is honoring different experiences—giving people a platform without making it a spectacle.

Legal concerns: The research department is a bridge between our actual legal team and the editorial team. We keep an eye out for things like libel issues, copyright infringement, food safety risks, protecting sources, or anything else that might need to be discussed with lawyers.

And for the aspiring journalists: How do you become a fact checker? What did you do before joining the Bon Appétit team?

I finished undergrad with a bachelor’s degree in chemistry and then spent six years managing operations of a thoracic surgery laboratory, studying forms of lung cancer. I reached a point in that career where I was asked to go back to school for a master’s or PhD. I realized if I were to invest all of my time in further education, I wanted to study something I felt really strongly about. And that was food media.

I entered the NYU Food Studies master’s program with the goal of studying the cultural impacts of food media. Early on, I got an opportunity to intern at Food & Wine. During my time there, I was asked to help with other projects beyond my internship, and that’s when I started thinking about food media not as just an academic subject but as a viable career. I eventually started working for other publications like Travel & Leisure and Lucky Peach (r.i.p.).

I also tried working at a brewery, though I had started there in February 2020. Dealing with the development of COVID was maybe not the best time to dip my toe in the industry, but I did get a better understanding of the pandemic’s impact.

When I saw the research director position at BA, I knew my previous experiences and education gave me a strong toolset to lead research efforts, and I am grateful for the opportunity. I’ve now been here five years.

You also teach food studies at NYU. How does that inform your work at Bon Appetit?

Whether I am building a lesson plan or reviewing a story for BA, I always try to understand things from the perspective of the recipient: the students or readers. Are we conveying the story clearly? What are they taking away from it? Guiding people to those lightbulb moments, from the classroom to the newsroom is why I love doing what I do.

It’s so cool that you started a research fellowship program here, so students can get real-world experience in media. Tell us more about that.

As mentioned, I got my start in the industry thanks to an internship. I never had formal journalism or media training, but it was thanks to that program that I could build relevant experience and make connections that opened up a chain of other opportunities. When I started at BA, it was my top priority in being able to pay it forward and help mentor a new generation of passionate food lovers.

I teach them the skills to be a great research editor and also try to give each fellow the freedom to pursue their own interests and network while here. Some fellows have spent time in our Test Kitchen, seeing how we develop and edit recipes. Some have pitched and written great stories and published them on our site. Some have helped our photo team and learned what it means to style a recipe for a shoot. Every fellow has different ambitions and I want them to get the most out of their time working with us and further their own goals.

What are your favorite types of stories to work on here at Bon Appétit? What are the most challenging?

I grew up watching Alton Brown’s Good Eats. Forever a scientist at heart, this influence had a life-long impact, nurturing a desire to understand not just the “how” but the “why” behind cooking, while simultaneously emphasizing creativity, ingenuity, and understanding. So when we take a deep, explanatory dive in processes, like our video on how Dr. Arielle Johnson makes a chocolate bar, that’s where I really feel like I am in my element. (Fun fact: Arielle and I were chemistry lab partners during our freshman year of college at NYU.)

The biggest challenge for me is when we cover wine. It has a lot of specific terminology, strict regulations across international borders to keep straight, and foreign spellings with diacritics that I have to be mindful of. Thankfully, I have built a contact list of passionate and educated people on the subject that I can consult when I inevitably have questions.

What’s the funniest rabbit hole you fell down on a BA research assignment?

We ran a story featuring Moe Girton and her bar Gossip Grill back in 2021. During her interview, she discussed the lesbian bar scene in San Diego when she first moved there in the late ’90s. There was a note from the author of the story to me: “note to fact-checkers to verify the names [of these bars].”

Of the three bars she mentioned, two were relatively easy to track down and confirm. But that third one did not have much of a digital footprint. I was looking through local newspaper archives, old community forums and message boards, and anywhere else that people might have left mention of this bar. Though I found some clues, sometimes pieces contradicted each other, and eventually I reached back out to Moe to see if she could give me any more info.

While she couldn’t remember too many specifics, I was able to get the cross streets of where she thought it was located. So I called businesses in that area, one by one, to see if: 1) They were around in the late ’90s and 2) They remembered a neighborhood lesbian bar nearby. A long shot, but as luck would have it, I eventually spoke with the owner of George’s Camera, a photography store that has been in business since 1965. Right next door to him used to be Patti’s Bar, a business he described as a “lesbian and biker friendly bar.” And the best part? He happened to have a photo of their storefront from back then, signs and all.

What’s the Bon Appétit recipe you make most often?

I absolutely love Shilpa Uskokovic’s French Onion Tart. It always impresses guests because it seems fancy and labor-intensive, but in reality, it’s so hands-off that I can be doing any number of other tasks (or taking it easy!) while it comes together. The deliciousness-to-effort ROI is through the roof. And two sheets of [puff pastry](https://www.bonappetit.com/recipes/slideshow/puff-pastry-recipe-ideas) in the box means one for the party, one for breakfast the next morning all to myself.

What’s your favorite obscure food trivia?

I am a product of my time and have spent more than my fair share in debates on “What is a sandwich?” But did you know, in New York, “sandwiches are generally subject to sales tax.” And so, the NY Department of Taxation and Finance has a whole page dedicated to what they consider a sandwich. Spoiler alert: They take a pretty broad stance. Hot dog? Sandwich. Burrito? Sandwich. A freaking buttered bagel? Sandwich.

Other parts of the country hold to a tighter definition. In 2006, the Worcester Superior Court of Massachusetts ruled that a burrito is not a sandwich. When a Qdoba opened in White City Shopping Center, an already present Panera Bread tried to enforce a clause in their lease with the mall that barred any competing sandwich shops from opening. After expert testimony arguing the sides of this case, Judge Jeffrey Locke concluded that a burrito is not a sandwich, allowing Qdoba to operate near Panera without violating the lease.

Maybe there isn’t a perfect answer to this question. Maybe the sandwich was really the friends we made along the way.

Can we convince you to apply to Jeopardy?

The great thing about this job is that I don’t need to know the answer to everything. I just need to know how to find who could answer it. And I don’t think Ken Jennings will let me use Google during filming.

How does AI intersect with or influence your current work?

AI is a powerful tool that can synthesize large volumes of data. But in the world of factual accuracy, there are all sorts of caveats and concerns with the content it generates. It’s still too early to safely adopt blanket applications of AI, but there are ways that it can be useful. Early in my career, when I was the bottom rung of the editorial team, I was often asked to transcribe other reporters’ interview recordings. An hour-long interview could easily take two or three times as long to transcribe by hand with my amateur skills. AI transcription services are far from perfect—and I still need to listen to the recording while reading to verify and fix portions—but it is a huge time saver.

What kitchen tool (or gadget or appliance) are you obsessed with right now?

I love my immersion blender! It can deal with so many tasks in my lovely but small NYC kitchen without needing any valuable countertop real estate. Pasta sauce, soups, salsa, smoothies, it pureés it all and doesn’t even risk any spillage when trying to move things from pot to pitcher and back to pot again.

Special shout out to chef Frank Proto’s tip to use a pair of tongs to juice citrus fruits too. Used this one recently while on vacation in the rental home’s bare-bones kitchen. From marinades to margaritas, we needed a lot of fresh squeezed juice and this trick really made things easier.

The movie or TV show you’d rewatch until the end of time?

One of my favorite shows is Happy Endings, a modestly successful sit-com that ran on ABC from 2011–2013. It’s a Seinfeld template of a group of friends navigating urban young adulthood. And like Seinfeld, it has characters that are acutely aware of the social games that they must struggle through to be a part of a functioning society.

I actually wrote an academic paper discussing how Happy Endings explores food and gender through its use (and sometimes subversion) of the big eater trope. It is performed across two characters of different genders and sexuality and represents a convergent idea of exactly that—performance. Society has expectations that can seem limiting or at least arbitrary. But when we see these characters act in ways that challenge these social conventions, it is encouragement for us to be able to do so as well.

The filing’s detailed token count is valuable precisely because it can be compared with later disclosures. It also exposes a blind spot in quick “ETH treasury” comparisons: two companies with identical token balances could have very different liquidity, financing and operational risk. Staking arrangements are an essential part of that distinction.

BitMine Immersion Technologies reported holdings of 6,060,506 ETH as of November 1 in a November 2 filing exhibit. The company said 5,227,037 ETH were staked, alongside smaller Bitcoin holdings, cash and other positions. These are issuer-disclosed balances at a specified date, not independently observed live holdings or a claim about Ethereum’s total currently liquid supply. The dated original disclosure is the starting point for the figures and product scope discussed here.

Staking means committing ETH to help secure Ethereum’s proof-of-stake network through validators or staking arrangements. Rewards depend on protocol conditions and operating choices, while access to staked assets can involve withdrawal queues or third-party arrangements. A substantial staked position may produce ongoing reward income, but it is not equivalent to holding the same amount in immediately transferable cash.

BitMine’s size makes its liquidity choices important. A large corporate ETH owner may hold a long-term position, but the market will still examine potential issuance, borrowing, staking income and the ability to rebalance. The issuer says its holdings represent a notable share of ETH’s supply; the comparison is a company calculation and should be tied to the supply and price assumptions used.

Ethereum has multiple sources of demand and risk: trading, staking, stablecoin settlement, application fees and long-term treasury allocation. A change in one part of that system should be tested against the others instead of promoted into a universal explanation. The market often overreacts when one publicly available metric is mistaken for the whole network economy.

For additional perspective, it is worth reading our existing reporting on BitMine’s earlier ETH accumulation and staking disclosure and our Ethereum market reporting. Those background reports help separate established facts from a new, still-developing announcement.

Cryptocurrency balance sheets are sensitive to valuation dates. A dollar figure derived from the company’s ETH price assumption can change materially without any coins moving. Staking rewards should also be separated from share-price performance and company operating costs. A large token position does not by itself tell shareholders whether their ownership is increasing or being diluted.

For investors, the right comparison is between liquidity, expected holding period and obligations. Staked ETH, ETF shares and treasury holdings may all track the same underlying asset, but their exit routes and operating constraints differ. A useful report therefore asks not simply how much ETH is involved, but how readily the exposure can be changed when conditions deteriorate.

Look for future reports distinguishing unstaked liquidity, locked or queued balances, reward income, cash uses and equity issuance. Those measures will tell investors more about treasury resilience than the headline ETH number alone. For Ethereum, the interesting network question is whether institutional-sized staking concentrations affect how observers assess operator diversity and risk.

If you're an aspiring science fiction novelist, you would do well to take the advice of author Andy Weir, who's red hot in publication and Hollywood thanks to the runaway successes of "The Martian" and "Project Hail Mary." That the former was self-published before getting turned into a bestseller and a blockbuster film directed by Ridley Scott — one that starred Matt Damon and earned seven Academy Award nominations, including a nod for Best Picture — is the dream of every up-and-coming writer.

So, genre authors have good reason to hang on Weir's every word, especially when it comes to capturing his profitable vibe. And while I'm a firm believer in finding your own vibe, there's a time-honored tradition in this industry of proving you can write comfortably within established formulae. With Weir, it's about straddling the line between hard science and uplifting, humanistic engagement with a vast universe that needn't be as harsh as we've been led to think. We can always, as Damon's character in "The Martian" says, "science the s*** out of" our various predicaments. I think that's naively hopeful in an age where the United States government is vehemently anti-science (unless it lines the pockets of AI-shilling tech billionaires), but Weir's got that storytelling magic that makes you want to believe we're not too far gone.

Weir is but one man, though, so if you're wondering where you can find his vibe in other work, he shared some suggestions with /Film's Jeremy Mathai, starting with his go-to recommendation:

"Well, if you consider an Andy Weir vibe to be just hard science fiction, with accurate science in there, then you got to go for [James S.A. Corey's] 'The Expanse.' That's really, really good hard sci-fi. That's really solid."

"The Expanse," for those not familiar, deals with a relatively realistic colonization of our solar system that does not initially involve interstellar travel. There's literally a lot of space to fill, but it's subject to the kinds of territorial disputes that have sparked wars on Earth. Ultimately, the narrative expands, bringing in aliens and the like, but, in its television incarnation, Mark Fergus and Hawk Ostby (who brilliantly adapted P.D. James' "The Children of Men" in 2006) approach the scenario with intellectual rigor.

Andy Weir also voiced enthusiasm for Blake Crouch's "Dark Matter," a frantically paced sci-fi novel that's been successfully adapted into an Apple TV series via the author himself. The small screen version of Crouch's parallel universe thriller stars Joel Edgerton and Jennifer Connelly, and it finds the former's character struggling to skip back to his reality (where his family is being appropriated by an alternate universe version of himself).

Finally, Weir is a proponent of Ben Bova's "The Grand Tour" novels, most notably the fourth entry in the book series titled "Mars." As Weir explained:

"That's a fairly hard sci-fi approach to a mission to Mars. It's about the first mission to Mars. It's got some pretty interesting twists in there that turn out to be ... at first, they look a little bit woo-woo, aliens, oh no. Then you find out the twist reveal of what's going wrong, and the thing is absolutely scientific and completely sensible and you're like, 'Ah, nice one.'"

Again, I'd advise you to find your own vibe, but if you want to catch the eye of publishers or studio execs, maybe check these works out and find your way forward the Weir way.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Botox loyalists claim something is amiss with their favorite injectable. The neurotoxin, which has been the go-to treatment to reduce the appearance of fine lines, is supposed to last three to four months. But some allege online that their Botox isn’t working as well—at least as well as it used to work.

From accusing providers of diluting solutions to get you to spend more on treatments to people believing they are now immune to Botox altogether, the theories are conspiracy-level complicated. But before anyone freaks out and cancels their next appointment, read what the experts have to say on what’s really going on with Botox.

The good news is that Botox itself hasn’t suddenly stopped working. As New York–based board-certified dermatologist Hadley King, MD, explains, because Botox arrives at a practice as a freeze-dried powder and is turned into a liquid with sterile saline before injection, there isn’t one universal correct way to dilute it. Different injectors use different dilution volumes based on their technique and treatment goals.

King says it’s more important to pay attention to the amount of units you’re receiving and not the concentration of the saline; the units you’re receiving determine how long it will last. “Twenty units is still 20 units whether it’s delivered in a smaller or larger volume,” she says.

In reality, there are many reasons why your Botox isn’t lasting as long as you’d like it to. Some common ones include:

King says that under-dosing is simply the biggest reason. “Many people are requesting a softer, more natural look (sometimes called baby Botox), which intentionally uses fewer units, [and] less product generally means a shorter duration of effect,” she explains.

Brian J. Abittan, MD, director of skin and hair rejuvenation and hair transplantation at the Kimberly and Eric J. Waldman Department of Dermatology at Mount Sinai, adds that patients want more movement in their faces and less of a “frozen” look, which can sometimes result in lighter doses that wear off faster.

King explains that people with very strong facial muscles (men, athletes, those who have very expressive faces, etc.) often metabolize Botox sooner because their muscles are more powerful.

How someone is going to respond to Botox varies from person to person. King says some patients may experience their Botox lasting three to four months, while others may only get to two to three months despite getting the same dose and treatment. It really just depends.

“Precise placement matters,” says King. “Even with the correct dose, injections that don’t optimally target the muscle can reduce the duration of effect.”

The type of practice where you get your Botox done also matters. If you’re going to a licensed professional, such as a board-certified dermatologist or plastic surgeon’s office, the risk of getting counterfeit Botox or improper injection techniques are slim to none. “Those situations are uncommon in reputable medical practices,” she says. “But [they] highlight why it’s important to seek treatment from qualified providers.”

King lists frequent intense exercise, high muscle activity, and a rapid metabolism as things that may make it seem like your Botox isn’t lasting as long as it used to. But she says evidence is mixed and these effects are generally modest.

For most patients, King says they don’t become tolerant to Botox. In fact, she says many long-term patients may find that they can sometimes maintain results with similar (or even go with slightly fewer) units because repeatedly relaxing the muscle may reduce its strength over time. She also points out that first-time patients might notice movement returning earlier than patients who have been getting treatment consistently over time.

There is a small subset of patients who do develop these neutralizing antibodies against Botox, she says, which will make treatments less effective. But these are usually seen in those who receive high doses for neurologic conditions, get frequent touchups, and get injections at intervals shorter than 12 weeks. Though possible, true resistance from Botox is uncommon. “An experienced injector will first rule out these more common explanations,” she says.

Many have already been feeling the Botox and filler fatigue and are looking to other procedures (XERF, facelifts, etc.) to fill other antiaging needs. Abittan says it’s important to pay attention to what each treatment actually does, as they each address different issues. Lasers, for example, can help with skin tightening, improve pigmentation or texture, and address wrinkles or lines at rest. Whereas neuromodulators like Botox work on movement-related wrinkles, he says.

“These treatments are complementary rather than competitive,” adds King. “The modern approach to facial rejuvenation is increasingly individualized and multimodal. Rather than replacing Botox, newer technologies give physicians more tools to address the specific cause of each patient’s aging concerns.”

And if you’re still unhappy with how long your Botox is lasting, consider a different product. She lists Dysport, Xeomin, Jeuveau, Daxxify, and Letybo as some options that are known to offer similar but longer-lasting results. Just be sure to go to a licensed professional before injecting anything new into your face.

In the risk factors section, Apple expanded its warnings about artificial intelligence. Apple’s caution underscores the risks tech giants and other companies are projecting after a string of AI safety incidents in recent months, which have prompted companies to release new security features and debate regulatory measures.

In its 10-K filing, which is made each year following the end of its fiscal year, Apple said “artificial intelligence agents and other technologies can take actions autonomously, including interacting with internal or external systems that in some cases they may not be authorized to access, executing or altering code, sending communications, and completing transactions.”

Such technologies, the iPhone maker said, “may behave in unintended or unpredictable ways, including by taking actions that were not directed, instructed or authorized, exceeding permissions, defying directions or resisting instructions.” And it noted that autonomous AI agents that go rogue could “conceal actions, hide, withhold or manipulate information, or generate inaccurate or deceptive outputs.”

Apple is the latest to warn about risks posed by AI agents. Google parent Alphabet also pointed out the risks posed by rogue agents in a recent filing. Google, however, was more pointed about the potential consequences, writing that during AI model testing, it had observed unexpected behavior, including agents taking actions outside their intended scope, which could ultimately lead to “severe or catastrophic security and societal incidents, or negatively affect human rights.”

The risks outlined by companies follow several major cybersecurity incidents involving AI models, most notably this summer’s attack by OpenAI agents on AI company Hugging Face. In Anthropic’s IPO prospectus, made public last week, the company listed many risk factors, including incidents in which its models gained unauthorized access to real-world systems during security testing. It also estimated that 5% to 7% of its revenue this year came from customers later found to be violating its usage policies.

Executives at Microsoft and other major tech companies have called for a greater focus on securing AI agents. Microsoft recently announced a set of controls to detect, monitor, and restrict AI agents, including cutting their permissions or stopping them if they behave suspiciously. Chief executive Satya Nadella has argued that companies should treat agents as potential insider risks and put security controls outside the AI model rather than trusting the model to police itself.

In its 10-K filing on Tuesday, Apple also outlined other pressures, including memory and storage component shortages that it said will intensify. It also said demand for cloud computing, storage, and other AI infrastructure has produced “constrained supply, extended lead times, and increasing costs” that could affect its AI rollout. Its research and development spending rose 32% year over year, driven by higher infrastructure-related costs, including AI investments.

On Monday, Apple chief executive John Ternus described agentic capabilities as “promising tools for users” but cautioned that users must be fully aware of what access they grant, and he stressed user security and privacy.

On Location: How Coastal Newfoundland Set the Scene for Netflix's No. 1 Series ‘Below’

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A mysterious sea creature is captivating audiences on Netflix as the Josh Hartnett-led series Below remains the No. 1 show in Netflix’s global rankings. The six-episode thriller paints an eerie image of small-town life in Newfoundland, and viewers are intrigued by the mystique of the striking Canadian backdrop.

Despite the fictional giant squid terrorizing Snook’s Arm, the show pulls on elements of reality, from climate change affecting the Atlantic Canadian sea life to the government-run resettling of remote fishing towns, which started in 1954 and continues to this day. It’s that history that drew production designer Emmanuel Fréchette into the project.

“When I found out that more than 300 towns in Newfoundland had been resettled, I was amazed,” he tells Traveler. “The script is very interesting, mixing that reality with the mundane life in Newfoundland—and this creature!” After all, creator Jesse McKeown (The Umbrella Academy) had frequented the region for years, inspired to bring an authentic depiction of its culture to the screen.

That started behind the scenes as the cast and crew engaged in Newfoundland and Labrador’s most famous tradition. Visitors, who they called CFAs (or Come from Aways—this is also where Broadway hit musical draws its name), get “screeched in” by kissing a cod to become honorary citizens. So they all went to St. John’s Christian’s Pub to pucker up. “The guy there is such a great character, and he has a routine and speech that’s very entertaining," Fréchette says.

When it came to filming locations, the remote regions posed practical challenges, so the majority of the sets were built by production, a joy for Fréchette, who was trained as an architect. “I was stunned by the humble architecture, built by boat owners and builders,” he says of the homes’ low ceilings. “The structures are very endearing and make you think of a boat, so I wanted to follow that thread.”

That’s also what helped root the fantastical storyline into reality. “This was vernacular architecture—architecture without architects—just following the needs of the people out of survival, and I found that so beautiful,” he says. “So I really tried to mimic what I was seeing all around me that survived the decades.”

And it’s clearly had an effect on audiences. According to data provided by Booking.com, not only have searches for the province’s capital of St. John’s risen 16.9% by Americans on the platform compared to last year, but the coastal town of Twillingate, home to the floating icebergs, has skyrocketed 123.7%.

Here, Fréchette shares the filming locations from the popular Netflix series Below.

The show is set in the actual resettled town of Snook’s Arm, which had a population of 10 when it was approved for resettlement in 2017, according to the CBC. But its remote location would have been difficult to film in with such a big crew, so Brigus South—a town with a population of about 700 at last count in 2021—stood in.

“We picked that place because it had a connection with the water,” Fréchette says of the inlet going deep into the land. “Lots of the characters are dragged into the water, so it was great to have this presence for the eeriness.”

But to get the cozy town ready for its close-up took work, as the crew designed and built about 80% of what appears in the series, including the fish plant, Fishermen’s Hall, and wharf. The Penneys home was an existing home they covered, and the church was also a house that they retrofitted, adding the bell tower.

When Fréchette was scouting locations, he stayed in a hostel by the sea in Twillingate, about 275 miles northwest of the capital. “We’d be having a coffee with the window open, and suddenly we were freezing,” he recalls of a sudden drop in temperature from 68 degrees Fahrenheit to 50 degrees Fahrenheit. That’s when they’d look up and see an iceberg floating their way. “It lowers the temperature all around the bay—it’s such an amazing phenomenon.”

As it turns out, the Atlantic town sits along Iceberg Alley, where from late April through July, the remnants of broken glaciers glide down the coast. Many of the shots of Calvin (Hartnett) harvesting icebergs were actual footage from these massive blocks of ice naturally floating along.

The capital served as the home base for the cast and crew, who were spread out across hotels and home rentals during filming. But its Ocean Sciences Centre, about 30 minutes north of downtown, found the spotlight as one of the most striking structures in the series, the marine research center where Dr. Fonda Howander (Mackenzie Davis) worked.

While Fréchette admits it took some work to convince the operating facility to shoot there, he’s excited it worked out because “it really adds production value and is a great location.” After all, the circular wooden structure reflects that government building architect of the 1960s and early 1970s, “at a time we were dreaming and imagining things beyond what was possible to build today.” Reminiscent of designs by Canadian architects like Arthur Erikson, he adds that “it was ambitious, but it had a vision—and is fantastic!”

About 20 miles north of St. John’s at the top of the Avalon Peninsula sits Pouch Cove, a town of about 2,000 residents. The art director told Fréchette about the Pouch Cove Heritage Society, a historical building that was closed since they were fundraising to preserve it, so one weekend, they went up to visit. “The floor was sunken in and it was structurally unsafe,” he says.

The show’s head carpenter and engineer got to work, fixing it up, temporarily transforming it into the interior of Below’s Fisherman’s Hall, while permanently restoring it for the community. “It was a win-win because they were happy to get the money from the location, but also the work we did on it was helpful for them.”

When Calvin returns to his abandoned childhood town of Little Pot Rock, that dilapidated setting was an actual resettled town, about a 30-minute drive down from Pouch Cove on a dirt road that the production had to grade in order to shoot there.

“It was quite tricky, but just stunning—really wild and rugged,” Fréchette says, calling it “the most amazing location.” All that was there was one tiny house, about 12 by 20 feet, but it never showed up on camera. Instead, the production custom built all of the Budgell family’s dwellings. But the area can be visited as part of a 4.3-mile hike along the East Coast Trail.

The fishing village of Bay Bulls is home to North America’s largest Atlantic puffin colony, as well as a prime whale watching destination. But for Below, it was all about the functional purpose of its Pennecon Marine Base. Though it never shows up on screen, its industrial harbor was essential in order to dock the boat used as the research vessel since it had a draft (depth) of nearly 10 feet, and they needed an appropriate spot that the cast and crew could board. Also shot here were the exterior scenes of the submersible, which was hung from the boat by a crane and lifted onto the dock.

Pittman’s fish farm played an integral part of the plot, as Calvin confirmed that the sea creature was real—but it turns out the location was landlocked. “It was very complicated to do this on the sea because we’d have to deal with tides and storms, so we decided to cheat it on a lake in Brigus,” he says of the town that’s actually about 60 miles northwest from Brigus South. There was no actual fish farm there, so they custom-built the floating dock and cabin.

If you’d told me even a month ago we’d get significant spoilers for The Batman: Part II, the next Robert Pattinson-led Batman movie, in a photo Heidi Klum posted for her annual Halloween costume, I would find that both weirdly specific and entirely unbelievable.

And yet, that looks to be exactly what happened this past weekend when prosthetics supplier The Makeup Armoury posted BTS photos of the work prosthetics expert Mike Marino (who also worked to transform Colin Farrell into The Penguin for the first film and subsequent HBO Max series) was doing for Klum. If you’ve somehow been off-grid for the last few years, you may have missed that the famed supermodel takes Halloween incredibly seriously, frequently donning costumes with extensive makeup and designs that make her unrecognizable. This year was no exception, but it might have gone largely unnoticed had that photo not accidentally revealed clues about the upcoming superhero sequel, which is currently in production with an expected release date of February 18th, 2028.

In the photo, Marino, who provided a helping, ahem, hand in Klum’s costume together for the year, had a number of molds lingering in the background, which have seemingly confirmed that Brian Tyree Henry will play Two-Face, Sebastian Stan will play Victor Zsasz, and that Part II will feature the famed Court of Owls.

Henry’s mold appears to show the Atlanta actor with a disfigured face that mimics the iconic villain. Some, including me, in this very outlet, have, speculated that he might be playing either Dent or Detective Harvey Bullock, a hardened Gotham City cop who frequently works alongside Jim Gordon. But this seems to confirm it. Considering writer-director Matt Reeves has cited Jeph Loeb and Tim Sale’s The Long Halloween as an influence on his version of the Caped Crusader, it’s not ultimately a huge surprise, but perhaps a slight disappointment considering Reeves had stated his intention to move the sequel into uncharted territory by featuring a villain that had “never really been done in a film before.”

Which brings us to Victor Zsasz, who Stan seems to be playing. There was some speculation when he shaved his head upon joining the production he might be playing the character, but the cast looks to confirm the speculation. While the serial killer—who carves tally marks into his body for every person he’s killed—shows up in Batman Begins as a minor enforcer in Carmine Falcone’s crew, he’s got a much larger role in the comic books themselves. In Long Halloween, Zsasz is hired by Falcone to assassinate Dent before he's intercepted by Batman. Might Reeves look to flip the script here and disfigure Henry’s Dent in the process? This interpretation of Zsasz would certainly fit the extremely grounded approach Reeves took in the first installment. But there’s also some confusion here, as Stan was rumored to be playing Harvey when casting first broke and even went so far as to say he’d be playing the role in an interview over the summer. The French publication Le Journal du Dimanche did, however, later retract his statement.

The last accidental spoiler is a big scoop for comic book fans. Simply labeled “Owl 3,” this likely confirms that the Court of Owls will finally make their silver-screen debut. Created in 2012 by writer Scott Snyder and artist Greg Capullo, the Owls are one of the biggest new additions to the Batman canon in recent memory. The Court is a clandestine criminal society made up of Gotham’s wealthiest and most influential citizens, whose power and influence stretch back to the city’s earliest days. They have immeasurable resources and frequently deploy assassins, known as Talons, to do their bidding. This tracks with the story Reeves told in The Batman of a Gotham beset by a history of corruption. Owls are also bats' natural predators; so that, too, is bad news for Battinson.

While this potential leak is exciting and unexpected, it also raises doubts about the identity of Scarlett Johansson’s character, as she was rumored to be playing Harvey’s wife, Gilda Dent. Either way, never in a million lifetimes could we have guessed that Heidi Klum would be the one to spill the beans about one of the most anticipated comic book movies of the next few years.

He Left Google to Fix a ‘Really Stupid’ Household Product. The ‘Delightful’ Solution Has Generated $20 Million in Revenue.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

You're viewing the US website. Looking for the Site edition?

Matic co-founder Mehul Nariyawala says the goal is to sell 30,000 robots by the end of the year.

For Mehul Nariyawala, a household product’s costly mistake was the jumping-off point for a $20 million company. Nariyawala had a $1,000 robot vacuum, which he watched destroy a rug in his home while he was away. The machine had powerful suction but wasn’t intelligent enough to free itself from the rug. It got stuck, kept running for 45 minutes and tore away a patch of the rug.

“That was a realization that, Wow, this thing is really stupid,” Nariyawala tells Entrepreneur in a new interview. Worse, he found that many robot vacuums could not reliably return to their own charging docks. “To me, it’s an oxymoron to call these things robots because if it’s a robot and it can’t even find its own dock, what good is it?” he says.

That irritation planted the seed for Matic, the home-robotics company Nariyawala co-founded with CEO Navneet Dalal after the pair left Google Nest in 2017. Nariyawala is Matic’s president. Their goal was not simply to make another robot vacuum. It was to build a machine that could safely and quietly navigate the disorder of an actual home, avoiding pets, toys, cords, spills, furniture and the people moving through it.

Nariyawala says Matic has sold more than 13,000 robots since its November 2024 launch, generating an estimated $15 million to $20 million in revenue. Matic’s robot vacuum costs $1,495. Nariyawala said the company employs about 110 people and is aiming to sell 30,000 robots by year-end.

Before they started building Matic, the co-founders did something less glamorous: They wrote a 21-page “problem memo.”

Instead of jumping to a solution, Nariyawala says, they asked what customers disliked about current products, why robot vacuums failed them and what a genuinely useful home robot would need to do.

“We just kept going again and again and again at every level until we deeply understood the problem,” he says.

That process eventually produced Matic’s first product, a privacy-focused robot vacuum designed around computer vision, on-device processing and a more human-like understanding of a home.

Nariyawala’s path to Matic began in India, where he grew up. He moved to the United States in high school and attended the University of Maryland for undergraduate studies. After graduating in 1999, he joined a startup based in Northern Virginia that burned through $30 million in 11 months.

“It turned out to be a spectacular failure,” he says. “That was my how-not-to-ruin-a-startup 101.”

After a stint in consulting at Accenture and an MBA from Chicago Booth, Nariyawala joined Like.com in 2005, an early Silicon Valley computer-vision startup. There, he met Dalal, a researcher and engineer whose doctoral work became influential in the field of classical computer vision.

Google acquired Like.com in 2010. Nariyawala and Dalal then formed Flutter, a gesture-recognition startup that let people control iTunes and Spotify using a webcam. Users could wave to change a song or make a shushing motion to mute audio. The app reached No. 1 in Apple’s Mac App Store in 73 countries in 2012, according to Nariyawala.

But the founders learned a crucial product lesson: A flashy interface does not necessarily create a business.

“No one wakes up in the morning and says, ‘Today I’m gonna buy gestures,’” Nariyawala says. “It’s a really cool app but not necessarily a product that can potentially generate revenue.”

Google acquired Flutter in late 2013. The founders later moved to Nest, where Nariyawala led products in the Nest camera portfolio, including the Nest Cam, Nest Hello doorbell and related devices. Dalal led perception and computer-vision work on the same set of products.

At Nest, Nariyawala learned that a product is more than its hardware or software. It encompasses every point a customer encounters the company, from learning about the product to purchasing, onboarding, receiving support and deciding whether to stay loyal to the brand or not.

“One of the biggest learnings there was that it’s really just about the entire experience of the product,” he says.

Customer support, he argues, is especially important for an expensive consumer device. “When they reach out to you, that’s the first time they’re meeting humans behind Matic,” he says. “How we respond to that is actually really, really critical.”

Matic’s founding idea grew from both professional experience and family life. While working on Nest cameras, Nariyawala noticed that many indoor cameras were pointed outside. He could personally relate. His wife had done the same thing, and after leaving Google, he says she did not want any indoor cameras monitoring their home.

“That’s when I realized that for her, privacy mattered,” he says.

The founders concluded they did not want to trade away privacy merely to automate a household chore. They wanted a robot that would process its understanding of the home locally rather than operate like an always-recording cloud-connected camera.

“As fathers and homeowners, we don’t want to jeopardize privacy just to keep our floors clean,” Nariyawala says. “Why would I invite something that is recording them and putting it out onto the cloud?”

The pair saw a broader opportunity in the gap between the robotics ambitions emerging in self-driving cars and the mediocre performance of household cleaning robots. If companies believed they could develop vehicles capable of safely navigating roads, Nariyawala wondered, why was no one making a household robot that could move through a living room without crashing into objects?

Nariyawala says that homes are among the most difficult environments for robots because they are cluttered, dynamic, and highly personal. Furniture shifts. Lighting changes. Children leave toys out. Pets cross a room. A cord may be on the floor one day and gone the next.

Matic’s answer was a vision-based system built around two RGB cameras and local computing. Rather than use the flat, circular shape common in robot vacuums, the company opted for a taller machine that gives its cameras a more useful perspective of the room.

“We wanted to build a vantage point that was much more like a human crawling child,” Nariyawala says.

That concept reflects Dalal’s broader philosophy: Do not bet against nature. Human beings learn to perceive their surroundings before they learn more complex skills, Nariyawala says. Young children learn where they can walk, what they should avoid, how to get from one room to another, and how not to fall down stairs. Matic’s early work focused on giving a robot some of those same foundational abilities.

“The indoor world is entirely built by humans for humans,” he says.

Nariyawala and Dalal did not immediately seek venture funding. Having founded companies before and seen what happens after an acquisition, they wanted to build Matic for the long haul. Their ambition extended beyond cleaning floors.

“We’re really building the Apple of home robotics,” Nariyawala says.

That long-term vision informed an unusual early decision. The cofounders self-funded Matic for roughly its first year and a half. They agreed not to raise outside capital until they had a working prototype and had addressed major technical risks.

“Raising funding is never easy, but when you have gone through an exit and you try to do a second startup, it is slightly easier,” Nariyawala says. “But because it’s slightly easier, you don’t actually spend more time thinking through and developing your idea, and that sometimes plays a catastrophic [role].”

The team focused on proving key capabilities: depth perception using two cameras, voice and gesture recognition, quieter cleaning and a wheel-base design. Its first prototype arrived in 2018. By spring 2019, it had developed an early demonstration in which a person could speak to the robot, point to a mess and tell it to clean a specific spot.

Only then did the founders begin raising money. “Venture capital funding, or institutional funding, is like rocket fuel,” Nariyawala says. “The best time to go get rocket fuel is when your rocket is ready and docked.”

In 2019, Matic attracted early investment from entrepreneurs including Jack Dorsey, Stripe cofounders Patrick and John Collison and Nest cofounder Matt Rogers.

Matic has raised $115 million in outside investment to date, with additional personal investment from the founders.

The journey took longer than planned. The founders originally thought they might ship around 2020 or 2021. Matic instead began shipping in November 2024.

In August, the U.S. Federal Communications Commission (FCC) banned foreign-made advanced robots, including robot vacuums. The ruling prohibited new foreign-made models from getting future import and sales approval.

Nariyawala says that the ban does not affect Matic and instead makes it “easier” for the company to build robots in the U.S. From day one, the company has been focused on security and privacy, he says. Their existing robot is already grandfathered in for sale in the U.S., and their next set of robots is heading in the right direction to move through the FCC ban. The company is determined to source its components in the U.S.

“We cannot guarantee the security and privacy of the robot unless we assemble it in the United States,” Nariyawala says. “So for us, designing and assembling it here and putting all the software here was not an optional thing. That was something we always wanted to do, and we did that from 2017 onwards.”

Nariyawala says he reached out to the FCC after the ban was announced and showed them a path to how Matic will source as many components in the U.S. as possible moving forward.

“The FCC and government have been very much accommodating and understanding what we’re doing,” Nariyawala says. “Their entire point is that yes, you guys are heading in the right direction. So as long as it’s heading in the right direction, we’re supportive of it. We just have to show that we’re pushing in that direction.”

Matic now positions its vacuum as an intelligent, autonomous machine rather than a device that follows a fixed or random cleaning routine. Nariyawala contrasts that vision with early robot vacuums, which he compares to a blindfolded person bouncing from wall to wall.

“Hardware is hard,” Nariyawala says. “If hardware is 10 times harder than software, then building a robot is 100 times harder than software.”

Many current robot vacuums use a form of LiDAR sensing, he says, which can help a machine detect walls and certain obstacles but does not provide enough context to distinguish among a person, a cabinet, a pet, or a pile of clothes.

“It has no contextual awareness,” he says.

Matic’s system is meant to create a three-dimensional, evolving understanding of a home. Nariyawala describes this as an “absolute map,” rather than a map that only orients the robot relative to its charging dock. The distinction matters, he says, because a robot should be able to understand its environment even if the dock is moved.

Matic recently introduced “Cues,” an update enabling users to communicate with the robot through voice commands and gestures. Through the update, the robot can understand more than 70 spoken languages, and users can point to an area they want cleaned to have the robot take care of a mess.

The robot has an accompanying app that remains necessary for onboarding and detailed settings, such as setting no-go zones, but the company wants routine interaction to feel more natural.

“If this is truly an intelligent robot, then we should be able to communicate with it in the same exact way that we communicate with each other,” Nariyawala says.

The approach is designed for households that include children, older adults, people who do not want to use another app and family members who may not speak English — personalized experiences for different users.

Matic has focused less on traditional marketing than on getting customers to talk about the product. Nariyawala says roughly two-thirds of orders have come through word-of-mouth referrals, and the company did little marketing before this year.

“The only evergreen marketing channel is a great product,” he says. “When you build something really unexpected, delightful that customers love, they can’t help talking about it.”

For Nariyawala, Matic’s floor-cleaning robot is only the first stage. The company’s roadmap imagines a “trusted organizer” that can handle simpler tasks, followed by a more capable household robot that can take on complex planning and execution.

But he returns repeatedly to the same principle that shaped the company’s original 21-page memo: The technology is not the product. The customer’s problem is.

“Customers don’t want hardware or robots,” he says. “They want a solution to their problems.”

It’s been 27 years—over a quarter century--since the Columbine High School massacre ignited the worldwide war against bullying. Yet many nations that enthusiastically embrace this war discover that bullying becomes a more serious problem.

South Korea is one such country. It has made bullying a major cultural issue, producing a large number of popular “K-dramas” (Korean TV series) depicting the brutality of bullying, including the recent Netflix hit, Teach You a Lesson. Despite this massive anti-bullying activism (or perhaps because of it?), a 2025 survey found that South Korea's violence victimization rate in elementary school hit a record high.

A basic tenet of antibullyism is that bullying must not be tolerated and needs to be met with tough and consistent punishments, or “consequences.” Make the price of bullying too high, it is assumed, and any bully’s appetite to cause people pain will evaporate. Although it has become widely recognized that punishment is not an effective response to bullying,1 the belief in punishment continues unimpeded, probably because it seems intuitively correct to the average person, and perhaps because nothing else bullying researchers have presented seems to work reliably.

Thus, South Korea has adopted what sounds like a tough but logical consequence for bullying. Students with bullying on their records are disqualified from acceptance into public universities. This punishment causes a huge financial loss for students and can seriously impact their future. Given the importance Koreans place on higher education, certainly such a policy must be an effective deterrence to bullying, right?

Not if we consider human nature. Let’s say you get a traffic ticket. When will you fight it harder? When the fine is $50, with no points against your driver's license, or $50,000, and your license gets suspended? Well, the same thing is true if the huge punishments are for bullying violations, as recent news stories from South Korea confirm.

Before we continue, it's essential to recognize that the bullying that typically occurs in Korean schools does not resemble the bloodcurdling cruelty that is depicted in the K-dramas. Such behavior should result in incarceration. As in many other advanced nations, bullying in reality consists of any behaviors that can cause someone to get upset.

Students don’t suddenly become nicer because anti-bullying policies threaten them with severe educational penalties. Instead, they—their parents, really—fight back. An increasingly popular way is to countersue the educational system. As the title of one article in the Korean JoongAng Daily informs us, As consequences of bullying records grow, perpetrators turn to countersuits for leverage. It reveals the nightmare unleashed by these Korean laws. These lawsuits typically last years and cost tens of thousands of dollars.

To help the cover the expense, a growing number of parents in Korea are purchasing bullying insurance policies, as described in another article titled, As school violence becomes legal issue, insurers cash in. :

The number of insurance payouts related to school violence among five major nonlife insurers… surged from 231 cases in 2021 to 1,297 in 2022, 3,388 in 2023 and 3,443 in 2025. This represents a nearly fifteenfold rise in just five years.... The increasing pace has accelerated further this year, with 2,056 cases already recorded in January and February alone.

Furthermore, these lawsuits are being filed even for the most minor of bullying complaints. The Korea JoongAng article presents one parent’s experience with a situation that hardly meets the criteria of bullying:

One parent…started looking into a school violence insurance program after their fifth-grader was mired in a 25 million won ($16,580) lawsuit for repeatedly asking to borrow items and was accused of “having a look” at another student in their school.

The local education office concluded that no action was necessary, but the other student’s parent did not accept the decision and filed a damage lawsuit. The court dismissed the case in full last month.

After going through both the committee process and a civil lawsuit, the parent faced significant psychological and financial burdens, leading them to seek insurance coverage.

The purpose of anti-bullying laws is to reduce the misery caused by bullying. In South Korea, however, the misery of bullying is being dwarfed by the financial expense and psychological pain inflicted by prolonged, expensive lawsuits.

Hill-Climbing Agent Quality with Agents CLI Evaluation

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A live walkthrough of the Agents CLI eval loop, from a first run to regression tracking, model-backend experiments, and automated alerting

Building an agent that works on a demo is a different problem from building an agent that stays working across model upgrades, tool changes, and new conversation shapes. The first takes a weekend; the second takes a loop. A team writes test cases, runs them, grades them, reads what failed, changes the agent, and runs the cases again. If the score goes up, the change was good. If it goes down, revert. Over weeks of this, the agent inches toward the ceiling of what the model, tools, and prompt can do together. The quality-engineering name for this is hill-climbing.

The Agents CLI eval namespace was built around this loop. The commands are small and framework-agnostic: eval generate runs the agent against a dataset of cases, eval grade scores the generated traces, eval compare diffs two run results side by side, eval analyze clusters failures into themes, and eval optimize runs an automated hill-climbing pass on the agent's prompt.

Four cases: a greeting, a weather question the mock tool handles with the hardcoded foggy response, a weather question for a city the tool defaults on, and an off-topic question that the agent has to decide how to handle. This is a small dataset by design; the hill-climbing loop pays off when the dataset is small enough to run on every change.

The mean drop by itself (−1.25) is a clear signal, but the per-case breakdown is what tells the developer where to look. A one-line system-prompt change propagated through three different cases in three different ways: greeting lost a point on tone, sf_weather lost two on tool usage, off_topic lost two on scope creep.

Two caveats on this experiment. First, model swaps interact with the prompt: a prompt tuned for a stronger model often leaks capability when ported to a weaker one, so the right comparison is "weaker model with a prompt tuned for it" versus "stronger model with its own tuned prompt." Second, the Vertex AI Eval Service calls a judge model to grade the traces, and the judge is held constant across the comparison. Changing the judge is a different experiment and should be done separately.

Agents CLI does not ship a scheduler, but the eval commands are designed to drop into standard infrastructure. The pattern we can use here is a cron job or Cloud Scheduler trigger that runs eval generate plus eval grade on a cadence and writes the results to a persistent location:

A nightly run against a stable regression dataset catches drift from model updates, data changes in the agent's tools, and dependencies that shift under the agent's feet. For a production agent, the scheduled run also captures the model vendor's quiet upgrades, which can move the agent's behavior without any change on our side. The dataset for the scheduled run is typically larger than the one used in the inner loop (dozens to hundreds of cases rather than a few), since it only runs once a day.

The scheduled run produces a result file every day, but a file nobody looks at is not an alert. The pattern here can be a wrapper script that compares each new run's mean against a baseline (the previous run, or a committed known-good) and exits non-zero when the drop exceeds a threshold. The non-zero exit is what triggers the alert from the surrounding scheduler.

For ADK projects, eval optimize runs an automated hill-climbing pass on the agent's prompt using the GEPA framework (not currently supported for the LangChain extension). The command takes a dataset and a target metric, proposes prompt variants, grades each one, keeps the variants that improve the metric, and iterates. The output is a set of candidate prompts ranked by their eval score, which a human then reviews and promotes to the agent definition.

Two things to understand about eval optimize. First, it is scoped to prompt optimization, not to arbitrary agent changes: the agent's tools, structure, and model stay fixed. Second, GEPA sometimes finds genuine improvements and sometimes finds prompt variants that score higher on the specific metric by overfitting to its wording. The reviewer is the control. For projects where the loop applies, the discipline is to run eval optimize on the regression dataset (where improvements are measurable) and to review the candidate prompts carefully before promotion.

A few patterns and anti-patterns from running this loop ourselves and watching teams do the same.

Lead with a small, representative dataset, not a comprehensive one. The temptation when starting an eval is to try to cover every edge case the agent will ever see. The result is a dataset that takes an hour to run and that nobody runs on a change. Four cases that run in under a minute, as above, exercise the main behaviors and get run on every change. A regression dataset of three hundred cases that runs for an hour becomes a release gate at best and shelfware at worst.

Snapshot result files, not scores. The score is a summary; the result file is the evidence. When a regression surfaces three weeks after a prompt change, having the full result JSON from before and after the change (not just the mean score) is what lets eval compare show the specific case that moved. The per-case breakdown in Step 4 above would not have been possible from scores alone.

Separate the dataset from its expectations. The dataset is the inputs. The eval config is the expectations. They evolve on different cadences: the dataset grows as new real-world cases surface in production, and the config evolves as the team's definition of "good" matures. Keeping them in separate files, both committed to version control, makes it clear which half changed when a score moves.

Treat summary metrics with suspicion. The model-swap experiment in Step 5 is the clearest example. The mean dropped by half a point, but the per-case breakdown showed the drop was concentrated in one case while the others held or improved. A team that only looked at the mean would have made the wrong call.

More evals do not automatically lead to more fixes. A recent controlled study we ran found that coding agents using agents-cli run four times as many evals as coding agents without it, but change the agent after an eval about as often as the baseline. The behavior gain comes from what the skills put into the build at the start, not from the inner iteration. Evals are necessary for quality, but the quality comes from acting on what the evals show.

Build Generative UI Apps with A2UI & CopilotKit Agents

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Agent-User Interactions are now evolving from text exchanges to rich UIs. Until recently, most conversations looked like this:

User: "Find me a holiday package to Bali in March." Agent: (six packages in prose, each with dates, hotel, flights, price) User: "The five-night one with breakfast." Agent: "Here's the link. You can book it on the operator's site."

Agents could plan the trip, but everything came back as walls of text. They had no standard way to render a selectable list of packages, a map of the destinations, or a date picker to carry you through the rest of the booking, inside the conversation itself.

Generative UI is the layer that lets agents build interfaces at runtime, shaped by what each request needs - giving users a rich, interactive experience. Implementing that takes two pieces: a way to describe the UI and an agentic stack that connects your agent to your application and renders that UI.

Generative UI covers a spectrum of approaches, from using predefined higher order components, to enabling your AI agent to have the flexibility to decide how to build the UI.

At the controlled end, you build each component ahead of time, and the agent picks which one to render and with what data. It's brand-safe but it does not allow for deep customization and personalization based on the user’s intent. At the open end, the agent returns a raw UI program written in HTML, Javascript and/or Dart that renders inside a sandboxed iframe. That covers anything you did not plan for, at the cost of a surface that drifts from your design system and executes code a model wrote.

A2UI works through the entire spectrum. It participates in controlled generation with decision models like Typesafe’s Jev and also open-ended code generation that may output A2UI JSON messages. The declarative mode, where agents assemble pre-declared components and functions, is the most popular. A2UI is the framework-agnostic standard for declaring UI intent, so local or remote agents can generate your UI from your existing component catalog using a common language.

Frontend developers can now ship the building blocks once, and the agent composes them for each request.

Cost per task: Using cheap classifiers to route across Gemini models and thinking levels

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Per-token API prices keep falling, yet engineering bills for autonomous coding agents keep climbing. Price per million tokens isn't the metric that actually governs production economics anymore. The dominating metric is now cost per successful task: total spend across every loop iteration, tool call, retry and internal thinking pass divided by the number of tasks that pass verification.

When an agent calls a high-thinking reasoning configuration for every micro-decision in its harness, you're paying deep-reasoning prices for questions a lightweight configuration already answers accurately. To prevent this unnecessary overhead, consider adding a lightweight routing layer that can intercept routine decisions before they ever reach an expensive reasoning model.That is the premise behind wiring TypeSafe's Jev classifier in front of Google Antigravity's model calls. Rather than generating text token by token, Jev—a specialized structured-output classifier designed for high-throughput decision pipelines—evaluates structured classification primitives in a single forward pass: a Choice from a list, a numeric Score or a Noul, which returns a 0 to 1 probability for a binary statement.

For full disclosure, Google has no partnership with TypeSafe, and this article is not an endorsement of TypeSafe or its products. Jev is used here as one example of a lightweight classifier, and the same routing pattern can be built with other classifiers, including Google-native ones. The Getting started with discriminative models codelab, for example, runs the same kind of classifier on DiffusionGemma, Google DeepMind's open-weights model, in your own Google Cloud project.

We ran a 50-question, auto-graded, coding-agent benchmark against live Gemini and Jev APIs to test whether a near-free classifier can route each task to the cheapest model that still gets it right. Here's what we learned:

The naive way to ask Jev to route a coding task is a single broad Choice question: simple or complex. When we tested that first on 16 coding-agent tasks, Jev routed nearly everything to simple, even tasks requiring an LRU cache trace or modular exponentiation. TypeSafe's documentation warns against this exact anti-pattern. If a question weighs multiple factors or requires a meta-judgment, decompose it into an atomic check.

When we replaced the broad Choice question with one atomic Noul question, it fixed the separation immediately. The question was: does answering this require performing more than one sequential calculation, simulation step or logical deduction, rather than a single direct lookup, format check or one-step classification?

On those initial 16 tasks, Jev routed 13 of 16 in agreement with our hand-labeled difficulty on the first run and 14 of 16 on the second pass. Many engineering teams ask whether they even need a classifier API when they can write a zero-cost regex and prompt-length heuristic in Python, matching words like sequence, modulo and race condition or escalating any prompt over 35 words.

On the 16 development questions used to write those exact rules, the local heuristic scored 15 of 16, or 93.8%. To test whether that heuristic survived contact with realistic coding-agent traffic, we froze its code without changing a character and added 34 unseen held-out tasks across seven coding-agent buckets: fact extraction, schema validation, short classification, single-hop code checks, state simulation traces, math or logic chains and subtle language-semantics bugs.

On the 34 held-out questions, the frozen heuristic dropped 23.2 percentage points to 70.6%. Realistic simple tasks such as extracting a port from a Kubernetes YAML manifest, a file path from a unified git diff header or a line number from a Python traceback exceeded 35 words and triggered false escalations. Conversely, concise multi-step code traces such as mutable default arguments across calls, Python dictionary key hashing on {1: "a", True: "b", 1.0: "c"} and C short-circuit evaluation lacked the heuristic's hardcoded puzzle words and were falsely routed to the simple path. Jev classified all 34 unseen tasks with 100.0% agreement, 34 of 34, reaching 48 of 50, or 96.0%, across the full suite.

A common mistake when building a fast-path router is assuming that setting a low maxOutputTokens cap on a reasoning model will make it spend less on easy tasks. Our 50-question data shows why that assumption fails.

When we compared gemini-3.8-flash with maxOutputTokens=300 against gemini-3.8-flash with maxOutputTokens=4096 while leaving thinkingConfig at the API default, both configurations spent almost identical thinking tokens on the 27 tasks Jev labeled SIMPLE:

Capping the model's output at 300 tokens didn't save any money on easy tasks. The cap is only a ceiling, and easy answers never came close to it. On the easy tasks, the capped version actually cost slightly more than the uncapped one. All the cap did was break 7 of the 25 hard tasks. On six of them, the model ran out of room while still reasoning and never gave an answer. On the seventh, it squeezed its reasoning to fit, finished on time and got the answer wrong.

So a Jev router that sends easy tasks to the capped model and hard ones to normal Flash got all 50 right. It still cost slightly more than skipping the router and using normal Flash for everything, and it was slower. If your cheap option doesn't actually make the model think less on easy tasks, no router can save you money. Not even a perfect one that costs nothing.

The obvious way to spend less on thinking is to ask the model to think less. So we ran a second round of tests with two lighter options: Flash-Lite, which doesn't think at all by default, and Gemini 3.8 Flash with its thinking level set to low. We added those results to the same results file, matched question by question.

The strongest setup without a router was Gemini 3.8 Flash on low thinking. It solved all 50 tasks. That's the number a router had to beat.

The Jev router beat it. It sends every task to Flash-Lite first and escalates only what Jev flags as complex. It also solved all 50 tasks, and it can help reduce both cost and response time compared with running low thinking on everything. On prompts this short the cost difference per task is small, so for an interactive agent the shorter wait is the bigger benefit.

Why it works. On its own, Flash-Lite answered every simple task in half a second but missed 6 of the 25 hard ones. Jev flagged all 6 as complex with high confidence and escalated them. That brought its cost close to what a perfect router would achieve.

The keyword heuristic was cheaper, but only because it got things wrong. It sent 5 hard tasks to Flash-Lite, and 2 of them failed, for 48 of 50.

Limits: one run per setup, 50 prompts with hand-assigned difficulty and simple automatic grading.

Two things in our results only show up once you look past it.

Routing and caching pull in opposite directions. Our test prompts were short, standalone tasks, and on those the Jev router helped reduce both cost and response time. Real coding agents often look different. They send the same big block of context on every turn, such as a map of the repository and the tool definitions. Agent Platform Context Caching stores that block so you pay 75% to 90% less to resend it. The catch is that a cache belongs to one model. If your agent switches between Flash-Lite and Flash from turn to turn, it either keeps two caches or loses the discount. The practical split: route short, self-contained tasks like log parsing or schema checks, and keep long, context-heavy turns on one model.

Match the model to the workload. Gemini 3.1 Pro is built for deep, multi-step reasoning, and it brings that same depth to every request, including short lookups that don't need it. For a workload made mostly of quick checks and extractions, a Flash configuration is the better fit, and Pro is best saved for the tasks that need its reasoning depth. Model configurations can also apply different safety settings to security-related prompts. In our run, the Pro configuration declined two short code-security review prompts that Flash answered. If your agent performs security reviews, test those prompts on the configuration you plan to use.

Other ways to build the router. Jev is one way to get a fast classifier, but not the only one. The Getting started with discriminative models codelab runs the same Choice, Score and Noul questions on DiffusionGemma, Google DeepMind's open-weights model, hosted on a Compute Engine GPU VM or on Cloud Run in your own Google Cloud project. An open-source server exposes the same API in front of it, so the routing code in this article only needs a different endpoint. Our results come from Jev, so measure accuracy and latency on your own tasks before switching, and keep in mind that a self-hosted model is billed by the GPU-hour rather than by the token.

Taken together, the results give three practical rules.

Start with Flash on low thinking before you build a router. It solved all 50 tasks and cost considerably less than the default setting. Lowering the output token limit is not a substitute, because it doesn't reduce how much the model thinks on easy tasks. Set the thinking level directly, with thinkingLevel: "LOW".

Use Jev to route short, standalone tasks. Send simple tasks to Flash-Lite and hard ones to Flash on low thinking. Ask Jev one simple yes-or-no question per factor. In our test, this helped reduce cost and response time compared with running Flash on low thinking for everything, and came close to what a perfect router would achieve. Keep long, context-heavy turns on a single model so caching keeps working. For high-volume warehouse workloads, Jeff Nelson's BigQuery and Cloud Run cascade pattern applies the same idea.

Measure what your agent actually spends. Send your agent's traces and thinking-token counts to Cloud Logging and BigQuery, so you can see which tasks drive your spend and catch truncation or retries that inflate it. The benchmark code will be published after open-source review.

In summary, the most cost-effective way to run an agent is to match each task to the model that fits it. Start with a fast model on a light setting. You'll often find it already gets the job done. Then add a small, almost free classifier like Jev to send only the hard tasks to a stronger model, and ask simple yes-or-no questions rather than one broad one. Judge every option by what it costs per task done right, not per token.

Medicare beneficiaries who remain in their current stand-alone Part D drug plans could face sharply higher premiums in 2027 if they don’t switch to lower-cost plans during open enrollment, according to a KFF analysis.

The average monthly premium for stand-alone prescription drug plans, or PDPs, is projected to rise 50%, from $34 in 2026 to $51 in 2027, if current enrollees remain in the same plans, according to the analysis from KFF, an independent source for health policy research, polling and news.

The increase would widen the gap between stand-alone drug plans and Medicare Advantage plans that include prescription drug coverage. KFF projects that drug coverage through Medicare Advantage plans that offer Part D coverage, or MA-PDs, will average $9 a month in 2027, compared with $51 for PDPs. The average PDP premium would rise by $17, compared with an increase of less than $2 for MA-PDs.

For Medicare Part D prescription drug coverage, the average beneficiary has 28 Medicare Advantage prescription drug plans (MA-PDs) and 9 stand-alone prescription drug plans (PDPs) to choose from, with varying costs associated with these different options. KFF analysts examined premiums and cost-sharing requirements for Part D coverage in 2027 and how these costs have changed over time, based on Medicare plan landscape and benefits data from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS).

The $51 figure assumes current PDP enrollees remain in their existing plans. The actual average premium could be lower as beneficiaries switch to less expensive plans during open enrollment and new beneficiaries select coverage.

CMS projected a much smaller increase in the national average Part D premium, from $35 to $36. KFF noted that CMS, which based its estimate on September 2026 enrollment, did not explain its calculation methodology.

Among PDP enrollees who do not receive low-income subsidies, 76%, or 10.4 million people, would pay higher premiums if they keep their current plans. About 2.4 million would see their monthly premiums increase by more than $50.

That's a sharp increase from 2026, when only 38,000 enrollees faced monthly premium increases exceeding $50, according to KFF. The Trump administration’s decision to discontinue the Part D Premium Stabilization Demonstration after 2026 ended the additional premium subsidies that were provided to PDP sponsors and the cap on the maximum allowable monthly PDP premium increase, which was set at $50 for 2026, according to KFF analysts.

Another 4.1 million beneficiaries without low-income subsidies are enrolled in zero-premium PDPs this year, but no zero-premium PDPs will be available in 2027.

“With some plans increasing premiums substantially, the stakes are higher this year for people with Medicare stand-alone drug coverage if they sit on the sidelines during open enrollment,” Juliette Cubanski, a vice president and director of the Program on Medicare Policy at KFF, said in a statement. “There are relatively low-premium PDP options, but people facing large premium increases need to take action if they want to avoid paying significantly more.”

According to KFF's analysis, if PDP enrollees who are not receiving Part D Low-Income Subsidies (LIS) keep their current plan in 2027, more than half (55%), or 7.5 million people, will pay premiums of less than $20 per month, but nearly 30%, or 3.9 million people, will pay at least $100 per month.

Average monthly premiums for the eight national PDPs are projected to range from around $6 to $204 in 2027. Among national PDPs, the two lowest-premium plans are Centene’s Wellcare Value Script Rx, an enhanced PDP with an average premium of $6.50 for 2027, up from $5.80 in 2026,  and Wellcare Classic, a basic PDP with an average premium of $10.90 for 2027, up from $8.60 in 2026, according to the analysis. The national PDPs with the highest average monthly premiums are United’s AARP Medicare Rx Preferred at $204.50, up from $126.00 in 2026, and Humana Premier Rx Plan at $137.40, up from $119.60 in 2026.

The analysis also finds that most Part D enrollees will face a deductible of several hundred dollars for drug coverage in 2027, including 85% of MA-PD enrollees, up from 80% in 2026, and virtually all PDP enrollees, 99.8%, up from 96% in 2026, based on current enrollment. The average Part D deductible in 2027 will be $643 for PDP enrollees, up from $540 in 2026,  and $489 for MA-PD enrollees, rising from $360 in 2026. The standard Part D deductible in 2027 is $700, up from $615 in 2026.

Most Part D enrollees will also face coinsurance rather than fixed copayments for preferred-brand and non-preferred drugs, potentially making their out-of-pocket costs less predictable. Virtually all PDP enrollees will face median coinsurance of 25% for preferred brands and 35% for non-preferred drugs, according to KFF.

‘Hinotama: Ball of Fire’ Review: Gentle, Handsomely Shot Domestic Drama Is a Promising Debut for Yaza Takenoshin

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The fresh-out-of-college director's first feature is inspired by the work of Ryusuke Hamaguchi, whose sometime DP Yoshio Kitagawa lends his expertise to proceedings.

The early moments of “Hinotama: Ball of Fire,” the debut feature from young Japanese director Yaza Takenoshin (who graduated from Harvard this past summer), almost move like clockwork, finding a pleasing rhythm in everyday mundanities — matching the repetition of numbers being called out for test papers to a mother having groceries counted at the checkout.

Showing in the Nippon Cinema Now selection — a space for new and upcoming Japanese filmmakers — at the Tokyo Film Festival, the film follows young Takeshi (Riku Onishi) as he watches his parents’ marriage crumble. It’s a slow but clearly inevitable process, the warning signs understated but clear to see. Takeshi is getting worn down by glimpses of his parents’ flaws, and becomes fascinated with the “ball of fire” that his old friend Akari (Himena Irei) tells him about — perhaps in the hope that seeing it will restore his childlike wonder about the world. Yaza has spoken of his admiration for filmmakers such as Ryusuke Hamaguchi, which is evident in his film’s neat and steady (but never inert or emotionless) camerawork and its patient observation of characters as they navigate fracturing relationships. The inspiration is rather direct: “Himotama” is indeed shot by Hamaguchi’s sometime cinematographer Yoshio Kitagawa, who offered to shoot the film when the student reached out for advice. Often, Kitagawa’s cinematography embraces symmetry, emphasizing the clean geometric lines of Takeshi’s home, picking an object or person to place at the centre of the frame. A lot of these orderly compositions soon give off the impression of a cage — if Mari Kusu’s performance as Takeshi’s mother, bristling with frustration, didn’t already imply as much.

Some terse dialogue soon cuts through that visual neatness, as Takeshi’s father hints at his dissatisfaction or frustration in a small aside — which then turns into a bigger fight, not long after as he “demands respect” and laments his children never thanking him. It all comes back to that earlier rhythm, a discontent from living as though on a factory line, where everything progresses with a subtle sense of automation and no feeling or passion. Takeshi drifts through all of these scenes without giving away his own dissatisfaction, until the dam bursts; destroying a pristine pair of sneakers by jumping in a muddy puddle, the moment all the more startling because of the stillness of the camera, and the length of Yaza’s takes to this point.

Takeshi’s father is a petty and proud man, in ways continually expanded upon throughout the film’s runtime, though how he’s actually framed never truly changes. Rather, Yaza patiently accumulates small details about him. He isn’t plainly a terrible, angry person, at least not right away anyway: The beginning of a propagandist campaign to his children for the eventual split comes with the leveraging of memories clearly meaningful to them. Yaza seeks subdued performances from his actors in general, particularly in the case of Onishi as the increasingly insular but troubled Takeshi, whose frustrations with his parents mostly manifest in questions about that titular ball of fire, when he’s not gazing into space.

Sometimes that muted approach can become a little overbearing — as in the shots of children standing in an orderly line as they watch Takeshi’s younger brother Kento (Koe Odani) walking to a new house. In moments, it feels a little unnatural, even compared to the deliberately uncomfortable family dynamic built in the film’s first third. A sudden jump forward to three months later destabilises the film to a greater extent, but there are still moments of pathos to be found, particularly as Kento is forced to grow up fast, taking initiative to try and alleviate the suffering of his family.

“Hinotama” eventually ends up taking one step further into a more allegorical tone, which doesn’t fit with the film’s stronger elements — notably, its gradual observation of the increasingly visible cracks in a traditional family unit. Still, it’s an incredibly promising first feature.

The Elder Scroll 6: Sentinel's Name Quietly Teases Juicy 'Skyrim' Sequel

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A closer look at how TES6 might connect to Skyrim and The Elder Scrolls Online

Eight years after the original announcement, we've finally learned the name of Bethesda's next RPG: The Elder Scrolls 6: Sentinel. Since the developer didn't show any gameplay footage, all fans can really do is muse on the meaning of the title. As disappointing as that is, Elder Scrolls loreheads do have plenty to chew on.

The subtitle has been an open secret for a while now — fans guessed it correctly years ahead of the reveal, and Xbox was teasing an eight-letter subtitle recently as well. The first and primary reason for this title should be obvious: it sounds cool. That's not the only reason, of course. Accompanying the title in the trailer was a short quote from Yazar, a Moth Priest serving High Queen Iradne of Hammerfell:

The scroll offered only a shimmer, a veil thinly lifted to my eyes. A future lost. A victory undone. Sands of time scattered by fate.

The Hammerfell setting has been rumored for a while now as well, but the quote cements it in stone. Hammerfell is a region to the north of Skyrim, and it is the home of the Redguard race. Redguards are known for their strength, combat prowess, and resilience in harsh climates. Accordingly, Hammerfell and its deserts, jungles, and mountain ranges were once known as the Deathlands.

Hammerfell will be immediately recognizable to anyone who has played The Elder Scrolls Online. And the most consequential region in Hammerfell is arguably — drumroll please — Sentinel. Sentinel’s proximity to the sea makes it a critical trading post for Tamriel at large. Five major areas of Sentinel are already explorable in ESO: the docks, a Redguard gym, a grand royal palace, a massive open-air marketplace, and a sewer system.

Moth priests are a part of a cult that devotes itself to reading the actual Elder Scrolls. These priests use the scrolls to foretell the future in an effort to help monarchs like the Emperor or, in this case, the regional queen. The timing of the quote — 4E 206 — places Sentinel five years after the events of Skyrim.

It's worth knowing that Hammerfell has spent centuries embroiled between its northern and southern regions, but the area was unified by the time Skyrim takes place. That unification was primarily meant to help save Sentinel from an opportunistic Elven incursion that tried to take advantage of the ongoing larger turmoil.

A five-year time jump would likely further Hammerfell's era as a sovereign state — but that doesn't necessarily signal stability. Hammerfell's independence separates it from the larger empire that could provide it support during a time of need. That's important, because the elves probably aren't done with Hammerfell, either.

There are two primary events in Skyrim that are of note here. One involves the Dark Brotherhood quest where the player assassinates Emperor Titus Mede II. A death like that opens the door for the elves to launch an assault while the wider empire tries to pick up the pieces after the assassination.

The other quest, which involves a Redguard noble person named Saadia, is a perfect encapsulation of what players can expect in The Elder Scrolls 6. In it, Saadia claims she is being hunted by elves for speaking out. After the player investigates further, it turns out that Saadia actually betrayed the Redguard to help the elves. This sets a precedent that the elves have already infiltrated the highest echelons of power in Sentinel — and will probably find more ways to sow discord in the new game.

The quote is ambiguous but could be read as an attempt by Hammerfell's High Queen to figure out how to proceed in what is undoubtedly a messy situation.

Hopefully, Bethesda won't keep fans guessing for too long. It's been nearly a decade since the game was originally announced, and the RPG has apparently been playable since 2025. Fans are ready for something more than just a title screen.

Tesla cuts FSD’s speed margin from 50% to 10% over the limit in Europe

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Tesla’s driver-assistance system in Europe can no longer be set to drive 50% over the speed limit. The Netherlands’ RDW, which approved the system in April, confirmed today that it signed off on an update capping the speed margin at 10% and officially renaming “FSD Supervised” to Tesla Assisted Driving.

The changes land weeks before EU member states could vote on approving the system across the bloc, and they match the conditions Germany set last month.

The RDW’s notice is the clearest official confirmation of how the system was configured when the Dutch regulator approved FSD Supervised on April 10. The “speed margin,” which lets the system deviate from the detected limit for “safety and traffic flow,” could go up to +50%. On a 100 km/h highway, that’s 150 km/h. In a 30 km/h city zone, it’s 45 km/h.

Now the margin tops out at +10%. It also resets to 0% every time the car starts, and the RDW says other speed-related functions are now disabled by default. It didn’t say which ones.

The new name shows up on the in-car display, in the owner’s manual, and in other driver information on HW4 vehicles. Tesla pushes all of it over the air.

The RDW describes the update as “several minor changes” that Tesla asked it to assess. The regulator says it checked the implementation, found no adverse or unintended effects, and folded the changes into the existing type approval without amending or extending it. It also stresses that the rename doesn’t change what the system does. It’s still driver assistance, and the driver is still responsible.

The RDW notified the nine member states that recognize its approval: Belgium, Croatia, Czechia, Denmark, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Slovakia, and Slovenia. That’s 10 countries counting the Netherlands, up from eight at the start of October.

The EU-wide decision still sits with the Technical Committee on Motor Vehicles (TCMV). The RDW says those talks are confidential and won’t comment on them. As we reported, the EU pushed the vote to December at the earliest, and passing it takes a qualified majority: 15 of 27 member states representing at least 65% of the EU population.

Speed has been the biggest problem for Tesla’s file in Brussels. In June, Sweden asked the EU to reject FSD unless Tesla removed the ability to set speeds above the posted limit. In September, a Brussels pedestrian safety group found the system speeding in 55% of the 30 km/h segments it tested, in a country that had already approved it. France has cited urban speeding as one of its reasons for refusing to recognize the Dutch approval.

Then, on October 6, Germany’s Transport Ministry said it would back EU approval after Tesla agreed to drop the “Full Self-Driving” name, which the ministry called misleading, and to cap speed tolerance at 10%. Today’s RDW update delivers both.

But 10% isn’t what Sweden asked for. Sweden wanted no setting above the limit at all. Several European countries are known for enforcing speed limits strictly.

Good, but that’s only part of the problem. Tesla’s biggest issue with FSD right now is that it doesn’t properly detect the speed limits.

Being locked at 10% over the limit doesn’t mean much if you don’t know the actual limit.

The name change is needed globally and it is past due. “Tesla Assisted Driving” describes what the system does. Meanwhile, Tesla still sells the exact same kind of software in the US as “Full Self-Driving (Supervised)” for $99 a month. If it’s misleading in Berlin, it’s misleading in California.

Practically, this update gets Tesla Germany’s vote. The question for December is whether a 10% cap is enough for France, Italy, and Spain, or whether Sweden’s zero-tolerance line wins out.

Collaborative research projects will focus on studies of cancer dormancy and metastasis

BRONX, N.Y., Nov. 3, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- The National Cancer Institute (NCI) has awarded a multidisciplinary and multi-institutional team led by Julio A. Aguirre-Ghiso, Ph.D., of Montefiore Einstein Comprehensive Cancer Center (MECCC), a five-year, $10 million grant to uncover the biological processes that drive breast cancer relapse and identify new opportunities to predict and prevent recurrence.

Up to an estimated 685,000 people die from breast cancer globally each year, according to the NCI, with nearly all fatal cases resulting from disease that has returned in other organs years or even decades after surgery, chemotherapy, or radiation. Research suggests that even a small, localized tumor may have already generated micrometastasesâsmall amounts of cancer cells that have spread beyond the original tumor but cannot be detected by screening or diagnostic tests. Because these cells may have traveled beyond their initial tumor location, they can remain in the body after the primary tumor is removed or treated, lie dormant for years, and later "reawaken" and cause breast cancer to recur.

Exploring Cancer Dormancy The new research will address one of the least understood and most consequential areas of cancer biology: the characteristics of early disseminated cancer cells that survive surgery and other treatments by remaining dormant. Understanding these cells' distinguishing features could help scientists detect them and develop therapies that eliminate them before the cancer returns.

"For two decades, we have been pioneering a new era of cancer research that could enable more precise, proactive treatments designed to prevent recurrence and improve long-term remission," said Dr. Aguirre-Ghiso, professor of cell biology, of oncology, and of medicine and the Rose C. Falkenstein Chair in Cancer Research at Albert Einstein College of Medicine. "By closing gaps in our understanding of breast cancer relapse, our goal is to identify who is most at risk of recurrence and find ways to intercept cancer before it returns." Dr. Aguirre-Ghiso also serves as MECCC's associate director of basic science, co-leader of the Tumor Microenvironment & Metastasis Program, and director of the Cancer Dormancy Institute.

The researchers will analyze clinical data and tissue samples from people with ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), an early, non-invasive form of breast cancer. They will use the findings to investigate the mechanisms that allow cancer cells to spread early and determine whether specific characteristics of those cells are associated with subsequent relapse.

Combining Expertise Across DisciplinesThe multidisciplinary project brings together investigators with expertise in cancer biology, pathology, epidemiology, imaging, and bioinformatics.

"Montefiore Einstein is bringing together researchers across institutions to tackle a question that no single discipline can answer: why do some breast cancer cells spread early and reappear years later?" said Dr. Aguirre-Ghiso. "By connecting patient data and tissue samples with laboratory studies and advanced imaging, we hope to identify who is at risk of relapse and find ways to prevent it."

The study, "Mechanisms of Early Breast Cancer Local and Distant Relapse," is supported by NCI grant 1P01CA295557.

Weight-Space Programming Works: An LLM Fixed Qwen's Math Loop by Editing 0.016% of Its Weights

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 3 • Originally published at dispatch-blog.hashnode.dev

One hundred thirty-one thousand numbers. That is the entire size of the edit that moved Qwen3.5-0.8B, a small open reasoning model, from 7.9% to 48.1% on MATH-500, from 13.8% to 67.1% on GSM8K, and from 21.0% to 62.3% on IFEval, an instruction-following benchmark. No gradient descent was involved, no training data, no fine-tuning run. A frontier model called GPT-6 Astra read the model's raw weights, identified a single direction written by two MLP layers that was trapping the model in repetition loops, and subtracted it. The whole repair touched 0.016% of the network's weights.

The result comes from "Sparks of Weight-Space Programming," a November 2026 research post by Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang, and Omar Khattab at MIT CSAIL. The claim behind it is larger than one clever repair: a general-purpose language model, used off the shelf with no task-specific training, can read neural network weights well enough to say what a network was trained to do, and can write weights well enough to beat reinforcement-learning policies trained for hundreds of millions of steps. The post is careful, the logs are public, and the failure cases are reported next to the successes. It deserves a close read, so here is one.

The authors give the idea a precise definition. In weight-space programming, a general-purpose LLM reads and writes a network's weights directly, not through an optimizer, and in particular not with gradient descent, working toward a natural-language specification, sometimes with no environment feedback at all. Three capabilities get tested in turn. Reading: given only the weights, say what the network was trained to do. Fixing: given a failing network, find the cause and repair it. Writing: given a task description, produce a working network from scratch.

The contrast with business as usual is the point. Today, weights are written by symbolic optimizers like Adam or Muon, by secondary networks trained to generate them, or by compilers from formal programs. Humans cannot meaningfully edit a weight matrix by hand, which is exactly why weights have remained the exclusive territory of those algorithms. The post argues this assumption is now breaking: the same reasoning ability that lets a frontier model debug code transfers, at least partially, to the continuous space of network parameters.

The first experiment is a clean test. Four policies were trained with PPO to play four different games, grid versions of Breakout, Pong, Pac-Man, and Freeway, on the same 10x10 grid with the same five actions and identical architectures: an MLP of shape 500 to 32 to 32 to 5, just 17,253 weights, trained for a billion steps each. Astra received the raw weights as text plus a description of the grid and the actions, without the list of candidate games. It named the right game for all four networks. Its reasoning, summarized in the logs, is the kind a human expert would attempt: the first layer's weights concentrate where each input channel matters, so for Breakout the agent channel's weights lie along the bottom row where the paddle lives, and the output layer separates different actions for different games.

The second experiment moves to generative models: three small diffusion models trained to draw circles, squares, and triangles on 12x12 grayscale images, 23,824 weights each. Astra named each model's shape from the weights alone, with no tools and no forward passes. The tell that it was reading rather than simulating: it drew every shape as an outline, while the actual models generate filled shapes. It was reading the geometry of the output layer, where each hidden unit's weights trace one segment of an edge, not replaying the network in its head.

The third experiment is the strongest read, and the one most worth your skepticism. Three character-level transformers of 7.16 million parameters were trained on a billion characters each of Python code, romanized Japanese literature, and English Wikipedia. Astra received only the four middle transformer blocks, no input embedding and no output layer, so nothing in what it saw maps any dimension to any character, plus a Python session for analysis and a hard ban on running the network on any input. It named the right language for all three models, in both of its attempts, and it listed every copying head, the induction heads that let a model repeat earlier text, matching the measured ground truth exactly: heads 2.1, 3.1, and 3.5 for Python, 3.3 for romaji, 3.1 for English. To do this it independently re-derived the induction-head analysis from Elhage et al.'s well-known interpretability work, a method nobody mentioned to it. The romaji detail is my favorite in the post: the final block Astra was given writes in fewer distinct directions, exactly what you would expect from an alphabet that never uses q, l, v, or x.

Reading is a parlor trick if it does not generalize to writing, so the authors escalated. For three MinAtar games, Breakout, Seaquest, and Asterix, Astra received the rules and source code, a fresh network of shape 600 to 32 to 32 to 6 with every weight set to zero, and nothing else: no data, no loss, no gradients, no tools, no rollouts in the environment. It wrote all the weights as text in a single reply. The results, scored over 1,000 episodes each:

Against PPO trained for 100 million steps, the hand-written networks win all three games. Against PPO trained for 500 million steps, they score twenty times higher in Breakout, come close in Asterix, and reach 60% of the trained score in Seaquest. How does it do it? It writes programs in neural form: for Breakout, it assigned the first layer to compute where the ball will land and the second layer to move the paddle to that position. That is not a metaphor. It is a hand-coded algorithm expressed in matrix weights.

The Go experiment then pushes the scale up. The target network for 9x9 Go has 1,480,274 weights, far too many for the context window, so a Python sandbox held the tensors while Astra edited them with numpy, unable to run the network except through a budget of forward passes, each one a batch of up to 128 practice games. With a budget of zero forward passes, meaning a single Python run of 336 lines, the authored network already won more than half its games against two smaller PPO networks. With 50 forward passes, it beat an equal-sized PPO network trained on 20 million games. The program it wrote is recognizable Go: early layers mark each side's stones and their liberties, middle layers propagate along chains to find groups in danger, and the final layer scores each move, capturing here, saving a group there, avoiding self-atari, never filling its own eyes.

The repair experiment sits between reading and writing. A PPO-trained car policy for a Kinetix ramp level, 171,664 weights and a billion training steps, had converged to a local minimum: it drove straight at the ramp and stalled, succeeding zero times in 256 episodes. Given the weights, a description of the scene, and one recorded episode, Astra explained the failure and rewrote 3.5% of the weights in a single reply. The car then succeeded in all 1,024 evaluation episodes. The mechanism it described is surgical: it added a small side path, a few units per layer, that reads the car's position and speed, triggers the back-up-and-accelerate behavior, then switches itself off once the car clears the obstacle so the trained policy drives the rest.

The headline experiment scales the repair to a real language model. Qwen3.5-0.8B, with thinking enabled, is prone to repetition loops; its own model card warns about this. The setup gave Astra a Python kernel over the model's weights with no GPU, practice problems drawn from the GSM8K and MATH training sets (7,469 and 7,275 problems, disjoint from every test set), and a budget of 10, 25, or 50 forward passes, where one forward pass means the model answers up to 512 problems or reads up to 1,024 texts. Training and gradients remained forbidden. Sampling followed the model card exactly: temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, top-k 20, presence penalty 1.5.

The failure mode was unmistakable once looked for: 85 of the base model's first 96 practice answers ran all the way to the 32,768-token limit, repeating themselves and never reaching an answer. Astra traced the looping to one direction written by the MLPs of layers 15 and 19, and subtracted that direction from 64 output columns in each layer, 131,072 weights in total.

The measured outcome, on benchmarks Astra never saw:

The loops are not specific to math, so the gains are not either: IFEval, which measures instruction following, nearly tripled. Lower budgets barely helped, since at 10 or 25 forward passes the loops mostly survived, which is itself useful information about how much interaction this kind of repair currently needs.

The post earns unusual credibility from its negative results. In the sycophancy experiment, two copies of Gemma 3 270M were fine-tuned identically, one to answer correctly and one to always agree with the user's suggestion, with embeddings and output layers frozen so the input-output distributions stay identical. From the weights alone, Astra failed: across four attempts it found many weight differences but could not map them to behavior. Only when the authors relaxed the setup, letting Astra write up to 16 texts of its choosing and inspect only the attention patterns of both models, never their outputs, did it identify the sycophant, correctly, in all three pairs. Its explanation was mechanical: in the sycophant, middle-layer heads shift attention from the question's content to the user's suggested answer. One head, 10.2, moves from spending 94.3% of its attention on the question's numbers in the honest model to spending 85.7% on the user's guess in the sycophant.

The second failure is the control experiment everyone should cite. Gemma 3 270M rarely loops, only 8.5% of its answers hit the token limit, so the authors asked Astra to simply make it better at math. It could not. Reading its failures, Astra found ordinary reasoning errors, wrong arithmetic and invalid algebraic steps, with no single component responsible for them. Its edits stayed small and targeted, and none moved MATH-500 by more than about a point, a gain Astra itself reported as not established. The boundary this draws is the most important sentence in the post: editing weights can remove an obstacle that hides an ability, but it cannot add an ability the model lacks.

Against fine-tuning, the Qwen repair wins on three axes and loses on one. It needed no training data and no gradient pipeline; it touched 0.016% of the weights, which sidesteps the catastrophic forgetting that broad fine-tuning invites; and the improvement generalized sideways to instruction following instead of trading one capability for another. What it loses is reliability and cost structure: the repair worked because the failure happened to have a single-direction cause, and 50 forward passes through up to 512 problems each on a frontier model is not free, merely cheaper than a training run.

Against mechanistic interpretability, the shift is one of reader, not target. The field has built excellent purpose-built tooling, from probes to circuit tracing to feature dictionaries, and more recently has trained models to read activations or explain single neurons. What is new here is that the reader is a general-purpose frontier model with no interpretability-specific training, working in natural language over raw tensors, and reproducing a known research method on its own. The sycophancy negative result matters here too: pure weight reading has a ceiling, and pairing the reader with a lightweight behavioral probe, the attention tool, is what broke through it.

Against reinforcement learning, the from-scratch results are genuinely startling at small scale: one-shot authored weights beating PPO at 100 million steps in three games, and beating a 20-million-game PPO run in Go with 50 forward passes. But note the parameter counts, 17 thousand to 1.5 million. Nobody wrote a 0.8-billion-weight model from scratch; the language-model result is a repair, not an authorship. Extrapolating the writing results to frontier scale is speculation the authors themselves decline to make.

The authors list the applications plainly, and each is worth tracking as an engineering direction rather than a shipped product. Auditing without evaluation: reading hidden behaviors, backdoors or deceptive circuits, directly from weights, without needing a test set that anticipates them. Zero-data steering and unlearning: removing a capability by editing a handful of weights instead of collecting data and fine-tuning, with the rest of the model left intact. Targeted hotfixes: the looping repair is exactly the shape of bug that currently forces a retrain or an embarrassed system-prompt patch. And true model merging: grafting a circuit from one model into another of a different architecture, instead of averaging weights, which only works between identical twins.

The near-term checkpoint for all of this is public. The authors are building a benchmark and harness for weight-space programming, and they have released complete logs, every prompt, every reasoning summary, every piece of code, every network, and every evaluation, at github.com/RikiyaT/lm_understand-logs. The stated bottlenecks are context length and harness efficiency, and the stated scope is early. If the logs hold up under community re-runs, this is the beginning of a real engineering discipline; if they do not, it was still the most interesting negative result of the month. Either way, the honest move for practitioners is the same: watch the benchmark, try the harness when it lands, and keep your training pipeline warm.

Most Java teams first meet large language models through a demo: a chat window, a clever prompt, a convincing answer. Turning that demo into a production feature is a different job. The model call becomes one more remote dependency with latency, rate limits, timeouts, and failure modes. Its output is probabilistic, so it needs validation before anything downstream trusts it. And it handles data that often belongs to customers, so security and auditability matter from the first request.

Claude, the family of models from Anthropic, fits well into this kind of backend. It is available through the Claude API, it supports tool use so the model can call your own functions, and it can process long documents and structured inputs. This article shows how to integrate it into a Java service in a way that you can test, observe, and operate. We will cover the request model, a minimal client, structured output with validation, tool use, error handling, and the operational concerns that determine whether the feature survives contact with real traffic.

The Claude API exposes a Messages endpoint. You send a model name, a token limit, an optional system prompt, and a list of messages alternating between the user and the assistant. The response contains content blocks, usually text, and metadata such as token usage and a stop reason.

Three headers matter. The x-api-key header carries your secret key, the anthropic-version header pins the API version so behavior does not change under you, and content-type declares a JSON body. Keep the key in a secrets manager or environment variable, never in source code. Use a dedicated key per service so you can rotate it without affecting other systems.

Model choice is an engineering decision. Smaller, faster models suit classification, extraction, and routing. Larger models suit multi-step reasoning and nuanced writing. Pick the smallest model that meets your quality bar, measure it, and revisit the choice as your requirements change.

Java 21 ships with a capable HttpClient, which is enough for a first integration without adding a dependency. The example below sends a single message and returns the text of the first content block. Two points deserve attention. First, the timeout is set on the request, not only on the connection, because a model call can legitimately take tens of seconds and a missing timeout can exhaust your thread pool. Second, the status check is explicit. A non-200 response should become a typed error in your domain, not a string that leaks into a user interface.

Free text is hard to use in code. For most backend features you want a predictable shape: a category, a list of fields, a score. The reliable approach is to ask for JSON, describe the exact schema in the system prompt, and validate the result before using it.

Ask the model to respond only with a JSON object matching this structure, then parse it with Jackson into the record. Validation is not optional. Check that the category belongs to your allowed set, that the priority is one of your known values, and that the summary is within your length limit. If validation fails, retry once with the validation error included in the prompt, and then fall back to a human queue. Treat the model as an untrusted input source, the same way you would treat a partner API.

Tool use lets the model request an action from your application. You declare tools with a name, a description, and a JSON Schema for their inputs. When the model decides a tool is needed, it returns a tool_use block. Your code runs the function, then sends a tool_result block back in the next message. The loop continues until the model returns a final text answer.

This pattern is powerful for banking, support, and operations workflows, where the model should look up an account balance or a ticket status rather than guess. Keep the tools narrow, read-only where possible, and authorize every call against the end user's identity, not the service's. A model should never be able to do something the requesting user could not do directly.

Set a maximum number of tool-call iterations, such as five, so a confused model cannot loop indefinitely and multiply your costs.

Calling a model is cheap for a demo and expensive at scale, so design for cost from the start. Measure token usage per request and log it with the feature name, so you can see which parts of the product drive spend. Prompt caching lets you reuse a long, stable system prompt or document across requests at a lower cost, which is valuable for support assistants that repeat the same policy text.

Rate limits and transient errors are normal. Implement retries with exponential backoff and jitter for 429 and 5xx responses, and respect any retry guidance in the response headers. Put a circuit breaker around the client so that a degraded provider does not drag your whole service down. Degrade gracefully: if the assistant is unavailable, show the standard workflow instead of an error page.

Observability is part of the feature. Track latency percentiles, error rates by status code, token usage, tool-call counts, and validation failures. A rising validation failure rate often signals a prompt regression or a change in input data before users notice.

Before sending any data to a model, decide what it may contain. Customer identifiers, account numbers, and personal data should be removed or pseudonymized unless there is a clear need and an approved basis to include them. Review your provider terms and your organization's policies for data retention and processing. In regulated industries such as banking, involve your security and compliance teams early, not after the prototype works.

Protect against prompt injection. Text from documents, emails, or web pages can contain instructions that try to override your system prompt. Keep untrusted content clearly delimited, never let model output decide authorization, and validate every tool call against the current user's permissions.

Test the parts you control. Unit-test the prompt builder, the response parser, and the validation rules with fixed inputs. Keep a small set of representative examples and expected categories, and run them as a regression suite whenever you change the prompt or the model version. Model outputs are not deterministic, so assert on properties, such as valid JSON, allowed categories, and presence of required fields, rather than exact wording.

For the client, use a fake HTTP server in integration tests to simulate timeouts, 429 responses, and malformed bodies. Your retry and fallback logic should be exercised without calling the real API on every build.

Claude gives Java teams a capable model behind a straightforward HTTP API. The hard part is not the first request; it is everything that surrounds it: validation, timeouts, tool authorization, cost control, and testing. Treat the model like any other critical dependency and design the boundary carefully, and it becomes a dependable part of your architecture rather than a fragile experiment.
