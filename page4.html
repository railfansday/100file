The 500e Isn't Fiat's Smallest Car, But It Is The Only One You Can Drive On US Highways

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

It may be true that the smallest production cars ever built make modern compacts look huge, but everything's relative. The tiniest new car you can get for 2026 in the U.S. — the Fiat 500e — is still quite small when compared to most other rides today. Oh, and speaking of tiny, that word applies to the current Fiat lineup as well. It only includes the 500e and the adorable Fiat Topolino low-speed vehicle that was designed for stunting on your suburban neighbors – not traveling on U.S. highways.

The all-electric city car is a mere 142.95 inches long and 74.12 inches wide (including its mirrors), and its height is 66.3 inches. Note that the 2026 Mini Cooper is even more vertically challenged, as it checks in at 57.6 inches in height, yet it's nearly 16 inches longer and roughly 3.5 inches wider than the 500e. Meanwhile, for comparison's sake, one of the biggest new passenger vehicles available right now is another EV, the Cadillac Escalade IQL — a 750-horsepower, 19-foot-long American luxury beast. To be exact, the IQL stretches a tick over 19 feet, at 228.5 inches, and it's a whopping 94.1 inches wide.

The 500e's sticker price is also on the small side, at least compared to the iQL. The Fiat starts at $35,700 before a $1,995 destination charge, while the king-sized Cadillac starts at $130,405 and requires a destination charge of $2,895. Unfortunately for Fiat, the cost of the 500e doesn't look so good when you look at other small EVs like the Nissan Leaf (MSRP: $29,990) or even the Chevrolet Equinox (MSRP: $34,995).

All that said, you've got to hand it to Fiat engineers. They managed to carve out a surprising amount of interior space in the 500e's cabin in their efforts to help win America's heart. You'll find a total of 101.2 cubic feet of interior volume waiting there. True, only about 7.5 cubic feet is dedicated for rear storage behind the back seats. But the 93.7 cubic feet leftover for passengers is more than in some noticeably larger cars. For example, the 2026 Toyota Corolla only has 88.6 cubic feet of passenger space despite being almost 40 inches longer than the Fiat. The 2026 Hyundai Venue SUV checks in with 91.9 cubic feet of passenger space, even though it's roughly 16 inches longer than the 500e.

The Fiat's specs also translate into a real-world advantage. When Jalopnik's own Collin Woodard drove the 500e in 2024, he was still able to fit a 6-footer in the rear seats. Also noting the car's relatively meager 149-mile range, the bottom line was that the Fiat proved why city cars are best as EVs.

The flipside to the roomy interior is a standard cabin-equipment list that's a bit lacking, especially in a car that sets you back close to $38,000 as a starting point. Remember, the 2026 Nissan Leaf opens at $29,990, before $1,575 in destination charges, and it comes standard with blind-spot monitoring/intervention, rear cross-traffic alert, a driver-attention monitor, a surround-view system, a 12.3-inch touchscreen, and even Nissan's ProPILOT Assist driver-assist system — none of which is standard for the base 500e.

7 Western Shows That Are Better Than 'Yellowstone'

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

In June 2018, a neo-Western premiered on the Paramount Network that would revitalize the genre. That show was called Yellowstone, created by Taylor Sheridan and John Linson, snagging the iconic Kevin Costner to lead a talented, stellar cast. The series takes place in Montana and follows John Dutton (Costner) as he and his family, the overly ambitious Jamie (Wes Bentley) and the smart, loose cannon Beth (Kelly Reilly) try to defend their ranch from those trying to poach it away from them — from the Indian reservation that wants to transform the Yellowstone-Dutton Ranch back to its pristine, original form, to a global conglomerate that wants to transform the land into a modern paradise for tourists. While trying to fight off those who want their land, the Duttons have to contend with their own family issues.

We didn't know it at the time, but what we turned into on June 20, 2018, was a show that would bend the rules of the traditional Western. Yellowstone wasn't your typical, family-friendly Western show. This series was gritty, violent, and filled to the brim with drama and treachery at every turn. Yellowstone is, by far, one of the best neo-Westerns of all-time, one that you can binge-watch over-and-over without ever getting bored with it. While the show has etched its name in pop culture history, there are Western shows out there that, for all intents and purposes, are just as good, or better, than Yellowstone. So, if you're on your 50th viewing, and are now trying to search out for something better, here are the Westerns that are definitely better than Yellowstone.

In the 1950s, Western shows were just gaining popularity, and one show that many viewers tuned into week-after-week was Bonanza. The NBC show, which premiered in 1959, is one of the longest-running Western shows of all time, and watching just one episode of this legendary series, you'll quickly see why it had so much staying power.

Set in the 1860s, Bonanza follows the Cartwright family, a wealthy clan who lives in Virginia City, Nevada during the Gold Rush era. That little gold nugget of information is important, because the show is basically centered on mining, especially silver ore, which was under the town of Virginia City and was discovered in 1859. Bonanza has all the Western tropes that one would love, and its opening theme is iconic in TV history. Notable for being one of TV Guide's 50 Greatest Shows of All Time, Bonanza is certainly one of the best Westerns ever.

When you think about how the Western genre on television came to be, you have to trace its roots back to The Lone Ranger, which brought popularity to Western TV shows and was ABC's first bonafide ratings hit. Starting out as a popular radio series, The Lone Ranger made the jump to television in 1949, and saying it was an instant hit is a drastic understatement.

The Lone Ranger follows a group of Texas Rangers who get ambushed, and all are nearly killed, except one, who is known as "The Lone Ranger" (John Hart for 52 episodes, Clayton Moore for 169 episodes), and teams up with Tonto (Jay Silverheels), a Native American who saved and nursed The Lone Ranger back to health. Much like Bonanza, The Lone Ranger helped popularize Westerns on TV, which was just coming into the mainstream. The episodes are exciting, and the characters are iconic. Unlike Yellowstone, The Lone Ranger is more friendly for families, which makes it a better watch for TV viewers who want to introduce Westerns to the little ones.

Before Yellowstone became the vanguard of the neo-Western, you could make an argument that FX's Justified was the show that popularized the sub-genre. Based on the novel Fire in the Hole by Elmore Leonard, Justified is a unique throwback to the Westerns of old, but with a more modern twist that contemporary Western fans would come to expect, especially after watching Yellowstone.

The series follows Raylan Givens (Timothy Olyphant), a Deputy U.S. Marshal who does his work the way Marshals did in the 1800s. This way of investigating makes him a frequent target for criminals, especially Boyd Crowder (Walton Goggins), a career criminal known for robbing banks. Just by reading that, you get the sense of what Justified is. Having an old-school Western show set in the modern age was a bold move for the show creators and runners to make, but it was one that paid off thanks to the awesome acting and intriguing storylines. If it wasn't for Yellowstone, we would be talking about Justified being the vanguard of neo-Westerns, without question.

When you think of Westerns, most people think of gunfights in the middle of town, and law enforcement officers trying to hunt down criminal bank robbers. While most of the best Westerns have these tropes, not all in the genre has to be action-packed, gun-fighting bonanzas. Take Little House on the Prairie, for example, the famed NBC Western that went the historical drama route to its claim to fame.

Based on Laura Ingalls Wider's Little House on the Prairie novel, the series follows the Ingalls family, who move to Walnut Grove, Minnesota in the 1840s, and most of the show revolves around their lives, which is told through the lens of Charles (Michael Landon) and Caroline's (Karen Grassle) middle daughter, Laura (Melissa Gilbert), and her maturation from child to adulthood. Little House on the Prairie is well-known for its story of "strength through family," with the Ingalls and other Walnut Grove townsfolks finding a way to make it through the strength of family and community. This is completely separate from what Yellowstone represents, which saw family as more of a hindrance than anything else. If you're looking for a Western drama that is more family-focused, Little House on the Prairie is definitely your show.

By the late 1950s, Westerns were the staple on television, especially for ABC, which already had a massive Western hit on their hands with The Lone Ranger. However, with that series coming to an end in 1957, they needed another Western to fill its timeslot and continue its ratings' dominance. Enter Maverick, a Western comedy created by Roy Huggins which is just as iconic as the series it replaced.

More comedy-focused than The Lone Ranger, Maverick follows Bert Maverick (James Garner), a poker-playing rounder who is always on the lookout for games with high stakes. Most of the storylines within Maverick center on Maverick's high-stakes poker-playing, and this is where the comedy is born. Maverick wasn't always about poker, however. Make no mistake, this was a Western in the 1950s, so you know there were villains that were able gunslingers as well. Maverick is a truly exciting Western series, one that we hope today's Hollywood will not touch and remake. It's that timeless.

With Yellowstone being a massive hit, there was little doubt that the show was going to spawn a franchise. The first show to spin-off from its parent series was 1883, created by Taylor Sheridan, and, if one gives it a chance, would see that it actually exceeds its parent show in both story and tone.

1883 is a prequel that follows the Dutton family at the end of the Civil War. As they pack up and leave Tennessee, they begin to head west, eventually settling in Montana and putting up stakes in the land that would later become the massive Yellowstone-Dutton Ranch. Filmed as a miniseries, 1883 is a great look at how the Dutton family came to be and the struggles they endured to make a home and living for themselves in those tough times. By ditching the heightened drama and toxic vitriol of Yellowstone, 1883 is a more traditional Western drama that follows the rules, not aims to break them. So, for fans of old-school Westerns who were turned off by Yellowstone's breakneck drama, 1883 is a show that's more your speed.

We've listed some truly iconic Western shows that are certainly better than Yellowstone. However, we have yet to discuss the show that outclasses them all, especially Yellowstone, and that show is Gunsmoke. Starting out as a radio series, like other Westerns at the time, Gunsmoke made the jump to TV in 1955, thanks to CBS. No one at CBS, however, would have guessed that Gunsmoke would be a staple of the network for 20 years.

Set in Dodge City, Kansas after the Civil War, Gunsmoke follows U.S. Marshal Matt Dillion (James Arness), who protects the citizens of Dodge City from criminals. When one talks about traditional Westerns, Gunsmoke is the show that is often brought up as an example of how to do it right, and exciting. You'll get it all here, from drama to exhilarating gun-fights, all packed in a single episode. With its strong Western tone, and great plotlines, there is no question Gunsmoke is certainly better than Yellowstone.

10 Best The Godfather Parodies, Ranked (Including Modern Family)

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Francis Ford Coppola's The Godfather has provided movies with an abundance of parody opportunities. Released in 1972, the crime epic established a remarkably distinctive cinematic language, from Marlon Brando's gravelly delivery and intimidating presence to the elaborate rituals of loyalty, family gatherings, and underworld negotiations. Yet, The Godafther's influence extends well beyond gangster movies, with countless filmmakers borrowing its imagery, dialogue, performances, and instantly recognizable musical themes.

Part of what makes The Godfather such an effective target for comedy is the contrast between its characters' deadly seriousness and the situations surrounding them. Vito's quiet authority, Michael's increasingly elaborate schemes, and the formalities of organized crime are all wonderfully easy to exaggerate. Even relatively minor details, such as kissing a mob boss's hand or making somebody an offer they cannot refuse, can become hilarious when transplanted into an entirely different setting.

The Godfather’s most recognizable sequences provide an endless supply of opportunities for visual comedy. The wedding celebrations, infamous horse-head incident, and climactic baptism montage have all become familiar enough to audiences that a relatively straightforward recreation can deliver an immediate hilarious punchline.

Animaniacs has a knack for sending up popular movies within a short scene. Its recurring "Goodfeathers" segments, which follow a trio of pigeons modeled on characters from gangster cinema, introduce an especially memorable take on Vito Corleone with the Godpigeon.

An obese, 96-year-old pigeon, the Godpigeon is the boss of the Goodfeathers and makes his authority known by expecting the trio to kiss his foot whenever he appears. His raspy, largely unintelligible mumbling is a direct nod to Brando's performance in The Godfather, and he usually needs Bobby to translate what he's saying.

Despite being treated like an intimidating criminal patriarch, the Godpigeon is generally jovial and would much rather enjoy life as a pigeon than get caught up in the trio's problems. The Godpigeon also appears in several Goodfeathers segments in Animaniacs, so the joke isn't limited to one fleeting appearance.

South Park finds an appropriately petty reason to recreate one of The Godfather Part II's most chilling sequences. In "Toilet Paper," the boys toilet-paper their teacher's house, but Cartman becomes increasingly concerned that Kyle's guilt will eventually urge him to confess. His solution is to take Kyle out on a boat at Stark's Pond and kill him.

The setup deliberately echoes Michael Corleone's attempt to have his brother Fredo murdered during a fishing trip in The Godfather Part II. The accompanying music also references the original film's "Godfather Waltz," giving Cartman's ludicrous plan the sort of ominous atmosphere usually reserved for a major betrayal within a criminal empire.

Of course, Cartman isn't Michael Corleone. He's a vindictive child trying to cover up a school prank, and his murderous plan is undermined by the fact that he only has a Wiffle bat.

Angelica Pickles may be a toddler, but she already understands the value of having people owe her favors. In Rugrats in Paris: The Movie, she adopts the persona of the "Bobfather," complete with a tuxedo and her beloved doll Cynthia positioned in her lap. It's an unmistakable take on Vito Corleone's introduction in The Godfather.

Angelica's fellow babies come to her with requests, and she treats their problems like business propositions. The scene climaxes with Phil and Lil producing the broken head of a wooden rocking horse they found in their crib. They ask the Bobfather what happened, only for Angelica to explain that it's what they get for wiping their boogers on Cynthia.

It's a wonderfully silly adaptation of the infamous horse-head scene, transforming one of cinema's most disturbing threats into a punishment for a pair of toddlers with poor manners.

Jim Abrahams’ Mafia! is an entire comedy built around sending up gangster movies, with The Godfather and The Godfather Part II among its principal targets. It depicts the Cortino family's rise from Sicily to the criminal underworld of America, with the story moving between generations in a deliberately familiar gangster-movie structure.

Mafia!'s most direct Brando parody comes through Lloyd Bridges as the older Vincenzo Cortino. Bridges gets to send up the famous tomato-and-death scene, while the movie repeatedly turns familiar mafia conventions into opportunities for ridiculous sight gags.

Even the family's journey to America gets a joke, when Ellis Island officials attempt to rename the immigrant Vincenzo after his Armani windbreaker. It may not be as consistently sharp as Abrahams' Airplane!, but its affectionate mockery of gangster cinema gives it plenty of entertaining material.

One of the funniest uses of The Godfather in modern animation comes from Disney's Zootopia, which introduces Mr. Big, an Arctic shrew who happens to be one of the city's most intimidating crime bosses. The character recreates Brando's Corleone with a tiny body, a raspy whisper, a formal tuxedo, and an entourage of enormous polar bear enforcers.

This contrast is particularly funny, juxtaposing the gangster grandeur with the diminutive creature. Like The Godfather, Judy Hopps and Nick Wilde are brought before Mr. Big during his daughter Fru Fru's wedding reception.

The entire sequence deliberately recreates the atmosphere and staging of the original film's opening, with Judy and Nick discovering that their investigation has taken them into considerably more dangerous territory. The filmmakers even recreated elements of the original film's set and dialogue.

Homer Simpson's brief career as a Krusty the Clown impersonator takes an unexpected turn when he is mistaken for the real Krusty and becomes embroiled with the Springfield Mafia. In The Simpsons season 6, "Homie the Clown," Homer is captured by Fat Tony to repay Krusty’s debt.

The episode's climactic sequence features Homer attempting to entertain Fat Tony and his associates with a dangerous bicycle stunt. While Fat Tony and his associates are already a Godfather spoof, the episode’s best reference sees Homer hit his head on a series of hanging wine glasses that ring out Nino Rota's famous "Love Theme” from The Godfather.

The mob standoff is perfectly executed and prompts some genuine tension. Of course, The Simpsons quickly undermines this, with the reveal that Krusty’s debt is just $48 becoming the biggest joke.

The Muppets have tackled plenty of famous movies, and their Godfather spoof gives Kermit the Frog a suitably imposing new identity as the Frogfather. In this 1999 Movie Mania short, Fozzie Bear visits him to ask for revenge against Statler and Waldorf, whose constant heckling has finally gone too far.

The premise is already funny because Fozzie's request is essentially a complaint about two elderly audience members who won't stop telling jokes at his expense. Kermit plays the part with the serious demeanor of Brando's Vito Corleone, treating the matter as though it concerns a genuine threat to the family business.

Naturally, the Muppets can't resist turning the idea of a mob hit into something considerably sillier. The Frogfather dispatches his henchmen to carry out a classic, non-lethal execution – smashing cream pies straight into the faces of Statler and Waldorf to permanently silence their heckling

Before Fat Tony became one of The Simpsons’ most recognizable supporting characters, he made his debut in "Bart the Murderer." The season 3 episode sees Bart stumble into a job at a mafia social club after a series of unfortunate events leaves him working as the group's bartender.

The episode draws on both The Godfather and Goodfellas, with Bart becoming increasingly comfortable around the mobsters as they treat him like one of their own. He serves drinks, listens to their stories, and enjoys the perks of his new employment, even as the gang's criminal activities become harder to ignore.

Things take a darker turn when Principal Skinner disappears after Fat Tony and his crew threaten to deal with him. The parody draws much of its humor from dropping a schoolboy into the criminal organization and letting him enjoy the lifestyle before discovering the consequences.

Marlon Brando parodying his own most famous role is an irresistible premise, and Andrew Bergman's The Freshman makes excellent use of it. Brando plays Carmine Sabatini, a powerful New York importer whose resemblance to Vito Corleone is so striking that Matthew Broderick's film student, Clark Kellogg, is immediately taken aback when they meet.

Clark initially accepts a job with Carmine after being introduced by Victor, a small-time crook played by Bruno Kirby. His work soon becomes considerably stranger than he anticipated. One of his first assignments is collecting a Komodo dragon from the airport and delivering it to a secretive gourmet club that specializes in endangered animals.

Brando wholly leans into the mannerisms and screen presence that made Vito Corleone so recognizable. Carmine is imposing, sentimental, and capable of making even an ordinary conversation feel like a negotiation. It's a wonderfully silly extension of his most iconic role.

Modern Family's "Fulgencio" perfectly recreates The Godfather’s famous baptism montage. Like the original, it intercuts the religious ceremony with a series of acts of revenge.

As Phil renounces Satan and all his works during the baptism, his son Luke is busy settling various suburban grievances on his behalf. He releases rats into Karen Sullivan's party, causes Lee Cooper's camper to roll into a lemon tree, shoots balloons covering Phil's billboard, and gets school bully Reuben to admit that he has been lying – who wakes up to discover the head of a stuffed zebra in his bed.

The sequence also plays with the original Godfather's closing image when Phil closes a door on Claire after she starts asking questions. It’s so unexpected but so perfect that it stands as the greatest Godfather parody.

Only one of the two deals is new business. Is the stock's drop an opening?

Bloom Energy (BE +3.42%) turned up in two big data center announcements on Nov. 5. NTT DATA said it had agreed to deploy 900 megawatts of the fuel cell maker's onsite power across data center projects around the world.

And Alphabet's Google said Bloom would build the fuel cells for a 702-megawatt plant powering its first data center in Wyoming, a $17 billion project.

The stock had a bad day anyway. Shares ended at $167.52, off around 16%, amid worries that rising opposition to new data centers after the Nov. 3 midterms might slow the build-out of artificial intelligence (AI) data centers.

The growth stock is still up over 90% this year. But it now sits about 52% below the $345.85 record close it set on June 22.

How much new business did Bloom win on Nov. 5? Less than the headlines may suggest, because just one of the two deals is new. I think the other still matters, though, and the stock looks much more reasonably priced than it did in October.

Google won't buy the fuel cells itself. American Electric Power plans to invest around $4 billion in the 702-megawatt project near Cheyenne, Wyoming, the utility said on Oct. 14. It'll sell the plant's output to Google under a 20-year power purchase agreement, and it expects the plant to be fully running by late 2028.

The fuel cell order came months earlier. In January, an AEP subsidiary agreed to buy solid oxide fuel cells for around $2.65 billion for a plant near Cheyenne. The purchase used most of an option for 900 extra megawatts under AEP's 2024 supply deal with Bloom.

For a sense of scale, that one order is worth over half of Bloom's full-year revenue forecast of $4.4 billion to $4.6 billion for 2026.

So, Google's news didn't add to Bloom's orders. The fuel cells were bought in January, and AEP had already named Google as the plant's customer in October.

That still means something, I think. A fuel cell order backed by a 20-year power contract with Google looks more likely to become revenue on time than one linked to an unnamed buyer.

NTT DATA's deal is the new business, and the companies called it Bloom's biggest international project so far. NTT's data center arm plans to use the fuel cells as its main power source, with the relationship meant to reach gigawatt scale across its global portfolio.

What the release didn't say matters, too. It gave no dollar value, no project locations, and no timeline beyond first shipments starting in 2027. This deal shouldn't show up in Bloom's 2026 revenue, and Bloom hasn't said how much of it falls in 2027 or later.

But 900 megawatts is a lot of fuel cells -- nearly a full year of output at the 1 gigawatt of annual production capacity Bloom's Fremont, California, plant had going into 2026. The company planned to double that capacity by year-end.

Bloom needs deals like this, because its quarter-to-quarter growth slowed sharply in the third quarter. Second-quarter revenue of $1.07 billion was 42% above the first quarter's $751 million. Third-quarter revenue then climbed 16% sequentially to $1.24 billion, up 138% year over year. And October's raised outlook (management's third raise this year) implies a sequential gain of 9% to 25% in the fourth quarter.

That's still strong growth. Keeping it up into 2027 and 2028, though, depends on deals like NTT DATA's moving from announcement to shipment.

Based on the midpoint of management's adjusted earnings forecast for 2026 ($3.10 to $3.40 a share), Bloom stock cost around 52 times earnings at its Nov. 5 close. The same forecast had it at around 67 times earnings at the $217.77 close on Oct. 22. On next year's expected earnings, shares now trade at around 31 times earnings.

Still, 52 times earnings is a premium price. It assumes Bloom keeps growing quickly well past this year.

But Bloom landed a big new deal on Nov. 5 as its shares got much cheaper.

Of course, a slower U.S. data center build-out might delay some deliveries. And October's weak spot hasn't gone away. Bloom's operations burned $27 million in cash in the third quarter, mostly because it booked revenue faster than it could bill customers for it.

Is Bloom stock worth buying after a 16% one-day drop? I'd consider buying some shares at this price, a few at a time. And I'd maybe add more when billing catches up with revenue.

10 Apple products and features that transformed under Tim Cook's leadership

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Apple changed a lot in the last 15 years.

When Tim Cook became Apple's CEO in 2011, he began leading what was already one of the world's most successful technology companies. The iPhone was driving Apple's growth, the iPad was still relatively new and the Mac was at the center of its computing business. Cook inherited an enormous ecosystem, then he spent 15 years finding new ways it could be put to use.

By the end of Cook's tenure, Apple had introduced entirely new product categories, moved the Mac to its own processors, turned the iPhone into a wallet and satellite communicator and built a services business worth more than $100 billion a year. Meanwhile, some of Apple's biggest changes weren't new products, but rather familiar ones that slowly evolved into something else.

There are now more than 2.5 billion active Apple devices, while the company brought in $416.2 billion in net sales in fiscal 2025. Apple's announcement on Cook's transition lays out the scale of the company he leaves behind. But the more interesting story is how under his leadership, Apple products took on entirely new forms and jobs.

When the Apple Watch launched in 2015, it was primarily an iPhone companion for notifications, communication, apps and activity tracking. Apple began expanding its health and fitness capabilities with Apple Watch Series 2, which added built-in GPS, swim tracking and more workout features.

The bigger shift came with Apple Watch Series 4 in 2018. With this, Apple added an ECG app and fall detection, giving the Watch capabilities that went beyond basics like tracking steps. The ECG could record a heart rhythm directly from the wrist, while fall detection could automatically contact emergency services if the wearer appeared to have taken a hard fall and remained immobile.

The watchOS 11 update in 2024 added sleep apnea notifications, the Vitals app and Training Load, giving you more ways to track and interpret health and fitness data. In 2025, the Apple Watch Series 11 introduced sleep scoring and hypertension notifications. While the watch started as an accessory for your iPhone, it now monitors a whole suite of metrics that the iPhone can't.

AirPods could have been just a pair of wireless earbuds for your iPhone, but Apple kept giving them more reasons to exist within the larger Apple ecosystem. Automatic pairing, seamless switching between devices, active noise cancellation and spatial audio made them an attractive option for all your music and other audio.

Apple has given AirPods an important role beyond entertainment, though. Starting with AirPods Pro 2, Apple added a scientifically validated Hearing Test, Hearing Aid functionality for people with mild to moderate hearing loss and Hearing Protection. These hearing health tools make support more accessible to lots of people. Meanwhile, the AirPods Pro 3 added heart rate sensing to track your workout effort with the buds you're likely already wearing while exercising.

Under Cook, Apple took a familiar product and expanded what it could do, without asking people to adopt an entirely new device.

For years, Apple built its own chips for the iPhone, iPad and Apple Watch while relying on Intel processors for the Mac. That changed in 2020, when Apple announced a two-year transition to its own silicon. The move gave Mac computers a common architecture with Apple's other products, providing developers with a more unified platform for building software across the ecosystem.

The first Apple silicon Macs arrived later that year. Apple put the M1 chip in the MacBook Air, 13-inch MacBook Pro and Mac mini, combining the CPU, GPU, Neural Engine and other components into a single system-on-a-chip. This helped Apple push the Mac toward the same performance and power efficiency that had come to define its mobile devices.

The difference between Apple's processors and Intel's isn't just speed. After Apple took over this core piece of Mac hardware, it had holistic control of the hardware design, chips inside and macOS itself. This helped the Mac overcome its midlife crisis.

What started as a convenient way to snap a picture became a sophisticated camera system with multiple lenses, computational photography, Night mode and some serious video capabilities.

The iPhone 11 was a big turning point. It featured an Ultra Wide camera, Night mode and Deep Fusion, which uses machine learning to process photos pixel by pixel for more detail and less noise. The iPhone 12 Pro then added Apple ProRAW, combining Apple's computational photography with the flexibility of a RAW file for those who wanted more control over their images.

Apple's more recent iPhones have continued this progress. The iPhone 15 Pro brought a 48MP main camera, multiple focal-length options, 48MP ProRAW and professional video features including ProRes recording to external storage. Our iPhone 15 review highlighted the camera as a major part of the upgrade. Later, our iPhone 17 review covered more camera improvements, including a new Center Stage front camera and upgraded rear cameras. For many people, their iPhone camera is the way they take pictures, and what all other smartphone cameras are judged against.

Apple Pay is easy to take for granted because it feels so normal now. But in 2014, putting a payment system directly into the iPhone people already carried everywhere gave the device a new role: replacing the cards in their wallet. Apple Pay eventually expanded beyond in-store purchases to apps, websites and other Apple devices like the Apple Watch.

10 years after its launch, the company stated that Apple Pay was used by hundreds of millions of people across 78 regions, at millions of websites and apps and tens of millions of stores. It was also supported by more than 11,000 banks and "network partners" at that milestone. Apple Pay later expanded to third-party iOS and desktop browsers, providing more ways to check out online without typing your card details into every site.

That idea of leaving your physical wallet behind has grown beyond payments, too. Wallet can hold other items like event tickets, transit cards, keys and even eligible IDs. In 2025, Apple started expanding its Digital ID for use with participating US states, plus support for Japan's My Number Card. Expanded Apple Wallet ticket features have also added venue maps, parking directions and other event information.

Some of the iPhone's biggest changes under Cook had more to do with connectivity than the phone itself, as Apple kept expanding the ways your iPhone can connect to the world around it. The iPhone 12 brought 5G to Apple's phones in 2020, while MagSafe introduced a ring of magnets that made wireless charging easier and created an all-new suite of snap-on accessories.

The iPhone 14 added something even more ambitious: direct satellite connections to message emergency services when cellular and Wi-Fi coverage isn't available. Apple also added the ability to share a location through Find My via satellite. In 2024, satellite connectivity was expanded to support Messages via satellite for messaging friends when you have no other options.

Not everything that changed under Tim Cook came in a box. Apple's services business grew from a relatively small part of the company into a $109.2 billion business, making it a core part of Apple's revenue alongside the iPhone.

The category is broader than just subscriptions. It includes the App Store, advertising, cloud services, Apple Music, Apple TV, Apple Pay and more. In 2025, the App Store alone averaged more than 850 million weekly users, while Apple said developers had earned more than $550 billion through the platform since 2008. Apple's services revenue also hit a quarterly record in early 2026, topping $30 billion.

Hardware still accounts for most of Apple's revenue, but the company's various services provide a huge business built around what people do after they buy an Apple device.

Apple had been selling digital content long before the Apple TV streaming service (formerly called Apple TV+) arrived in 2019. But with it, the company was no longer just offering access to content made by others. Apple TV+ launched with an original catalog of shows, movies and documentaries, including The Morning Show, See and For All Mankind.

Apple quickly built a reputation around those originals. CODA became the first streaming film to win the Academy Award for Best Picture in 2022, while shows like Ted Lasso and Severance became some of the service's biggest hits.

Apple TV+ earned a record 81 Emmy nominations across 14 Apple Original titles in 2025, led by Severance with 27 nominations and The Studio with 23. Both shows also took home awards that year. By September 2025, Apple said its originals had accumulated 620 wins and 2,816 award nominations.

Vision Pro is different from almost everything else Apple released under Cook. Instead of putting another screen in front of you, Apple built a computer that adds your digital life into the space around you. When Apple unveiled Vision Pro in 2023, it called the device its first "spatial computer," with visionOS and an interface controlled by your eyes, hands and voice.

What may be more interesting than the $3,499 headset is where Apple thinks computing could go next. Apps can float around a room instead of being confined to a traditional display, which provides immersive environments with three-dimensional content. More than 600 apps and games built specifically for Vision Pro were available when it launched.

As our M5 Vision Pro review found, the device is unlikely to become the next big Apple platform unless future revisions make it more accessible. But that's why it's worth a mention here: Vision Pro is Apple trying to create a new platform instead of solely relying on what's worked in the past.

Its bigger innovation is how the tracker taps into Apple's existing Find My network, which uses nearby Apple devices to detect an AirTag and anonymously report its approximate location to its owner. An AirTag doesn't need its own cellular connection or GPS to be useful.

Apple describes AirTag as "the bestselling item finder", and continues expanding what it can do. The second-generation AirTag, released in 2026, added a longer Bluetooth range and more precise finding. Apple's Share Item Location feature also lets you securely share an item's location with participating airlines to help recover lost luggage.

The second-generation AirTag's primary usefulness comes from Apple's enormous network of devices, giving it access to a vast web to help locate a lost item.

We Tracked The World's Longest Airbus A321XLR Flight: Here's The Detour It Takes Every Day

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

On October 25, IndiGo started operating its flights between Mumbai Airport (BOM) and Amsterdam Schiphol Airport(AMS) with the Airbus A321XLR, replacing the leased Boeing 787-9 Dreamliners previously used on the route. At approximately 3,707 nautical miles (6,865 km), this is now the world's longest scheduled route operated by the A321XLR, with a scheduled flight time of around 10 hours from India to the Netherlands.

However, the actual distance flown is considerably longer due to airspace restrictions across the Middle East and South Asia. Using the Simple Flying Flight Tracker, we examined the routing of flight 22 from Amsterdam to Mumbai on November 1, as well as the flight times recorded during the aircraft's first 11 days on the route. Interestingly, the data shows that although the A321XLR is smaller and generally slower than the Boeing 787-9 Dreamliner, its average flight time to Amsterdam has been shorter.

On November 1, IndiGo flight 22 departed Amsterdam at 2:31 PM local time, operated by an A321XLR registered VT-NLC. According to our flight tracking data, the aircraft spent approximately nine hours and nine minutes in the air before landing in Mumbai at 4:10 AM local time the following morning. Rather than following the shortest possible route toward India, the aircraft followed a more southern trajectory through Europe and the Middle East.

After departing Amsterdam, the aircraft headed southeast across Central Europe before continuing toward Turkey and Iraq. From there, the route passed through the Gulf region and Oman, avoiding Iranian airspace, before continuing toward India's western coast and eventually Mumbai. The tracking data shows how the aircraft avoids both Pakistan and Iran, despite these countries lying along the more direct route between Mumbai and Amsterdam.

The distance between Mumbai and Amsterdam is about 3,707 nautical miles (6,865 km) along the shortest route. However, according to Aerospace Global News, the necessary detours can increase the actual distance flown to approximately 4,200 nautical miles (7,778 km), or around 13% longer. This puts the flight much closer to the A321XLR's advertised maximum range of 4,700 nautical miles (8,704 km).

IndiGo's 8-hour Bali flights showcase the A321XLR's potential to transform aviation route planning as we know it.

One of the main reasons for IndiGo's detour is the ongoing closure of Pakistani airspace to Indian airlines, in place since April 2025 following rising tensions between the two countries. According to the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO), the restrictions apply to aircraft registered in India, as well as those owned, operated, or leased by Indian carriers. Pakistan has since extended the restrictions several times.

At the same time, the ongoing conflict in the Middle East has also made flying through Iran increasingly difficult. Although Iranian airspace is not subject to the same ban for Indian airlines, the European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA) currently advises operators to avoid Iranian airspace at all altitudes.

For IndiGo, Pakistan's restrictions and the security situation in the Middle East considerably reduce available routing options between India and Europe. The airline must therefore follow a longer route, which increases fuel requirements and operating costs. The implications are particularly significant for the A321XLR, as the aircraft has far less flexibility to carry additional fuel and passengers on such a long flight than a widebody such as the Boeing 787-9 previously used on the route.

Super Monkey Ball’s main event lets players choose one out of four monkeys. The simian is then enclosed in a ball that resembles the capsules we used to collect from toy machines at the supermarket as children. From there, they roll toward goal posts on floating platforms, collecting bananas along the way to improve their score. If they fall off the platform or run out of time before reaching their destination, they lose a life.

In the traditional sense of the term, Super Monkey Ball isn’t a platformer. There’s no sign of Mario, for instance, and jumping isn’t a central mechanic. But there are platforms and pits, so the label fits in a more general sense.

When Super Monkey Ball debuted, it reminded me immediately of an old NES favorite of mine, Marble Madness. But that pioneering older game could be completed in five minutes. There were only a few stages, all of them far too short for my liking. I wanted more of that sort of experience, which Super Monkey Ball delivered. There are dozens of stages. At the start of Super Monkey Ball, rolling through the goal tape is easy enough. Even by the end of that first set of courses, however, the process grows tense. Then there are 30 more courses of more difficult vintage to tackle, and an even larger and more demanding batch after that.

As they advance, players control not the monkeys themselves but the platforms they occupy. Mastery requires the player to tilt each board, which in turn allows the balls to build up enough speed to launch from small bumps in order to clear gaps, or to negotiate rougher terrain, all without moving so quickly that it becomes impossible to proceed along razor-thin beams that span wide gaps.

A full-fledged remake of that original game could add visual polish. It might also double down on the personality of the starring characters, who make irresistible mascots. That compilation is welcome, gathering several recent installments in the series, like Banana Rumble. It also includes Banana Mania, which remastered the stages from the original GameCube releases. But none of those modern installments have managed to have quite the impact that the original one once did, and might again if it were to receive an ambitious remake like Nintendo produced with Ocarina of Time. Those white cameras mounted above traffic lights serve a little-known purpose

📅 Nov 09, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Unlike Flock cameras, they're only meant to keep traffic flowing.

Automatic license plate readers (ALPRs) are nothing new, but reported abuses have made drivers understandably wary of the cameras made by Flock and other companies, to the point of wanting to find out where they are so they can be avoided. While that added attention has made intersection cameras seem more visible than ever, not every camera you see mounted above the road serves the same purpose. Most cameras on roadways aren't used to track individual drivers, but rather to monitor traffic conditions.

Aside from ALPRs, cameras meant to track people running red lights are also controversial. They're banned in many states, and they're rare even where they're allowed. Far more common are cameras used to monitor traffic conditions in real time or automatically control traffic lights.

Distinct from cameras used to catch drivers speeding or running red lights, traffic cameras aren't designed to capture evidence for law enforcement. Instead, they're meant to be watched live to monitor the current state of traffic and keep an eye out for accidents. This real-time data can be used by local transportation departments to help keep traffic moving, by overriding automatic light timings in some cases, or to alert first responders when needed. These systems can also be used to track traffic patterns over time, helping traffic engineers determine when changes need to be made to roadways or signals to control congestion.

In many cases, the cameras mounted on traffic lights are tied to automatic signaling systems rather than providing a live video feed. These cameras send data to control systems that determine when lights should cycle. Engineers can designate a section of the video frame that the camera should monitor for the presence of cars. When a car enters that space, it triggers the light to change based on a timing sequence that pre-programmed for each individual roadway. In addition to video cameras, these systems often use radar, infrared cameras and electromagnetic detectors built into roads to detect the presence of vehicles.

Exactly what the cameras on roadways are used for varies widely based on where they are. Local laws determine which types of cameras are allowed to be installed in the first place, and how they're used depends on the needs of a particular intersection. While there's plenty of justified concern over the prevalence of Flock cameras and other ALPRs, chances are when you see a camera mounted above a traffic light, it's just there to keep things moving.

Denis Villeneuve Teases James Bond Progress at LACMA Art + Film Gala: ‘I’m Having a Great Time’

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Villeneuve and artist Vija Celmins were feted at the gala, which raised nearly $7 million, setting a new record

When Denis Villeneuve arrived at LACMA on Saturday night, his black Gucci tuxedo made him look more like James Bond than a director who’s spent the last decade in the desert of Arrakis. Perhaps that’s fitting since the filmmaker is currently putting the final touches on the 70mm Imax print of “Dune: Part Three” while simultaneously starting work on his Bond movie.

“It’s a very personal film and a project that is very close to my heart,” Villeneuve told Variety about wrapping up his “Dune” trilogy. “It’s also the project that is the completion of 10 years of work in the deep desert. I can just say that it has been a privilege of a lifetime to spend, and I enjoy every minute of it.”

On the topic of James Bond, news broke Friday that Villeneuve and producers Amy Pascal and David Heyman are inching closer to selecting the new 007, with finalists including Robert Pattinson, Nicholas Hoult, Callum Turner, Josh O’Connor and Jack Lowden.

On the red carpet, Villeneuve politely declined to comment about the casting speculation.

“I should not comment right now because every word I’m going to say is going to be scrutinized,” Villeneuve said. “I would just say that I’m having a great time.”

But inside the gala, he was ready to make some news. “For many years, LACMA has been a temple for me. Tonight feels like home, and to express my gratitude. With the blessing of my new friends at Amazon, I will reveal to you tonight who will be the next James Bond,” Villeneuve began, causing an audible stir in the crowd.

“The actor who will play the next James Bond is…” Villeneuve continued, pausing dramatically.

“And I will stop right now because Amy Pascal is about to have a heart attack,” he said, laughing. (Yes, the Canadian director was only joking.)

Read on for more from Villeneuve’s remarks and a peek inside the high-fashion gala.

Nicole Kidman admitted that she was sweating bullets as she prepared to present to Denis Villeneuve.

“I’m just going to disclose I have sweaty armpits,” Kidman, wearing Chanel, joked. “Let’s keep it real here.”

Then, Kidman revealed that she’d traveled 15 hours from Australia (overnight!) to make the event because she’s such an admirer of Villeneuve — even though they haven’t worked together yet.

“About six years ago, I made a commercial for a little company called AMC Theaters,” Kidman said. “So much of what inspired me to be a part of that campaign and to champion the importance of returning to cinemas was the opportunity to experience films from the likes of tonight’s honoree, Denis Villeneuve.”

Despite the fact that Kidman and Villeneuve had never really met before Saturday night, the Oscar winner said she feels like she’s gotten to know “the deepest parts” of Villeneuve through his films.

“That is what extraordinary filmmakers do. They let you in. They reveal themselves to us through their work,” Kidman said. “They invite you into their imaginations, into their dreams, into their questions, and into their fears.”

She continued: “I feel like that transcends anything that needs an introduction, because his films explore the great mysteries of our existence: time, memory, identity, destiny, the things we cannot explain, and the possibility that there is something that we do not understand. That we will never understand.”

Kidman also recalled her experience watching an 11 p.m. screening of “Dune” at her local cinema and plans to return to the theater for “Dune: Part Three” when it hits theaters on Dec. 18.

“I will always be one of your audience,” she said.

Onstage, Denis Villeneuve reflected on a fortuitous visit to LACMA while he was preparing to film 2016’s “Arrival.”

“I was looking for guidance. Then I walk into the James Turrell exhibition … I watched people standing in a room in front of a brilliantly colored light in silence and in awe. And then I thought, ‘OK, I know how to direct the film now.'”

Villeneuve shared that he discovered the power of images early in life. “My first contact with the English language as a boy in Quebec was through cinema,” he explained. “I didn’t understand the words, so I watched the images, and I imagined what the characters were saying.”

In addition to the support from his “Dune” star Timothée Chalamet and Kidman, Villeneuve was also saluted in a clip reel that included filmmakers Steven Spielberg, James Cameron, Chloé Zhao, Alfonso Cuarón, Alejandro González Iñárritu, composer Hans Zimmer and producer Mary Parent.

“When I was 12, all my friends were raving about hockey, and all I cared was about Steven Spielberg,” Villeneuve said. “He became my hero. Steven is the reason I’m standing here today, and I’m lucky enough now to call him a friend.

“Then came James Cameron,” Villeneuve continued. “These are titans. James has always been ahead of his time, and I hope he was wrong when he predicted Skynet, the self-aware artificial intelligence Terminator. These friendships meant the world to me because I’ve always felt isolated as a director in my own bubble.”

He saluted Zhao, Cuarón and Iñárritu as “three phenomenal artists pushing the boundaries of cinematic expression in the most original ways.”

Villeneuve also thanked his frequent collaborators and his crews. “It takes many people to bring cinema to life. I want to honor their contribution in making my dreams come to life on screen,” he said.

At the heart of each of Villeneuve’s films, there’s a question that he seeks to answer. “Dune Part Three,” Villeneuve (seen here with his wife and producing partner Tanya Lapointe) centers on Chalamet’s Paul and Zendaya’s Chani fighting to give their children a better world than the one they were given.

“We are at the crossroads, asking what defines a human being and what human expression is worth,” Villeneuve said. “Every parent on Earth is a little worried about the world our children will enter it.”

“For a filmmaker today, it is very real. I have a choice. I can create something with a prompt,” Villeneuve said, referencing the advent of AI tools. “Or I can go into the desert with actors, with a crew, and go on a real adventure where I learn about life and become better at my craft. Those are two different things.”

Even though one of those options is “more expensive than the other,” Villeneuve cracked, he firmly believes that the process of filmmaking is more important than the result.

“The process is how the human experience acquires its meaning,” Villeneuve said. “In this moment in time, it is more important than ever that human beings keep making art together. That is the heritage I want to leave to my children.”

Anya Taylor-Joy dropped by the gala to support her “Dune: Part Three” filmmaker Villeneuve. What makes him unique?

“It’s the vision,” Taylor-Joy told Variety. “But it’s also the fact that when you’re working with him, he makes you feel that you’re a part of something bigger than yourself. He never makes anybody feel small, and he leads with such love and conviction, and I adore him.”

Anya Taylor-Joy, looking like a piece of art in archival Pierre Cardin, also weighed in on Villeneuve’s Bond movie.

“I’m excited to see what he’s going to do with all the fight sequences. I think he got a taste of it on ‘Dune,’ and I think he might be really, really good at it,” Taylor-Joy said.

The actress has also co-starred with four of the five reported finalists — Robert Pattinson (in “Dune”), Nicholas Hoult (“The Menu”), Josh O’Connor (“Emma”) and Callum Turner (“Emma”).

“I’ve been very lucky with my older brothers in film,” Taylor-Joy added. “They are delightful. They have taken very good care of me, and they’re all wonderful to watch on screen. So I’m excited to see who it’s going to be.”

Viola Davis — here with Julius Tennon, both in Gucci — recalled working with Villeneuve on 2013’s “Prisoners.”

“Denis is one of the greatest directors I’ve ever worked with,” the Oscar winner told Variety. “My favorite thing is when he loved a take and he said ‘Perfect,’ but in a French accent. I would say, ‘Say it again, Denis.'”

For her next act, Davis will star in “The Children of Blood and Bone,” reuniting with “The Woman King” director Gina Prince-Bythewood and star Thuso Mbedu. “She’s an extraordinary talent,” Davis said. “I think this is going to be her time.”

“Club Kid” filmmaker Jordan Firstman told Variety he most admires Villeneuve’s “restraint” as a director.

“He really knows how to hold back and when to go for it and let you ride the worm,” Firstman, wearing Ferragamo, told Variety. “That’s what I tell all my students.”

As Villeneuve pivots to the Bond franchise, Firstman has a pitch: “I want to be a Bond gay. Like, instead of the Bond girls, I think they should make them gay guys.”

Benito Skinner, wearing ERL, joked that he was on a mission to figure out who Villeneuve chose to play the new 007.

“He must be so over it, like I have to stop fucking reading about it. We gotta just pick it. Are we close? I’m gonna ask him tonight,” Skinner told Variety, laughing. (For the record, he’d like to play Bond’s tech genius, Q. “I’m like, use that pen. It’s a gun.”)

“He builds worlds so beautifully,” Skinner said. “We don’t get that anymore. We need blockbusters. We need butts in seats at AMC the Grove. He’s just a true visionary, and we don’t have many, and that scares me. Even if [the world] is as insane as ‘Dune,’ you still feel the humanity in it.”

Jessica Williams (sparkling in Phan Huy) chose “Arrival” as her favorite Villeneuve film.

“I just really liked the way people responded to aliens,” Williams told Variety. “People were so overwhelmed with it, and I thought that was a really cool take.”

As a fan of the filmmaker, the “Shrinking” star quickly conjured a pitch to join his Bond movie.

“I’d be a really cool-ass logistics person. I feel like I’d be really good in the Bond office,” she said. “I play a lot of parts where I’m like a lady that knows stuff, and I’d like to be a lady that knows stuff in that universe.”

“La Bola Negra” filmmakers Javier Ambrossi and Javier Calvó (aka Los Javis), both in Gucci, said Villeneuve’s 2010 film “Incendies” was a key reference to their 2023 series “La Mesías.”

“He’s one of the best directors,” Ambrossi told Variety, noting the innovative ways Villeneuve uses music and stages his scenes.

Calvó added: “He’s so full of idea and the way he works with actors — with the small parts [or] with the big parts, like the stars — it’s amazing. He’s a master.”

Ashley Park — seen here with Anthony Ramos and Alex Garcia and wearing Prabal Gurung — said her favorite Villeneuve projects are the “Dune” movies and “Sicario.”

“Watching that film really shook me in a lot of different ways,” the “Emily in Paris” star told Variety about “Sicario.”

Vija Celmins (seen here with fellow honoree Denis Villeneuve, presenter Nicole Kidman and LACMA CEO Michael Govan, CEO) delivered a charming acceptance speech.

“You guys are just crazy party people,” Celmins said onstage, as the well-heeled crowd burst into laughter.

The recognition from LACMA is special, Celmins explained, because “Los Angeles had a big influence on me. I had never lived next to the ocean, and I have never seen the land, and it sort of crept into my work.”

After Rachel Kushner presented the award to Vija Celmins, the pair posed with LACMA CEO Michael Govan.

During the program, Art+Film Gala co-chair Eva Chow announced she’d be stepping down from her post after 15 years.

“Together, we have raised close to $60 million for LACMA. That is something we can all be incredibly proud of,” Chow said. (She co-chairs the event with Oscar winner Leonardo DiCaprio.)

“It is difficult to put 15 years of memories, friendship, experiences into a few words,” Chow added. “To have started with an idea and to see it grow into one of Los Angeles’ most celebrated traditions has been one of great privileges of my life. I leave this chapter with tremendous pride, wonderful memories, and above all gratitude.”

Mammograms May Offer Early Insight Into Cardiovascular Disease

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Mammograms are widely used to help detect breast cancer, but they may also offer insight into heart health.

The routine X-ray screening may identify breast arterial calcification (BAC), which is the presence of calcium deposits within the walls of the small arteries in the breast.

On a mammogram, BAC is commonly identified as thin, white, parallel lines that give a “tram-track” appearance.

While BAC is harmless to the breasts, it is associated with a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular disease. A 2022 study found women with BAC were 51% more likely to develop heart disease or experience a stroke than those without it.

Laxmi Mehta, MD, a cardiologist at The Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center, said BAC is associated with cardiovascular risk factors, such as diabetes, high blood pressure, and high cholesterol. Additionally, BAC has been connected to:

“The reasons for BAC resulting in higher risks for cardiovascular disease are not definitely determined; however, there are some potential mechanisms, one being that they share similar risk factors that result in vascular injury or metabolic stress that result in vascular calcification and atherosclerosis,” Mehta told Healthline.

Cheng-Han Chen, MD, interventional cardiologist and medical director of the Structural Heart Program at MemorialCare Saddleback Medical Center, added that because BAC and heart disease share many of the same risk factors, “calcium in breast arteries therefore suggests disease in the heart (coronary) and brain arteries as well,” he told Healthline.

While 1 in 39 females in the United States dies from breast cancer each year, cardiovascular disease causes one out of every three deaths, resulting in 1 death per minute.

“Cardiovascular disease is still the most common cause of death in premenopausal women,” Richard Reitherman, MD, PhD, a radiologist and medical director of breast imaging at MemorialCare Breast Center at Orange Coast Medical Center, told Healthline.

“The presentation of cardiovascular disease in premenopausal women is notoriously different than in postmenopausal women, primarily [because] there are no typical symptoms such as chest pain.”

Reitherman said that a prevalent and common health screening procedure such as a mammogram can lead to the early identification of women with silent cardiovascular disease.

“Cardiovascular outcome studies that have included breast arterial calcifications as a risk factor are increasingly reported in the medical literature,” he said.

“The hope is that identifying younger women with silent cardiovascular risks [like BAC along with] appropriate integrated risk identification, analysis, and interventions can significantly reduce mortality in this age group.”

Mehta said there are several reasons BAC has been overlooked as a risk factor for heart disease.

“Mammograms are completed for assessment of breast cancer, and BAC has been historically an ‘incidental’ finding, which is not clinically relevant from an oncologic standpoint,” she said.

Additionally, there is no standardization on quantification of BAC severity, nor is it a required component of mammogram reports, said Mehta.

However, routine reporting of BAC on mammograms is an urgent goal of the American College of Radiology, Society of Breast Imaging, and Medicare, said Reitherman.

In fact, it is gaining traction in the United States. A new Maryland law, passed on October 1, requires mammography centers to notify women when a BAC is detected on their mammogram.

“Automated identification and reporting of these calcifications detected on screening mammograms will become increasingly prevalent in the coming months [and] years,” said Reitherman.

The next step is to identify this group of women to primary care physicians so that appropriate risk evaluation can take place, Reitherman added.

In addition to doctors routinely screening for conditions related to heart disease, such as diabetes, high blood pressure, and high cholesterol, he hopes BAC will also be routinely considered.

“Under current development are protocols to identify patients needing a cardiologist’s training and expertise to appropriately stratify this group of patients,” said Reitherman.

“The common healthcare behavior [of] having regular mammograms not only reduces risk of morbidity and mortality from breast cancer but will hopefully contribute to the reduction of morbidity and mortality from cardiovascular disease in younger women.”

He said this type of integrated healthcare approach demonstrates cross-specialty integration and coordination among women, radiologists, and their healthcare providers to improve women’s lives.

“This is an amazing game-changing opportunity,” Reitherman said.

Mehta said it’s reasonable for women to ask if BAC is present on their mammogram report.

“But if it is not uniformly reported by the radiologist, the individual clinician may not have the answer,” she said.

More research is needed before BAC on mammograms can be used routinely to predict heart disease.

Finding out your BAC could provide additional information to help you and your doctors decide how aggressively to address your cardiovascular risk factors, Chen said.

Reitherman agreed. “The goal of reducing morbidity and mortality from any cause such as cardiovascular disease is based on recognition of risk factors, followed by appropriate lifestyle and pharmacologic interventions,” he said.

I Gave My Accounts-Payable Agent Hindsight Memory. It Stopped Asking.

Every month, an invoice from a steel distributor arrives priced 3% above the purchase order, and every month someone in accounts payable stops, opens the contract, finds clause 7.2 (monthly steel-index adjustment, capped at 5%), and approves it. The matching software flags it perfectly. It just has no idea that this exact question was answered last month, and the month before.

That is the gap I built Ledgerwise for: an agent that sits after the 3-way match and resolves invoice exceptions the way your desk resolved them last time — and knows when that history isn't enough.

An AP desk matches three documents: the purchase order (what we agreed to buy), the goods receipt (what actually arrived), and the invoice (what the vendor wants paid). When they disagree you get an exception: a price variance, a short shipment, a GST rate that doesn't match the contract, a surcharge line nobody ordered, a re-sent duplicate, or a vendor letter saying "we've changed our bank account."

Detecting those is a solved problem, and in Ledgerwise it's deliberately boring: matching.py is deterministic Python with no model and no memory. It finds exceptions and measures them — a percentage for price, rupees for a surcharge, units for a short shipment.

The memory layer is Hindsight, an open-source agent memory system from Vectorize. I picked it over rolling my own vector store because AP history isn't a pile of documents to search — it's a set of decisions with actors, amounts and dates, and I wanted something that extracts and organises those facts rather than just embedding paragraphs.

The obvious design is: recall similar past decisions, and if the top result says APPROVE, approve. Run that against the replay data and it looks great for a few weeks — until a logistics vendor sends a ₹6,800 fuel surcharge. Every earlier surcharge from that vendor was approved, but they were ₹1,200–₹2,400, and the agreed cap is ₹2,500. Similarity says "same vendor, same exception, approved." The money says otherwise.

That case reshaped the whole design. The question stopped being "what does memory say?" and became "is memory enough here?"

Each human decision is retained as one string with two parts: a prose sentence that Hindsight's retain pipeline extracts facts and entities from, and a structured line my policy can parse back out.

document_id keeps a re-decision on the same invoice from becoming a duplicate memory, and timestamp uses the invoice date rather than "now", so temporal questions work.

The magnitude matters as much as the decision. "Approved a 2.8% variance" and "approved a 4.2% variance" are different precedents.

1. The reason field is the product. I made the UI refuse to save a decision without a typed reason. Those sentences are what escalations show, what reflect summarises, and what makes the memory auditable. "Approved" is a log line; "approved under clause 7.2, index sheet attached" is knowledge.

2. Store the magnitude, not just the outcome. Similarity search will happily match "same vendor, same exception" and ignore that this one is three times bigger. Every precedent carries its number, and the policy compares numbers.

3. Decide what memory is allowed to override before you write any recall code. Bank changes, unknown POs and anything outside a learned range are human-only by design. Writing those guardrails first made the rest of the policy much easier to reason about.

4. Don't let the agent cite itself. Retaining the agent's own decisions is useful for audit and reflection. Counting them as precedent is how a system drifts.

5. Make memory configurable, not magical. A skeptical, literal bank disposition (skepticism 5, literalism 4) fit an AP desk better than the defaults. One approval of a 3% variance should not become permission for "price variances" in general.

The honest limitation: Ledgerwise is only as good as the reasons people type. A desk that clicks "approve" with "ok" gets an agent that can count but can't explain.

Drummer Chuck Comeau talks TikTok, Good Things Festival, and why a farewell tour is "not in the cards"

Simple Plan have been playing the same songs for 25 years. Lately, they’re playing them to a lot of people hearing them live for the first time.

“Every night we ask people, ‘Hey, how many people are here for the first time?’” drummer Chuck Comeau tells Billboard. “Sometimes it’s like 85% have never seen the band.”

The Montreal pop-punk band is drawing fans who weren’t born when “I’m Just a Kid” arrived in 2002, alongside the original listeners who are now bringing their own kids to shows. “And you’re like, ‘What are you talking about?’” Comeau says.

“There’s nothing cooler than what’s happening to our band right now,” he adds.

Much of that can be traced to “I’m Just a Kid” going viral on TikTok, where it soundtracked a challenge encouraging users to recreate their childhood photos. Comeau calls the trend a “gigantic gift,” but says meeting fans online has always come naturally to the band, which was filming backstage antics and sharing them through its website and DVDs long before TikTok existed.

“It’s the same thing,” he says. “It’s just a new platform.”

The renewed attention builds on a strong early run. The band’s 2004 album Still Not Getting Any… debuted at No. 3 on the Billboard 200, while “Perfect” and “Welcome to My Life” reached Nos. 24 and 40, respectively, on the Billboard Hot 100.

Next up is a return to Australia for Good Things Festival, where Simple Plan join a bill led by Limp Bizkit and featuring Spiritbox, the All-American Rejects, Bad Religion and MxPx. The festival hits Melbourne’s Flemington Racecourse on Dec. 4, Sydney Showground on Dec. 5 and Brisbane Showgrounds on Dec. 6, marking the band’s third trip Down Under in three years.

“We love Australia. What can I say?” Comeau says. “Any excuse, we come back.”

The lineup mixes pop-punk with nu-metal, hardcore and heavier rock, the kind of bill Comeau admits might once have made him nervous. “Being on this lineup would have been a bit more intimidating 25 years ago,” he says.

He credits streaming with breaking down the scene boundaries that once divided fans, making it easier for younger listeners to move between genres. The band has seen it firsthand at European festivals, sharing stages with acts like Slipknot.

“At first, you’re a little terrified,” he says. “You’re like, ‘How’s that gonna go?’ And then you actually realize that people are stoked.”

He has his own Good Things wish list. Bad Religion, whom he calls his favorite band, are near the top, along with Swedish punks Millencolin, who are playing Pennybridge Pioneers in full. He’s also keen to finally see Limp Bizkit live. “I’ve seen a lot of crazy videos right now that are popping up everywhere,” he says.

Meanwhile, Simple Plan have started writing their seventh studio album, with work set to continue into 2027. There’s no release date, but Comeau says the band has a batch of songs it’s excited about, and no intention of rushing.

“We’ve been around for 25 years and we only have six albums,” he says. “So we take our time.”

The record will follow 2022’s Harder Than It Looks, and Comeau says the aim is to refine the band’s sound rather than overhaul it. “We know who we are,” he says. “We’re not going to turn around and make a black metal record.”

The harder challenge is competing with the band’s own past. Songs that soundtracked fans’ teenage years carry a weight that new material has to earn.

“When you get to this stage of your career, you’re almost playing against yourself,” Comeau says. “You’re playing against your history.”

“You’re not the new shiny young band anymore,” he adds. “So you’ve got to work harder to have people pay attention.”

That’s why he won’t put a deadline on it. “There’s never a good time for a bad record, and there’s never a bad time for a good record,” he says.

Next year also marks 25 years since the band’s debut album, No Pads, No Helmets…Just Balls. Comeau says a special-edition vinyl and a few anniversary performances are on the table, but not a full album tour.

“I think we’re bigger than just one album,” he says. “But that album was really consequential for us. It’s the one that started it all.”

Another milestone arrives March 9, 2027, when the band releases its first children’s picture book, I’m Just a Kid: The Story of Simple Plan, illustrated by Jomike Tejido. Comeau says it grew out of watching the band’s original fans grow up and start families of their own.

“We were with them throughout their difficult moments,” he says, recalling how songs about breakups, school and friendships became part of listeners’ teenage years. “Fast-forward 20 years, 25 years, now they’re starting families, they’re having kids. We want to be there with them in this new chapter of their lives.”

The book explores friendship and perseverance and includes nods for longtime fans. “It’s as much for the parents as it is for the kids,” Comeau says.

For all the anniversaries and nostalgia, Simple Plan have never broken up, gone on hiatus or staged a reunion, which is rare among their peers. Comeau believes the years since the pandemic have been some of the band’s best, with bigger crowds and a sharper focus on the live show.

“When we were young, you were going out and you were partying,” he says. “Now it’s like, no, we don’t do that anymore. We’re all doing yoga and stretching.”

“We’ve always just kept going,” he says. “I feel like there’s a lot of work to be done.”

“There’s no talk about a farewell tour,” Comeau says. “Not in the cards for now.”

However, his journey into the world of comic book-inspired adventures began long before he picked up Captain America's shield.

In 2005, Evans played Johnny Storm / The Human Torch, in Tim Story's Fantastic Four. He reprised the role in 2007's Fantastic Four: Rise of the Silver Surfer, bringing plenty of charisma and humor to the hot-headed member of Marvel's First Family. Nearly 20 years later, Evans stepped into the shoes of Johnny Storm again with a memorable cameo in Deadpool & Wolverine.

Between his appearances as the Human Torch and his eventual transformation into Captain America, however, Evans headlined another superhero adventure that deserves considerably more attention than it reserves. Released at a time when Hollywood was becoming increasingly fascinated with comic book stories about people with extraordinary abilities, this ambitious science-fiction thriller offered a decidedly different take on the genre.

It featured an intriguing mythology, an international setting, and a collection of characters whose powers made them anything but traditional superheroes. Unfortunately, the film never became the franchise starter its creators might have hoped for.

Push is a 2009 science-fiction action thriller directed by Paul McGuigan and written by David Bourla. Released in theaters on February 6, 2009, the film stars Evans alongside Dakota Fanning, Camilla Belle, and Djimon Hounsou. Rather than adapting an established comic book, Push creates an entirely original world populated by people with extraordinary psychic abilities. Its premise revolves around a secret government organization called Division, which captures and experiments on individuals with special powers in an effort to turn them into living weapons.

Evans plays Nick Gant, a young man with telekinetic abilities who has spent much of his life hiding from Division. His circumstances change when he encounters Cassie Holmes (Fanning), a teenage clairvoyant who can see glimpses of the future. Together, they search for Kira (Belle), a woman with the ability to manipulate people's thoughts and memories, while attempting to stay one step ahead of Division and its dangerous agents. As their mission develops, the characters must use their individual abilities to outsmart an organization that knows almost everything about their capabilities.

What makes Push particularly interesting is how it approaches the superhero genre. Instead of relying on familiar costumes, secret identities, and spectacular displays of power, the movie builds its mythology around different categories of psychic abilities. Telekinetics, clairvoyants, and mind controllers operate within a shared world, and their powers come with limitations that make confrontations more about strategy than brute strength.

The film’s Hong Kong setting also gives it a distinctive visual identity, combining neon-lit streets, cramped apartments, and elaborate action sequences. The result is closer to a supernatural espionage thriller than a conventional superhero blockbuster.

Despite its ambitious concept and talented cast, Push struggled to win over critics when it arrived in theaters. Its complicated mythology and purportedly convoluted storytelling became major points of criticism, with some reviewers arguing that the movie introduced too many ideas without giving them enough room to develop. Rotten Tomatoes currently lists the film with a 23% critics' score, reflecting the largely unfavorable response it received.

Nevertheless, dismissing Push entirely overlooks some of its most appealing qualities. Evans delivers a charismatic leading performance, while Fanning brings an intriguing combination of confidence and vulnerability to Cassie. The film's visual style, creative approach to psychic abilities, and willingness to experiment with familiar superhero conventions give it a personality that separates it from many other genre releases of its era. It also arrived during a particularly interesting period for superhero entertainment, when films like Iron Man and The Dark Knight were helping reshape audience expectations.

Push might not be a masterpiece, but it remains a fascinating chapter in Evans' expansive superhero career. Its original mythology and unconventional approach make it worth revisiting, particularly for viewers interested in seeing the actor outside his familiar Marvel roles. Before he became the face of Captain America, Chris Evans was already leading an ambitious superhero adventure that deserved a little more recognition.

When adapting a narrative, it's important to either try to stick as closely to the source material as possible, or to veer away from the foundations in meaningful ways while also keeping the spirit and heart of the story and characters.

If an adaptation attempts to adapt a story faithfully but makes a handful of changes, it will often be torn apart by the fanbase as they pick apart how it lost sight of what made the series great in the first place. Alternatively, if the story feels so drastically different that it fails to even evoke a sense of what the original story inspired, it begs the question of why it was considered an adaptation in the first place.

So, when Prime Video's The Boys franchise took its lead from the comics as imagined by Garth Ennis and Darick Robertson, it was exciting and refreshing to see where the show changed course to make a story that would be engaging and surprising for longstanding fans of the franchise and newcomers to the series.

While The Boys comics serve as the foundation for the TV show and its spin-offs, Gen V and soon the prequel series Vought Rising, they are fundamentally different to the Prime Video series. Just on the surface level, the show makes a major narrative arc around how members of The Boys are people without powers, and they generally despise supes, but in the comics, all of the members of the team take Compound V to gain permanent powers and fight against the corrupt supes.

Additionally, there is a pivotal difference in the fact that the show positions the story as this group of underdogs fighting against a massive corporation, Vought International, as they expose corporate greed, manipulation, and coercion. In the comics, The Boys are more focused on challenging three core teams, The Seven, Payback, and the G-Men.

While discussing the G-Men, these characters also serve as the foundation and inspiration for Gen V, which expands the story to include Godolkin University and explores how young supes are trained in the corporate machine to become a part of the super cycle for Vought. In the comics, The Boys simply fight and kill the G-Men, which serves as a small side plot in the wider narrative.

Evidently, the show, and showrunner Eric Kripke, had a lot of love for the comics and the ideas they introduced in a landscape dominated by more traditional superhero stories, but they were able to make it their own and create something that ultimately feels much more prescient and relevant for modern audiences.

But, if The Boys had truly departed from the source material completely, it would lead to a subpar series that failed to capture what the original creators set out to create. Instead, The Boys took the core of the story, an examination of what flawed people would be like if given incredible and veritable powers, and the core cast, and made something that felt fresh while honoring the source material.

Many of the main characters are carried over from the comics to the screen, and in many ways, they thoroughly resemble their drawn counterparts. From the abrasive and gruff Billy Butcher, to the arrogant supe with a god complex, Homelander, these characters feel largely the same.

As a result, the series benefits massively as it draws in the established fanbase alongside fresh eyes, and delivers something that genuinely feels like it belongs in the world of the comics, but does not remain constrained by what came before. With that, The Boys and its wider franchise have become one of Prime Video's biggest success stories.
