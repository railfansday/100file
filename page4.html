Know Your Foe: Oklahoma Sooners vs. Ole Miss Rebels

The Oklahoma Sooners will be back at home in Week 11, with a record of 5-4 overall and 3-3 in the SEC. Head coach Brent Venables and his team will be looking to win consecutive games for the first time all season and get their first home win in conference play in 2026.

The up-and-down season began with a win over UTEP, but then a loss to Michigan. The Sooners beat New Mexico, but lost to Georgia. After the bye week, OU beat Texas, then lost to Kentucky. Oklahoma then beat Mississippi State, but lost to South Carolina. After beating Florida last week, the Sooners are looking to build on their first win of the season.

This week, the Ole Miss Rebels will be in Norman. They went 13-2 last year, made the College Football Playoff with an 11-1 record, and beat Tulane at home and Georgia in the Sugar Bowl in the first two rounds. A Fiesta Bowl semifinal loss against Miami ended their season just short of the title game.

It's been a whirlwind 12 months for the Rebs, beginning with the infamous end-of-regular-season departure of former head coach Lane Kiffin, who left Oxford to coach LSU. Kiffin's defensive coordinator, Pete Golding, was hired as the full-time head coach and led the team through the CFP run. Now, Golding is in his first full season as the program's head coach. He arrived at Ole Miss after stints as the defensive coordinator at UTSA and Alabama. He was the defensive play-caller for 'Bama's 2020 national title team that went undefeated. He was Ole Miss' defensive coordinator for three seasons before landing the head-coaching job.

In 2026, the Rebels are 6-3 and 4-3 in the SEC. A strong start to the season saw the Rebels beat Louisville (41-38), Charlotte (41-9), and get revenge against Kiffin and LSU with a 32-24 win at home. A loss to Florida (52-28) followed, but Golding's team bounced back after its bye week, beating Vanderbilt (52-17) and Missouri (31-3). However, the last three weeks have seen the Rebels lose to Texas (34-27), beat Auburn (45-15), and get dominated last week by Georgia in a 42-7 home loss.

This game pits two teams coming off of very different emotional results in great primetime atmospheres, and if the SEC has taught us anything this year, it's that it is definitely a week-to-week league in 2026.

On offense, the Rebels are led by co-offensive coordinators John David Baker and Cody Woodiel. Baker is the play-caller, with Golding having hired him away from East Carolina ahead of this season. He spent two years there, after he'd been on Kiffin's staff in Oxford from 2021 to 2023. He'll be matched up with Venables, who will call the defense for the Sooners. Woodiel comes over from Miami, where he'd coached for the last four years on Mario Cristobal's staff.

Baker's offense is very similar to what Ole Miss has run for the past few years with Kiffin and former offensive coordinator Charlie Weis Jr. at the controls. There are elements of the Art Briles-style veer-and-shoot that OU has already seen this year against Mississippi State and South Carolina, but there are also plenty of pro-style or more traditional spread elements in the mix. Baker and the Rebels put a lot of pressure on opposing defenses, and they have the talent to play well against anyone. Wide receivers coach L'Damian Washington has worked under Venables before, as he was OU's interim WRs coach in 2022.

It all starts with star quarterback Trinidad Chambliss, who came out of nowhere to take the starting job and run with it for Ole Miss last year. He won a court case this offseason to get to come back and play one more collegiate season, and he is one of the most dynamic dual-threat QBs in the nation. Chambliss' playmaking ability and accuracy gave the Sooners fits last year, and it's yet another week against an athletic signal-caller for Venables' defense.

Star running back Kewan Lacy is out for the season, which means the ground game will lean on Makhi Frazier (if he's able to play) and Shekai Mills-Knight. The Rebels certainly won't be shy about running Chambliss as well. Through the air, wide receivers Deuce Alexander and Horatio Fields are the top targets, along with tight end Caleb Odom. The Rebels have a dynamite passing attack, and OU's secondary will have to be up to the challenge this week. Offensive lineman Troy Everett will return to Owen Field this week after a long career as a Sooner, including serving as a captain last year.

On the other side of the ball, OU interim offensive coordinator Jason Witten will be in his second game as the play-caller, after taking over for Ben Arbuckle last week. He'll match wits with Golding, who calls Ole Miss' defense himself. Golding, a great defensive mind and longtime play-caller, consulted Venables when he took the head coaching job about continuing to call plays, as Venables has wrestled with the play-calling duties at OU. Upon Venables' suggestion, Golding decided to continue calling his own plays.

Co-defensive coordinators Bryan Brown and Marcus Woodson will be aiding Golding as he looks to get the best of Witten. Brown has worked for Golding since 2024, after serving as defensive coordinator at both Louisville and Cincinnati. Woodson is in his first season in Oxford, after stints at Memphis, Auburn, Florida State, and Arkansas.

Golding's unit has experienced peaks and valleys this season, and linebackers Keaton Thomas, Luke Ferrelli, and Tah'j Butler lead the team in tackles, though Thomas won't play this week. Three players do most of the getting after the quarterback for this team. Defensive end Blake Purchase (4.5 sacks), linebacker Suntarine Perkins (3.5 sacks), and defensive end Will Echoles (2.5 sacks), will draw plenty of attention from the OU o-line, especially as it's looking likely that Echoles will play this week.

Oklahoma quarterback John Mateer needs to build on his performance against Florida last week, and you can expect that Oklahoma is going to try to continue to bleed the clock, shorten the game, and keep the ball on the ground. It worked well last week against the Gators, and that's where the Sooners would love to live again this week against the Rebels.

OU special teams coordinator Doug Deakin will face off against Ole Miss special teams coordinator Jake Schoonover this week, and he's in his fourth year at Ole Miss, arriving at the same time as Golding, though he came from Arkansas State. Schoonover's unit is one of the nation's best, led by star kicker Lucas Carneiro, who is one of the nation's best. Oklahoma's special teams had a poor day in bad weather against the Rebels last year, and with rain in the forecast again this weekend, kicker Tate Sandell and company will have to be on point against a very strong group.

OU and Ole Miss have just three meetings all-time between, but they're now annual rivals for the next four years, at least according to the SEC. The first meeting came in the 1999 Independence Bowl in Bob Stoops' first season at the helm of the Sooners. OU lost a tight 27-25 game that night in Shreveport, but went undefeated and won the national championship the next year.

The last two meetings have come in the last two seasons as SEC foes. Oklahoma went to Oxford in 2024, losing 26-14. Ole Miss came to Norman last year and again defeated OU, improving their record against the Sooners to 3-0 all-time. The 34-26 loss was the final loss of Oklahoma's 2025 regular season, as Venables' team won out in November to advance to the CFP.

Ole Miss is wounded, desperate, and hungry heading into Saturday afternoon's game. They must win out to have any shot at returning to the playoff, and they got embarrassed on a big-time stage last week. Venables' Sooners have typically played better football in his tenure when their backs are against the wall, and they've struggled when the other team is the one who has to punch its way out. Building on success is not something Oklahoma has done well this season, and it's certainly been a talking point inside the walls of the Switzer Center this week.

Ole Miss is talented and explosive enough to pull away in this game, especially if OU's offense reverts back to its old self for long stretches of this game. However, if the Sooners get help on offense, contain Chambliss, and get a few breaks on defense, they are good enough to win this game against a Rebel team that has a few more issues than it did last year.

One more win would ensure Oklahoma's 28th-straight bowl berth, but the focus right now for Venables' Sooners is on finishing out this year in the best possible fashion. Last week's cathartic win over Florida was impressive and an encouraging sign after last week, but can Oklahoma build on that success and string together wins? It's something this team has been unable to do all season long, but the next opportunity to change that comes this week. Seven or eight wins looks a whole lot better than five or six wins, and a strong finish to the year could at least salvage a little pride from a 2026 season gone wrong.

FAA ground stop delays KC Current’s flight to NWSL semifinal against Gotham FC

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Federal Aviation Administration's ground stop Friday affected the travel of the Kansas City Current, who were scheduled to fly to New York City for their National Women's Soccer League semifinal against Gotham FC on Sunday.

The team planned to charter Friday evening and boarded their plane shortly before the ground stop, according to a source with knowledge of the Current's travel plans who spoke on the condition of anonymity due to the ongoing nature of the team's planning. The team deplaned after it became clear the delay was indefinite.

A spokesperson for the Current confirmed the team's travel disruption.

"We are collaborating with key stakeholders, including the NWSL and our charter partner, to identify next steps in order to arrive safely ahead of our match against Gotham FC on Sunday," the spokesperson said.

The Utah Royals, who will face the Washington Spirit in the other semifinal, at noon ET on Saturday, traveled Thursday and were not affected by the nationwide ground stop, a Royals spokesperson confirmed to The Athletic.

The NWSL semifinals are part of a full weekend of sports, including college football and basketball games, and NBA, NHL and NFL matchups. The U.S. men's national team is scheduled to play Haiti on Saturday in Miami. That match will not be affected, a spokesperson for Concacaf, the regional governing body in charge of the match, told The Athletic. Both teams and match officials are in Miami, and the U.S. team's flight from Atlanta touched down just before the FAA issued the nationwide ground stop.

"Everyone is already on-site, and the match will proceed as scheduled," the spokesperson said.

U.S. Secretary of Transportation Sean Duffy said in a social media post that FAA technicians are investigating a "communication issue impacting all Air Route Traffic Control Centers."

This is not the first disruption NWSL teams have faced ahead of this weekend's penultimate weekend, although the other was unrelated to travel.

After Gotham FC and the Washington Spirit secured hosting rights to the semifinals last weekend, the league and teams had to scramble to secure venues and locations. The Spirit were able to negotiate with D.C. United and organizers for the Secretaries' Cup, an annual NCAA Division III college football game between the U.S. Coast Guard Academy and the U.S. Merchant Marine Academy, to play at their typical home venue of Audi Field at noon ET. The Spirit will play ahead of the football game, which will start at approximately 4:30 p.m. ET. The field will be lined for both sports (NWSL lines in yellow and football yard lines in white) to avoid having to repaint between games. The bases for the field goal posts will also be in place and padded during the NWSL match.

In New Jersey, Gotham had to move their home game from Sports Illustrated Stadium to Icahn Stadium on Randall's Island in New York due to poor pitch conditions at their usual home stadium. That match is scheduled to take place at 3 p.m. ET on Sunday with Gotham providing complimentary transportation services to the 5,000-seat venue for fans in the New York area.

10 Best Sci-Fi Reads Better Than Black Mirror

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Charlie Brooker's Black Mirror is technically science fiction, but it's so much more than that. It shines a light on our society and technology in a way that's disturbingly relatable. Watching it feels uncomfortable because, as unsettling as the stories it portrays are, sometimes they're not too far off from the lives we actually live.

If you've binged the entire show and are still looking for more content with the same vibes, there are many science fiction books that feel like reading a Black Mirror episode. They coat social commentary in a tense and unsettling atmosphere. After reading these, you might come away understanding society a bit better than when you started... for better or worse.

Every day, there's an increasingly large number of mouths to feed. In Tender Is the Flesh by Agustina Bazterrica (available for purchase here), the problem becomes even worse when a vius makes all animal meat poisonous to humans.

The solution that the government in the novel comes up with is raising humans as livestock. The protagonist, Marcos, works at a processing plant for this "special meat." When he's gifted a live woman for his consumption, he starts to warm up to her and begins to do the unthinkable: Treat her like a human being.

We live at a time when human beings are increasingly looking to artificial intelligence for companionship. Annie Bot by Sierra Greer (available for purchase here) gives a fresh take to the concept of the perfect computer-powered girlfriend, which has been seen in media and movies plenty of times.

The story is told from the point of view of Annie Bot, the AI woman who was created to be the perfect companion for Doug. She sees to his every whim, keeps the house clean, and tries to give him what he wants.

But the more human she becomes, the less perfect she gets. Slowly, it becomes apparent that Doug might not really know what he even wants, and that Annie Bot might be able to look beyond her role as companion.

Everyday things have been disappearing in The Memory Police by Yoko Ogawa (available for purchase here). It starts with small things, like hats, roses, birds. These objects don't just vanish from the world, but even from people's memories, like they never even existed in the first place.

The protagonist is a young woman who's one of the few with the power to remember the lost objects. This puts her at risk from the overbearing Memory Police, who exist to make sure that the forgotten remains forgotten, at any cost.

Little by little, more serious things start to disappear with horrible repercussions. All the protagonist can do is watch as life changes completely, while everyone around her remains oblivious that anything wrong is even happening: a situation that would feel right at home on Black Mirror.

When considering how technology interacts with society, it's impossible not to mention the classic Do Androids Dream Of Electric Sheep? by Philip K Dick (available for purchase here). This sci-fi novel inspired the Blade Runner series and remains one of the most beloved sci-fi novels.

The book takes place in a post-apocalyptic world that's shrouded in a cloud of radioactive dust, and most of humanity has escaped to Mars. Rick Deckard remains on Earth with his pet electric sheep to do a job: He's an android killer who tracks down and kills rogue robots for the police.

His latest assignment is to track and destroy six androids on the run from authorities. The only problem is that these androids are very smart, and they look and sound exactly like regular people.

There Is No Antimemetics Division by an author known only as qntm (available for purchase here) is an SCP novel. If you're unfamiliar, the fictional SCP Foundation secures, contains, and protects anomalies and strange supernatural phenomena and entities.

The titular Antimemetics Division is a branch of the SCP that deals with anti-memes, or ideas that can't be remembered or spread. These entities mess with people's minds and prevents them from remembering or sharing information about them, obviously making it extremely difficult to trap or contain them.

The story is told over the course of several seemingly unconnected short stories. Eventually, you realize two things: everything is connected, and there's a horrible threat looming over humanity, but no one can remember that it exists. This is a strange and unsettling read that comes together in a very satisfying way and explores the way human beings think and act, much like an episode of Black Mirror.

Many of the novels by John Marrs are Black Mirror-esque and explore a world where technology and science are just a little too far advanced for its own good. The sci-fi novel The One (available for purchase here) is the first entry in the Dark Future collection, and a great place to start if you're looking for your next realistic dystopian fix.

In The One, a new service has rolled out genetic matchmaking. A new DNA test can match you to your perfect partner, who you're the most genetically compatible with. The book follows five people from all walks of life who find out that being matched to their soul mates might not be all it's cracked up to be.

Blake Crouch is a much more action-focused sci-fi novel than many of the others on this list, but Dark Matter (available for purchase here) still feels very much like a story straight from Black Mirror. Jason Dessen is a physics professor on his way home when he's knocked out by a mysterious person. When he wakes up, he discovers that his life is completely different.

His wife isn't married to him, his son doesn't exist, and his work is a lot more significant than his teaching was. In this world, Jason has done something impossible, but he doesn't want the fame and status. All he wants is to go back home to his wife and son. This sends him on an incredible journey, pursued by an enemy that might be indestructible.

The Test by Sylvain Neuvel (available for purchase here) is a short read, but it's one that will stay on your mind long after you've finished it. Written by one of the contributing writers for Black Mirror, The Test asks what it takes to be a good contributing member of society.

The book's protagonist, Idir, is facing the British Citizenship Test: a brief questionnaire of just 25 multiple-choice questions. It begins simple enough, with a few easy questions. But before long, the testing descends into a dystopian life or death situation that will make you question how you value life and what makes a good citizen.

Exhalation by Ted Chiang (available for purchase here) is an anthology consisting of nine short stories. Each tale has the dreamy, slightly "off" feel of a Black Mirror episode, and scenarios that feel like you get not one but nine whole new episodes.

The collection explores moral and philosophical questions. If you had the option to go back in time and fix some mistakes, would you? How would multiple universes change the way we see free will and choice? These profound and speculative stories will make you reexamine what you think about the world and human nature.

The movie version of Kazuo Ishiguro's Klara and the Sun—a story about a solar-poweed, child-like companion android—has recently brought the author's dream-like writing to the public eye. Never Let Me Go (available for purchase here) is another story worth checking out for fans of Black Mirror.

In this book, we spend some time learning about Kathy, a 31-year-old woman with fond memories of her exclusive private school. As she remembers her life as a student, the memories are tinged with nostalgia and an air of unease.

There's something very wrong about the school, that has unsettling ramifications for Kathy's future. If you're a fan of the eerie atmosphere of Black Mirror, this one might be just right for you.

Why New Boeing Planes Are Green Before Delivery, But Airbus Aircraft Aren't

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The observation that pre-delivery Boeing aircraft are green, and Airbus aluminum aircraft aren't, is partially true, but it is far from the full picture. There are significant differences in the substances that Boeing and Airbus' various divisions and subcontractors use, and this is partially driven by environmental regulations differing by location. But unfinished Boeing aircraft are also typically photographed at an earlier stage of development compared with Airbus.

After the peelable Temporary Protective Coating (TPC / "Spraylat") is removed, Boeing aircraft look different, but they are rarely photographed at that stage. This is the stage that Airbus aircraft are typically photographed. Importantly, the 'green' conversation requires aluminum aircraft, as composite material structures are treated differently. Here is what to know about Boeing and Airbus coatings.

One of the most distinctive features of new aircraft is the stage they go through when they are in their primary coats of paint. Boeing aircraft are green as they roll out of the assembly facility. The green coating is a corrosion-protective primer applied to the aircraft structure before its final exterior paint scheme. The layer also helps subsequent coatings to adhere to the aircraft. Other aircraft manufacturers such as Airbus, Embraer, ATR, and COMAC use broadly comparable surface-protection coatings, although they are not quite the same.

It is also important to distinguish between the aircraft's three basic stages of coating. Aircraft receive structural primer, intermediate coating, and final decorative topcoats. What looks like a single coat of green paint on Boeing aircraft is actually a combination of two distinct coatings, obviously not the final decorative one. What is seen is typically the Temporary Protective Coating (TPC / "Spraylat") and the Corrosion-Inhibiting Primer (The True Undercoat).

This does not apply to the composite-material-constructed 787 Dreamliner (more on that below). Instead, it is applied to traditional aluminum structures. It is worth noting that while the Boeing 737 is now in its fourth (MAX) generation, its fuselage remains a fundamentally aluminum structure and a 1960s design. Something similar is also true of the heavily updated Boeing 777X soon to enter service. It may come with new wings, but its fuselage remains primarily aluminum.

Boeing's clean-sheet Boeing 787 is its only 'new' aircraft in the narrow sense of being a clean-sheet design able to take full advantage of today's advanced composite materials. The only 'new' Airbus analog is the Airbus A350. The A330neo and A320neo series are upgraded older aluminum fuselage aircraft. The Boeing 787's fuselage and wings are built with carbon-fiber-reinforced polymer (CFRP) rather than aluminum and so do not suffer from galvanic or atmospheric metal corrosion.

Consequently, Dreamliners do not come with green TPC or green chromate primers. As they roll out of the assembly line, they appear white or light-gray/beige, being coated with conductive/UV-protective primer coats. The Dreamliner's primary competitor, the A350, comes with a tan/beige primer coating, which serves a similar function. Sections in white or beige are usually composite materials, which don't corrode and don't need the coating. That's why the composite-bodied 787 looks less green than the aluminum 737.

This is perhaps the most striking on the Boeing 777X. As stated, it retained the 1990s-era aluminum fuselage. Accordingly, the fuselage is painted green in the signature glossy emerald-green Temporary Protective Coating (TPC). Meanwhile, the wings are made of composite and so receive a white or light-gray UV-protective topcoat that matches the Boeing 787. A closer look at the paint and production processes of Boeing airliners.

Airbus' aluminum aircraft are similar, but also different. These receive chromate-free exterior primers, corrosion-protective coatings, and decorative topcoats. But while the basic process is the same, they are still chromate-free. Airbus started introducing chromate-free exterior primers on the A380 in 2016, almost a decade after the Superjumbo entered service. While the Superjumbo was a clean-sheet aircraft, it was not a 'new' aircraft in the sense of the Boeing 787 and Airbus A350.

The A380 was built using the traditional aluminum-alloy fuselage. One of the criticisms of the program was that it was a big airplane more than it was a new one. Airbus and Boeing operate in different regulatory environments. The European Union has strict REACH chemical regulations that are phasing out hexavalent chromium more aggressively than in the United States.

In 2025, CIRS reported, "the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) unveiled a proposal to restrict the use of carcinogenic hexavalent chromium (Cr(VI)) substances across the EU. The measure aims to safeguard human health and the environment from the cancer risks associated with these chemicals." This has pushed Airbus to adopt chromate-free primers faster than Boeing. These typically appear tan, beige, or pale yellow. Airbus generally doesn't use Boeing's glossy, peelable emerald green 'Spraylat' transport film. The structural primer is therefore directly visible.

Another distinctive feature of Airbus aircraft is that when assembled, their coatings are a patchwork of shades. Boeing is a national company; Airbus is a multinational company formed from the merger of major European aerospace companies. It has four home countries (the UK, France, Germany, and Spain). Accordingly, it uses a distributed European manufacturing model.

Barrels, wings, and tails are fabricated, anodized, and primed at different sites and at different subsidiaries across these countries. Each facility applies its own corrosion-inhibiting primer before they are shipped to be assembled. Those primers have slightly different chemistries, resulting in different shades. When the aircraft is pieced together, it looks like something of a patchwork of mismatched greens, yellows, and tans.

Major contractors include GKN Aerospace (Fokker Aerostructures) in the UK and the Netherlands, Latecoere in France, and Spirit AeroSystems. Airbus radomes are often matte gray or tan (made of composite materials), the nose and forward base are pale yellow-green, the center fuselage is slightly darker olive or lime green, the belly fairing is tan or beige, the wings are light gray over green primer, and the aft is olive green aluminum or tan/gray in composite areas.

Unlike Airbus, Boeing uses a peelable green coating to protect the aircraft's aluminum skin. The aluminum skin is relatively thin, and even minor scratches and indentations can require inspection. It protects against environmental damage during manufacturing and transport. Like Airbus, Boeing has components manufactured across a broad geological footprint, and these require transportation.

While Airbus transports many of its components on climate-controlled BelugaXL aircraft (and ferries), Boeing relies on the Open-Air Cross-Country Rail Transit for its core 737 aircraft. Those fuselages are built in Wichita, Kansas, and need to be transported 2,000 miles (3,200 km) by open railcar to Renton and Everett. Boeing's highly modified Boeing 747 Dreamlifters are used for the 787, not normally the 737. The train route is primarily operated by BNSF Railway, with the journey going through Nebraska, Wyoming, Montana, to Washington via Spokane.

The journey takes around ten to 12 days, with trains taking three to four fuselages at a time (six maximum). In 2018, Aviation Week reported, "In today’s world on the massive airplane you see going down the rail or you see entering the Renton factory the fuselage is already integrated and they are able to start the same day installing the blankets and wiring and going through the production process without having to do any structural work to the airplane."

Another key factor to consider when comparing Boeing and Airbus during their manufacturing process is that they are typically photographed at different stages of their manufacturing cycle. This creates an apples-to-oranges mismatch. Most familiar photos of green Boeing aircraft show them with the TPC peelable coating, as they are on the railcars or parked at Renton/Everett facilities.

Airbus photos are typically taken when they are being unloaded from a BelugaXL or rolling off the Final Assembly Line. They may be conducting their first customer acceptance flights. This is a later stage than the Boeing aircraft, and they typically sport a raw structural epoxy primer that gives them a flat, chalky, matte finish.

Other factors also explain why Boeing and Airbus aircraft may appear different. Boeing aircraft parked in Washington are frequently pictured in overcast skies, while Airbus aircraft in Toulouse are often in warm Mediterranean sunlight. Even the digital camera may react differently. Rinnai has launched a new lineup of electric heat pump water heaters that adds wifi controls and more installation options to an appliance that can cut the energy needed for hot showers.

The REHPW series comes in 50-, 65-, and 80-gallon sizes. It builds on Rinnai’s existing REHP lineup, with changes designed to make the units easier to install, monitor, and maintain.

Heat pump water heaters pull heat from the surrounding air and transfer it into the tank. Moving that heat requires less electricity than producing it with conventional electric resistance elements. There’s no fuel combustion at the appliance, although emissions from its electricity supply depend on the grid.

Rinnai lists a Uniform Energy Factor, or UEF, of 3.75 for the 50-gallon model and 4.0 for the two larger versions. Higher numbers mean greater efficiency under standardized testing; actual bills depend on electricity rates, hot water use, and installation conditions.

The lineup delivers up to 91 gallons of hot water in the first hour, depending on tank size. That rating includes both stored hot water and what the unit can heat during that hour – useful context when several people take consecutive showers.

There are five operating modes, 4.5 kW backup heating elements, and a variable-speed fan designed to keep noise down. The heat pump can operate with ambient air as low as 37°F.

Installation is where some of the more practical upgrades show up.

The new Rinnai heat pump water heater units have water connections on both the top and side, giving installers more options for connecting existing plumbing. Both the air intake and exhaust are on top, allowing zero side clearance where Rinnai’s installation requirements permit.

An optional ducting kit adds flexibility for tighter spaces. Carrying handles help with moving the tank into place, and the air filter slides out horizontally from the side for cleaning.

Rinnai has also added a third tank temperature sensor to the 50- and 80-gallon models to help manage hot water delivery.

Wifi adds remote monitoring and control. Rinnai’s updated Central app supports the REHPW series, including scheduling and tools to use time-of-use electricity rates to manage energy use.

The units also carry CTA-2045 AC Level 2 certification for demand response. That gives compatible utility programs a way to communicate with the water heater and adjust its electricity use. Rinnai says the certification can help qualify homeowners for local and state utility demand-response rebates; availability depends on the program.

The series is Energy Star certified, meets NEEA Tier 4 requirements, and complies with California’s Title 24 requirements, including JA13, according to Rinnai. Warranty coverage includes 10 years for the tank and other parts, plus one year for labor.

Painter Sascha Braunig Pushes Through the Void

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Sascha Braunig’s paintings have a tendency to produce visceral reactions: a pit in the stomach, an eyebrow raise, a need to move closer. This is not unintended.

“I want the viewer to have a bodily experience,” Braunig says.

The paintings are bright, smooth, candy-colored—lighthearted contrasts to the psychologically intense shapes: luminously rendered heads and torsos often bound by wiry tentacles. At times the figures recede into their matching optic backgrounds, as if hiding in plain sight.

Braunig, who is 43 and splits her time between New York and Maine, has been painting with this funky verve for close to two decades. Not long after she got her MFA from Yale’s painting program in 2008, she was having solo exhibitions of her small paintings of heads at the now-defunct Foxy Production in Manhattan. Inclusions at the New Museum Triennial and MoMA PS1 soon followed. (Attention from critics came quickly as well; one called her an “inspired reanimator of Surrealism,” another a “phenom.”)

Braunig is excellent at capturing the inherent tension of living in a body—especially one that is subjected to societal expectations of femininity. She’s playing with the ways women have been contorted and cinched and squeezed for time immemorial, asked to fit into archetypes that seem at turns glamorous or sinister. Often they are both.

“Our aesthetic, our personhood is formed in relation to these cultural forces and imagery,” she says. “You can’t separate yourself out from what you’ve seen throughout your whole life.”

These themes of gender performance and corporeal control permeate “Wrist Painter,” Braunig’s most extensive exhibition to date, which opens November 13 at the Portland Art Museum. The show brings together some 45 works, including paintings, sculptures, works on paper, and sketchbooks. It will be her first solo institutional show in Maine. (A special occasion, as almost all of the pieces in the show were made a block and a half away, in her studio at the Portland arts nonprofit SPACE, over the last 15 years.)

“Sascha really stands out here in this arts ecosystem. She’s doing something really singular,” says Sayantan Mukhopadhyay, a curator at PMA who organized the show. When people think of Maine painters, they usually think of landscapes. But Braunig leans toward the cerebral, with a bit more bite. “The paradoxes are what really excite me about the work,” Mukhopadhyay says.

And the dichotomies are many. “Sascha has a way of presenting an abstraction that can read both soft and strong, feminine and violent,” says Olivia Smith, whose New York City gallery, Magenta Plains, now represents Braunig. “I think that’s what I’ve always loved: that she offers a way for the viewer to re-associate, which ends up telling them more about themselves than about Sascha.”

One of the earliest artworks in “Wrist Painter” is Sequins, from 2010. It’s from Braunig’s head series, which she began in grad school. Her process starts with a drawing, then she brings her sketch to life by making an embellished three-dimensional model. Then she’ll light the model and paint what she observes. She applies her lustrous oil paints with such precision that these strange bodily shapes start to look “real.”

She continued this system as she moved on to her bodies series a few years later, using clay resin, wire, and paper to make her maquettes. This haptic process is why works like Warm Leatherette, a 2015 painting at PMA that looks like a snatched Michelin Man encased in chartreuse padding, seem so tactile. (She has often described her choice of colors as “seasonal” or “dietary”—she finds a palette that works for one show and then cycles to a new one for the next.)

Her maquette process also lends a certain theatricality. She is literally setting up a scene, as if the entire idea of femininity were on a velvet-curtained stage, the whole specter aglow under klieg lights. What is gender if not a performance?

Braunig, who grew up in British Columbia, Canada, liked drawing and painting from a young age. Her father (a dentist) and mother (a nurse) enjoyed creative pursuits outside of work, and the house was full of art books. She loved watching Pee-wee’s Playhouse, which she credits as being just as formative, even more so, than going to museums.

Her parents encouraged her to attend an intensive summer art program at Skidmore College in upstate New York when she was 16. It was there that she first learned about Cooper Union, the New York City school where artists Lee Krasner and Wangechi Mutu got their start. Braunig decided it was the place for her.

New York City was the perfect place for the budding artist. “I was totally in love. It maybe ruined me forever,” she jokes now. She went to Yale right after Cooper, and then she moved to Maine in 2010, in her mid-20s. “I was really fortunate to have a New York gallery as I left the city, because I was able to have shows and have my work represented here even though I wasn’t here.”

Living somewhere far less expensive was also a game changer—she didn’t need to pile on jobs to make ends meet, and could instead focus on building her deeply personal style. “I had been exposed to so many influences coming to New York. And maybe those first five years in Maine were a time when I was able to process them alone.”

Braunig knows her art history, and she gets why people connect her style back to Surrealism. But her artistic comrades are more like French self-portraitist Claude Cahun or even Frida Kahlo, she says, “who’s really making work that is influenced by events of her own life.” Her art might feel dreamy (or perhaps like an acid trip), but “it’s not a dream space. I really am thinking about actual forces in the actual world.”

For many years Braunig used her work to illustrate broader ideas around culture and gender, but her most recent output has taken a turn toward the autobiographical. A few years ago she went through a creative block that left her stranded from her art practice, unable to make anything. As she emerged from this period last year, she started making her most personal paintings yet.

That work went into her radiant show “Cauldron” at Magenta Plains this past spring, its title a reference to the messy, transformative stew of midlife. Its anchor painting was My Death (2025), which, along with several others from the show, will travel to Portland for “Wrist Painter.”

Sascha Braunig, My Death, 2025, oil on linen over panel.

She calls My Death an “explicit self-portrait” about her own reanimation—an emergence through the void of creative block. It’s juicy and haunting and beautiful. She employed those tentacles again, but this time they are like a life force attached to a sleeping Braunig. A mysterious figure made from strips of vermilion orange pulls on them like marionette strings, bringing the artist back to life.

“I’ve come to understand that painting is a survival practice,” she says. “That can be a framing practice where you’re using it to talk about ideas in the world, or it can be a practice where you’re directly processing your own experiences. Maybe those two things aren’t that different.”

For the PMA show, Braunig has made one brand-new work, titled Torsion. At 78 inches tall by 60 inches wide, it is the largest painting of her career. She previously used the title for a museum show in Norway, but realized she’d never given it to an individual painting. The term, in physics, refers to the twisting of an object around an axis: a towel wrung out to dry.

This new Torsion is a painting of a fleshy pink figure with a tiny, twisted torso standing on tiptoes within a gray box. The figure’s muscular back is to the viewer, its knees fanning out in a plié. Dramatic tentacles in orange and yellow trace its exaggerated outline.

The figure’s head is cut off by the box’s ceiling, and its arms stop around the elbows, where they encounter the side walls. Maybe the figure’s been blocked, held back by the confines of this shadowy encasement. Or maybe, somehow, it has broken free.

NiCE’s next-generation Cognigy bets the hard part of AI agents is running them, not building them

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

GUEST COLUMN by Zeus Kerravala

The contact center has been the low-hanging fruit for the artificial intelligence industry. Ask any information technology or business leader what they want to use agentic AI for, and they will say it’s to improve the customer experience, which starts with the contact center.

However, to date, the industry has spent the past two years arguing over which AI agent is the smartest. That’s the wrong debate. Model capabilities are improving so quickly and are getting so much cheaper that raw intelligence and reasoning are becoming the least differentiated part of the stack.

The real challenge for enterprises is operating a fleet of AI agents reliably across complete customer journeys, with the visibility and control a large business requires. Without that, the human, or worse, the customer, is left connecting the dots among the outputs of the growing number of agents they are communicating with.

That’s the thesis behind the next generation of NiCE Cognigy, which NiCE Ltd. launched this week. The release includes a redesigned Agentic Experience Studio, an AI Assist companion spanning the building, testing and improvement of agents, reusable “skills” that package instructions and tools, turn-by-turn tracing and native voice-to-voice model support.

Default speech-to-text, text-to-speech and large language models are now included in the per-conversation price, with premium models from Deepgram, ElevenLabs and Anthropic available with a few clicks and billed by usage.

Ahead of the launch, I attended an analyst briefing with Nhu Ho, senior product marketing manager at NiCE Cognigy, and with Clement Boutier, director of product management, who leads the team that built the release and owns the platform roadmap.

Here are the important points from this release:

Ho opened with a chart showing how long AI can work on its own before a human has to step in. In 2020, it was seconds; in 2024, it was minutes; and this year it’s roughly 16 hours. The footnote was interesting, as it calculated that figure at a 50% success rate. “Capable and dependable do not always mean the same thing,” Ho said. “That is exactly where the platform matters.”

For CX leaders, that’s a solid benchmark to consider. A 50% success rate is a research milestone. In a contact center, it’s a churn event or a compliance violation. As models commoditize, value shifts to the system around them: testing, tracing, guardrails and deterministic fallbacks. NiCE doesn’t build frontier models, and frontier model builders don’t run contact centers. Positioning Cognigy as the layer that makes AI dependable is a smart place to plant a flag.

Cognigy earned its pre-acquisition leadership position by handling complex production deployments for large brands such as Lufthansa, Mercedes and DHL. But that flexibility and customizability added complexity. Boutier put it this way: “The large number of options when configuring an agent creates friction to get started, which slows things down.” The new studio lets a builder stand up an agent in a few clicks, with models already provisioned, while power-user tools, including full access to the agent’s system prompt, are available in advanced sections.

This is important because NiCE aims to create a “1+1=3” value proposition for its current customers. NiCE needs Cognigy to expand broadly across the CXone installed base, not just among expert teams at the largest enterprises. Boutier described three builder personas: smaller businesses building simple agents, contact center administrators and team leads, and engineers at technically sophisticated companies. AI Assist is aimed squarely at the first two.

In the demo, it built an agent, tested it, fixed the problems it found, and deployed it to WhatsApp. The flip side is that lowering the barrier means more agents built by less experienced people, which makes the governance features the real story of this release.

Despite all the agentic hype, most enterprises aren’t ready to hand over entire journeys to an autonomous agent. Boutier outlined three modes: traditional natural language understanding, hybrid and fully agentic. Hybrid “is actually where most of our customers are,” he said, while fully agentic deployments are at the “beginning of the adoption curve.” This approach lets customers ease into agentic, build confidence and move to fully autonomous at their own pace.

NiCE designed the product for that approach. An agent can serve as the orchestration layer, calling existing flows as tools. Flows can also be placed in front of agents, for example, to scrub credit card numbers or other personal data before any data reaches a model. Ho described the release as “an evolution, not a replacement,” with production deployments, application programming interfaces and integrations continuing to run unchanged.

That’s a meaningful advantage over AI-native challengers whose pitch effectively requires a rebuild. Contact centers are the fastest-moving environments, and deterministic workflows aren’t legacy baggage. They’re how a business enforces what a large language model shouldn’t be deciding.

“You can have the best builder experience, but if you don’t have any feedback loop on what you’re building, you’re never going to build a great agent,” Boutier said. The new tracing shows each turn: which skill loaded, which tool was called and with what arguments, and when the agent handed off to a flow.

The Simulator now supports multivariate testing of up to 10 variants, using an LLM-as-judge to run synthetic customers against an agent. Boutier ran the same agent on two models and found that one was faster and consumed fewer tokens. This shifts model selection from a preference to a data-backed economic decision.

Voice-to-voice support may be the first feature customers deploy. Boutier noted that buyers are open to using a speech-to-text and text-to-speech cascade, “but what they realize is that voice-to-voice models sound just better.” In the demo, the agent delegated tool calls to a separate model while continuing to talk, making the conversation sound far more natural.

Cognigy now serves as both a Model Context Protocol client and server and supports agent-to-agent connections, including one customer linking Cognigy to agents built with Microsoft’s tooling. NiCE is also co-developing the Personal Agent Protocol with Meta and Sierra, which isn’t live yet. Boutier described Cognigy as “the front door” of the contact center, where a consumer’s personal agent must authenticate before it can proceed. It’s great to see NiCE collaborating with Sierra, a direct competitor, on this, as it makes things easier for a customer.

The industry views agent identity as a shared responsibility. But as Sierra outlines, companies can also let personal agents work through their websites and APIs, so the front-door position depends on enterprises routing that traffic through Cognigy. This lets brands interact with personal agents safely.

There has been a wave of companies banning personal agents, which I think is a mistake. If something is good for a consumer, it will happen, so it’s better to be on the leading edge of enabling it rather than fighting it.

This release is less about new capabilities and more about NiCE betting on where value will accrue as AI agents mature. Intelligence is getting cheaper. Dependability, governance and the ability to blend autonomous reasoning with deterministic control are not.

NiCE is the market leader in contact center as a service, as Cognigy was in conversational AI. Now the challenge is to get an easier-to-work-with Cognigy into the hands of thousands of CXone customers without creating a sprawl of individually governed agents. This will allow NiCE to maintain its industry leadership as personal agents arrive. As it does that, the Cognigy acquisition will move from an add-on to a core component of the NiCE platform.

Starship flight 15 powers to orbit—setting up a possible catch on Saturday

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

One way or another, the company will get some more data on Saturday.

STARBASE, Texas—Starship lifted off right on schedule Friday from South Texas, making its 15th test flight in more than three years.

The largely successful launch took place in the early evening; as the massive rocket rose into the atmosphere, the Sun sank toward the western horizon. Conditions were ideal for the launch, with the atmospheric winds so calm the rocket’s white plume did not dissipate for many minutes. The weather in South Texas is not always comfortable; summers are long and hot. Friday evening was perfect at Starbase.

The first stage ascended toward space flawlessly, releasing the upper stage above the Gulf of Mexico. From there, the Starship upper stage ignited its Raptor engines and powered its way into a nominal orbit. This was the second time SpaceX has flown Starship into low-Earth orbit.

When it came time for the first stage to return to the ground, it dropped like a rock. However, due to an unspecified issue, the vehicle was commanded to divert into the Gulf rather than return to the Starbase facility to be caught by the launch tower. It created a fantastically powerful explosion, even for observers more than 10 miles away.

The primary aim of Friday’s launch was the deployment of 26 Starlink V3 satellites from a narrow door at the base of the spacecraft. This occurred about 40 minutes into Starship’s flight, and all 26 satellites—which have solar panels that stretch 60 meters end to end when fully deployed—safely made it into orbit, where they will now use onboard propulsion to raise their altitude.

This means that Starship is now an operational, orbital rocket. It is the largest and most powerful machine humans have ever launched into space. The challenge for the company now is to make Starship reusable and dramatically increase its flight rate.

In that sense, the first part of Friday’s mission was not a success. SpaceX had hoped to return the first-stage booster, refurbish it, and fly it again. The company has previously landed and reflown Starship boosters but has yet to do so with the latest iteration of the vehicle, version 3.

However, SpaceX still has a chance to make history on Saturday with the potential return of the Starship upper stage. SpaceX has never attempted to catch the vehicle’s upper stage.

Doing so will require Starship to pass a series of tests in orbit and subsequently during its return to Earth to ensure it is fully healthy. This is critical because Starship will be passing over parts of Mexico and the United States before returning to Starbase, and SpaceX does not want to endanger people on the ground. Additionally, it does not want to risk damage to its launch towers.

If Starship passes these health checks, it would land at about 3:15 pm local time in Texas (21:15 UTC). If it does not, the company has earlier options to de-orbit the vehicle into an ocean.

Successfully returning Starship’s upper stage would be historic, as well as significant for SpaceX’s efforts to reuse the massive rocket. It would also give the company valuable information about the vehicle’s performance during reentry.

One of the biggest hurdles left in Starship’s development is the vehicle’s heat shield, which must protect the upper stage during its return through Earth’s atmosphere. More than that, SpaceX aims to make Starship rapidly reusable, and to do this, Starship’s tiles must be light and resilient.

SpaceX has been experimenting with tiles since the beginning of the Starship program, trying to find the right combination of low mass, heat resistance, and durability so the tiles require minimal refurbishment between launches. For this flight, SpaceX said it is applying learnings from the previous test flight in September, including additional retention mechanisms on tiles in areas deemed to be at the highest risk of falling off during ascent and addressing areas with a flow path for plasma to get behind the tiles.

Another problem on recent flights has been heat incursion into the small gaps between the edges of the tiles. According to SpaceX, this Starship has a large portion of the heat shield equipped with a curved tile design that has shown the ability to reduce heating in the gaps between tiles.

Twin estrangement is a really important issue that is often ignored because twin mythology suggests that all twins should get along, as their closeness creates a perfect interpersonal relationship. Raising twins is hard, and estrangement is very predictable when there is not enough focus on individual development. Believing that all twins should and will be able to get along causes shame and humiliation for twins and their parents when they don't. This is an unfortunate problem.

For most of my adult life, I have been writing about twins or gifted children. In general, my focus is on the social and emotional problems related to identity development that twins or other types of multiple births naturally experience because they share their caregivers and home environment. Twin development, in general, is quite different and more complicated than the developmental identity challenges of non-twins. The most common problems that twins have when they are young are questions and concerns that revolve around the sharing of their parents, day-to-day experiences, friendships, and education. Twins also have concerns about their twin's opinion, and their need for their twin to be present in stressful situations and understand immediately what is wrong.

Specific conflicts or pressures that lead to downright unhappiness, intense arguments, or confusion with life without their twin include the following:

The most common and unfortunate disagreements occur when twins continue to be estranged during the holiday season and cannot get the support they need from their twin. Twin estrangement is often seen when one twin is favored over the other by their parents or other caregiving adults.

Social development, including making relationships with non-twins, is also unique and very complicated as twins develop and mature. I can say with great certainty that fitting into the non-twin world is enormously difficult for most twins, who feel ashamed of themselves for fighting with their twin sister or brother and at the same time lonely for their twin sister or brother’s companionship and advice. Outsiders directly question why a twin cannot get along with their truest companion. These questions about closeness frustrate and annoy twins who are trying to get along and falling short of doing so.

Estrangement can grow out of frustration when twins disagree. When differences can be reconciled, the harmony between twins can be reestablished, and estrangement will be reduced. Twin estrangement is both very serious and hard to control. Do not minimize or ignore estrangement. Parenting for individuality helps twins get over estrangement more easily than twins who are encouraged to compete with each other and stay alike.

My interest in twins and their unique development is related to my own identity as a twin and why my twin and I could not resolve our love-hate relationship. As a USC graduate student in psychology and education, I learned a great deal about how twins were and are still used in psychological research to understand the relative importance of nature, nurture, and parenting in development. When genetic determinants are important, twins are often used to figure out what is inherited and what is a function of proper environmental care. There is no agreement about what is more important, genes or environmental care, for identity development, but there is a lot of research on this issue. In my opinion, all of these influences are extremely critical to twin identity development. In particular, parental attentiveness is extremely valuable.

Taking care of twins is extremely time-consuming and difficult physically, emotionally, and personally. Finding help and paying the bills for all of the extras is a challenge. Here are some of my suggestions for reducing your parenting stress levels.

1. Make sure that you get to know each of your twins' likes and dislikes. Know your twins as individuals with unique interests and feelings.

2. Teach twins how to get along with each other, even if this seems impossible when they are fighting intensely or arguing with you or each other.

3. Make sure that your twins know that some toys, food, attention, friends, and interests cannot be shared. This point of view will promote individuality and reduce anger, fighting, and estrangement.

4. Help your twins understand that friendships should not be shared.

5. Find twin families that want to be your friends and arrange family or twin play dates.

It is hard to be a twin, and it is hard to raise twins. Try to get the best help you can. Educate yourself on what twins need to thrive.

Gov. Bob Ferguson is proposing $10 million to fund basic infrastructure needed for the Northwest Advanced Clean Energy Park, a master-planned industrial area in Eastern Washington that will foster development of large-scale clean energy, advanced manufacturing and supply chains. The proposal will be included in his 2027–29 capital budget.

If approved by lawmakers, the funding would support sewer, water, electrical and road infrastructure at the park.

“This investment will support thousands of good-paying jobs in the trades and advanced industries, and unlock billions in private investment,” Ferguson said in a statement.

The 1,641-acre site was formerly part of the Hanford Nuclear Reservation, a sprawling federal property along the Columbia River near the Tri-Cities that is undergoing a massive environmental cleanup. Hanford was established during World War II to produce plutonium for the Manhattan Project.

According to the governor’s office, two projects have committed to locating at the park once infrastructure construction is complete: Atlas Agro’s low-carbon fertilizer production plant and the utility-scale Ruby Flats Solar Farm.

State officials said General Matter is also considering the location for producing advanced nuclear fuel for small reactors. That siting could potentially be beneficial for another project planned for the same area: Amazon, X-energy and Energy Northwest are developing plans for the Cascade Advanced Energy Facility, a nuclear plant comprised of clustered, small reactors.

In 2024, the Norwegian company Cenate expressed interest in building a factory at the park to produce lithium-ion battery components, according to the Tri-Cities Area Journal of Business, ultimately withdrew its plans.

The Tri-Cities is also attracting fusion-energy investment. Last year, Seattle startup Avalanche Energy landed a $10 million grant from the Washington State Department of Commerce’s Green Jobs Grant Program to develop FusionWERX, a commercial-scale testing facility for fusion technologies. The project is being developed in an existing Port of Benton building in Richland.

Washington is heading into its 2027–29 biennial budget with a multibillion-dollar shortfall, but officials have not yet settled on a definitive public estimate.

Ferguson has acknowledged the scale of the gap but has not publicly specified an amount. The shortfall concerns the state’s operating budget, which is separate from the capital budget funding proposed for the energy park.

HBO’s 5-Part Crime Drama Has One of the Best Seasons in TV History

📅 Nov 14, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Wire’s fourth season provided a far deeper look at Baltimore’s problems

The Wire’s fourth season provided a far deeper look at Baltimore’s problems

At the turn of the 21st century, HBO was the place for prestige crime drama. The launch of The Sopranos in 1999 paved the way for the 2002 premiere of The Wire, which focused on drug dealers in Baltimore and the police officers trying to disrupt their operations. The series is still one of the best shows of all time, and provided breakout roles for huge stars like Idris Elba and Michael B. Jordan. But while it was compelling from the beginning, The Wire truly achieved perfection in season 4 when the scope expanded beyond the drug trade to examine the cycle of institutional failure.

Season 1 of The Wire was tightly focused on the drug empire of Avon Barksdale (Wood Harris) and his business-savvy second-in-command Stringer Bell (Elba). Showrunner David Simon quickly established a brilliant mix of humor and pathos. The show could find comedy in something as simple as cops struggling to get new furniture into their office, and then gut audiences with the death of a relatable drug dealer. Season 2 shifted the focus to the city’s port system, a jarring change as many characters faded into the background to make room for the tragic tale of the Sobotka family. Season 3 was the most tightly linked to the show’s first season, largely completing the story of the Barksdale gang.

That made season 4 perfect for a soft reboot, a shift that was defined by star Dominic West all but disappearing after his relentless detective, Jimmy McNulty, was demoted for pissing off his bosses too many times. The detectives he previously worked with in the major crimes unit continue their work, investigating a vicious new gang that’s filling the void Barksdale left behind. Lester Freamon (Clarke Peters) effectively steps into the McNulty role of pursuing the truth, even when his supervisors would love for him to be a little less dogged, bringing a new methodical nature to the investigations instead of McNulty’s brash confidence. But while the cops vs. criminals beats are familiar, they become just one part of a far more ambitious season that fully capitalized on Simon’s knowledge of the city. (Simon worked as a journalist at The Baltimore Sun for 12 years before creating The Wire.)

The season’s largest secondary plot involves the students at a Baltimore middle school where former cop Roland Pryzbylewski (Jim True-Frost) begins teaching. The young actors do a phenomenal job establishing likable characters in terrible circumstances. You want to root for all of them, from the ambitious Randy Wagstaff (Maestro Harrell) to the fiercely protective Michael Lee (Tristan Wilds). By the end of season 4, the system has profoundly failed almost all of these kids, driving them to lives of addiction and violence. The Wire was always good at making most of its characters sympathetic, but seeing children trapped in such a brutal cycle really drives home the show’s themes of how crime thrives when institutions fail.

That theme also plays out in the mayoral campaign of Tommy Carcetti (Aidan Gillen). Carcetti was introduced in season 3 as an ambitious city council member, but he really shines in season 4 as he runs on reforming the Baltimore Police Department — and then quickly breaks his promises. Gillen’s oily performance as a political survivor who would doom his city to satisfy his own ambitions was what landed him the role of Littlefinger in Game of Thrones. Carcetti is even more compelling a character because he feels like a man who could have done good if he’d been willing to make sacrifices rather than feed his own ego.

The Wire season 4 is able to build so many new arcs because of the strong foundation established in the previous three seasons. The show is dense with rich characters who have grown significantly over the years even as they continue to struggle with many of the same problems. The cops and drug dealers alike feel world-weary as they deal with a more violent new generation represented by Marlo Stanfield (Jamie Hector) and his vicious enforcers Snoop (Felicia Pearson) and Chris Partlow (Gbenga Akinnagbe). Combined with the dark fate of most of the kids and Carcetti’s broken promises, the season creates the bleak feeling that everyone is trapped in a cycle of misery and decay.

Season 5 gave most of The Wire’s characters satisfying ends to their arcs and delivered a scathing critique of the downfall of American newspapers, commentary that has only become more profound 20 years later. But overall, it’s a big step down from season 4, spending too much time on a goofy fake serial killer plot and not enough on the nuanced character-driven stories that made season 4 feel so authentic. There are no bad seasons of The Wire, but season 4 perfectly achieved the show’s goals. It’s an alternately funny, tragic, and profound look at the complex ways that people are betrayed by systems that never really give them a chance to succeed.

In this, one of the key proposals is to introduce a daily transfer limit of 5 million Thai baht per customer at each regulated digital asset operator. That said, the limit would apply separately to incoming and outgoing stablecoin transfers. However, the value of these transactions must also be consistent with the customer’s declared income and financial position.

However, it’s important to note that transfers between a customer’s own accounts at two SEC-supervised operators would be exempt from the limit, provided both operators follow the Travel Rule.

Simultaneously, the SEC also wants to strengthen wallet ownership verification. In this, the SEC has enabled customers to deposit stablecoins from their own verified wallets and withdraw them to wallets registered in their names.

Additionally, operators would also need to follow the Travel Rule, analyze customers, identify suspicious accounts, and track transactions connected to high-risk wallets.

These requirements would be done in order to make it harder for criminals to move illicit funds through regulated platforms.

Finally, the proposal also introduces stricter responsibilities for exchanges and brokers. The Trump administration is giving states an optional six-month grace period for enforcing new Medicaid work requirements, which were due to go into effect January 1.

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) announced Friday an optional "hold harmless" period through June 30, 2027, to give states a transition period to further modernize systems, improve processes and streamline the applicant and beneficiary experience.

During the "hold harmless" period, states will still assess whether Medicaid applicants and beneficiaries meet the work requirements or qualify for an exclusion, but otherwise eligible people will not lose or be denied coverage solely because they fail to meet the requirement, CMS said.

CMS says the transition is intended to give states time to improve eligibility systems, automate verification, train workers and improve the beneficiary experience.

"States have made real progress, and they’ve been clear with us about what it takes to get this right for beneficiaries—at scale and for the long term," Dan Brillman, Deputy Administrator and Director of the Center for Medicaid and CHIP Services, said in a statement. And when someone needs support, they should know exactly what to do, when to do it, and where to go. That’s how we build a Medicaid program that works better for people, connects them to work, education, and training opportunities, and supports paths to prosperity."

During the six-month enforcement pause, CMS encourages states to use existing data to automatically verify qualifying activities and exemptions before requesting additional information from beneficiaries.

States are also encouraged to simplify notices and make the process easy to navigate so beneficiaries understand what the state has already verified, what information is still needed and how to respond or get help. States should also test eligibility systems end-to-end, particularly how the systems handle missing, outdated or conflicting information, to resolve operational issues and minimize inappropriate coverage disruptions.

CMS also called on states to coordinate with community partners to connect applicants and beneficiaries who need assistance with opportunities for employment, education, job training and volunteering.

The work requirements were established as part of last summer's massive H.R. 1 legislation, or the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. Under the mandate, certain Medicaid applicants and beneficiaries ages 19 to 64 must meet a statutory work requirement, unless excluded. There are exemptions in place for the medically frail, disabled and others who are unable to meet the mandates.

CMS’ June 2026 interim final rule outlines qualifying activities, including 80 hours per month of work, community service or participation in a work program; enrollment in an educational program at least half-time; a combination of qualifying activities totaling at least 80 hours per month; or meeting the specified monthly income threshold.

State Medicaid administrators, health insurers, physicians and hospitals have pushed back on the Medicaid work requirements, citing concerns about financial and operational challenges, a spike in uncompensated care and the rule's strict "medical frailty" exemptions.

Industry groups argue the requirements impose onerous documentation burdens. The Alliance of Community Health Plans said that the final rule adds documentation requirements and tightens criteria for exemptions to the requirements, which will make it harder for individuals who need coverage to secure exemptions they may qualify for.

The organization also noted that most individuals enrolled in Medicaid are already working or in school.

De là est née la structure des quatre co-présidents : j'avais un vice-président avec moi et Luc Chabert avait le sien de son côté. On s’est dit qu’on n’allait pas faire d'histoires et qu'à quatre co-présidents, on ne serait pas trop pour gérer le club.

Les choses se sont déroulées de la meilleure des façons. Au-delà de la présidence, on est tous devenus amis, ainsi que nos épouses respectives. Quand tu as quatre co-présidents comme ça qui mènent la barque, tous les gens derrière voient que ça marche super bien et l'engouement est parti. Au niveau de la répartition : Bruno Piccon s'occupe de toute la partie jeunes, Luc Chabert gère les partenaires, François Baudet s'occupe de la communication et du matériel, et pour ma part, je m'occupe des instances, des terrains, de la mairie de Vallières, du district, de la Ligue et de la Fédération. Je fais également partie du comité directeur de la Ligue où je représente les clubs nationaux de la région Rhône-Alpes, ainsi qu'au siège à Paris.

En dehors de cela, on a tous notre boulot, bien sûr. Toutes les trois semaines, on se réunit et on mange ensemble pour échanger sur les perspectives, les actions en cours ou à venir, et ce qui a été bien ou moins bien fait. Derrière, on a un comité directeur pour décider des orientations dans les différentes commissions, et trois semaines après, ces commissions nous rendent compte de leurs résultats. On avance comme ça, sans compter les coups de téléphone et les messages tous les deux jours... On communique beaucoup ! On est obligés de travailler comme cela maintenant parce qu'on a quand même 800 licenciés, ce n'est pas rien.

Comme tu le disais, le club est né d'une fusion en 2018, et il se retrouve aujourd'hui à une très belle place en championnat (le club est actuellement 2e de sa poule en National 1, à 3 points de Nîmes avec un match de retard, ndp²). Le club a aussi connu une épopée fantastique en Coupe de France en 2021. Quel regard portez-vous sur cette ascension fulgurante et est-ce que le monde professionnel est une ambition ou un doux rêve ?

On a l'habitude de faire les choses les unes après les autres. Quand on est montés, on voulait voir à quel niveau on pouvait se situer. Aujourd'hui, il ne faut pas se leurrer, les joueurs comme le staff sont pratiquement tous professionnels. Comme nous avons un budget serré, nous souhaitons avancer pas à pas et ne surtout pas brûler les étapes.

Au niveau du coach, nous avons un gars super, Cédric Rullier, qui a essentiellement recruté régionalement. On ne voulait pas recruter trop loin parce que la vie est très chère en Haute-Savoie et qu'il faut se loger. Maintenant qu'on a un niveau honnête avec de l’ambition, on va chercher un petit peu plus loin, mais toujours des joueurs ciblés sur leur mentalité et leur respect. C'est après les qualités sportives bien sûr, mais un joueur qui ne rentre pas dans notre moule mentalement, on ne le prend pas. On veut garder notre esprit familial, même si on s'est beaucoup professionnalisés.

On a énormément de bénévoles et on veut que la mayonnaise prenne entre eux et les joueurs. C'est ce qui fait notre force aujourd'hui : le groupe vit très bien, l'entraîneur est exceptionnel et les bénévoles sont hyper dévoués. Dès que tu demandes quelque chose, ils sont tous derrière toi pour t'aider. Aujourd'hui, c'est fantastique !

La Coupe de France, cela n’a rien à voir avec le championnat, les émotions sont multipliées par dix ou quinze. En 2021, lors de notre exploit, on a commencé en gagnant aux tirs au but contre un club de district, un truc de dingue. Petit à petit, on a passé les tours : on a sorti Prix-lès-Mézières et le FC Annecy aux tirs au but, puis Le Puy en huitièmes, Toulouse en quarts, avant de tomber contre Monaco en demi-finale. C'était complètement fou.

Le seul bémol, c'est que ça s'est joué pendant le Covid, donc il n'y avait pas de supporters au stade. Mais ça ne nous a pas empêchés de nous retrouver le soir des victoires. La mairie et la gendarmerie ont été très sympas avec nous parce qu'ils savaient qu'ils ne pouvaient pas faire autrement. J'ai une petite anecdote pour le match contre Monaco : le préfet avait donné l'autorisation à deux ou trois équipes de jeunes de nous accompagner à la sortie du restaurant à midi. Sauf qu'on s'est retrouvés à 3 000 dans les rues ! Les gendarmes n'ont rien pu faire, c'était un truc de fou. C'est une expérience qu'on ne reverra peut-être jamais, en tout cas pas moi, mais c'est tout le mal que je souhaite au club.

Depuis, ça fait trois ans qu'on se fait éliminer aux tirs au but. On s'était dit que cette année, il fallait que ça change pour aller chercher un 32e ou un gros club. On n'est pas encore en 32e, on a eu quelques soucis et on est encore passés aux tirs au but au tour précédent. J'ai l'impression que c'est dans la tête : quand tu rencontres une équipe plus faible, tu te mets à son diapason. Mais avec les joueurs qu'on a aujourd'hui, si on tombe contre une équipe plus forte, ils savent hausser leur niveau. On ne lâche rien. Par exemple, hier à Saint-Priest, il y avait 2-2 à un quart d'heure de la fin. On fait rentrer le banc, pouf, on met 4-2, et si le match continue on en met encore deux ou trois. C'est formidable.

Parlons un peu des infrastructures du club et de vos perspectives d'évolution. De quels terrains disposez-vous actuellement ?

On a un terrain en herbe à Rumilly avec un synthétique à côté. Le centre de Vallières est consacré aux jeunes et aux matchs de Ligue pour nos réserves (l'équipe 2 joue en R1 et l'équipe 3 en R3). Là-bas, on a un terrain en herbe et un synthétique tout neuf, deux jolis terrains.

Là où le bât blesse aujourd'hui, c'est à Rumilly : notre synthétique doit être refait et on voudrait améliorer le terrain en herbe. En ce moment il est beau parce qu'il pleut, mais l'été il est sec et l'hiver il gèle. Ce n'est pas une vraie pelouse de stade, elle a 50 ans. Les jardiniers s'en occupent très bien, mais il faut refaire la surface. On hésite entre synthétique, hybride ou herbe. On se bat un peu avec la mairie et la communauté de communes pour avoir une vision sur les prochaines années. Un beau terrain amène du beau jeu et des spectateurs, c'est un tout. Les municipalités ne sont pas contre, mais il faut trouver les fonds et les subventions.

Vous pouvez bénéficier d'aides financières des collectivités ?

Oui, la mairie nous donne 100 000 euros et la communauté de communes 100 000 euros aussi. Pour le département, les subventions dépendent de la division dans laquelle tu évolues. Avec la réforme des championnats et les changements d'appellation, on est en train de regarder à quoi on a droit en tant que premier niveau amateur de France. C'est un combat de tous les jours. Il faut rien lâcher, tout en gardant un bon état d'esprit et en évitant les conflits. Ça a déjà bien avancé par rapport à nos débuts, mais il en faut encore plus.

Quel est votre budget actuel et est-il stable ?

On est à 1,9 million d'euros. Quand on a fusionné, on était à 600 000 euros. Aujourd'hui, on doit avoir le dixième budget de notre poule. On est dans une poule Sud très relevée avec des clubs comme Nîmes ou Toulon. C'est quasiment une petite division supérieure, capable de rivaliser avec le milieu de tableau au-dessus.

Il y a aussi quatre clubs de la région lyonnaise dans votre groupe. Comment vous positionnez-vous face à eux, notamment sur le recrutement ?

Ils ont un gros avantage sur nous : ils ont des pépinières de joueurs partout, avec 20 ou 25 clubs au kilomètre carré. Nous, à part Annecy avec qui on travaille en partenariat, on est assez isolés. Lyon est à 120 kilomètres. Et puis à Rumilly, on est à côté d'Annecy et de Genève : un T2 coûte 800 ou 900 euros par mois, ce n'est pas 300 euros comme en Lozère ou dans le Centre. C'est pour ça qu'on fait très attention à qui on fait venir.

Justement, comment êtes-vous organisés pour recruter sans cellule de recrutement professionnelle ?

Nos deux coachs travaillent énormément à la vidéo et vont voir beaucoup de matchs. On a aussi un directeur sportif à plein temps et une petite cellule composée d'anciens joueurs du club. On les envoie observer des matchs à Mâcon, Bourg ou Thonon avec des fiches précises sur un joueur ciblé. On travaille aussi avec quelques agents qu'on connaît bien, même si ce n'est pas notre tasse de thé parce qu'un agent ne te dira jamais que son joueur est mauvais.

Avant de recruter un joueur, il est visionné trois ou quatre fois, en vidéo et en physique. Ensuite, le directeur sportif l'appelle pour sonder sa mentalité, et celle de sa compagne ou de sa famille, car c'est très important. On leur explique le coût de la vie ici. Niveau infrastructures, on est équipés avec salle de muscu et bains froids, mais on n'a pas des salaires de 4 000 euros comme dans le Midi. On ne veut pas qu'un gars arrive et dise qu'il ne peut pas vivre ici. Après, on se voit, on discute beaucoup avec Luc Chabert, le directeur sportif et les coachs. On marche au feeling, et en discutant avec la maman ou la copine, on voit vite si on ne se trompe pas sur la mentalité. En moyenne, entre la visio, les appels et les repas tous ensemble, on se voit plusieurs fois avant de signer. On prépare déjà la saison prochaine, en regardant plutôt vers le nord que vers le sud, car un joueur qui habite à Nîmes ou Hyères au bord de la mer avec un bon salaire est très dur à faire venir en Haute-Savoie.

Vous faites aussi monter des jeunes de votre réserve, j'imagine ?

Oui, mais on part de loin. Quand on a lancé la fusion chez les jeunes en 2016, le niveau n'était pas très élevé. Aujourd'hui, toutes nos équipes de U15, U17 et U18 évoluent en Régional 1. Notre équipe réserve, qui joue aussi en R1, est composée à 60 % de jeunes du club. Ils ne sont pas encore prêts pour briguer une place de titulaire en équipe première, mais chaque année, le coach en intègre quelques-uns. Cette saison, trois jeunes du club font tous les entraînements avec le groupe, et un ou deux sont vraiment prometteurs parce qu'ils haussent leur niveau.

C'est très dur d'atteindre le niveau national quand des gamins n'ont pas eu une grosse formation dès l'âge de 7 ou 8 ans. Maintenant, on commence à récolter les fruits de notre travail : ceux qu'on a pris à 7 ou 8 ans en ont 13 ou 14 aujourd'hui, et on voit qu'il y a un vrai niveau. Pour nous, faire monter des jeunes du club, c'est aussi l'avantage de ne pas avoir à leur chercher un appartement ! Mais en attendant, on recrute surtout des joueurs qui ont un bon niveau en National 2 et qui ont faim, plutôt que d'aller chercher des gars de Ligue 2 pour qui venir jouer chez nous serait vécu comme un échec.

Tu disais que les joueurs fonctionnent comme des pros. Ils vivent tous du football aujourd'hui ?

Statutairement on est amateurs, mais dans le fonctionnement, on est comme des pros. À part deux ou trois jeunes en service civique qui montent dans le groupe, tous les joueurs vivent de ça. Ils s'entraînent une à deux fois par jour. Comme les matchs ont souvent lieu le vendredi soir et qu'il faut partir dès le jeudi midi pour aller à Châteauroux ou ailleurs, il est impossible qu'ils travaillent à côté.

Sans entrer dans les détails indiscrets, quel est l'ordre d'idée des rémunérations ?

Je ne vais pas donner de chiffres précis. On fait des contrats d'un an, et on a deux ou trois joueurs qui sortent un peu du lot. Mais globalement, nos salaires sont 30 à 40 % moins élevés que dans les clubs du Midi. Ils en vivent, mais ce ne sont pas des salaires de cadres. Après, il n'y a pas que l'argent : les joueurs connaissent les conditions et le cadre de vie avant de signer. S'ils acceptent, ils savent où ils mettent les pieds et ça se passe très bien.

Un mot sur le vestiaire et le staff : vous avez notamment Cyril Martin-Pichon, formé à l'ASSE et qui a beaucoup joué dans les équipes de jeunes et même en réserve, qui est un cadre chez vous, et un staff très fidèle au club.

Oui, Cyril Martin-Pichon a quitté le système pro parce qu'il n'y adhérait plus tellement. Il pense au collectif et au plaisir avant tout. Ça fait trois ans qu'il est chez nous, c'est un excellent joueur avec une mentalité irréprochable, un vrai cadre.

Pour le staff, notre entraîneur Cédric Rullier est passé par Annecy, Chambéry et Lyon. Ça fait trois ou quatre ans qu'il est là. Il est diététicien à l'hôpital de Chambéry et, depuis l'an dernier, il a mis son métier entre parenthèses pour se consacrer uniquement au club. Tout le reste du staff est issu du club : son adjoint Damien Guerrier, notre jeune entraîneur des gardiens qu'on a formé, et nos deux préparateurs physiques. Ce ne sont pas des gens arrivés sur le tard, on les connaît depuis dix ans, depuis tout petits. Ils sont rémunérés par le club, sont exigeants — on améliore nos équipements chaque année — mais ils sont surtout dévoués à 100 %.

J'ai été impressionné par les statistiques de Rumilly cette année : vous avez une puissance de feu phénoménale en attaque ! Le nombre de buts que vous marquez est impressionnant.

C'est bizarre parce qu'en début de saison, on se disait justement qu'on était un peu courts en attaque ! Et finalement, on marque beaucoup de buts, je crois qu'on a la meilleure attaque de toutes les poules confondues. C'est grâce à notre force mentale et au coach qui tient bien son groupe. Les joueurs savent que si ça ne va pas, ils peuvent partir, mais à côté de ça, ils ont un cadre hyper convivial avec les bénévoles qui sont aux petits soins pour eux.

Il y a des joueurs qui se révèlent, comme Fortier qui avait dû marquer trois buts l'an dernier et qui est dans tous les bons coups cette saison. Et puis on a des gars très techniques au milieu comme Cyril Martin-Pichon, Yoann Martelat ou Quentin Foulay, qui donnent d'excellents ballons devant. J'espère que ça va durer.

Dans votre championnat, il n'y a que le premier qui monte, ou le deuxième joue des barrages ?

Il n'y a que le premier qui monte, il n'y a pas de barrages pour le deuxième. Après, comme chaque année avec les passages devant la DNCG, il arrive parfois que des places se libèrent et que le meilleur deuxième des trois poules soit repêché.

Et la Ligue 3 professionnelle, si l'occasion se présente, vous y pensez ?

Comme je le disais au départ, ce sont les résultats qui décideront. L'an dernier déjà, à la trêve, on était premiers et on commençait à se poser la question. Sportivement, on peut y arriver si les joueurs le méritent. Mais aujourd'hui, je trouve que la Ligue 3 n'est pas encore prête au niveau de la Fédération, et les aides financières ne sont pas forcément à la hauteur de ce qui avait été annoncé.

Un bon copain qui est au Puy m'a prévenu que le cahier des charges est énorme : il faut des caméras, des parcages visiteurs, un directeur de club. Si tu ne remplis pas tous les critères, on te retient des pénalités sur les subventions, et sur les 300 000 euros prévus, il ne t'en reste plus que 150 000 à l'arrivée. Les déplacements ne seraient pas un problème puisqu'on traverse déjà toute la France, mais il faudrait vraiment de grosses aides du département, de la mairie et de la communauté de communes pour mettre le stade aux normes.

Revenons à la Coupe de France. Quelle a été la première réaction au club quand le tirage au sort a désigné l'AS Saint-Étienne ?

Moi, j'étais à Paris pour le tirage. Trois journalistes m'ont sauté dessus avant même la fin pour me demander ce que j'en pensais et où on allait jouer ! Ce n'est pas un soulagement, c'est l'opportunité qu'on cherchait : tomber sur un club pro dès le 7e tour.

La question du stade s'est tout de suite posée parce qu'à Rumilly, on a une capacité de 2 500 places, impossible d'accueillir les Verts. Sébastien Faraglia, le président du FC Annecy, m'a tout de suite appelé pour nous dépanner. C'est un ami, je l'ai connu joueur et entraîneur à Rumilly, et Luc Chabert est aussi partenaire du FC Annecy avec ses entreprises. Il y a une vraie osmose.

Au club, ça a été l'embrasement général, tout le monde ne parlait que de ça alors qu'on avait deux matchs de championnat à jouer entre-temps. L'entraîneur a très bien géré le coup. Dès le lundi après le tirage, il a enfermé les joueurs dans le vestiaire et leur a dit : « Le premier qui parle de Saint-Étienne ou qui s'économise, avec moi ça ne marchera pas ». Résultat, on a gagné 5-0 contre Istres et 4-2 hier. Maintenant, on peut se consacrer à Saint-Étienne.

Comment abordez-vous ce match face à un monument du foot français ?

Il y a deux divisions d'écart, ce sont des pros, et Saint-Étienne est un club que tout le monde adore. Après, c'est la trêve internationale, donc ils ont pas mal de joueurs partis en sélection. Il restera une très belle équipe, on ne va pas se cacher, mais ce ne sera pas l'équipe type. En coupe, les niveaux se resserrent et les équipes inférieures haussent souvent le ton. D'habitude, on dit que le terrain avantage le petit, mais là on jouera sur la pelouse hybride d'Annecy, l'une des meilleures de France. Au niveau technique et du jeu, ça va vraiment être le top.

Le Parc des Sports d'Annecy sera à guichets fermés ?

Pas encore. Saint-Étienne a eu une ouverture prioritaire pour ses Ultras — il y aura d'ailleurs deux groupes d'Ultras de Saint Etienne qui feront le déplacement. La billetterie grand public a ouvert depuis avant-hier. Le stade fait 13 000 places, mais la jauge de sécurité sera fixée autour de 10 000 ou 10 500 spectateurs. Pour des raisons de sécurité, les spectateurs en tribune ne peuvent pas croiser le parcage stéphanois pour aller aux toilettes, donc on doit condamner un quart de virage, ce qui enlève environ 2 000 places.

On a vendu entre 5 000 et 6 500 places les deux ou trois premiers jours, et là on tourne à 100 ou 150 billets par jour. S'il fait beau dimanche, tous les papas avec leurs enfants vont se dire qu'ils prennent une place à 10 euros pour voir le match. S'il pleut, ils viendront moins. Ils annoncent un petit peu de pluie, on verra bien.

Vous sentez toute la région derrière vous ?

Oui, on sent que les Verts arrivent à Annecy. Bon, les Annéciens sont un peu habitués puisqu'ils les jouent en championnat. Ici, les gens ne parlent même pas de notre équipe, ils parlent des Verts qui débarquent ! C'est un club emblématique, historique, c'est comme ça. Quant à nos joueurs, je les ai vus hier soir : ils sont confiants, dans leur bulle. Ils pensent à Saint-Étienne mais n'en font pas une maladie, pour ne pas jouer le match trois fois dans leur tête avant dimanche. Mais à partir d'aujourd'hui, ça va forcément commencer à trotter.

Si tu devais signer tout de suite pour un scénario de match — même une qualification stéphanoise à l'arraché après une rencontre dantesque — qu'est-ce qui te ferait dire que l'événement est une réussite totale ?

L'événement sera une réussite à partir du moment où le stade sera plein. Enfin, il sera plein, j’en suis sûr ! Ensuite, il faut qu'on fasse un match à la hauteur de notre niveau. Même si on perd, si c'est un beau match, les gens seront contents : ils auront vu une belle équipe de Saint-Étienne et ils auront vu qu'on a su répondre présent. Ce sera une fête réussie, en espérant derrière que ça nous laisse quand même un petit reliquat financier. On aura fait notre boulot face aux Verts.

Saint-Étienne devrait vous laisser sa part de la recette, normalement ?

On verra. Je pense que si on accueille bien les spectateurs, si on fait une belle fête, si les supporters — parce que ce sont eux qui font l'ambiance — sont bien reçus et qu'il n'y a pas de problème avec l'équipe de Saint-Étienne, ils la laisseront. Ils nous ont demandé plein de choses au niveau des vestiaires, mais si tout se passe bien, il n'y aura pas de souci. De toute façon, ce n'est pas dans notre habitude de recevoir des équipes en se disant qu'on va les matraquer. Quand on a reçu Monaco, ils avaient fait notre éloge pour l'accueil, parce qu'on n'avait pas joué pour blesser ou pour détruire. Ce sera exactement dans la même lignée : on n'est pas là pour casser du Vert, loin de là, au contraire, on est là pour profiter. Par contre, si on a l'occasion de passer, on passera, c'est sûr !

Tu parlais des demandes de Saint-Étienne pour les vestiaires, ils ont beaucoup d'exigences ?

Ça ne m'a pas trop surpris. Comme notre coach est diététicien, quand on se déplace, on a déjà pas mal de choses : différentes eaux, des minéraux, des barres de chocolat, des casse-croûtes, des tapis, des biscuits... Là, c'est dans le même style, en amplifié : de l'eau, des barres de céréales, des fruits, etc. C'est assez énorme, mais ça ne nous surprend pas parce qu'on fait déjà un peu la même chose.

Qui sont vos interlocuteurs au sein du club de Saint-Étienne pour organiser tout ça ?

Quand j'étais à Paris pour le tirage, un ancien joueur de Saint-Étienne qui fait partie de l'encadrement m'a contacté en me disant que le directeur allait m'appeler, et cinq minutes après, il m'appelait. On est en contact avec le directeur du club (Jean-François Soucasse, NDLR) et le directeur de la sécurité. Les relations ont été très sympathiques avec des gens très gentils. Nous avons de très bons rapports.

Une dernière question : est-ce que tu serais d’accord pour que l'année prochaine,  peut-être à la même époque, on refasse un petit point pour voir un peu où vous en êtes un an après avoir joué les Verts ?

Oui, il n'y a pas de souci. Ce sera avec plaisir.

Eh bien écoute Bernard, un grand merci pour ton temps, bonne journée et bon match pour dimanche !

Attackers in Binance Breach Targeted Known Weakness, Missing Controls

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

News, news analysis, and commentary on the latest trends in cybersecurity technology.

Blockchain complexities, such as attacker-deployed sweepers, hobble security controls designed to limit the damage of an exchange compromise.

On Oct. 29, cyber thieves transferred around $6.34 billion in various cryptocurrencies from at least 11 wallets owned by exchange Binance to attacker-controlled addresses. Binance seemingly had not put in place security controls to quickly detect malicious authorizations and disable them through a pre-tested kill switch, experts say — which should put other crypto firms on notice.

While Binance's internal security systems issued an alert in minutes, the attackers exploited a feature of Ethereum smart accounts — a relatively new type of account abstraction introduced to Ethereum with the EIP-7702 proposal in 2025 — to essentially install malicious code, known as a "sweeper." The sweeper code transferred all the funds held in a primary treasury wallet at Binance — consisting of more than five dozen cryptocurrencies totaling approximately $2.5 billion — to the attacker's address created minutes before.

Related:Getting Past the Post-Quantum Cryptography Discovery Phase

In the end, the wallet was left with a single ETH coin, according to an analysis by blockchain security firm BlockAid.

Large transactions from other wallets to the same address, initiated by the attackers, triggered internal alerts at Binance. The company took countermeasures, but the smart-account authorization continued to function for about four hours, siphoning additional funds.

"An EIP-7702 authorization [is] a standing delegation," says Ido Ben-Natan, co-founder and CEO of BlockAid. "Once it is on-chain, attacker-controlled code can move that hot wallet's funds, and anything later deposited to it, until the same wallet ... [revokes the authorization]. It carries no amount and no destination, so withdrawal controls never see it."

Despite a sweeper function enabled through EIP-7702 authorization being a known threat, Binance noted in a Nov. 3 post-mortem report that the authorization hobbled the companies efforts to stop transactions from flowing out onto the blockchain.

"Once established, these authorizations enabled the attackers to continue moving assets independently of Binance's normal withdrawal infrastructure," the company stated in a post-mortem report. "Binance needed additional transactions to revoke these on-chain authorizations ... suspending withdrawals and shutting down hot wallet services did not automatically invalidate existing on-chain authorizations."

Developers were the initial weak point, enabling the EIP-7702 authorization attack to occur. One developer downloaded a compromised version of Homebrew, an open source package management tool for Macs, while a second developer fell for a fake job interview, during which they were given a software "assignment" that compromised his system, according to Binance's post-mortem.

In the end, the attackers used their compromise of the two developers' systems — as well as a third developer's system — to gain signing privileges, creating 50 pre-signed authorizations to flood the targeted wallet, known among blockchain analysts as "Binance 14," at the right time.

The same targeting of developers happened during the ByBit Ethereum heist on Feb. 21, 2025, says Avi Sambira, vice president of North America at incident-response firm Sygnia. In that case, cyber thieves compromised the Mac of a Safe{Wallet} developer nearly three weeks before the actual theft, according to Sygnia's analysis of that incident. Safe{Wallet} is used by Ethereum and other decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs) to enforce multisignature security.

"[A]n AWS token was used and attackers operated inside Safe{Wallet}'s cloud environment for roughly two weeks before modifying the signing interface," Sambira says. "Binance's disclosure appears consistent with the same model, with initial reporting indicating endpoint and internal-tool compromise preceded credential, token, and[/or] container access to wallet signing."

Like that incident, the FBI placed blame directly on North Korean cybercriminal operators, releasing a list of more than 600 Ethereum addresses, 19 Bitcoin addresses, and 1 SOL address linked to TraderTraitor, a moniker for the group behind the attacks.

Exchanges and crypto-finance firms need to treat their developers, vendors, and suppliers as critical elements of their infrastructure. Attackers are increasingly looking to compromise developer identities, repositories, CI/CD pipelines, cloud control planes, and third-party integrations, Sambira says. Those assets only sit one or two trust boundaries away from withdrawal authority.

"TraderTraitor's evolution is less about abandoning old techniques than combining them into a more sophisticated trust attack," he says. "Fake recruiters and malicious coding assignments establish the foothold, [while] stolen sessions, cloud credentials, and trusted interfaces turn that foothold into transaction control."

When the cyberattackers used their pre-generated authorizations, they sent 50 at the same time, because every transaction needs a valid nonce — a sequential counter — so the attackers used a window of 50 authorizations and needed only a single one to match.

The nonce spray to install a malicious sweeper is a new, but known, threat. In a paper presented at the USENIX Security conference in August 2026, researchers from Hong Kong University of Science and Technology and two other Chinese schools revealed that at least 924 malicious smart contracts had been enabled through the EIP-7702 mechanism, leading to at least $2.3 million in losses.

In its own research, BlockAid saw at least $1.1 million in losses across four instances, and predicted that the first 10-figure attack would arrive before the end of 2026. They were off by a factor of 100.

The attacks can be stopped — or at least blunted — by a combination of controls. Any company processing cryptocurrency transactions in real time should use simulations to detect malicious rules, enforce policies on the allowed size of transactions and their destinations, issue alerts based on detecting a burst of large transactions, and deploy a fast kill switch that has been pre-built to pause signing and stop wallet refills, says BlockAid's Ben-Natan.

Binance seemingly had none — or only some — of those controls in place. In the end, it took nearly four hours — the attack started at 20:05 UTC and the last transaction was recorded at 23:56 UTC — for the company to stop transactions from being recorded on the blockchain, subsequently freezing and recovering about $2.85 billion in attempted stolen assets. The company did not respond to requests for comment.

Binance highlights the importance of having good monitoring and the ability to detect anomalous transactions.

The difficulty in responding to the attacks underscores the problems that cryptocurrency exchanges and infrastructure firms face to combat theft and fraud. In many ways, Binance had created an overlapping set of controls to detect attacks and block transfers in near real time. However, the state-sponsored attackers found a way to bypass those controls through the EIP-7702 sweeper.

The pressure on cryptocurrency firms will only rise, because attackers can exfiltrate the funds quickly, says Ari Redbord, global head of policy at TRM Labs and a former federal prosecutor and US Treasury official.

"For years we've seen hacks against large financial institutions, tech companies and even government agencies — the difference is that in crypto you can steal funds in real time," he says. "There's no clawback window, and the money is gone in minutes."

Crypto-finance firms need to have visibility across four domains: identity and authority governance, infrastructure and execution integrity, custody and transaction authorization, and on- and off-chain detection, says Sygnia's Sambira. Companies that only monitor transactions on the blockchain are seeing changes too late, he adds.

In addition, crypto firms should rigorously track all identities and systems that can initiate, approve, modify or execute transactions, and be ready to suspend those accounts' privileges, he says.

"Controls with the greatest containment value involve the pre-authorized ability to revoke identities and tokens, pause transfers, isolate wallets and rebuild affected infrastructure within minutes," Sambira says. "Those capabilities must be tested before a crisis hits and retested regularly."

Testing and practicing these controls are critical, because companies need to know that they will work in real-time, says TRM Labs' Redbord.

"The actionable part for any company touching crypto is that these patterns are recognizable — if you watch in real time, you can act in the first moments, when it matters most," he says. "This is all about speed."

Monitoring transactions can detect suspicious activity quite quickly and, in the case of the cryptocurrency thefts targeting Binance and ByBit, can stop attacks before too much value is lost. The keys to adequate responses are speed and relationships to the various stakeholders, says Redbord.

"Issuers like Tether and Circle can freeze the addresses holding stolen tokens, and then burn those tokens and reissue the same amount to the rightful owner," he says. "The funds don't have to be chased down. They can be made whole on-chain."

Nearly all issuers, exchanges, blockchain intelligence firms and many global law enforcement agencies are part of the Beacon Network, created by TRM Labs and other industry players, for early detection of and intelligence sharing about cryptocurrency incidents. In addition, cryptocurrency-reliant businesses should ensure that any exchange with which they do business has withdrawal limits and allowlists, isolates their signing systems on computers reserved for that purpose, and constantly trains their engineers against social engineering, Redbord says.

Organizations should also reduce measures of time to detect, time to contain, and time to freeze to seconds. The approval of major transactions should require multiple signatures from systems that are only used for that purpose, and businesses need to have a response plan that keeps them operational while they recover.

Binance has taken some of these steps, including identifying the affected systems, cleaning or replacing them, and replacing wallets, it stated in its initial incident report. They've also completed "enhanced" security scans across employee devices, rotated credentials, and deployed enhanced AI-based behavioral anomaly detection, the company stated.

"There are a number of security measures we have implemented and are implementing that will remain private to prevent malicious actors from compromising our security," the company stated.

How Agent Anomaly Detection scores on benchmarks we didn't write

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AI agents now read email, book travel and move money for the people who use them. Every tool call also pulls in text that someone else wrote, and whoever controls that text can try to steer the agent. OWASP calls this agent goal hijack and ranks it first in its Top 10 for Agentic Applications (2026).

In September we announced Agent Anomaly Detection in Private Preview on Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform. It reads your agents' traces and logs asynchronously, without adding latency, and flags sessions where an agent acts outside its intended boundaries. We tested this feature against benchmarks for this top risk of agent goal hijack.

Security tools tend to look good on tests their own team wrote, so we ran five non-Google-written, public agent-security benchmarks through Agent Anomaly Detection without changing anything. In four of them, the harmful instruction hides in data that a tool returns. In the fifth, the user asks for harm directly.

The model itself didn't follow about half of the harmful instructions it read. It refused three in four direct requests for harm, and ignored or failed at about 30% of the instructions hidden in tool output. Of the ones that were executed, Agent Anomaly Detection flagged 87% for post-execution review. It raised false alarms on 2% to 16% of benign sessions, depending on the tools the agent uses. Poisoned memory is the clear gap: when instructions planted in the agent’s long-term memory made it act, Agent Anomaly Detection caught 4 of 18.

We ran each benchmark case through an agent built with the Agent Development Kit (ADK) using gemini-2.5-flash, on the benchmark's simulated apps. The agent sent its traces and logs to Agent Anomaly Detection, as a customer's agent would. Then the benchmark's own Python check read the final state of the apps, such as whether money left the account, and labeled the session. Deterministic code decides the label, not a model, so an injected instruction can't mislead the grader, and Agent Anomaly Detection cannot cheat the benchmark. Each agent first ran at least 300 benign sessions, because Agent Anomaly Detection learns how each agent usually behaves.

We also recorded whether a tool returned the harmful instruction to the agent. In many cases, the agent never opens the email or file that holds it. So each harmful session ends one of three ways:

In AgentHarm, the user sends the request, so the agent always reads it.

The five benchmarks are AgentDojo (NeurIPS 2024), InjecAgent (ACL 2024 Findings), Agent Security Bench (ASB, ICLR 2025), AgentDyn (2026) and AgentHarm (ICLR 2025). ASB plants the instruction in the agent's long-term memory. In all, Agent Anomaly Detection scored 1,774 sessions.

Agent Anomaly Detection flagged 87% of the harmful instructions that the agent carried out, and raised a few false alarms on ordinary office work. It's strongest on goal hijack and tool misuse, and weakest on poisoned memory.

Each finding carries a threat category, and four of Agent Anomaly Detection’s five categories align with the OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications. We graded each OWASP category on what this study showed, not on what Agent Anomaly Detection is designed to catch.

Public benchmarks concentrate on goal hijack and tool misuse, so our evidence is strongest there. We did not find public benchmarks with labeled agent traces for cascading failures or for identity and privilege abuse. Shared benchmarks for those categories would help the whole field.

By benchmark, Agent Anomaly Detection flagged 85% of the carried-out harmful instructions on AgentDojo, 87% on InjecAgent, 99% on AgentDyn and 81% on AgentHarm. Over all harmful sessions, including the ones that the agent never read or resisted, it flagged 45% to 62%. In the 141 sessions where the agent never read the instruction, nothing happened, and it flagged just 1.

Results held across the apps. In 8 of the 11 apps, Agent Anomaly Detection flagged at least 95% of the carried-out harmful instructions. The lowest rate was in AgentDojo's Slack app, at 63%. Many of those instructions ask for one small action, such as a page visit, which looks like normal browsing.

Agent Anomaly Detection also flags many requests that the agent refuses. In AgentHarm, the agent refused most requests and carried out 108 of 412, and Agent Anomaly Detection still flagged 55% of the refused sessions, because the request sits in the user's own message, which it reads. When the agent didn't follow an instruction hidden in tool output, it flagged only 11% to 25%, because the instruction sits deep inside a tool result.

False alarms were rare on ordinary office work: on the three injection benchmarks, whose benign sessions are office tasks, Agent Anomaly Detection flagged 2% to 4% of benign sessions. On AgentHarm it flagged 16%. Those benign tasks are twins of the harmful ones, so they post to social media, run terminal commands and download files. High-impact tools in normal use look more like misuse.

Agent Anomaly Detection did less well in five areas. It flagged only 11% to 25% of the instructions that the agent resisted, because a resisted instruction changes little in how the agent behaves. It missed more small actions, like single page visits, because they look like normal work. And it raised more false alarms for agents whose everyday work uses high-impact tools. If your agent is one of them, expect more findings at first, and triage them by severity and threat category.

The other two areas are new agents and poisoned memory. Some Agent Anomaly Detection methods need a few hundred sessions of history: on AgentDyn, it flagged 33% of carried-out harmful instructions when each agent had about 220 benign sessions, and 99% with 360. And it caught only 4 of the 18 sessions where poisoned memory made the agent act.

Protect your agents with defense in depth. Use controls that stop harmful actions before they run, and Agent Anomaly Detection to find what gets through.

We used one agent model and simulated apps, and the benchmarks' benign tasks are likely simpler than your production traffic, so your false-alarm rate can differ. Agent Anomaly Detection returned no result for about 6% of the sessions we sent; counting those as misses lowers the overall rates by 1 to 2 points.

Catch Go bugs that fuzzing misses with Gemini and a constraint solver

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

I help lead Google's agent skills program, currently with 175 agent skills that supplement community collections like go-skills. To keep that many skill files consistent, I wrote skill-lint, a standalone Go CLI that checks SKILL.md files and applies LLM-generated line edits before they ship. Even with skill-lint's own unit and fuzz test suite, a small slice or line-number mistake inside its Markdown parsers or edit applier can sit dormant until an unusual file or edit range triggers a runtime panic in the linter.

In Go, every slice or index operation that can panic at runtime compiles down to an explicit bounds-check trap in machine code, after the compiler's optimizer strips out the checks it can already rule out. To hunt for crashes, I compiled key functions from my code to see which panic traps survived optimization, and Gemini 4 Argon translated the path to each trap into rules for the Z3 constraint solver. An LLM-written solver model isn't a sound proof system, but it gives the agent an independent way to reason about control flow and synthesize concrete Go reproducers, and every crash it finds is real because go test runs the offending bytes directly. That workflow caught all three bugs I planted in test parsers and turned up three latent bugs in skill-lint.

In this post, I'll walk through how the Gemini and Z3 pipeline works, why go test -fuzz missed two of those crashes (and the one-line change that fixes it), and where this approach fits in a day-to-day Go workflow.

A recent post from Anthropic showed how formal verification catches latent bugs in C and C++ by having frontier AI models write machine-checked Rocq proofs across OSS-Fuzz libraries. Instead of sampling random inputs like a fuzzer, a formal tool works backward from each failure condition to solve for the exact input that triggers it. Full theorem proving is still too heavy for most day-to-day Go projects, so I wanted to see how much of that bug-hunting power an agent can get from an LLM and a constraint solver in about a minute per function.

In C and C++, reading or writing past the end of a buffer corrupts memory. Go catches out-of-bounds access at runtime instead, compiling every slice or index operation it can't prove safe into an explicit branch to runtime.panicBounds that panics, crashing the process unless recovered.

Before generating machine code, Go's SSA prove pass already eliminates most of those checks by tracking slice lengths and loop variables. That optimization pass leaves behind only the runtime.panicBounds traps the compiler couldn't rule out on its own, giving the solver a short list of candidate crash sites to target.

I compiled each target with GOOS=linux GOARCH=riscv64 go build -gcflags="-S -d=ssa/check_bce/debug=1" (-S prints the assembly listing and ssa/check_bce/debug=1 logs every surviving bounds check) and passed both the Go source and the disassembly to the model. Looking at Go source alone, you can't see which bounds checks the compiler already removed or where a register gets truncated to 8 bits; in the disassembly, both show up. I targeted RISC-V because it's the instruction set Anthropic's post targets for its Rocq proofs, and Go cross-compiles to it out of the box.

From there, Argon read the Go source and disassembly and returned a Z3 script modeling the path to each surviving trap, along with a patched function whenever it flagged a bug. Z3 then checked whether those constraints could be satisfied, and whenever it found a crashing input, go test replayed those bytes against the real function. Using an LLM saved me from translating assembly into solver constraints by hand. Argon's translation dominated the runtime, at about a minute per function, while Z3 itself finished in milliseconds.

Anthropic's approach relies on interactive theorem proving, where experts model machine semantics and Rocq checks LLM-generated proofs against those rules. In contrast, my workflow uses a constraint solver based on satisfiability modulo theories (SMT), relying on Argon to translate assembly directly into Z3 constraints. While a constraint solver doesn't provide a complete correctness proof without a semantic model, it is highly effective for finding bugs. Z3's check-sat command returns sat when input constraints can be satisfied and unsat when they cannot.

A sat answer ("definitely buggy") comes with concrete bytes aimed at the trap, and go test runs them against the real function. Once go test panics, you have a concrete bug reproducer regardless of whether Argon's model was sound. Like a Bloom filter for bugs, an unsat answer only means "maybe correct." Trading away a correctness proof is what makes the approach so practical: you get most of the bug-finding value of formal methods for a tiny fraction of the effort.

Anthropic's setup proved no-crash properties on whole binaries under a 64 MB budget; I checked individual functions on fresh 64-byte slices where cap(buf) == len(buf), so any slice past len(buf) panics. (If you want the full-binary Rocq setup for Go, theorem-labs/oss-verify includes a Go 1.27 runtime overlay that makes the runtime single-threaded and deterministic.)

For the fuzzing baseline, I ran unseeded go test -fuzz on Go 1.27.3 against each planted bug: one 3-second run and three 60-second runs, each with a fresh fuzz cache so a short time budget wouldn't explain a miss.

I started with three safe parsers to make sure the pipeline didn't flag clean code. The compiler's prove pass removed every bounds check in a TOML key parser on its own, and Z3 returned unsat on the remaining traps in an HTTP header scanner and a varint decoder.

Then I wrote three parsers with subtle panics. Here's the first one, ParseTwoChunkFrame:

In the RISC-V disassembly, the bug is much easier to see:

total := hdrLen + bodyLen adds two uint8 values, so MOVBU wraps the sum at 256 before the length guard checks it, while endBody adds the full 64-bit ints. Argon turned those instructions into a Z3 query asking whether a fresh slice of up to 64 bytes can pass the guard and still fail the endBody check at 0x0092:

Z3 returned a 59-byte counterexample with hdrLen = 48 and bodyLen = 236: their uint8 sum wraps from 284 down to 28 (so 28 + 4 ≤ 59 passes the guard), and buf[endHdr:endBody] then slices to index 288 and panics.

At 59 bytes, 19.4% of all possible (hdrLen, bodyLen) pairs trigger this crash. Yet a 3-second fuzz run missed it, and so did three 60-second runs.

Go checks a slice expression like buf[endHdr:endBody] against the slice's capacity, not its length, and Go's fuzzer rarely passes a slice without spare capacity. In a 10-second probe of 386,741 fuzz calls, 89% of inputs were views into a shared ~100 MB buffer, and only 0.07% had cap == len. With that much spare capacity, endBody (at most 514) never exceeds cap(buf), so the buggy parser returns bytes past the end of buf instead of panicking.

The second target, ExtractQuotedToken, hit the same blind spot when a trailing backslash pushed the index one byte past len(buf). Z3 caught it immediately with the 2-byte input []byte{'"', '\\'}, while the fuzzer absorbed the overrun in spare capacity.

Clipping capacity to length with a full slice expression turns any slice past len into a panic:

After that change, the fuzzer crashed both ParseTwoChunkFrame and ExtractQuotedToken in all three runs (median 0.28 s and 0.17 s). The third target, ParseMagicPacket, was a control because its signed int8 length bug panics on a negative index regardless of capacity, and the fuzzer found it in every run either way. Fuzzing still can't give you an unsat signal across the remaining paths, which is how I checked the three safe parsers and Argon's patches.

Next, I pointed the pipeline at skill-lint itself. A whole Go CLI is too large to hand to a solver directly. Even a 10-line parser links 1,559 symbols from the Go runtime, and the 28 MB skill-lint binary has 32,321 symbols in total. Following Functional Core, Imperative Shell, I left CLI flags, network calls, and file I/O to go test -fuzz and go test -race, and ran the solver only on pure functions and slice helpers extracted from file-reading code.

Of the five functions I tested, two scanners came back unsat (including a CRLF line splitter with six surviving bounds checks), and the other three had bugs. The sharpest was in ApplyReplacementChunks, which validates LLM-generated {StartLine, EndLine} edits against the initial line count and sorts them bottom-to-top so earlier edits don't shift later line numbers:

Z3 immediately returned sat because sorting by StartLine doesn't prevent two chunks from overlapping. In the extracted helper, which checks bounds once up front, replacing lines 3 and 4 of a 4-line file with one line shrinks lines to 3 elements, so a second chunk spanning lines 2 through 4 evaluates lines[4:] and panics with slice bounds out of range [4:3]. In the shipped ApplyChunks, a per-iteration bounds check turns that case into a misleading range error, while overlapping chunks that stay within len(lines) silently overwrite the earlier edit. Rejecting overlapping chunks after the sort fixes both.

The other two bugs were similar accounting errors. In the local fixer, target line numbers were checked once per violation before the inner chunk loop applied deletions that shrank the slice (a latent panic if a fixer ever returns a second chunk after a deletion, though DeleteLineFix only returns one today). In the prompt packer, a truncation check reserved a flat 100 bytes for a ~70-byte wrapper plus file path, overshooting the 2 MB context budget in the shipped code when a path exceeds about 30 bytes and panicking in the extracted helper.

When you check a function modularly like this, the target has to include its own entry-point guards (like the bounds check and sort in ApplyReplacementChunks). If an internal helper assumes preconditions that are only enforced by its caller or stated in a doc comment, Z3 will happily invent inputs that violate them. Because each extracted helper included its entry guards, false positives weren't an issue: across all six buggy functions in my tests, the first sat assignment Z3 returned triggered a real panic in go test without needing to filter out non-crashing candidates or loop back into the solver.

Once I pulled these helpers away from file I/O, a 3-second fuzz run caught all three bugs too. To make sure Argon wasn't leaning on source comments in my test files, I also reran the skill-lint targets with all comments stripped; Argon and Z3 still caught all three bugs and returned unsat on the two scanners and all three patches.

Staying entirely inside the target language makes a whole class of bugs hard for an agent to see. When an LLM reviews Go source directly, a line like if int(total)+4 > len(buf) looks like a textbook bounds check, and when it runs go test -fuzz, target-language runtime details like spare slice capacity or multi-step precondition checks can hide the crash indefinitely. Asking the agent to translate Go and assembly into a constraint solver forces a change of representation that strips away surface syntax and makes every bit width and slice bound explicit, while go test turns any solver hit back into a concrete Go reproducer.

For most Go code, fuzzing pure helpers with slice capacity clipped to length will catch bounds bugs in seconds. A solver is worth the extra minute on the traps that survive both the compiler's range prover and a quick fuzz run, especially in parsers and editors that compute offsets from multi-field headers or line ranges.

Single-threaded solver models don't see data races or deadlocks, so concurrent code still belongs with the race detector, deterministic concurrency testing, and Go 1.27's goroutine leak profiles.

A Practical Workflow for Writing Better AI Image Prompts

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The way you write a prompt will impact how the generator interprets it. A detailed description of the prompt gives the generator a better understanding of the Subject, location, Composition, Lighting, and Style of the visual. The good news is that creating a prompt is a repetitive task that can be approached as a structured workflow. Rather than guess which words are likely to generate successful images, you can build a prompt step-by-step, test it, review the results, and improve the details. Here is a practical process to follow.

First, you need to determine the Image you really want to create. Many poor prompts start out as vague ideas and not concrete visual objectives. Example: "create an Image of a Coffee Shop." This identifies an object (a coffee shop) but leaves nearly every other aspect undefined. The resulting Image could be a contemporary coffee house, a coffee cart on the side of a busy street, a Minimalist coffee shop interior, or a bustling restaurant. To help you avoid this type of generic prompt before you write it, ask yourself some general questions. What is the primary Subject of my Image? What is the Subject doing in my Image? In what location does my scene take place? What would I want the viewer to see first in my Image? Do I prefer a specific visual Style? Do not feel obligated to write an entire paragraph of detail here; just jot down a few key thoughts. Example: Subject: barista preparing coffee Location: small neighborhood cafe Action: pouring steamed milk into a cup Lighting: soft morning light Visual treatment: realistic Editorial photography Atmosphere: calm and relaxing These initial notes serve as the basis for developing a more effective prompt.

In order to develop a standardization to your prompts, consider developing a template for organizing your prompts. A possible way to organize your prompts is based upon the following categories: Subject + Action + location + Composition + Lighting + visual Style + important features Each category answers a distinct question. Subject: what is the principal feature? Action: what is happening? Location: where is it occurring? Composition: how is the scene organized? Lighting: what kind of light characterizes the picture? Visual Style: what kind of aesthetic should the generator strive for? Features: which features such as texture, color, background elements etc. Are critical? Using this format let’s break down our previous example. Initial basic prompt: “barista making coffee.” Expanded prompt: “barista pours steaming milk into a ceramic coffee cup inside of a small neighborhood coffee shop. Wooden countertop. The espresso machine is visible in the ground. Soft morning window light. Close up. Editorial photography. Warming natural colors.” While both prompts contain the same Subject and location, the expanded version of the prompt provides additional detail about the Setting and the Composition and therefore provides greater clarity to the Image generator regarding the desired outcome.

Adding too many extraneous details can overwhelm both you and the generator. Too many extraneous details can cause conflicting directions toward what aspects of the Image are visually emphasized. Think about it this way: imagine developing a scene description including all of the following: Minimally styled Very highly detailed Darkly atmospheric Brightly lit Cinematic Flat illustrated Photographically realistic Some of these stylistic directions may compete depending on what you intend for your final Image. Determine which stylistic characteristics are essential to your final Image. If you plan to create an Image resembling a product photograph, then you may want to emphasize product styling (product, materials, background, Lighting and framing). If you are working on an environmental scene then you may want to focus more on location/Setting, atmosphere, spatial relationships, size, placement and proximity of various elements within your Image. There is value in separating essential data from non-essential decorative data. Data necessary to convey what your Image intends to communicate constitute the essential Details of your Image. Optional Details add character or personality to your Image, but they should not detract from your fundamental message.

Your description can influence how the Image appears. For instance, when describing a scene as “cinematic photography” you are suggesting a very different visual path than if you described it as “clean commercial product photography,” or “Editorial hand drawn illustration.” It is helpful to view vocabulary choices through the lens of intended visual outcomes versus merely accumulating adjectives that sound impressive.
