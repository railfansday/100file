Team Meat has announced a new remake of its classic 2010 platformer: Super Meat Boy Again+, this time with a fresh coat of bloodsoaked paint and entirely new levels.

The brutally tough platformer became a cult hit more than a decade ago and spawned one sequel, one 3D spin-off and a whole speedrunning community dedicated to beating its masocore levels without dying. Super Meat Boy Again+ is more or less the same game, but with much more bolted on top.

"This version is just like the original Super Meat Boy game but with new art, music, cutscenes, levels, secrets and unlockables...new everything," the game's Steam page says. "For our old fans, we want to present the game as you remember it through the wonderful lens of nostalgia. For our new fans, we want to give you a great looking, easy to pick up but difficult to master platformer that you may have missed because it came out like 4 years after YouTube was invented," the studio explains.

There's no release date yet for Super Meat Boy Again+ and the only confirmed platform thus far is Steam.

What's Your Most Unforgettable Close Call?

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

People are capable of doing some supremely stupid things in cars. We've seen the warnings from informational videos over the decades, and our personal blunders and experiences have demonstrated this point, too. We can, of course, be attentive behind the wheel, but that doesn't account for the others who aren't while on the road. It's partly why dash cams and their resulting videos have gained so much traction in recent years. While someone's dumb decision may end up in disaster, others might end in close calls — moments where destruction was inches away.

Close calls happen more often than you might expect, especially when we live in an era where vehicles are equipped with every warning along with literal bells and whistles to tell drivers they might hit something. This week, we don't want to know about the little near-misses — we want to know about the big ones that haunt you. Tell us about the misses that had they been hits, would've made for calamity.

Years ago, while in the city for the New York International Auto Show, I was lucky enough to be driving a well-equipped 2021 Maserati Levante GTS. I had taken it on a scenic drive along the Belt Parkway to Amityville, and on my return to Brooklyn, I hit a bit of traffic. About 10 minutes into my drive, in a 35 mph residential zone, an Audi Q5 came speeding up behind me. I reactively slowed down to let it fly past.

I watched as this Q5 blazed through a red light and hit another car, a fairly new Subaru, before the Q5 went spinning into the other side of the intersection. That was near miss number one. Two people got out of the smashed Q5 and bolted. Instinctively, I wanted to stop and help, but I was in a part of New York I was unfamiliar with in a six-figure vehicle that wasn't mine. Instead, I opted to get around the scene.

Seconds later, a Ford Taurus came up on my bumper, then quickly maneuvered ahead to cut me off before making a turn into a parking lot where it then stopped, and a cop hopped out and proceeded to stop a kid who had run from the crash scene. The Taurus was never put in park, though, and it started rolling towards the Maserati. I panicked, trying to figure out how to reverse it with its weird shifter.

The cop saw what was happening and was able to catch the car about an inch away from the Maserati. Near miss number two. Meanwhile, another cop had arrested the kid. After a deep breath and a phone call to my husband, still quite shaken, I made my way back to Brooklyn.

It might not have been the greatest near-miss, but the feelings I had nearly five years ago remain strong to this day. With that chaos in mind, what was your most unforgettable close call? Tell us all about it and why it holds critical space in your memory bank in the comments below.

GTA 6 Streaming Guidelines In China Prohibit Car Theft, Murder, And Attacking Cops

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Chinese streaming regulations are notoriously strict, so much so that GTA was banned from being shown on platforms like Douyin, Huya, Bilibili, and PandaTV. GTA 6 may be the exception, but with a laundry list of caveats that essentially turn Grand Theft Auto into a sightseeing simulator.

In China, livestreaming games that haven't received the necessary government approval is prohibited under national regulations introduced in 2022.

According to local media outlets such as IT Home (via Yahoo), a document titled 'Guidelines for Compliance During Livestreams of GTA 6' was reportedly leaked by a Douyin creator, and its URL originates from ByteDance's corporate domain, lending legitimacy to their claim.

Access to the document has been blocked, but not before its contents were shared to the internet. The guidelines stipulate that streamers must avoid revealing clothing or certain emojis, and must black out the screen during gory scenes, or adjust blood effects to appear gray or green (something that many games in China do already, such as PUBG and Counter-Strike: Global Offensive). Streamers must also avoid attacking bystanders, assaulting police officers, or, bizarrely, stealing cars.

However, local outlets report that "the online rumor about 'securing streaming rights' has no basis in fact." The document is simply a reminder of platform rules.

GTA 6’s Extended Look is so censored on Bilibili that a few scenes are almost unwatchable.

It's unclear right now if GTA 6 will even launch in China, as it does not yet have approval, and streaming unlicensed foreign games is explicitly prohibited. However, Rockstar did launch a verified Douyin account on November 12 to promote the game. That doesn't necessarily mean the studio has obtained government approval, but it's the strongest evidence yet that GTA 6 will be permitted. Until we have official confirmation, of course, there's no guarantee that platforms like Douyin will be allowed to host GTA 6 videos. At the very least, they appear to be preparing for approval, hence the leaked guidelines.

If the game is allowed on Chinese streaming platforms, it's hard to imagine what content creators can show. The story will undoubtedly feature confrontation with the police, and revealing scenes have already been shown in the trailer — with Jason and Lucia having a steamy shower together in the most recent trailer — so the experience will need to be heavily censored or frequently obscured by a black screen. Something that already happened to the Extended Look in China, making several scenes completely unwatchable.

Realistically, the most that streamers will be permitted to show is Jason and Lucia cruising through Leonida, taking in the sights of Vice City. That probably won't make for a very exciting viewing experience.

Even when GTA 6 is downloaded and ready to go on November 19, sharing spoilers risks landing you with a DMCA takedown.

Gen Z’s job crisis has reached the ultrarich. Wealth advisors say billionaire parents are now planning for kids who may struggle to land and keep jobs

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Even the wealthiest parents in America are worried about their kids’ financial futures. The unemployment rate for recent college graduates has held at about 5.6%, and 42.5% of recent grads were underemployed at the end of 2025, the highest share since 2020. A college degree no longer guarantees a steady paycheck, and economists warn AI could upend the entry-level work that recent grads have historically done.

In a world where seemingly no one is immune to the impacts of AI on the workforce and an increasingly stiff job market, ultra-high net worth people are starting to have the same concerns as middle-class and low-income families: Will my kid be able to get a job and support themself?

“Billionaires have the financial resources to support their children, but they sometimes struggle to determine what else is needed for their children to succeed,” Tom Thiegs, managing director of leadership and legacy at Ascent Private Capital Management with U.S. Bank, told Fortune earlier this year.

Millionaires and billionaires “are recognizing this is not the same game they had to play,” wealth manager Patrick Dwyer told CNBC earlier this year. “Families have to rethink … what it means to support their children. And we’re not talking about spoiling your kids. We’re talking about: What if your kid needs retraining at 33?” Dwyer is managing director at Aligned by NewEdge Wealth, a boutique wealth management firm based in Miami. He works with clients with net worths between roughly $100 million and more than $1 billion.

Dwyer said his clients are concerned their children, typically between age 22 and 35, are struggling to secure and hang on to jobs that are historically associated with security and status, including technology, law, and health care. That means the wealthy will have to plan for a future in which they’re passing on more of their fortune to their kids.

“[They’re] realizing that if they don’t pass on more meaningful wealth to their children, or their children are not able to accumulate wealth … their kids could have [less] agency over their lives than they did,” he told CNBC.

While this may sound like an irrational fear from America’s wealthiest, it’s a reality more wealth managers recognize.

“This is a very real concern I’m hearing from ultra-affluent families right now,” Thiegs said. “On the surface it can sound irrational: ‘Why would a billionaire worry about their child getting a job?’ But realistically, no matter how much money you have, parents still want their children to succeed and lead fulfilled lives.”

The job market jitters Gen Zers face directly impacts how their parents can help them plan for their financial futures.

But the crux of the problem isn’t that parents fear they won’t financially be able to help their children, it’s more that they’re worried they won’t have the same career outcomes and sense of fulfillment as past generations.

“They’re not usually worried about the financial security of their children; rather they worry that the job market will impact their child’s sense of purpose, identity, and confidence,” Thiegs said. “They also worry that significant wealth will dampen their drive or desire to work.”

But that doesn’t mean Thiegs encourages his clients just to bankroll their kids for the foreseeable future. Instead, estate planning, investing, and other long-term financial planning is a must.

“When parents are worried about their children’s job security, we recommend creating a system that provides opportunities for growth and development rather than just a financial safety net,” he said.

It’s more important to implement plans that support a child’s self worth than just their net worth, he added.

Trent Von Ahsen, a certified financial planner and managing partner at Cedar Point Capital Partners, also says his ultra-high net worth families view the risks of today’s job market as less of a financial stability concern, but more about whether they’re setting their kids up to be indefinitely dependent on them.

“This cohort of parents seem more concerned about over-supporting their children, than under-supporting them,” Von Ahsen told Fortune earlier this year.

The shift away from historically high-paying, white-collar jobs is already evident in the choices Gen Z is making. Facing mass layoffs in white‑collar sectors and anxious about AI, many young workers are peeling away from traditional corporate routes in favor of jobs they think might offer more control or faster cash—from becoming content creators to opting for blue-collar jobs in manufacturing, electrical work, and other technical work.

In some cases, college‑educated Gen Zers are even competing for six‑figure nanny and tutor roles in elite households, chasing financial “freedom” outside of a traditional office career. A 2026 Deloitte global survey found just 6% of Gen Z and millennial respondents said reaching a leadership position is their primary career goal. Instead, many say they favor stability, skill-building, and well-being over climbing the ladder quickly.

This all means, though, billionaires and other high-net worth individuals have to financially plan differently from how they might have in the past. They have to design financial plans that “encourage growth and responsibility” instead of only making large inheritances all at once, Von Ahsen said.

“We see more emphasis on education funding flexibility, mentorship, and phased wealth transfers,” he said. It’s “really an attitude moving toward providing opportunity without removing initiative.”

Why did Google Pixels stop using Snapdragon chips?

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google ditched Snapdragon in 2021 to build phones around its own AI silicon.

It seems like it was just yesterday when Google dropped Qualcomm's Snapdragon chips and launched the Pixel 6 on its proprietary Tensor platform – but it's not. We've just passed the five(!) year anniversary in October, so what better time than now to look at why Google did it. The decision saw Google swap out the mobile gaming pedigree of Snapdragon in the Pixel 5a for better AI capabilities (like transcribing a rambling voice note) in the Pixel 6 with Tensor silicon shaped around Google's machine learning models.

Tensor spent its first four generations as a heavily reworked Samsung Exynos design. Google only recently moved to a chip of its own base design with last year's Tensor G5, fabricated in Taiwan by TSMC. The Tensor G6 in the new Google Pixel 11 continues that lineage, while the Pixel Watch 5 that launched beside it still carries a Qualcomm chip.

In August 2021, Google's SVP of Devices and Services, Rick Osterloh, wrote in an announcement that the company had "run into computing limitations that prevented us from fully pursuing our mission" in AI, so it built its own chip. Its centerpiece was the tensor processing unit (TPU), a custom application-specific integrated circuit dedicated to running Google's speech recognition, translation and computational photography models on the phone instead of in the cloud.

Five generations on, the workload has changed but the overarching design hasn't; Google says the Tensor G6 packs 50 percent more TPU compute than the G5. Paired with the latest Gemini Nano model, it also claims up to 3.5 times faster on-device AI tasks that use up to 3.5 times less energy.

Testing by Android Authority measured Qualcomm's Snapdragon 8 Elite at more than twice the Tensor G5's multi-core CPU performance and around 2.5 times its graphics performance. The publication puts this discrepancy down to Google's financial priorities rather than any failures in engineering, but there's no dancing around the fact that a flagship Snapdragon or an Apple A-series chip beats Tensor on things like raw power and sustained gaming loads (and probably battery life under heavy use), and that's been true of every Tensor Pixel since 2021. Google is a services company before it's a silicon specialist after all, and it has accepted deficits in benchmarks as the price of running its Gemini features the way it wants. That's great if you're a Gemini power user, but not so great if you're any other type of power user.

The Pixel Watch 5 was announced alongside the Pixel Tag and Pixel 11 series, and runs on Qualcomm's Snapdragon W5 Gen 2. Indeed, the company hasn't yet made any wearable Tensor products and still relies on Qualcomm's silicon wherever it doesn't use its own.

Tensor itself began as a Samsung co-development codenamed Whitechapel. A 2021 analysis of the first chip found that it shared clock management, power management architecture and memory controllers with Samsung's Exynos processors. Samsung was responsible for manufacturing every Tensor through the G4 and supplied the modems, and its processes were blamed for the line's thermal issues and its Exynos modems for the connectivity problems. The Tensor G5 in last year's Pixel 10 was the first one built without Samsung, and the G6 is understood to have swapped the Exynos modem for a MediaTek part. That's according to leaked specifications that have since been partly confirmed, thus removing the last major Samsung part from a platform it helped build.

Qualcomm's flagship phone chips are said to cost manufacturers $240 to $280 per unit in the current Snapdragon 8 Elite Gen 5 generation. That's an increase of $40 to $80 compared to the 8 Gen 3. In designing Tensor, Google has managed to dodge that markup and divert more budget to refining the TPU its AI features are built around. Interestingly, Samsung has gone in the other direction: Its Galaxy S26 Ultra uses that Gen 5 chip across all regions. On top of that, all North American S26 models use Snapdragon. Alas, while the switch from Snapdragon to Tensor didn't make Pixels cheaper than their rivals, it did give Google what it wanted: its own chip roadmap.

Vitamin C could help block cancer-linked chemicals in your stomach, study finds

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Vitamin C may do more than support everyday health. New research from the University of Waterloo suggests that it could also help prevent potentially cancer-causing chemicals from forming during digestion. Using mathematical modeling, scientists investigated how vitamin C influences chemical reactions in the digestive system that have been linked to cancer risk.

Exposure to nitrates and nitrites has increased in North American diets over the past several decades. These compounds are found in cured meats such as bacon and salami, as well as in fruits and vegetables grown in soil or water affected by pollution. Although nitrates and nitrites contribute to important functions in the brain and cardiovascular system, they can also participate in potentially harmful reactions once they reach the stomach.

One of these reactions, called nitrosation, can produce substances that researchers suspect may contribute to cancer development. Understanding what causes these chemicals to form, and what might prevent their formation, has been a longstanding challenge.

Why Vitamin C Could Change the Cancer Risk Equation

"Since at least the 90s, researchers have been studying the link between cancer and these compounds, with conflicting results," said Dr. Gordon McNicol, a post-doctoral researcher in applied mathematics and the first author of the study. "Our work suggests that the presence of dietary Vitamin C may help explain these inconsistencies."

To investigate this possibility, the researchers developed a mathematical model that simulates how nitrates and nitrites travel through the body and undergo chemical changes. The model incorporated several interconnected systems, including the salivary glands, stomach, small intestine, and plasma, the liquid portion of blood.

Their simulations revealed that vitamin C consumed alongside nitrates and nitrites may interfere with the formation of potentially harmful compounds. This could help explain why foods containing nitrates do not necessarily carry the same risks.

For example, leafy vegetables such as spinach naturally contain both nitrates and vitamin C. According to the model, the presence of vitamin C could limit the chemical reactions associated with cancer risk, potentially helping explain the protective effects suggested by some dietary research.

The researchers also explored whether vitamin C supplements might offer similar benefits when taken after eating.

Their findings suggest that taking vitamin C after each meal could moderately reduce the formation of nitrosation products associated with cancer risk. This effect could be particularly relevant when consuming foods containing nitrates and nitrites, including processed meats such as bacon and salami.

However, the results come from mathematical simulations rather than clinical trials. Although the model predicts that vitamin C can reduce the production of potentially harmful chemicals, further research is needed to determine whether this translates into a measurable reduction in cancer risk in humans.

New Clues for Future Cancer Prevention Research

The researchers believe their model could help scientists better understand the complicated relationship between diet, digestive chemistry, and cancer risk. It may also provide guidance for future experiments examining how nutritional choices influence the formation of potentially harmful compounds.

"This work provides a mechanistic roadmap for future clinical and laboratory studies by identifying the key interacting drivers of these potentially harmful chemical reactions, including nitrite exposure, antioxidant intake, meal timing, gastric conditions and oral microbiome activity," said Dr. Anita Layton, professor of applied mathematics and Canada 150 researcher chair. "This model can help researchers design more targeted experiments and interventions, focusing on when and in whom nitrosation is most likely to occur."

By identifying how factors such as vitamin C intake, meal timing, stomach conditions, and bacteria in the mouth interact, the model could help researchers develop more precise strategies for studying and potentially reducing cancer-related chemical reactions during digestion.

The study, "Vitamin C as a nitrosation inhibitor: A modelling study across dietary patterns and water quality," was published in the Journal of Theoretical Biology.

The flight up the valleys, around and between the snow-covered mountains, was a sight so beautiful that we almost forgot we were in a little single-engine pontoon plane and at times many miles from any water to sit down on. We were in camp and ready to hunt on the morning of April 28.

Our camp consisted of one frame cabin, one tent cabin, and an outhouse, without door, 100 yards north on the main bear trail. All the camp equipment, including two outboard boats for lake travel, had been flown in.

Karluk Lake looked a lot like one of the fjords of Norway. The blue water of the lake was now entirely clear of ice, but there were deep snowdrifts on the mountain peaks above the lake. If we’d never seen a bear, the wild beauty of the place would have repaid me for the trip in full.

We started the hunting campaign with all energy, and in two days we saw and classified 16 bears. I refused to stalk any of them because they failed to meet my specifications. John eventually suggested that I was displaying the stubbornness of a mule, rather than the calm judgment of a purchasing agent.

Our guides were by reputation the best on the island, and they soon proved that they deserved their standing. Some would call them Aleuts, but in reality they were members of the Koniaga Tribe, which has been considerably diluted by the Russians who owned the island until we bought Alaska from Russia in 1867. Our guides were proud of their Russian and Koniaga lineage, but they’re loyal United States citizens.

My guide was Grishka Nicoli, and John’s was Nick Malutin. These guides spoke their native Koniaga language, which is spoken only and not written. They also spoke fluent Russian and awkward English. They spend about three months of the year as hunting guides and four months as commercial salmon fishermen. For the rest of the year they’re Koniaga gentlemen of leisure. Like my hunting partner John Yenter, the guides were convinced that I was entirely too meticulous in my trophy specifications.

The only encouragement I had came from Kim Clark, who had become a very important part of our expedition. His full name was Webster Kimberly Clark, and he was a refuge biologist employed by the U. S. Fish and Wildlife Service. Kim had been reared in Greenfield, Massachusetts, had studied at the universities of Michigan, Massachusetts, Missouri, and Yale. He has a Doctor’s degree. Clark had done a vast amount of field research and had become one of the top authorities on North American bears. He was stationed on Kodiak Island by the federal government to study Kodiak bears.

Kim Clark had read my stories in Outdoor Life.* When Kim learned from outfitter Alf Madsen that I was planning a Kodiak hunt, he met me in Kodiak Village and asked if he could join the group to observe and record the data on bears killed. We were glad to have a man of his ability and knowledge.

During the early part of our stay on Karluk Lake, Kim worked alone and permitted us to hunt as hunters, not as scientists. He was a great addition to our expedition, because his lectures at night permitted us to do practical field work the next day in connection with our hunt. Between hunting, field and night classes, we were extremely well occupied.

Had it not been for Kim encouraging me to stick to my specifications, I might have given up. He encouraged me because accurate field data on a big male brown bear just out of hibernation is apparently very scarce.

By the third day my partner John was adjusting his own specifications to suit the bear that was in sight. We were glassing a brownie on the shoreline across the lake, and through the glasses it looked good — perfect hide, pleasing color, but slightly under my requirements as to size. John wanted it.

In a few minutes we were on our way across the lake, approaching very quietly so as not to alarm the bear. We landed several hundred yards from the bruin, and John started stalking the animal with his guide. In spite of the skill they displayed, the bear caught the man smell. He was on his way up the mountainside before John could shoot. He and Nick stood on the shore watching the occasional motion of a bush which indicated that the bear was going higher and away.

The bear was 150 yards from the lake shore when he came into a little opening in the brush where we could see him. At that point he made the biggest and last mistake of his life. He stopped and looked back. At that moment John fired. The bear fell. Two more shots finished him completely. John’s performance was a credit to him, to his .375 H. & H. Winchester, Model 70 rifle, and its Bausch & Lomb scope sight.

It was without question a big bear, the color was excellent, the 8½-feet-square hide was prime and perfect. John was a thoroughly happy hunter.

With his bear hide strung up to dry, John concentrated on the wonderful trout fishing near camp, with time out now and then to think up some new criticisms of my ideas about a proper trophy.

At the end of the ninth day we sized up the situation and found that we had glassed 40 bears, killed one. I was still holding out for a bigger and better one.

The weather was perfect for the first three days, which was probably why we saw so many bears. There was rain every day after that, and visibility was often too poor for good work with binoculars. There’s no climate on Kodiak, just a vast assortment of unexpected and undesirable weather.

When we started out the 10th morning, we found that what had been rain at the lake camp during the night had been snow above the 1,500-foot level on the mountain. In spite of that, our hunting began early, and at 8:30 we saw a bear that looked very interesting. He was a mile from the lake shore, 1,600 feet higher, just above the new but temporary snow line.

Trying to get a better look at the bear, we moved up the bed of Cottonwood Creek. This took us out of sight of the bruin momentarily, but we knew exactly where he was because the snow line established his elevation and the pass made in the mountains by the creek marked his location the other way. Yet, an hour’s looking from our new position failed to locate him.

We then climbed up high on the right bank of Cottonwood Creek, and from there we glassed constantly until noon. We felt sure that the bear was there because we certainly would have seen him if he’d walked away.

At noon we abandoned that position and moved to a spot across the lake, a mile farther away, which gave us a better perspective and a view of more of the ground area. We took turns eating and resting, but we were constantly looking. The rain returned in full force and it was a very uncomfortable situation.

We never saw a sign of the bear until 4 o’clock, when suddenly he stood up and was in plain view again. It was now evident that we’d first seen him when he finished his morning feeding, and he’d bedded down for the day. Now he was up feeding again.

After a few minutes of careful observation, the guides decided that this bear would meet every point in my specifications. This was a fine display of skill on the part of the guides and is something I could never hope to duplicate. To the inexperienced, a bear two miles away through a 20X Bausch & Lomb telescope is just a bear, but my guide was able to classify him very accurately as to sex, size, and condition.

I knew enough by now to trust this guide’s judgment, and in a few minutes we were on our way back across the lake to start the stalk.

Before we reached the far shore the clouds had dropped so low that the mountains were completely obscured.

Since John had killed his bear, we decided that he’d stay with the boat and the two guides would accompany me on the climb up the mountain. It took a few minutes to strip for action, which meant down to heavy underwear, a sweat shirt, rain suit, and hip boots. This sounds like unusual mountain-climbing equipment, but the first mile of our climb would be accomplished by wading up the creek. It was much easier that way than climbing through the murderous alder thickets on either side.

By 5:30 we’d accomplished the most difficult part of the climb and were at about the same height as the bear and 500 to 800 yards to the right of him. At this point we climbed out of the creek and dived into the alder thicket. All alder thickets are nasty, but this one was the worst that I’d ever seen.

Soon we picked up a prominent bear trail, a path so worn that one of the guides called it “a bear highway.” It was obviously the main bear trail on that particular mountainside, and we felt sure that my bear was on it. We had no idea which way he was traveling, so it was possible that we’d meet him at any moment. It was also possible that he’d park a few yards off the trail to observe and properly take care of anything that might be using the bear highway. It’s this kind of thinking that prevents daydreaming on brown-bear stalks.

Grishka, who was in front of me, explained that he’d lead and watch ahead. Nick would bring up the rear and watch behind us and to the sides of the trail. I was free to watch any angle that suited my fancy. I think this was a polite way of telling me that they didn’t trust my powers of observation.

I was planning what I’d do if certain things happened. I realized that any shooting would be at very close range because of the thick alders and the heavy fog. While my guides were both carrying .348 Winchesters with open sights, I was carrying a .375 Winchester with a Bausch & Lomb scope. No scope is ideal equipment for a quick shot at 10 yards, and I wished for an old-fashioned V notch and front post.

Nick must have been reading my mind. He said, “Bill, if we meet the bear and you have trouble with your rifle, just throw it down and grab mine. I’ll be right behind you.”

Nothing could have done me more harm than that speech. I knew now that he had no more confidence in my equipment than I had, and I was scared stiff. I had enough acid in my stomach to pickle a ton of iron castings.

We soon came out into the little open spot on the mountainside that we’d marked as the location of the bear when last seen almost two hours before. This clearing was about 100 feet in each direction — no alders, just dead grass almost knee high. We spent 20 minutes searching the open area and forcing our way into the brush around the edges, trying to pick up a trail or some sign of the bear.

Suddenly my head was gripped by Grishka, a powerful man but only five feet tall. He pulled my head down to his level, slapped his wet face against mine. Pointing to the right, he whispered in my ear, “Bear there. Shoot!”

About 75 yards away, through a little opening in the brush, I could see a dark spot. It could have been a bear. As I dropped to one knee, my perfect guide braced his knees to my back to steady my position. His 9X binoculars were tight to his eyes and just above my head as he watched the bear.

When I picked up that brown spot in my 2½X scope, I couldn’t tell whether it was a bear or not. If it was, I couldn’t for the life of me see which way he was pointing.

In a whisper, I asked Grishka, “Which end is the head on?”

After a full minute’s careful observation through his glasses, he put his mouth to my ear and said, “Other end. Shoot!”

That concise data from Grishka was fed through my fire-control center down to the trigger finger, and my gun fired. I was so excited I didn’t hear the report of the rifle, nor was I conscious that there was any recoil. But I did hear the bullet hit something, and then a fraction of a second later I knew it was a bear. He roared his complete disapproval of the whole situation.

Then it seemed that the brown bulk went up in the air several feet. An instant later it was on the ground and for a split second was completely still. At that moment I fired my second shot, and the bear’s reaction to that was a quick lunge to the right, toward the alders. I fired my third shot as he disappeared.

Grishka had said the first shot was good, but I didn’t see how he could possibly know. The bear had disappeared into the brush just as though he hadn’t been touched.

Both guides were now running as hard as they could to the right — in the same general direction the bear was going when we last saw him. It was quite obvious that they were attempting to get ahead of the bear before he reached the creek bed, which was 600 yards away. I caught them quickly and stayed right with them, not due to any bravery on my part, but simply because it was obviously much safer to stay close to them and their two rifles.

Our travel in the next few minutes was parallel to that of the bear, and we could follow his location by the noise he made. I thought — and hoped — that the amount of noise was due to the fact that he was badly wounded, running blind and knocking brush to one side.

A few seconds later we were on the edge of the creek where we could look down a steep bank to the water 100 feet below. Then I realized that the noise of the bear was not to my left, as it had been, but almost back of me and getting closer. I turned to face the noise. A second later that bear came into sight less than 20 yards away.

It was the most stirring sight a thrill hunter could ever hope to see. That bear was coming at us at top speed, and it looked as though he knew exactly what he was going to do when he got there.

I slammed the gun to my shoulder and looked at the bear in the scope sight. The contour of the ground was such that I could see the back of his neck, so I selected a spot where the neck joins the shoulders. The crosshairs settled on that spot. I tightened on the trigger, adding pressure and waiting for the recoil.

But the bear rolled dead as Caesar before my rifle went off. I lowered the weapon still cocked and loaded. I never have felt so cheated in all my life. In another 1/10th of a second I could have been the Man Who Stopped the Charging Brownie.

Later examination showed that the first shot had gone very effectively into his side just back of the ribs, forward under the spine, just grazing it, cutting the main blood vessels just back of the heart. Clearly the bear had been too far gone to make a determined charge. He was only making a blind run with his last spark of life, and we happened to be in his path.

That bear was coming at us at top speed, and it looked as though he knew exactly what he was going to do when he got there.

I examined the bear closely, and was glad that I’d stuck to my specifications. It was a male, the color dark brown, the hide in perfect condition, and the size was enough to flatter any desk hand. It was the 41st bear we’d seen on this trip.

Suddenly I realized that it was beginning to get dark. We’d have to leave the bear and return the next day to do the skinning. I was worried about what might happen to the hide overnight, but Grishka said, “We leave strong man smell here. Nothing bother.” With a grin he added, “The strongest thing here is white-man smell of your sweat shirt. We leave it.”

I peeled off the shirt and he tied it to a limb above the bear. When we returned the next morning the shirt and the bear were there and nothing had bothered it.

The guides, Kim Clark, and I left camp early next morning and climbed the mountain to skin the bear. John remained in camp. He and the cook, Pete Unger, had planned a fishing trip.

Kim was keenly interested in the bear from our description so we went in loaded with camera equipment, scales, surgical instruments, test tubes, and specimen bottles. Very few Kodiak bears have ever been accurately weighed and examined, and this is especially true of big bears just out of hibernation.

My bear was an ideal specimen for studying the effects of hibernation, because he evidently had just come out of that lethargic state. The stomach was found to be about 1/10 full, and the contents weighed 16 pounds. Carefully separated and weighed, we found that the contents were 55 percent blue-joint grass, 20 percent nettle, 20 percent wild celery, and 5 percent poque, which is a parasitic growth on the roots of the alders. There was no animal matter present.

The intestine was then examined and the length was accurately determined to be 77 feet 6¾ inches. It was then split from end to end for internal examination. There was no food in it. This indicated two things: first, that the bear had not been out of hibernation long enough for food to pass through the stomach into the intestine, and second, that he had gone into hibernation with the intestine completely empty, which conformed to the generally accepted theory. Further examination revealed there wasn’t a sign of ulcer, scar, scab, or scale in the stomach or intestine.

We also observed that there were no parasites of any kind in the entire digestive tract. This is one of the strange results of hibernation, because this bear during the six months of feeding eats carrion of all kinds, specializing in dead fish. He also digs up and eats worms, insects, and roots. When winter comes, he’s literally and actually a fur coat full of worms. There are thousands of them, including 10-foot-long tapeworms. The bear knows instinctively that he cannot go into hibernation with any food in his body; it would ferment and cause serious trouble before spring.

So when winter approaches he goes on a bedtime diet of certain plants and berries. Then, after a few days of fasting, he’s completely empty. The worms go with the food, and he goes to sleep physically clean. His stored body fat keeps him going till spring.

Every piece of this bear was carefully weighed. He weighed 840 pounds at the time he was killed. Kim Clark figured his weight when he was fattened for hibernation in the fall would have been about 1,300 pounds.

His age was estimated at ten years. The rough field measurement of the skull indicated a Boone and Crockett rating of 28½, against the world record of slightly above 30 inches. Unless the skull shrinks more than an inch during the drying period that must precede official scoring, the trophy should rate a place on the Boone and Crockett record list. This was in every respect an outstanding bear. I learned a lot from him, and much more from Kim Clark.

The skull and all the data collected were sent to Dr. Robert Rausch, Arctic Health Research Center, Anchorage, Alaska, where it became a part of their records.

A fireplace should be a source of warmth, comfort, and connection in your home, not a source of worry, says Chris Maxson, owner and CEO of Acucraft Fireplaces in Big Lake, Minnesota. But it takes some effort and upkeep to ensure your family and home stay safe. If your fireplace hasn’t been cleaned or professionally inspected in years, don’t light a fire until you’ve had it evaluated.

“Just because a fireplace looks fine on the outside doesn’t mean it’s safe to operate,” warns Maxson. Even when a fireplace sits unused, the chimney can develop obstructions from animal nests, leaves, and other debris. In addition, moisture intrusion, deterioration, and damaged components can create potential safety concerns. Whether you have a traditional wood-burning fireplace or a modern gas fireplace, you’ll want to understand how best to clean and maintain it.

It’s not safe to use a fireplace that has been neglected for an extended period. In general, you should clean a wood-burning fireplace and the chimney once a year before you start using it. Likewise, gas fireplaces need cleaning once a year or as recommended by the manufacturer. Here are the reasons to clean fireplaces, according to Maxson:

Wood-burning fireplaces produce soot, ash, and creosote as wood burns. Creosote is highly combustible, and accumulated deposits can increase the risk of a chimney fire. Both gas and wood-burning fireplaces also rely on properly functioning venting systems to direct combustion byproducts away from occupied spaces. Obstructions or damaged venting can contribute to dangerous conditions, including carbon monoxide exposure inside homes.

A clean, properly maintained fireplace is more likely to operate as designed. On the other hand, excessive buildup, restricted airflow, and neglected components can interfere with ignition, combustion, and draft.

Cleaning a fireplace isn’t just about making it look good. It’s about making sure the entire system—including the components you can’t see—is functioning properly. Preventive maintenance protects both your home and your investment.

Maxson recommends that homeowners have their fireplace professionally inspected at least once a year, even if they don’t use it very often. “Cleaning removes buildup, while an inspection helps identify hidden issues that cleaning alone won’t solve,” he explains.

For wood-burning fireplaces, annual chimney inspections are essential. “A qualified chimney professional can determine whether sweeping is necessary based on creosote accumulation, soot, debris, and other conditions,” explains Maxson. “Homeowners who burn wood frequently may require additional cleaning during the heating season” for their fireplaces. CISA listed BeyondTrust Remote Support CVE-2026-102764 on October 16 with forensic triage required. BeyondTrust says it was exploited as a zero-day on August 29.

Edited by Emilio Santos. October 21, 2026.

On Thursday, October 15, 2026, BeyondTrust published security advisory BT26-05 for its Remote Support product. The flaw is CVE-2026-102764, scored 9.3 on CVSS version 4 and rated Critical. BeyondTrust describes it as improper origin validation in an internal messaging component that could let a remote attacker perform file operations on an affected appliance. Every Remote Support version up to and including 26.2.3 is affected. The next day, October 16, the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency added the CVE to its Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog. The catalog row carries a federal due date of October 19 and marks forensic triage as Yes.

The patch is the easy part. BeyondTrust’s own account says the bug was used as a zero-day in late August, nearly seven weeks before the advisory. If you, or a supplier that reaches into your network, run a self-hosted appliance, the question this week is not only “are we patched?” It is “was the box used before we patched, and could we tell?”

BeyondTrust’s blog post on BT26-05, last updated October 16 at 12:00 p.m. EDT, gives this timeline:

As of the October 16 update, BeyondTrust says it knows of only those two affected customers and has seen no adversary activity since August. It assesses with moderate to high confidence that the actor is the China-nexus cluster Mandiant and Google Threat Intelligence Group track as UNC5221, which Mandiant now calls ARMORY CASTLE. It also warns that this actor tends to use a zero-day selectively and then, once the flaw is public, move to broad exploitation of every instance still unpatched.

Two details matter for buyers. Privileged Remote Access shares the affected code; BeyondTrust found no way to exploit it there but released the patch for it anyway. And self-hosted Remote Support customers without automatic updates must patch by hand, after first upgrading if they are older than 24.3.1.

The catalog entry links to Binding Operational Directive 26-04, which CISA issued on June 10, 2026. It binds only federal civilian agencies, but it is the clearest public statement of how the government ranks urgency. It asks four questions: is the asset publicly exposed, is the CVE in the catalog, can an attacker automate the exploit, and does the attacker gain partial or total control. For the top tier, an agency must remediate within three days and carry out a forensic triage to check whether the system was already compromised.

CISA’s implementation guidance sets recommended, not mandatory, targets: scoping within two hours of the catalog addition, evidence collection and patching within 24 hours, and an escalation decision within 48 to 72 hours. Evidence comes first, because patching can destroy it.

BeyondTrust’s account explains why that order is right here. It says the actor planted a web shell, escalated to full administrative control within minutes, installed persistence designed to survive a reboot, read the appliance’s stored secrets, and then deleted logs and its own tools before leaving. Evidence kept only on the appliance “may not survive,” the company writes, and what is there “cannot be implicitly trusted.” Logs forwarded off the box are what count.

BeyondTrust was one of nine additions to the catalog between October 13 and October 16. CISA added Ivanti Connect Secure CVE-2026-104935 and Windows CVE-2026-96951 on October 13; Palo Alto Networks PAN-OS CVE-2026-0322, Cisco Secure Email Gateway CVE-2026-76519 and two AhsayCBS backup-server flaws, CVE-2026-105133 and CVE-2026-105134, on October 14; an Atlassian Data Center file-access flaw, CVE-2026-21589, on October 15; and Fortinet CVE-2025-61624 alongside BeyondTrust on October 16. Six more, four of them Plex Media Server, followed on October 20. Every one of the nine October 13 to 16 rows, apart from AhsayCBS CVE-2026-105133, is marked for forensic triage.

As in the October 1 catalog briefing and the NetScaler note, most of the list sits at the network edge: VPNs, firewalls, email gateways, remote access and backup servers.

A remote-support appliance is privileged access by design. BeyondTrust says so plainly: the appliance “stores and brokers privileged credentials across an estate,” which makes it both a credential source and a map of who connects to what. Treat a compromise of one as a compromise of every credential it injected.

The second lesson is about your detection stack. BeyondTrust says its sensors fired in real time when the attacker escalated privileges, but the automated triage feature in a third-party detection platform closed those alerts as false positives. The company is now working with that vendor so automated triage cannot close high-severity signals on its own. Ask your managed detection provider, in writing, which high-severity alerts its automation can close without a person.

The exposure is often not your own appliance. Managed service providers, outsourced IT desks and software vendors use remote-support tools to reach their customers. If one of them runs self-hosted Remote Support, your systems sit one hop away from a box that was exploited as a zero-day. That is a third-party risk question with contract consequences: notification clauses, audit rights and who pays for forensic work.

Forensic triage costs money even when it finds nothing; budget for it as part of patching. If you carry cyber insurance, check now whether the notice clause is triggered by a suspected incident or only a confirmed one, and whether the insurer requires its own forensics firm. For a public company, a confirmed compromise also raises the Form 8-K question. The four-business-day clock under Item 1.05 starts at the materiality determination, not at the advisory date; the SEC clock briefing and the 8-K materiality worksheet cover how to record that decision.

BeyondTrust says it is withholding technical details while self-hosted appliances are still being patched. When those details are published, expect wider exploitation of anything left unpatched, which is the pattern the company itself predicts for this actor. Watch the BT26-05 blog and the catalog row for changes. Under BOD 26-04, federal agencies’ Phase III deadline, 180 days from issuance, falls on December 7, 2026 by our count; agency patch reporting will keep edge-device rows in the news through year-end.

How to Build an IT Risk Register in Excel

Write the scales first, then look scores up from a 5 by 5 grid. Columns, formulas, severity bands and a worked example.

Edited by Emilio Santos. October 25, 2026.

The IT or security manager who needs a working risk register in a spreadsheet, and the finance or audit lead who has to read it.

An IT risk register is only as good as the agreement behind its numbers. The spreadsheet part takes an afternoon. Getting two people to give the same risk the same score takes longer, and it is the part that matters.

This guide builds a register in Excel (it works the same way in LibreOffice Calc and Google Sheets) around a 5 by 5 likelihood by impact matrix: the columns, the lookup formulas, severity bands that set how often each risk is reviewed, and a worked example with the arithmetic shown.

A risk register lists the things that could go wrong with your technology, how likely and how damaging each one is, what you are doing about it, and who is accountable. It is a decision tool: it tells leadership where to spend money and attention, and it shows an auditor, insurer or board that risks are known and owned.

Keep it separate from two neighbouring lists. A vulnerability or findings tracker holds individual technical issues with fix dates. A risk exception register holds approved, time-limited departures from a control. A risk register sits above both: "a critical system is compromised through an unpatched internet-facing service" is a risk; a specific missing patch is a finding; an approved decision not to patch one server until March is an exception. The three can reference each other by ID, but merging them makes each harder to read.

Calculated columns follow: inherent score and severity, residual score and severity, next review due, and an overdue flag.

Write risk statements in the form "because of [cause], [event] could happen, leading to [consequence]". "Phishing" is a topic. "Because staff can approve payment changes by email, an attacker impersonating a supplier could redirect a payment, leading to a direct financial loss" is a risk someone can score and treat.

Before anyone scores anything, write a one-line definition for each level, and put them on a settings tab next to the matrix. For example:

Where you can, attach numbers to impact levels that fit your company: a dollar range, hours of downtime, number of customer records. "Major" means something very different to a 30-person firm and a 3,000-person one. If you use a published method such as NIST SP 800-30, follow its structure, but the definitions still have to be written for your organization.

The middle levels are the hardest. If Possible and Likely cannot be told apart in writing, most risks will end up scored 3 by 3, and the register will look tidy while saying very little.

Put the matrix on the settings tab as a grid: likelihood 1 to 5 down the rows, impact 1 to 5 across the columns, and the score in each cell. The default is likelihood multiplied by impact, so the grid runs from 1 in the top left to 25 in the bottom right. Say the grid occupies Settings!C12:G16.

With Starship catch, SpaceX still shows it can dazzle the world

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

STARBASE, Texas—The black and silver ship fell out of the sky, emerging from the cloud deck near the very end of its flight. Incongruously, the rocket flew horizontal until the last moment when three Raptor rocket engines ignited and it swooped into a vertical position.

Slowly, gently, haltingly, the Starship upper stage eased its way toward the waiting tower where two arms extended outward, awaiting expectantly. The margins are so incredibly narrow. Two nubs on either side of the rocket, each protruding only about one foot, were the only purchase the rocket would have on the arms.

Then, less than a minute after it came into view, the Starship upper stage was snagged by the launch tower, as snug as you like. And so it was all over. SpaceX, nearly a quarter of a century after its founding, had just taken a hugely significant step toward developing and optimizing a massive, rapidly reusable rocket.

This was a breathtaking feat; a brilliant technical achievement that required the best engineering minds to think through a million different, difficult problems. And on Saturday evening, following a handful of practice runs into the Indian Ocean, they did it before the entire world.

Watching Starship launch from here a little less than a day earlier, and knowing it had orbited the planet multiple times, added to the sense of unreality in seeing this spaceship return to Earth under its own power, and be captured by the tower.

It felt like stepping into the future. SpaceX has dazzled us before, by landing rockets on barges and returning humans to space with Crew Dragon. But this flight took things to another level. Starships are huge. They carry a huge amount of energy coming back from orbit. And until a few years ago, it seemed beyond fantastical to think about capturing a rocket with what are essentially outsized chopsticks.

Humans have built reusable spacecraft that flew into orbit before. In the 1970s, flush with confidence after the triumphant Moon landings, NASA designed and built several Space Shuttles. They had solid rocket boosters that were fished from the ocean and reused. The large orange external tank was not recovered after each flight, but the orbiters went to space for two weeks, or even longer, before returning to Earth and landing on a runway.

The shuttles were engineering marvels. But during the return to Earth they acted as gliders, not igniting their engines. And their heat shields were delicate flowers, requiring painstaking refurbishment after each flight. At the outset of the shuttle program, the space agency contemplated sending dozens of humans into space at a time, and carrying cargo at a cost as low of $25 per pound.

Reality never matched these rosy projections. The crew space capped out at seven astronauts, and the cost-per-pound was closer to $25,000. Each orbiter cost an extraordinary amount of money to build; NASA only ever built five that went to space.

SpaceX has five Starships in an advanced state of development at Starbase today; and its factory houses the components for a dozen more. The company can currently manufacture a Starship upper stage in its South Texas Starfactory every three weeks. The factory was sized to produce a ship every three days. When SpaceX founder Elon Musk discussed these build targets in an interview five years ago, he sounded mad. But he no longer does.

The Space Shuttle was nearly a fully reusable space launch system, but it fell far short of low-cost, rapid launch. Over 30 years the vehicle made 135 flights. Within a few years SpaceX aims to fly 135 times a year, then thousands of times.

Such was the significance of Saturday. SpaceX aspires to fundamentally change the relationship between humanity and the heavens. The first step is to get the entire rocket back. SpaceX has now returned both elements of a Starship rocket safely to its launch tower. It will take time to reach a high rate of success with such landings, but Saturday’s safe return offered a proof of concept.

The landings will come with increasing frequency; and so will the launches. Over time SpaceX will perfect the heat shield that is likely to be the most difficult aspect of rapid reuse. And eventually, as difficult as it may be to believe, what we experienced with amazement this weekend will become routine. One day, we will take this all for granted.

Richard Sterban, the Oak Ridge Boys’ bass singer who memorably lent his vocals to their beloved “Elvira” cover, has died. He was 83.

Sterban died Nov. 14 in Hendersonville, Tennessee, due to complications from bronchitis and pneumonia after fighting a battle with cancer, his family and the Oak Ridge Boys announced.

“We appreciate all of your love, support, and prayers. Please continue to lift us up in this difficult time, we find comfort knowing that he is currently face to face with Jesus,” Sterban’s family said in a statement. Born April 24, 1943, in Camden, New Jersey, Sterban began singing at six in Sunday school as a soprano. He sang tenor in his seventh-grade glee club, and upon his eighth grade, his voice dropped to bass.

He studied music at Trenton State College (modernly known as the College of New Jersey), and began singing in a gospel quartet where he met Joe Bonsall, his future Oak Ridge Boys bandmate.

While singing part-time, Sterban joined J.D. Sumner and the Stamps, whom Elvis Presley hired to sing behind him. He then sang and recorded with the King of Rock and Roll, and also appeared in the 1972 concert film Elvis on Tour.

Sterban was then offered the opportunity to join the Oak Ridge Boys after the departure of Noel Fox, which he accepted in 1972. Bonsall joined the group one year later, with Duane Allen, William Lee Golden and Sterban already in the group, which carried on as the faces of the band for five decades.

They performed with Johnny Cash, who helped them sign with Columbia Records in 1973, though it was after opening for Roy Clark in 1975 when they met his manger, Jim Halsey, who would later sign with the group.

They reached success with “Y’all Come Back Saloon,” which reached No. 3 on the country chart in 1977, and led to a string of hits. “I’ll Be True to You” became their first No. 1 song, though 1981’s “Elvira” would become one of their largest successes.

Originally recorded by Dallas Frazier, the Oak Ridge Boys’ version of the track went to No. 1 on the country chart and No. 5 on the pop chart, sold more than two million copies and won a Grammy. Sterban’s bass refrain was a major touchpoint on the song.

The Oak Ridge Boys went on to also become members of the Grand Ole Opry, the Gospel Music Hall of Fame and the Country Music Hall of Fame.

Sterban released his memoir From Elvis to Elvira: My Life on Stage, written with Steven Robinson, in 2012.

Sterban is survived by his wife, Donna; his daughters, Lauren (Tyler) and Tori (Michael); his sons, Rich (Chele), Doug, and Chris; his grandchildren, Mali (Zachary), Nikolas, Tyler (Ryann), Madelyn, Alexandra, Rex, Owen, and Charli Grace; and his great-grandchildren, Salem, Asher, and Theo. He was preceded in death by his mother Victoria Sterban, brother Joseph Sterban and grandson Zachary.

Investing - Theory, News &amp; General • Re: Article: Do Bond Investors Get Rewarded for Taking Dur. Risk? (P. 1) Edward F. McQuarrie, William J. Bernstein, 11/6

1. diversification within the bond space is difficult, because almost every type is correlated near .90 with the others.
2. There is no good answer to your questions about correlations, because these are not stationary over time. However, as a general rule, Treasuries have a correlation with stocks near 0, while corporate bonds have a higher, non-zero correlation.
Regarding point 1, term risk, credit risk, and prepayment risk are sufficiently uncorrelated for bond diversification if the goal is to enhance expected return and reduce volatility of a bond portfolio. But it cones at the expense of increasing correlation with equities, so that the volatility of a stock and bond portfolio typically will be increased.

Regarding point 2, sample correlations or sample covariances are not stationary over time. That is an artifact of sample bias and general low quality of investment return samples. Actual correlation or covariance is a property of the distributions and joint distributions of future return. We don’t know what those distributions are, or if they are non-stationary, and even if they are, we don’t know how rapidly they change. There are some explicit transitional phenomena, like Fannie and Freddie dropping assumability as an allowed property of conforming loans, and the impact of that on extension risk of MBS.

Ultimately, though, what most investors care about with respect to using bonds to diversify equity risk is what the sample correlations look like during major corrections and bear markets. Even more salient is the performance of an overall portfolio during times of market stress.

Thus, when I evaluate different bond portfolios, my preference is to stress test different equity and bond portfolios using a collection of different periods of market stress— bear markets, periods of inflation acceleration, etc.

The Best Peanuts Character of ‘A Charlie Brown Thanksgiving’ Isn’t Charlie Brown

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

One would think that, by having a character's name in the title, that character would be the most important in the TV show. There is no point in calling the series Malcolm in the Middle if there isn't a Malcolm that is front and center, right there in the middle, for example. By that logic, the most important character in the beloved TV special A Charlie Brown Thanksgiving is Charlie Brown himself, right? Wrong. The most important character in A Charlie Brown Thanksgiving is arguably its most unassuming: the bookish Marcie (Jimmy Ahrens).

Back inside his home after Lucy (Robin Kohn) pulls the football away at the last moment, when he's running to kick it, yet again, Charlie Brown (Todd Barbee) answers the phone. It's Peppermint Patty (Christopher DeFaria), and after the obligatory "How you been, Chuck?" Patty tells Charlie Brown that because her dad's out of town, she can come to his house and share Thanksgiving with him, ending the decidedly one-sided conversation. He barely has time to collect his thoughts before Patty calls back, saying she's invited Marcie along for dinner. Then she calls back again, saying that she's now invited Franklin (Robin Reed).

The problem is that Charlie Brown won't even be there, with him and his family spending Thanksgiving at his grandmother's house. He can't call Peppermint Patty back, because, as he says, "You never get to say anything." But dinner isn't until 4:30, so he has time to put together something, so it's all hands on deck as he, Sally (Hilary Momberger), Linus (Stephen Shea) and Snoopy (Bill Melendez) set up a table, chairs, and prepare a meal consisting of toast, pretzel sticks, popcorn, and jelly beans.

It's not turkey, but given the time and resources available, it's pretty decent, especially for children (jelly beans for dinner sounds great). So when Patty, Marcie and Franklin arrive, everything's set to go. Linus leads them in a history lesson wrapped up in a grace. The dinner is unveiled and everyone digs in... except for Patty. She is irate, wondering who the blockhead is that came up with the menu when it's supposed to be turkey, mashed potatoes, pumpkin pie, and all the other conventional offerings of a Thanksgiving dinner. As she's ranting, Charlie Brown slinks away, having failed once more.

If Patty wasn't going to let anyone else get a word in edgewise before, she sure as Dickens wasn't going to in her enraged state. Yet it's Marcie — quiet, bookish, intellectual Marcie — that stands alone in the face of Hurricane Peppermint Patty. She doesn't raise her voice, but rather speaks calmly, with a gentle, quiet strength, and challenges Patty, saying: "Now, wait a minute, sir. Did he invite you here to dinner, or did you invite yourself and us too?"

Confronted by the truth, Patty realizes she's 100% in the wrong and has hurt Charlie Brown deeply by being unappreciative of the efforts he made to create something special regardless. It's a complete reversal that only happens because Marcie is brave enough to call Patty out. Marcie then pushes Patty to apologize directly to Charlie Brown, but quickly realizes Patty is too ashamed to do it herself. So she's the one who sets things right by apologizing to Charlie Brown on Patty's behalf, but goes even further by helping Chuck understand that he didn't ruin Thanksgiving, but rather fulfilled its purpose, in this exchange:

"Thanksgiving is more than eating, Chuck. You heard what Linus was saying out there. Those early pilgrims were thankful for what had happened to them. And we should be thankful too. We should just be thankful for being together. I think that's what they mean by "Thanksgiving," Charlie Brown."

A long-time 'Peanuts' character finally gets his time in the spotlight.

In this regard, she serves the same purpose as Linus did for A Charlie Brown Christmas, delivering the lines that underscore the meaning behind the season. With that, they all come together, and as a group goes to Charlie Brown's grandmother's for a true Thanksgiving dinner after she invites them to participate, the proverbial happy ending. None of this happens without Marcie, making her the most important character in the special. Peppermint Patty completely sours the proceedings, Charlie Brown sits by himself to wallow in his own self-loathing over a situation he never even created, and the friend group lies fractured on a day it should be celebrated. So thank you, Marcie, for being the voice of reason for the Thanksgiving season. Enjoy your toast.

What are the limitations of eGPUs vs onboard graphics cards for laptops?

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

An eGPU can bring desktop-class graphics to a laptop, but cables, bandwidth limits and extra costs can eat into the advantage.

Connecting a desktop graphics card to your laptop seems like a neat solution to one of the oldest problems in portable computing. Use the thin machine for work and travel, and connect to a much more powerful GPU when you get home. An external GPU (eGPU) can do just that, but all that extra graphics power still has to travel through Thunderbolt or USB4 instead of the direct internal connection used by a built-in laptop GPU. That bandwidth bottleneck can eat into the desktop card's advantage, and the cable it's connected to holds sway as well.

In this comparison, "onboard" primarily refers to a dedicated GPU built into the laptop, not the integrated graphics in the laptop's processor. Even when a laptop GPU shares its name with a desktop GPU, the hardware can be vastly different. The RTX 5090 Laptop GPU features 10,496 CUDA cores, 24GB of GDDR7 memory, and a GPU subsystem power range of 95W to 150W. Meanwhile, you'll find 21,760 CUDA cores and 32GB of GDDR7 memory on NVIDIA's desktop RTX 5090. That's a significant gap before even considering bottlenecks, like Thunderbolt.

A modern eGPU typically communicates with the laptop via Thunderbolt or USB4, rather than directly on its PCIe bus, and that's where some of the desktop card's edge is lost. A built-in laptop GPU uses an internal PCIe connection instead.

Thunderbolt 4 has 40 Gbps of overall bandwidth and requires 32 Gbps of PCIe data throughput, while Thunderbolt 5 upgrades the base speed to 80 Gbps and doubles that PCIe requirement to 64 Gbps. Internal laptop GPUs can use wider multi-lane PCIe connections, depending on the laptop's design, giving them more room to move data without the Thunderbolt bottleneck.

That bottleneck is more noticeable when it comes to a powerful desktop card. NVIDIA's desktop RTX 5090 has 1,792GB/s of memory bandwidth and a 575W total graphics power rating, compared with 896GB/s and a 95W to 150W GPU subsystem power range for the RTX 5090 Laptop GPU. Those aren't PCIe bandwidth figures, but they show how much more raw hardware the desktop card brings before the external connection starts holding it back.

Thunderbolt 5 also has a 120 Gbps Bandwidth Boost mode, but that isn't 120 Gbps of PCIe bandwidth for the GPU. Intel says PCIe data throughput remains at 64 Gbps. While USB4 can also support PCIe traffic, a USB-C-shaped port alone doesn't guarantee eGPU compatibility. The host hardware needs the right support, and the enclosure, drivers, GPU and power requirements must all cooperate (because apparently one cable was getting too easy).

Using the laptop's built-in display can add another penalty because rendered frames may have to travel back through the same limited connection. This is why some brands suggest connecting an external monitor directly to the eGPU for better performance. There's no universal performance-loss percentage, since the GPU, laptop, connection, resolution and game all affect the result.

A laptop GPU begins with less raw hardware, but it doesn't have to deal with most of this plumbing. It operates within the laptop's cooling and power constraints, doesn't have to send rendered frames back over an external cable, and remains in the laptop when you pick it up. The portability benefit is more like a suggestion with a traditional desktop-card eGPU. You now have an enclosure, a desktop GPU, and maybe an ATX power supply on the desk, while a big card can add size and noise to the mix.

Let's not forget to talk about the cost associated with eGPUs. As an example, Razer's Thunderbolt 5 Core X V2 supports full-size NVIDIA and AMD desktop cards up to four slots wide. The enclosure also has its own 120mm cooling fan, but it doesn't come with a GPU or even the necessary ATX power supply to keep it running. Those would be added expenses on top of approximately $350 for the enclosure itself.

That math gets considerably friendlier if you have a suitable GPU sitting around after a desktop upgrade. At that point, the enclosure could significantly upgrade your laptop without buying another computer. It's more difficult to justify purchasing a high-end desktop card specifically for an eGPU, especially since you're paying for all that power and then sending it through a cable that might not allow you to use it all.

The sign for a roundabout shows three arrows forming a circle, and once again, it will be a yellow-and-black diamond-shaped warning sign. Roundabouts always run in a counter-clockwise direction in North America. They can have numerous lanes and are an alternative to crossroads. Here, the right of way rules are simple and uniform: Drivers must always give way to traffic on the left — in other words, the cars that are already on the roundabout.

Getting the right lane is crucial for avoiding accidents; the leftmost lane is used for turning left, whereas the rightmost lanes are used for turning right, and both can be used for going straight on. These are the rules on multi-lane roundabouts unless signposted otherwise — this way, traffic in either lane does not cross unnecessarily.

As for whether drivers need to use turn signals on a roundabout or not, the answer is yes. It's never a bad idea to let other drivers know where you are going; even if you are comfortable in your own abilities to get across safely, it's best practice to help other motorists out by indicating to them your next intended move.

Despite being a late sleeper at home, I always seem to wake up early on trail. Today, I wake up before 6 am, but I force myself to sleep until 6:30. The trail follows a road before heading sharply downhill on singletrack. There seems to be no one else out enjoying the cool morning hiking hours. It is beautiful, the sun glinting off the ocean and the dew.

The trail dives into a valley before climbing to the airport. The valley is lush and green, and I even encounter a small, stagnant stream at the bottom — remarkable given the dry reputation of the trail.

What goes down must go up, though. After reaching the bottom of the valley, the trail immediately twists up towards the airport. I could see the airport from the top of the valley before descending, and it looks barely closer. The sun has come out and is beating down, even this early. This side of the valley is more exposed, with no trees and minimal foliage. The descent actually had a few trees, but it’s clear this ascent is closer to what the rest of the day will look like.

Airport in the Sky taunting me as I climb toward it.

Breakfast “sandie” – eggs, bacon, cheddar, avocado on sourdough.

I sit on the porch and enjoy the chance cool down, rehydrate, and fuel up. More and more people arrive on planes and from trail as the morning continues. It seems less crowded if you arrive for breakfast than for lunch. The line is much longer when I leave an hour and half after arriving.

My only regret is that I never got to meet the airport’s resident black cat.

As I head out, the sun is blazing in the middle of the sky, no cloud in sight. Today’s hike is much shorter and easier than yesterday’s, but the temperature is much warmer. As I scope out my path ahead, I see few trees and lots of road walking. It’s going to be a warm afternoon.

The trail alternates between singletrack and dirt road. I spend a couple of miles cruising along the wide, shadeless road before diving back into the landscape, but the road walk is not unpleasant. The views are perpetual and the track is relatively quiet. I quickly realize, though, that with minimal tree coverage, bathroom breaks require creativity or a lack of modesty — or both. The hillside often steeply climbs away from the road, making it even more difficult.

A lovely blooming cactus flower from along the side of the trail.

As I leave the road for singletrack, I notice movement on the side of trail. A small lizard freezes as I approach. I’ve noticed two distinct types of small lizards, but this one is my favorite. I have already seen quite a few of them, and will see many more before the end of my hike.

The side-blotched lizard, known for the male’s throat colors indicating their mating style.

I continue to dodge more lizards under foot as I climb up a crest, and immediately my eyes are drawn forward. The trail winds along the very top of ridge before beginning the descent into Little Harbor. It’s rare for me to experience a trail on a ridge like this, and it takes my breath away.

The ridge walk is lovely, but the constant sun starts to make me weary. The temperature keeps increasing, no clouds or shade breaks to be found. I stop to sip my electrolytes and re-apply sunblock. Just ahead, at the bottom of this hill, I see a semi-shaded bench at a trail junction. It becomes all I focus on, my entire motivation for the remaining ridge walk and descent. Get. To. Shade.

The palm trees come into view, making me both smile (camp is near!) and chuckle (palm trees are not native). Mostly, I am thrilled at the view of trees. Trees mean shade. Shade means relief.

Even so, I stop and take in the view. It really is a picturesque sight.

Once at the campground, I head for Campsite #10, which turns out to be at the very front — right by the beach. Talk about incredible luck. When I reserved it, I was not sure what I was getting. A giant palm tree provides much needed shade over a fire ring and picnic table. A stream inlet burbles past, while straight ahead is the harbor itself.

I spend the rest of the afternoon exploring the camp and the beach. There is a water spigot in the next camp over, just like last night — though this isn’t typical. A row of porta potties on the road just outside the campsites serve as the toilets. Not my favorite, but at least there is a hand washing station!

I run into some fellow hikers I met at the shaded bench earlier. With wide eyes, one asks me, “Did you see the bison as you walked into camp?” Surprised, I reply that I did not. Apparently, it was lounging under a tree next to the trail into camp and I walked right past it. It must have blended with the dirt, obscured in the shade.

What I believe to be a brown pelican hunting in the slough next to my campsite.

The Catalina Fox, staring longingly at our food. It looks a little like a stray dog.

Tomorrow is the day I am most nervous about. The temperatures are supposed to be highest tomorrow, and the official trail has a brutally steep climb and descent with zero shade, which I will face in the full afternoon sun. Somehow, though, I don’t feel too daunted. I feel excited, rejuvenated by my lovely evening. I knew the trail was short, but tomorrow is my last full day on trail. I can’t believe how fast it has flown by.

I crawl into my tent early, ready to get an early start to beat the heat tomorrow. Perhaps tomorrow is the day for my bison sighting!

Where was Nvidia’s security team?

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Outsiders had little reason to notice when Nvidia’s longtime chief security officer and head of product security stepped down in August. Just two and a half months later, CEO Jensen Huang himself left the company over growing questions about his extramarital relationships — starting with an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore data center company that U.S. investigators suspect of funneling Nvidia chips to China, then widening to reports about two separate affairs with Nvidia employees.

At the time, David Reber, who spent four years as CSO and seven at Nvidia overall, said he was leaving for health reasons. “This wasn’t a pivot I had planned. But I know what needs to come first: family and health,” he wrote on LinkedIn in late August, disclosing that he’d been diagnosed with young-onset Parkinson’s more than two years ago.

Reber’s replacement, Saša Zdjelar, previously spent 17 years at ExxonMobil in Houston, leaving as a senior IT manager before spending one year at Salesforce and several years at the cybersecurity company ReversingLabs. Zdjelar didn’t respond to questions Thursday about the transition or what the security team knew.

Nothing public connects Reber’s or Zdjelar’s role changes to the Megaspeed matter, but it seems fair to wonder what Nvidia’s security team knew about the government’s scrutiny of Megaspeed and about Huang’s relationships, and whether anyone raised concerns. Reber didn’t respond to questions Thursday. Nvidia also has not answered our related questions.

Not all of those relationships are the business of the security team. Relationships with subordinates typically fall to HR, legal, and the board. But an affair with an executive at a company under federal investigation is a different animal. (Megaspeed has denied any wrongdoing.) Security specialists say affairs can create vulnerabilities that foreign intelligence services look to exploit.

Catching that kind of exposure isn’t easy. The way intelligence services collect information, much of it through everyday conversation rather than hacking, makes it especially difficult for security teams to identify risks, according to retired senior intelligence officer Nicholas Eftimiades, who says he has trained more than 1,000 corporate security professionals.

Some people inside Nvidia clearly knew parts of the picture. Nvidia’s top lawyer was engaged with the government’s Megaspeed inquiry more than a year ago, The New York Times reported earlier this week. (Nvidia has said it regularly contacts officials.) Reports about Huang’s relationships with employees suggest colleagues also knew of them, even if the behavior is not expressly forbidden by Nvidia’s code of conduct. The Times cited five Nvidia employees who said he was involved with a vice president who reported directly to him, and Reuters, citing two people, later reported a second relationship. What’s unclear is whether any of that reached security.

Even if it had reached Nvidia’s security team, Eftimiades, who wrote Chinese Espionage Operations and Tactics in 2020, said a security team’s resources are often limited. For one thing, they’re often underfunded because, unlike some other cost centers, they don’t drive revenue. It’s astonishingly common for CEOs to answer a security team’s warnings with “not a nickel more than compliance,” he said.

Many companies also define security very narrowly, specialists say. In an opinion piece in The Hill, Don Aviv, CEO of the corporate investigations firm Interfor International, and author Jeremy Hurewitz, wrote, “Too many companies still approach security primarily as a matter of protecting facilities, preventing cyberattacks, and responding to physical threats.” The two argued that “[s]ecurity teams need to pay greater attention to insider threats, including the cultivation of executives and employees by foreign intelligence services, suspicious business relationships, conflicts of interest and attempts to obtain sensitive information through trusted personal contacts.”

Still, some of it is beyond what any team can do. Speaking generally, not about Huang, Eftimiades offered a hypothetical in which a CEO complains that he can’t do something because “I’m fighting a board who doesn’t want to do this.” That tells an intelligence asset who the obstacle is. The service then asks, “Who’s the sticking point here?” and looks for ways into that person’s email and phone.

What a rival intelligence service wants is often less technology than policy insight, including who’s pushing to change export rules and who the biggest blocker is. “Intel world tends to be like cancer,” he said. “Once it gets into the body, it looks to spread and looks to reach out.”

The playbook isn’t new, Eftimiades added, “particularly for China.” Any executive dealing with the country “will tell you that they’ve been approached.” He said there are also “tons of diplomat stories” of U.S. diplomats walking into a hotel room to find a woman on the bed. He said what’s newer compared with Soviet era spying is the relationships these services cultivate now often last years, not weeks.

Technology can augment that human intelligence, Eftimiades said, pointing to IMSI catchers are one example. These devices mimic a cell tower so that nearby phones connect to them. A basic version can cost about $1,000 and fit in a suitcase, he said, though government-grade models cost far more.

Eftimiades says no one knows yet if these were used against Huang, whose phone was presumably hardened such that compromises could be detected, if not stopped altogether. But either way, what Nvidia’s security team knew, and whether it could have acted on it, should become clearer as the board’s independent investigation and federal inquiries proceed.

In the meantime, Eftimiades said he hopes the whole episode is “a wake-up call” for U.S. industry, which he said is mostly “clueless” about China’s tactics. “We have lost trillions [of dollars] in intellectual property,” is his guess. “Whatever we think we’re doing isn’t working.”

Florida city cancels St. Patrick's Day parade, citing new anti-DEI law

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis (shown here in October) signed the anti-DEI law that Delray Beach officials say prohibits them from putting on cultural celebrations, like a St. Patrick's Day parade. Alex Wong/Getty Images hide caption

One of the first casualties of a new Florida law that prohibits taxpayer dollars from going toward diversity initiatives is a St. Patrick's Day parade in a beach town that has been celebrating its Irish American community for nearly 60 years.

The city of Delray Beach announced this week that it was canceling its popular St. Patrick's Day festivities in compliance with a new law that takes effect on Jan. 1. The city will also withdraw its sponsorship of LGBTQ Pride celebrations in June, officials said.

The law, backed by Republican Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis is part of the state's ongoing crackdown on diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) initiatives that the governor has called discriminatory and said have "no place" in public institutions.

The city lawyer for Delray Beach, a community in southeast Florida with picturesque beaches and a historic downtown, told the mayor that any government-sponsored celebrations of culture, ethnicity or heritage that are not a national holiday (for example: Independence Day) could run afoul of the new anti-DEI law. So, Mayor Tom Carney said, he made the difficult decision to cancel one of the city's biggest events of the year.

In past years, the parade, an annual event since 1970, wound its way through the downtown core of Delray Beach with spectators lined up several-people deep along the route. Local businesses and organizations sponsored floats and giveaways. School bands played as they marched through the streets. Participants included members of the Ancient Order of Hibernians, the largest and oldest Irish Catholic organization in the country.

"I've said it's engrained in the DNA of Delray and I do mean that. It's one of our signature events," Carney, a Republican, told NPR.

The mayor said he's hopeful that the city can find a path forward — and a way to preserve the event.

If a private sponsor were to volunteer to take over the St. Patrick's Day parade, or the June Pride celebrations, Carney said, the city would gladly hand over the reins and support the continuation of these festivities.

"These types of events we should be celebrating because in this country, people can have these events. In other countries, you can't do this. We have great freedoms here," he said. "And if the community wants something and it's not against public policy then I would support them to do it if they're using private dollars. If we can do it, we should do it."

Carney, who is of Irish descent, said the parade was privately run until about eight years ago — so returning to a private-sponsorship model would be a callback to the parade's origins. His own family, he said, was involved in keeping the parade going during the 1980s and 1990s.

Delray Beach officials said they have been inundated with emails, calls and social media messages from constituents who are angry that the community's decades-long celebration had been axed.

"It was kind of a call to action to me," Carney said. "The community really wants to have it, so we should go ahead and have it, we just need to get it privately funded."

The governor, meanwhile, has accused Delray Beach of weaponizing the new anti-DEI measure to make a political point. DeSantis said the city was "engaging in "malicious compliance" in a message he posted on X following the city's cancellation announcement.

"What they are doing is not mandated by Florida law," the governor wrote. "It's just an attempt to make a political point — a point that legacy media will no doubt try to amplify. We've seen these stunts throughout the years. Rinse and repeat."

The mayor said the city is merely trying to stay out of trouble with the law. The measure, SB1134, asserts that any official that violates the provision could face civil or legal consequences.

"The legislation has unintended consequences. The governor may genuinely believe what he believes and I take him at his word; our city attorney came up with a different conclusion," Carney said. "I'm going to leave it at that."

Firefly’s 14-episode run was met with mixed reviews and underwhelming ratings, but that wasn’t the end of the space opera’s story.

Firefly’s superb DVD sales, as well as the outspoken fan community online, prompted a movie spinoff in the form of 2005’s Serenity. Even though Serenity wasn’t a box office hit, the movie was beloved by critics, and a massive array of subsequent sci-fi shows acknowledged Firefly as a formative influence. There is SyFy’s space opera Killjoys, which often feels like the show Firefly could have been, as well as 2015’s Dark Matter, which was a love letter to Firefly in tone and premise alike.

In 2016, Jonathan Nolan’s Westworld reboot took Firefly’s mix of a futuristic sci-fi setting and traditional western elements and turned these same ingredients into a darker, more austere dystopian mystery box series. More recently, Netflix’s latest high-profile show has blended western and sci-fi tropes in a fresh, original story that has more than a touch of Firefly to it. An animated show created by LeSean Thomas, Bass X Machina takes place in a steampunk wild west where supernatural threats, giant robots, and gunslinging cowboys vie for dominance.

Within this pitiless milieu, Brian Tyree Henry voices the titular Bass Reeves, a bounty hunter and Civil War veteran. Joining Bass is Tati Gabrille’s Dana, a teenage engineer searching for her uncle who has disappeared, and Cree Summer’s Ahni, a magical mystic. With a superb ensemble voice cast including Wayne Knight and Janelle Monáe, Bass X Machina has no shortage of star power.

With epic, immersive battles, constant twists, and a wild mix of genres at its core, Bass X Machina is a truly fresh take on the real-life story of Bass Reeves that is unpredictable, inventive, and brimming with the unlikely fusion of sci-fi and western plot elements that made Firefly so memorable all those years ago.

On the big screen, attempts to mix sci-fi and westerns have often fallen flat. One need only look at expensive misfires like 2011’s Cowboys & Aliens and 1999’s Wild Wild West to know that, for whatever reason, the pairing doesn’t seem to attract interest from audiences at the box office. Moreover, even the kindest critics would struggle to call these movies overlooked classics.

Westerns and sci-fi don’t seem to mix at a practical level when it comes to feature films, at least not on a blockbuster scale. In contrast, Bass X Machina proves that Netflix’s animated series could take the same character who was the subject of a po-faced Taylor Sheridan Western series, Lawman: Bass Reeves, and have him fighting killer machines alongside paranormal mystics in a twisted reinvention of the Wild West.

With stunning imagery that calls to mind Netflix’s earlier genre blender Blue Eye Samurai, Bass X Machina admittedly makes a pretty dramatic visual contrast with Firefly’s deliberately low-tech vision of the far future. However, fans of Firefly’s fast-paced action, sharp dialogue, themes of found family, and truly original mix of the two genres will find plenty to love here.

Only time will tell if Bass X Machina will receive a second season, or if the show will suffer the same ignominious fate as Firefly as well as sharing its atypical mix of genres. However, for now, Netflix’s Bass X Machina is an ideal substitute for any Firefly fans who still miss the influential show.

Calvin and Hobbes’ 5 Greatest Spaceman Spiff Comic Strips

Calvin and Hobbes wasn't Bill Watterson's first pitch for a comic strip. C&H's protagonists began as side characters in Watterson's comic The Doghouse before his original syndicate suggested that they ought to be the focus. "Marvin" became "Calvin," the rest of The Doghouse's cast was dropped, and while United Feature Syndicate still didn't take the resulting comic, Universal Press Syndicate did.

Before then, Watterson had drawn cartoons about a spacefaring character as early as college. Watterson went on to make that character the focus of one of his rejected comics: a mustachioed, cigar-smoking parody of adventurers like Flash Gordon. Shortly after Calvin and Hobbes began, Watterson reworked "Mort" into Calvin's fantasy alter ego Spaceman Spiff.

Over the course of Calvin and Hobbes' run, Spiff was one of Calvin's most frequent escapist characters. Whenever Calvin was caught in a boring or unpleasant situation, he frequently retreated into his Spiff persona to pass time or even make a hasty retreat. In practice, that gave Watterson a handy excuse to take Calvin and Hobbes' art well outside its usual mundane setting, with tons of gross aliens, impossible landscapes, and great phrases like "eat hot electric death" that don't usually come up in everyday conversation.

Much like the rest of Calvin and Hobbes, Spaceman Spiff really hit his stride in the comic's last three years, when Watterson began doing his trademark "unbreakable" Sunday strips. Some of the most colorful and evocative C&H comics are Spaceman Spiff adventures from the mid-'90s, but Spiff made a few solid appearances before that point. Here are some of our picks for the best Spaceman Spiff comics in Calvin and Hobbes.

When Calvin gets sick during his summer vacation, his first reaction is to get annoyed. Sick days are for the school year. I think we all can relate.

Unfortunately for all involved, Calvin's mom takes him to the doctor. That includes a checkup, an injection, and a retreat into Calvin's Spiff mode, as he imagines himself as a prisoner of aliens rather than a little kid trapped in an examination room.

Calvin's teacher Mrs. Wormwood catches a lot of strays in Spaceman Spiff cartoons. Sometimes I want to see a version of C&H from her perspective. Most of her class are apparently boring normal kids who do the reading and don't get in unusual amounts of trouble. Then there's Calvin, who's as much of a chaos agent as a six-year-old can be. Mrs. Wormwood and Mr. Wilson from Dennis the Menace would have a lot to talk about.

In this early installation, Calvin gets called on in class and seamlessly incorporates Mrs. Wormwood's history question into his daydream. It's not being called on in class; it's being interrogated by the Hideous Zorgs of Planet X-13. (Look at those bug-men. Those are aliens that deserve the word "Hideous" being part of their proper noun.)

The most extreme (in the '90s sense) Spaceman Spiff adventure was a week-long story where Calvin got deeply enough into character that he staged a breakout from elementary school. Called to the board to answer a math problem, Calvin instead decides to shoot his way out (with a rubber band), then run for the door.

Through what one assumes is the relative indifference of school staff, Calvin manages to make it all the way home before his mom catches him. Unfortunately, Spiff can't get Calvin out of the ensuing consequences.

Right from the start, Bill Watterson has said that one of his primary influences on Calvin & Hobbes is George Herriman's Golden Age newspaper comic Krazy Kat, which ran from 1913 to 1944. That was already some inside baseball in the 1980s when Calvin and Hobbes was new, as Krazy Kat’s modern following is almost exclusively made up of historians, fanatics, and other cartoonists. It’s not dead media yet, though, as historians and preservationists have made a point of keeping collections of Krazy Kat in print. Curious readers can check out Fantagraphics' George Herriman Library hardcover collections, which are still in print at time of writing.

If you check out Krazy Kat, its influence on Calvin and Hobbes is obvious immediately, particularly in its colored Sunday comics. Herriman's characters inhabited a cartoonish version of the Arizona desert, and many of Watterson's Spaceman Spiff strips feature him visiting alien planets with similar arid backgrounds. This largely-wordless strip from 1993 is a perfect example, with so much of it devoted to Calvin's imagined spaceship journey from an alien valley to home.

One of the best Spaceman Spiff strips comes in the last year of Calvin and Hobbes. Without dialogue, we see the entire comic from Calvin's perspective, as he's entertaining himself during yet another boring class by imagining himself behind the stick of Spiff's flying saucer.

Suddenly, Mrs. Wormwood interrupts his daydream and breaks his train of thought. As soon as she isn't paying attention anymore, Calvin switches to a new scenario: this time, he's a dinosaur stalking prey.
