How to Run a Feedback Round That Actually Changes the Product

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Early feedback has a familiar shape. You show someone your product, they say "looks great, I'd definitely use it," and you leave feeling encouraged and no wiser.

That isn't because people are lying. Most are being kind, and kindness is the enemy of information. A useful feedback round is designed to get past politeness and into behavior: what people do, what confuses them, and what they'd actually give up time or money for.

Here's a simple process we'd use with a small team and a small number of users. It doesn't need special tools.

Feedback without a question is a pile of opinions. Before you talk to anyone, write down one to three things you're unsure about. For example:

Each uncertainty shapes who you ask and what you ask. "Do they understand it?" needs fresh eyes. "Do they have the problem?" needs people from your target audience. Mixing them gives muddled answers.

Five to eight people from your real audience will teach you more than fifty from anywhere. Friends and family are comfortable to ask and unreliable to learn from; they want you to succeed.

Aim for variety within the audience: a newer user and a more experienced one, a skeptical person and an enthusiastic one.

The most reliable method is also the simplest: give someone a realistic task and watch silently.

Five people stumbling on the same step is a clear signal, and nothing you'd get from a questionnaire shows that as vividly. You'll often find that the thing you assumed was obvious is the thing everyone misses.

People are poor at predicting their own behavior. "Would you use this?" invites a hopeful yes. "Would you pay for it?" invites an optimistic yes. Both are cheap to give.

If someone has no current workaround and has never looked for one, the problem might not be painful enough. If they've built a spreadsheet and three reminders to cope, you're near something real.

Avoid leading questions, such as "Don't you think this is easier?" Ask neutral ones: "How did that compare with what you do now?" And when you've asked, stop talking. Silence is the most effective follow-up question.

Be cautious with polite compliments. "Nice, clean design" is pleasant and tells you almost nothing. "Can I keep using this next week?" is a much better sign.

The aim isn't statistical certainty. With a handful of people, you're looking for clear, repeated patterns, and being honest that this is a small sample.

Resist the urge to build everything people ask for. A product assembled from every suggestion tends to serve no one in particular. Your job is to understand the need behind the request, then choose the simplest way to meet it, or decide not to.

It costs little and builds the kind of relationship that makes the next round easier to run.

Small, regular rounds beat one big study, because you learn while you can still change direction cheaply.

Building a Planar-Graph Floor Plan Room Solver in Pure TypeScript

When a user drags walls on a 2D canvas—or uploads a hand-drawn sketch that gets parsed into approximate wall segments—you do not start with clean room polygons. You start with a messy soup of line segments:

If you try to run polygon detection straight on raw wall segments, room areas vanish, doors float off their host walls, and exterior square footage breaks. You do not need a multi-megabyte WebAssembly CAD kernel to solve this cleanly in the browser. A deterministic planar-graph pipeline in pure TypeScript handles the whole topology problem in under a millisecond on typical residential plans.

Here is the exact architecture we built in src/lib/cad/healer.ts for PlanCaliber, our browser-based 2D/3D floor plan CAD engine.

Before extracting a single room polygon, we run a deterministic four-pass healer (healPlanDocument) on a cloned wall graph. Order matters—welding before splitting prevents near-coincident corners from creating microscopic triangles when T-junctions get projected:

In Pass 1 (weldVerticesInPlace), vertices within weldRadiusIn (default 8" for rough sketch imports, tighter for direct CAD drags) are grouped using a disjoint-set (Union-Find) with path compression.

A subtle trap when merging a cluster of vertices is taking a blind arithmetic mean. If three existing CAD vertices already sit cleanly at x = 144.000" and one sloppy hand-drawn endpoint lands at x = 146.500", averaging drags all four vertices to 144.625", nudging perpendicular walls out of square.

Instead, we bucket candidate coordinates at 0.05" precision first:

If two or more vertices already agree within 0.05", that exact coordinate wins and the stray vertex snaps to it. Only when every vertex in the cluster disagrees do we fall back to a quarter-inch snapped mean.

When an interior partition terminates within 6" of an exterior wall's span, we project the endpoint perpendicularly onto the host wall, insert a shared vertex, and split the host wall into two child segments.

Because doors and windows are stored parametrically (wallId + offsetIn measured from startId), splitting a wall must remap every attached opening onto the child segment that now contains its center—recomputing offsetIn relative to the new split vertex—and Pass 4 (clampOpeningsInPlace) ensures no door jamb overlaps a mitered corner (>= 3" margin from both ends).

Once every intersection and T-junction shares a vertex, the wall graph is planar—except for dangling wing walls, kitchen peninsulas, and open closets whose endpoints have degree 1.

If you leave degree-1 edges in the graph while tracing half-edge faces, the traversal walks down the dangling wall, spins 180 degrees around the dead end, and walks back along its twin—producing degenerate slit polygons that ruin label placement and interior offset geometry.

Getting the basic half-edge walk right is only half the story. Residential plans immediately hit three practical geometry edge cases:

Walls have physical thickness—typically 6" for exterior framed walls and 4.5" for interior 2×4 stud partitions.

Separating these three measurements in the solver—rather than fudging one number—is why you can simultaneously get accurate interior drywall/flooring takeoffs and a real exterior-face GLA total. (We also broke the standalone ANSI rules out into a free ANSI Z765 Gross Living Area Calculator.)

If a homeowner draws a freestanding pantry or fireplace chase completely inside an open-concept great room without walls connecting it to the outer shell, the half-edge extractor yields two separate closed cycles: the pantry and the great room.

Using point-in-polygon checks across extracted faces (findEnclosingParentIndices), we detect when cycle B sits strictly inside cycle A, subtract B's footprint from A's net floor area, and pass B's polygon as a hole when placing A's room label.

A polygon's naive centroid frequently lands outside an L-shaped hallway or right on top of an island hole. ensureInteriorLabelPoint(poly, holes) tests the centroid first; if it falls outside the polygon, inside a hole, or too close to a wall, it sweeps interior grid candidates and picks the point maximizing minimum distance to every boundary and hole edge—so room tags and 12'-4" × 15'-8" callouts always sit in open floor space.

This entire healer, planar face extractor, clearance auditor, 3D cutaway extruder, pdf-lib scaled PDF writer, and zero-dependency AutoCAD R12 .DXF serializer runs live in PlanCaliber—no account required to draw, drag walls, calibrate a sketch, and watch rooms re-solve in real time.

AI Engineer vs Machine Learning Engineer vs AI Automation Engineer

A machine learning engineer builds and operates models and ML pipelines. An AI engineer usually builds applications and systems that use models, retrieval, agents or other AI services. An AI automation engineer connects existing models, business data and software tools into controlled workflows. The titles overlap, so choose by the work described: model ownership, product/application ownership, or business-process ownership.

Ask what the engineer is primarily responsible for keeping correct. A machine learning engineer owns the path from data to a trained, served and monitored model. An AI engineer owns a product or application that uses AI capabilities. An AI automation engineer owns a business workflow in which AI is one decision-making component among databases, SaaS tools, rules and human approvals.

These boundaries are practical, not official. A small company may call one person an AI engineer while expecting all three kinds of work. A larger organisation may split model training, AI product development and business integration across separate teams.

Accessible text equivalent: ML engineers centre the model lifecycle; AI engineers centre an AI-enabled application; AI automation engineers centre an end-to-end business process with integrations and human control.

A machine learning engineer turns data and modelling work into a reliable production capability. Current Google Cloud and AWS role guides include data preparation, model or foundation-model development, training, serving, pipeline orchestration, monitoring, security and ongoing improvement.

This path fits people who enjoy mathematics, experiments, data quality and infrastructure as much as model output. Typical projects include demand forecasting, recommendation, classification, computer vision, model fine-tuning or a reusable inference service. The engineer must explain metrics, data leakage, generalisation, drift and the operational cost of serving.

In this comparison, AI engineer means the application-focused role. The engineer selects or integrates models, builds retrieval and agent logic, connects the system to a product, evaluates task behaviour and operates the application. Microsoft’s current AI-103 profile names planning, generative and agentic solutions, information extraction, security and cross-functional collaboration.

The role still needs software engineering. A model call without authentication, schemas, tests, permissions, monitoring or failure handling is not a finished application. Some employers use “AI engineer” as an umbrella that includes ML engineering, so the vacancy must confirm the boundary.

An AI automation engineer begins with a business process: lead intake, document handling, reporting, knowledge access, customer requests or internal operations. The engineer connects triggers, models, databases and business applications, then adds rules, approvals, logs and recovery.

The model should not control permissions or consequential decisions. External messages, payments, access changes and sensitive actions remain behind deterministic rules or human approval. No-code platforms can reduce programming requirements, but they do not remove the need for data contracts, API knowledge, testing and operational judgement.

These are transition patterns, not hiring guarantees. Prior experience remains valuable because it supplies domain judgement, but each target still needs technical evidence.

Nine years is an eternity in crypto. Yet one Zcash whale appears to have spent that long sitting on an investment made during the privacy coin’s early days. On Thursday, blockchain monitoring platform Lookonchain reported that the investor had moved 15,750 ZEC, worth $11.39 million, to Kraken.

The coins date back to 2017, when Ethereum was still a relatively young network, decentralized finance barely existed and Zcash was attempting to establish itself as a more private alternative to Bitcoin.

Almost a decade later, the wallet is active again. And its owner’s reported acquisition price makes the transaction particularly interesting.

According to Lookonchain, the whale originally acquired 23,412 ZEC at an average price of approximately $215, representing an investment of just over $5 million.

The latest transactions account for roughly two-thirds of that original position, with at least 7,750 ZEC transferred to a Kraken deposit address.

At the reported transfer valuation of approximately $723 per ZEC, those 15,750 coins are worth around $8 million more than their estimated acquisition cost. That would represent a return of approximately 236% if the investor sold them at that price.

What makes this wallet fascinating, however, is what happened between those two valuations.

Zcash spent much of 2024 and early 2025 trading around $16 to $20 before staging a remarkable comeback. At $20 per coin, the whale’s original investment would have been worth less than $470,000, a paper loss of more than 90%, assuming the position remained intact.

Holding an asset through a temporary market correction is one thing. Holding it through years of declining relevance, while newer cryptocurrencies attract developers, investors and attention, is something else entirely.

Whatever the owner’s reasons for keeping the coins untouched, the decision has aged considerably better than it appeared to during Zcash’s quieter years.

There is something almost ironic about the fact that investors are following the movements of a whale holding one of crypto’s best-known privacy coins.

Unlike networks where transaction privacy is mandatory, Zcash supports both transparent and shielded transactions. Transparent addresses allow observers to examine balances and transfers, while shielded transactions use zero-knowledge cryptography to conceal transaction details.

That distinction explains how blockchain monitoring platforms can identify certain large ZEC movements despite the cryptocurrency’s privacy capabilities.

It also explains why the available information tells only part of the story.

Lookonchain can identify an apparent exchange deposit and estimate its value. Establishing who controls the wallet, whether ownership changed privately during those nine years or what the investor intends to do next is considerably harder.

Even the reported acquisition price is an estimate rather than a verified record of the owner’s personal trading history.

That is an important limitation of on-chain analysis, particularly when dealing with a network explicitly designed to give users greater control over their financial visibility.

The renewed enthusiasm surrounding Zcash has attracted a different kind of investor from the one buying ZEC in 2017.

Last week, ICAO Headquarters in Montréal hosted the Seventh Worldwide Air Transport Conference, where Member States and industry stakeholders gathered to set global policy priorities on aviation economic regulation, infrastructure financing, resilience, consumer protection and sustainable growth.

Over 1,500 delegates from 154 Member States and organizations attended the week-long event thirteen years after the Sixth Conference in 2013. They discussed high-level topics in commercial aviation: modernizing economic and legal frameworks, investing in infrastructure and skilled workforces, and ensuring that innovation keeps pace with rapidly changing technologies and global demands.

“Broad agreement was reached on the need for harmonized, risk-based, and proportionate economic regulation that facilitates the adoption of emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence, automation, sustainable aviation fuels, and advanced air mobility solutions,” ICAO said in a press release during the Conference.

Participants agreed on the value of frameworks that support innovation and investment for economic development. They stressed this especially for smaller landlocked States, which depend heavily on air connectivity for growth.

The Conference supported modernizing air navigation infrastructure in line with ICAO’s Global Air Navigation Plan. It also supported liberalizing air services agreements without making liberalization conditional on infrastructure development, since new air links fostered by more liberal agreements are seen as key to economic growth.

However, all initiatives should be flexible enough to address local circumstances and should not burden developing States. ICAO’s statement continued: “The Conference also reaffirmed that international standards are key to building effective, market-enabling policies and avoiding fragmentation that could impede cross-border air services. Delegates pointed to ICAO’s Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation (CORSIA) as a successful example of this approach.”

Participants were asked to express their views on the documentation proposing various initiatives with regards to consumer protection legislations that have become an integral part of the legal framework for all air operators.

The report for the Seventh Conference invites States to modernize their consumer-protection frameworks, keeping them evidence-based, proportionate, accessible and responsive to the changing realities of commercial air transport. It also says legislation should establish clear responsibilities for carriers, intermediaries and other providers regarding information, complaints and disruption assistance.

ICAO is expected to review its Core Principles while preserving national and regional flexibility. It is also expected to assess whether existing policies adequately cover online intermediaries and other overlooked situations.

Many Member States cautioned against the ICAO Air Passenger Handbook proposed by IATA, because producing and maintaining a single repository covering many different national laws would be too costly.

The Conference asked ICAO to “examine the need for harmonized, risk-based guidance on the use of AI in aviation, including common terminology and meaningful human oversight.”

The rapid development of AI and its unknown effects on aviation operations is a topic Member States will need to monitor. ICAO will coordinate and facilitate international exchange, while States will oversee implementation to ensure that the technologies adopted are secure, interoperable and consistent with cybersecurity and data-protection objectives.

ICAO will handle AI within its existing structure, as the proposal to create an ad hoc unit was not supported.

During the final two days of the Conference, ICAO held an Extraordinary Session of its Assembly to elect the four additional Council members required by the entry into force of the 2013 protocol amending the Convention on International Civil Aviation. The Council is the Organization’s permanent governing body, accountable to the Assembly and responsible for setting priorities and implementing its directions. Its membership increased from 36 to 40, and there are no permanent seats.

Six candidates competed for the four new seats: Ethiopia, Indonesia, Kazakhstan, Oman, Thailand and Türkiye. In the vote on the final day, Kazakhstan and Thailand were not elected, and the other four States won seats.

Never put the backup server in the same Windows domain as production

Welcome, once again, to PWNED, where we’re thankful every time someone leaves a password sticky note on a monitor or fails to delete a terminated employee’s admin account. But neither of those common occurrences is what we’ll be talking about today. Instead, our subject broke the number-one rule of backups: you gotta keep ’em separated.

Our story comes courtesy of Da Li, who serves as global alliance manager at Info2Soft, a company that provides data protection and recovery software. At one point, a partner called him into a case where a Chinese vehicle parts manufacturer fell victim to a ransomware gang. Unfortunately, its backup was compromised too.

The manufacturer had religiously backed up its manufacturing execution system (MES) and SAP orders, inventory, and finance systems every night. There was even a green dashboard that showed the backup process had succeeded, and someone checked that every morning.

However, one Friday evening, crims broke into the network and found out that the backup server was joined to the same Windows domain as the rest of the network. The hackers found a plaintext password for the backup service account in a maintenance script on a file share and discovered that someone had used the very same password for a domain admin account.

So the first thing the crooks did was delete every restore point on that backup server. Then they started encrypting the production servers to ransom them.

“Every backup job had succeeded for three years,” Li said. “The failure was that the thing meant to save them could be reached with the same keys as the thing being attacked.”

"The time to prepare for a cryptographic breakthrough is before it happens," the team wrote.

While practical breaking of the pairing-based cryptography used by Sapling remains highly speculative, Zakura also recommended a network upgrade to disable payments into Sapling. Roughly 400,000 ZEC, or approximately 2% of supply, remains in the pool, according to Zakura, worth roughly $291 million at current prices. An earlier Zcash forum proposal in April had suggested waiting a year after a community decision to make Sapling withdrawal-only. At the time, the proposal put its balance at 600,000 ZEC, down from 1.1 million at the start of 2024. Zakura did not suggest a new deadline in its Thursday post.

Zakura pointed to a reported proof of the Riemann hypothesis, which concerns the distribution of prime numbers, and unsubstantiated rumors that AI labs had also made advances in integer factorization, the process of splitting large numbers into their prime factors. "Practical pairing breaks are highly speculative," Zakura said, adding that transparent transactions and Ironwood do not rely on factoring or pairings.

Nevertheless, a hypothetical break in Sapling's pairing-based proof system would allow forged proofs, Zakura said, putting the funds in the pool at risk. The same cryptography does not secure users' private keys or their cryptographic records of shielded funds, so the team said developers could replace the proof system without replacing those records. However, Zcash would have to freeze Sapling before an attack compromises its state and users could then later recover their funds using a new ECDLP-based or hash-based SNARK, Zakura said.

Vitalik Buterin is taking Ethereum’s decentralization philosophy down to the silicon level. It could have implications far beyond Ethereum, and computing hardware shouldn’t remain a proprietary black box.

Could Ethereum eventually move beyond blockchain infrastructure and build its own hardware ecosystem? Vitalik’s latest exploration makes that prospect an interesting thought experiment, particularly as Nvidia dominates the computing industry.

Vitalik’s argument starts with a familiar problem in crypto: trust. Software can be audited, code can be published, and blockchain transactions can be independently verified. Hardware, however, remains considerably harder to inspect.

A chip can contain hidden vulnerabilities or malicious modifications that users cannot easily detect. Even a secure wallet application cannot guarantee safety if the hardware underneath it has been compromised. This has happened recently with Ledger, and Ledger is not the only one, with Trezor and other hardware wallets being recently compromised.

“Not your silicon, not your keys.” – Vitalik put the problem bluntly.

The phrase extends crypto’s self-custody mantra into the physical computing layer. Owning private keys might not mean much if users cannot trust the hardware handling them.

This is why Vitalik argues that “the hardware layer must be open for participation and tinkering too.” He wants hardware designs to become accessible to developers, researchers, and independent auditors, allowing them to examine and modify components rather than blindly trusting manufacturers.

This could eventually create opportunities for Ethereum hardware focused on security, cryptographic computing, and decentralized infrastructure. Ethereum validators, hardware wallets, and zero-knowledge proof systems all depend on computing components that users need to trust.

Nvidia built its dominance by turning GPUs into the backbone of artificial intelligence. Its hardware, software ecosystem, and CUDA platform have made competition increasingly difficult.

But Vitalik’s vision displays a different approach. Instead of chasing the fastest processors, Ethereum hardware could prioritize openness, verifiability, and security.

Vitalik acknowledges that open-source hardware faces performance limitations. Advanced chip manufacturing remains expensive and tightly controlled, leaving open designs behind the industry’s most sophisticated commercial processors.

Yet that trade-off might not matter for every workload. Vitalik writes that “Secure hardware may be 10x less efficient or more” while still being sufficient for many computing needs. That’s a striking contrast to an industry obsessed with squeezing more performance from increasingly expensive chips.

Specialized silicon designed for cryptographic operations, privacy-preserving computing, secure data processing, or decentralized networks could benefit from open designs. Developers could modify the architecture, researchers could inspect the circuitry, and manufacturers could compete to produce chips from publicly available specifications.

Ethereum already has an extensive developer community experimenting with cryptography and distributed computing. Extending that culture into silicon could give the ecosystem a foothold in hardware without immediately challenging Nvidia’s AI infrastructure.

The ambition could be less about building another GPU giant and more about creating a competing hardware philosophy.

The eminent evolutionary biologist and modeller John Maynard Smith was once asked about Karl Popper and the philosophy of science. His answer was not flattering. His impression was that scientists who take the philosophy of science seriously, and allow their research to be influenced by philosophical preconceptions, are "far more likely to do themselves harm than good". He loved reading philosophy of science and was always ready to argue about it, he added, but he did not believe that one should allow oneself to be influenced by it when actually thinking about science.

This is why I've recently published an academic article on the topic. These are quite radical words, and though Maynard Smith's generalisation is untenable, there is an important insight to be extracted.

If modellers approach the philosophical literature on models with the goal of figuring out what models are and what a good model is, they might easily find themselves lured into naive monistic views of models that fail to represent the full epistemic richness of modelling practices, thereby turning them into worse scientists than they could have otherwise been.

This could happen in several ways. For instance, models with epistemic functions not recognised by narrow monist accounts might be discarded. Consider, for example, models with weak predictive power, but great generality and insight into complex systems.

Perhaps Maynard Smith's worry is most strongly illustrated in economics, where Milton Friedman and Karl Popper are taken to have the "last word" in any methodological debate, and are frequently invoked to both defend and criticise the work of modellers. They are perhaps paradigm cases of monist thinking. Friedman argued that the only relevant desideratum in science concerns predictive power, while Popper argued that what matters is falsifiability.

These accounts of science have received extensive criticism over the last decades. Indeed, the philosopher Alex Rosenberg argued that Friedman's naive picture single-handedly justified the existence of philosophy of economics as a distinct profession. Economists, however, are unlikely to read much if any work on methodology beyond Friedman and Popper. As the economist Dani Rodrik notes, economic methodology is relegated to specialised journals and, more importantly, is not really being taught in graduate school.

The result is that economists seem to believe that their science operates according to a simple form of falsificationism, a picture of science that, most contemporary philosophers of science would agree, both misrepresents scientific practice and provides the wrong ideal of how science ought to function.

The obvious counter to Maynard Smith's concern is that it is not an argument against the philosophy of science, but rather against bad philosophy of science, which limits, rather than fosters, insights into the richness of the epistemic tools available to scientists.

Philosophers are not innocent here. Some debates in the philosophy of science rest on such monist motivations to seek univocal accounts of laws, explanation, species, functions, preferences, and many more. As Peter Godfrey-Smith emphasised, one of the hazards of philosophy is the temptation to come up with theories that are "too broad and sweeping". This hazard has not been avoided in the philosophical literature on models. Many philosophers of science seem to be under the impression that once we figure out what "models" are and how they work, we can finally get a handle on all the other pressing questions in the philosophy of science. I argue that this way of thinking about models is grounded in a mistake.

A philosophy of science worth reading takes the plurality, diversity, and richness of models and modelling practices seriously. It does not hand scientists a single criterion for good science.

Economists, Rodrik argues, need a better story about the kind of science they practise, and some economists have recently engaged with philosophical questions themselves, seeking contact with philosophers of economics in the process. Philosophers of science, in turn, should turn more of their attention toward both the social sciences and the philosophy of the social sciences. Their lack of engagement with this rich literature is a historical omission that may have provided a misguided understanding of how science works.

Should scientists ignore philosophers? They should ignore those who tell them that there is only one way of doing science well.

Is Micron Stock a Buy After HPE Said Memory Will Stay Scarce Into 2028?

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A big memory buyer and a bearish bank are now at least a year apart on when the shortage ends.

By Daniel Sparks – Nov 26, 2026 at 8:17AM EST

10 stocks we like better than Micron Technology ›

Hewlett Packard Enterprise (HPE +1.57%) told investors on Nov. 23 that it still can't get all the memory chips it wants. And it doesn't think that'll change soon.

"We expect supply of memory, NAND, and many other critical components to remain constrained to at least the second half of calendar 2028," CEO Antonio Neri said on the company's earnings call that evening.

That seems like good news for Micron Technology (MU -4.53%), which makes DRAM (the memory computers use while they work) and NAND flash storage chips. But the memory maker's shares slid anyway. Micron closed at $707.70 on Nov. 23, just before the call. As of this writing on Nov. 25, the stock traded near $550, less than half the $1,213.56 it closed at on June 25, its record.

Meanwhile, BNP Paribas downgraded the shares, saying memory supply might catch up to demand a year sooner than it had thought.

The tech firm said in March that DRAM and NAND make up over half the cost of the parts in a traditional server. And its inventory more than doubled in fiscal 2026, from around $6.4 billion on Oct. 31, 2025, to $11.8 billion by July 31 and $14.1 billion by Oct. 31, 2026.

HPE's timeline seems to have stretched, too. In March, Neri said he expected high DRAM and NAND prices to "persist well into 2027."

CFO Marie Myers said the $14.1 billion inventory reflected higher costs for commodity parts like memory, purchases for customer orders, and what she called "strategic buys." She added that HPE's commodity costs climbed by significant double-digit percentages in 2026, and she expects smaller increases ahead. But smaller increases are still increases, not the price drops a glut would cause.

BNP Paribas thinks one could. In a note reported on Nov. 24, it cut its rating on Micron to neutral from outperform. It now says DRAM and NAND supply and demand might approach balance starting in mid-2027 and drive average selling prices down. It flagged risks to big cloud companies' data center plans for 2028 and new factory capacity coming online.

Micron's stock dropped 11% that day, and again on Nov. 25, when research firm TrendForce said NAND flash might move into oversupply in 2027. TrendForce still expects DRAM to stay tight through 2027, and DRAM made up about three-quarters of Micron's revenue in fiscal 2026.

Part of the new factory capacity BNP mentioned is Micron's own. Its first new fab in Idaho should start making wafers in mid-2027, and a facility in Taiwan should begin meaningful shipments around then too. But Micron says output at a new fab ramps up over a few quarters. And its other planned fabs, including a second one in Idaho and a new NAND factory in Singapore, aren't set to start output until the second half of 2028 or later.

"[W]e do not have line of sight to when supply and demand will return to balance," management wrote about DRAM in Micron's prepared remarks for its Sept. 30 earnings call.

HPE's 2028 date matches Micron's building schedule. BNP's earlier date seems to rely more on demand weakening.

Micron's fiscal 2028 ends on Aug. 31, 2028, so HPE's outlook covers nearly all of the year. If the outlook holds, Micron could spend most of fiscal 2028 selling into a market still short on chips. If BNP is right, prices could be dropping for most of it.

The stock price looks closer to the second case.

At roughly $550, Micron trades at a price-to-earnings ratio of about 7.4 on its fiscal 2026 results. Based on the average analyst estimate for fiscal 2028, the price is below 3 times earnings.

Even BNP sees Micron's earnings per share bottoming at $86 in 2029, over the $74.33 per share Micron earned in fiscal 2026 under generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP). The stock looks cheap even on that bearish estimate.

One buyer's view could be wrong, of course. But I think HPE's outlook undercuts the glut worries, especially for DRAM, Micron's largest business. A buyer still stocking up at higher prices is tough to square with a stock priced for falling memory prices by mid-2027.

I'd consider buying some Micron shares here, in a position small enough to ride out more days like Nov. 24. If HPE starts pulling that 2028 date forward, like BNP just did with its own, I'd rethink that.

UK public sector websites are directing visitors to gambling promotions through old links to defunct local enterprise partnerships (LEPs), research by digital consultancy Digital Royalty has found.

The consultancy identified 93 links across 79 pages on websites belonging to 26 public bodies.

LEPs brought together councils and businesses to promote economic growth in England under a coalition government initiative launched in 2010. Central government funding ended in April 2024, with their core functions transferred to local and combined authorities.

Among other things, Digital Royalty builds and looks after websites, which means chasing down old links. In August, it found that a link on Surrey County Council's website led to a former LEP address now displaying casino content. It went looking for more.

Digital Royalty founder Alex De Sousa said: "Slow is usually a sign of caution and diligence, but 31 months after the LEPs wound up, it's crossed into inefficient and dangerous."

The consultancy offered The Register four examples of websites run by the Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport (DCMS), Hull City Council, Parliament, and the Local Government Association. One example is a government page about the Digital Skills Partnership, originally published under an earlier incarnation of DCMS.

The page includes a link that appears to be for the Humber Digital Skills Partnership within the Humber LEP website, but in fact takes users to a site advertising "Independent Non GamStop Casinos UK – New Casino Sites Outside GamStop." GamStop is the self-exclusion scheme designed to help people restrict their online gambling, which makes the links more concerning than ordinary website clutter.

The same link appears on the Hull City Council website under a list of Joint Committees, Partnerships and Trusts where it appears as Local Economic Partnership (Humber LEP).

An old page for the Parliament website detailing the Department for Business, Energy & Industrial Strategy committee's Industrial Strategy report includes a footnote (135) that appears to be for the Business Central funding finder service, but takes the user to the "Highest RTP Non GamStop Casinos for UK Players."

The Local Government Association's (LGA) sustainability and waste route map included a link to the former York and North Yorkshire LEP website. The destination initially appeared legitimate but contained links promoting crypto casinos. The LGA now appears to have removed the link.

All the organizations mentioned have been contacted by The Register to offer them the opportunity to comment.

Digital Royalty checked former web addresses associated with about 70 LEPs and growth hubs, then used a backlink index to identify roughly 3,000 incoming links from public sector sites. Most were simply outdated rather than leading to gambling content.

8 Modern Thriller Books To Read Before Kicking the Bucket

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

There's plenty of adrenaline to be found in modern thriller books. With anxiety, crime, and social tensions seemingly at an all-time high, these novels keep readers up past midnight, tapping into some of society's biggest fears. Whether it’s fractured relationships or institutional corruption, thriller books translate these tensions into pages.

Whether you're into psychological mind games or constant plot twists, there's something for every kind of thriller fan. From an international murder conspiracy to a desperate attempt to prove someone's loyalty to both sides of a war, here are modern thriller books to read before kicking the bucket.

Shutter Island is proof that books can be better than their movie adaptations. Dennis Lehane's novel follows U.S. Marshal Teddy Daniels and his new partner, Chuck Aule, who are sent to a remote island to investigate a patient's disappearance from a hospital for the criminally insane. The staff's strange behavior makes their investigation anything but routine, and a storm soon leaves them with no way off the island.

Unlike the movie, the novel allows Teddy's descent into madness to progress more organically. The limited point of view makes his increasingly questionable thoughts and assumptions feel convincing, and that's all thanks to Lehane's ability to hide Teddy's real experiences with eloquent prose. With the hospital staff constantly making him question his own judgment, readers are left just as uncertain as Teddy about what is real and what isn't.

Viet Thanh Nguyen's The Sympathizer explores the Vietnam War through the eyes of someone living in a constant state of in-betweens. A half-Vietnamese, half-French communist spy, the novel's unnamed narrator is forced to flee Saigon for Los Angeles. Despite having to rebuild his life in a new country, he secretly keeps tabs on South Vietnamese military officials living in exile.

It's a never-ending losing game in The Sympathizer, with the narrator unable to please either side without betraying the other. He is constantly caught between his political beliefs, personal identity, and the need to stay alive. No matter where he goes, he cannot simply leave his past behind. The Sympathizer explores this constant duality between two identities, two loyalties, and two countries, showing just how difficult it is to start over when his own allegiances keep pulling him in opposite directions.

BookTok sensations do not always deserve their stellar reputation, but Alex Michaelides' The Silent Patient is a cautionary tale of what it means to be a silenced woman, quite literally. Alicia Berenson seems to have the perfect life as a successful painter married to Gabriel, until she shoots him five times. After his death, she stops speaking and is admitted to a psychiatric facility. Six years later, psychotherapist Theo Faber takes on her case.

Michaelides complicates the story by bringing in two near-convincing perspectives. Theo's investigation and Alicia's diary entries reveal different sides of the events surrounding Gabriel's murder, though neither account can be trusted entirely. With nobody telling the whole truth, readers must decide whose version to believe: the accused murderer, or a man convinced he has her figured out.

Lucy Foley's The Guest List is essentially Agatha Christie meets Knives Out. Magazine publisher Jules Keegan and television personality Will Slater gather their handpicked guests for a glamorous wedding on a remote Irish island. But the celebrations take a grim turn when a storm cuts off the island from the mainland. Before the couple can even say their "I dos," Will is found stabbed to death, leaving everyone a potential suspect.

Unlike most murder mysteries, The Guest List doesn't exactly make readers feel sorry for the victim. The guests' motives trace back to an incident from years earlier, one that could ruin Will's reputation (before he died). There's something almost satisfyingly karmic about his tragic fate. But then again, no one wants to be on the same stranded island as an unknown killer.

Toni Collette and Kit Harington are shooting a new Miss Marple and 20th Century has several more Christies in development, so the drawing room is busier than it has been in decades. Here is which of them you are, and the case for yours being the best.

Order, method, and a quiet confidence that you are the cleverest person present. Poirot is a retired Belgian police officer who solves murders by sitting still and thinking, and who is vain enough to make the reveal a performance every single time.

People tell you things because they cannot imagine you being a threat. Marple solves murders by comparing everybody to somebody she once knew in St Mary Mead, which sounds like a joke until you notice it works every time, because human beings are not as original as they think.

You have far too many ideas and roughly a fifth of them are excellent. Ariadne Oliver is a successful crime novelist who is sick of her famous foreign detective and keeps saying so, which is Christie writing about Christie and getting away with it completely.

You are steady, decent and the reason the difficult genius has anybody at all. Hastings narrates the early Poirot novels, misses most of the clues, and is the only character in the books that Poirot is unambiguously fond of, which tells you what he is really for.

Anyone who enjoyed Stephen King's Misery should give Taylor Adams' novel a read. No Exit follows college student Darby Thorne, who gets stranded at an isolated highway rest stop during a Colorado blizzard. With four strangers for company, Darby is horrified to discover a kidnapped nine-year-old girl locked inside a van outside. If you had to guess which used pickup found a buyer fastest in October, the Toyota Tacoma would be a sensible bet. It’s America’s best-selling midsize truck and has a reputation for holding its value. Or you might pick the super frugal Ford Maverick Hybrid. But the truck that beat them both was the Tesla Cybertruck.

The data from the new iSeeCars study covers 1.1 million used vehicles that changed hands in October, so this was before Tesla announced it would discontinue the Cybertruck in 2027. The study looked at one- to five-year-old vehicles and ranked models by how long they’d been listed before selling.

The Cybertruck surpassed all other pickups, averaging 39.1 days, compared to 65.5 days for used pickups overall. The Maverick Hybrid came closest at 43.3 days, followed by the Rivian R1T at 45.2 and the F-150 Lightning at 47.4. The Tacoma came in fifth position.

The gap between the two electric pickups at either end of the list was even more striking. A used Chevrolet Silverado EV took 107.5 days to sell, putting it last among the 24 trucks in the study. That’s a 68-day difference between two American electric pickups. Across the wider used market, EVs averaged about 46 days to find a buyer, around nine fewer than hybrids and 15 fewer than gas vehicles.

How quickly a used Cybertruck sold seems to have changed a lot since May 2024, when some sellers were cutting as much as $30,000 from their asking prices after listing their trucks for close to $200,000. By December of that year, we reported that used Cybertrucks were piling up on dealer lots, with data at the time showing they stayed for 75 days, up from 27 in May.

Then owners got a look at what Tesla itself would pay for a used Cybertruck trade-in. One year later, in May 2025, one owner of a $99,990 dual-motor Foundation Series truck got a trade-in estimate of $65,400 after driving it just 6,211 miles.

So even though the Cybertruck was the fastest-selling used pickup in iSeeCars’ new study, it didn’t report what buyers paid, so we don’t know whether owners got their asking price or had to lower it to sell.

Tesla will build the last Cybertrucks at the end of summer 2027, and this will reshape their used market. Elon Musk originally announced production would end around the middle of the year, but then extended the run to fill more orders. And if you order a Cybertruck now, the only one you can get is the $110,000 tri-motor Cyberbeast, so anyone who wants a less expensive dual-motor truck has to look for a used example.

Once the last new Cybertruck is sold, I’d expect collectors and everyday truck buyers to look at used ones differently. Someone after a clean Foundation Series truck with low miles might pay a little extra for one close to home, especially since it could be months before another comes up for sale nearby.

At the same time, someone who just needs an electric pickup to tow a trailer has more options. If a used dual-motor Cybertruck costs about the same as a Ford F-150 Lightning or a Rivian R1T, I suspect that buyer will care more about mileage, condition, and how much battery warranty is left than when Tesla built its last truck.

Anthropic (ANT +1.08%) just completed the largest IPO in history, raising around $93.5 billion at $83 per share. The stock closed Nov. 20 at $88.91.

Given that it's now one of the largest publicly traded companies in the world, you might assume its addition to the S&P 500 (^GSPC -0.25%) is imminent. It isn't, actually, and won't be for a while. But the stock will show up in several other major indexes within days.

When Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX +1.30%) debuted earlier this year, there was a lot of discussion around how the major indexes would handle it. Typically, companies go public at much smaller market caps, not with market caps over $1 trillion. Index committees needed to decide whether to "fast track" these mega IPOs into the indexes sooner, given their outsize influence.

Several providers made changes. But Standard & Poor's did not. It elected to keep the existing 12-month waiting period and generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP) income requirements -- companies need to be profitable in the most recent quarter as well as the sum of the past four quarters in order to qualify.

Anthropic, so far, falls far short on the GAAP income requirement, reporting a net loss of roughly $104 billion in the first nine months of this year.

Given these requirements, the earliest we're likely to see Anthropic in the S&P 500 is late 2027.

The S&P 500 will actually be the exception, not the rule, when it comes to adding Anthropic.

The Nasdaq-100 index updated its inclusion criteria as a result of the SpaceX IPO. Now, if a company's entire market capitalization ranks within the top 40 current constituents in the index, it can be added to the index after just 15 trading days. Since Anthropic is listed on the Nasdaq, it could show up in the Invesco QQQ ETF (QQQ -0.65%) before the end of the year.

The Morningstar indexes, which acquired the widely-used CRSP indexes, could add Anthropic after the close of the fifth trading day. The Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF (VTI -0.18%) would be one of the biggest funds impacted by this. FTSE Russell works on a similar timeline.

A lot of index funds will weight stocks according to the number of publicly available shares, not necessarily the total market cap. Since only about 5% of Anthropic's total market cap is now traded publicly, its starting weight will likely be small. Vanguard has projected only a 0.055% to 0.15% weighting for Anthropic in the Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF.

When and if additional shares become publicly available, the anticipated weighting of these stocks would likely grow along with it.

If you really want to own Anthropic, you should probably just buy Anthropic.

The stock won't show up at all in the S&P 500 for the foreseeable future. For many of the major large-cap indexes and corresponding ETFs, the initial weight probably won't get much above 0.25%. The exception might be the Nasdaq-100, the Invesco QQQ ETF, and the Invesco Nasdaq 100 ETF (QQQM -0.66%). They could see an allocation closer to 1% given the more concentrated nature of that index.

It'll likely be a similar story to what we saw when SpaceX went public: a lot of hype but minimal impact to the ETF marketplace and your portfolio in general.

Long-term investors almost certainly shouldn't be making big asset allocation changes based on this. Even if you want to overweight tech based on a belief that Anthropic might spark the sector, your tech ETFs aren't going to meaningfully change in the future.

Three Ancient Cosmic Collisions May Explain Why the Moon Has Two Faces

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists have narrowed down when three massive collisions reshaped the Moon, bringing them closer to understanding why its Earth-facing side looks so different from its far side.

Billions of years ago, a collision powerful enough to melt enormous quantities of lunar rock created the Moon’s largest impact basin. Subsequent collisions broke apart and scattered some of that ancient material across the surface, leaving behind a geological record that scientists are now piecing together.

Located on the Moon’s far side, the South Pole-Aitken (SPA) basin is one of the oldest and largest impact structures in the solar system. Understanding its history could help explain why the Moon’s near and far sides are so different, what lies beneath its surface, and when major collisions occurred during its early evolution.

An international research team led by Professor Guochun Zhao, Mok Sau-King, a professor in the Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences (DEPS) at the University of Hong Kong (HKU), and Dr. Yuqi Qian, the study’s first author, has identified two types of magnesium-rich rocks with remarkably different origins. Published in Science Advances, the research also establishes a clearer timeline of the major impacts that shaped the region.

For decades, the basin remained largely unexplored. That changed in 2019, when Chang’e-4 completed the first successful landing on the Moon’s far side, followed in 2024 by Chang’e-6, which brought the first rock samples from this largely unexplored region back to Earth.

Using Chang’e-6 samples allocated to HKU DEPS and surface measurements from both missions, the researchers obtained the first direct geological evidence from the two landing sites for reconstructing the basin’s impact history. Satellite observations and numerical simulations helped them trace the origins of rocks across the wider basin.

One group of rocks, known as moderately magnesium-rich norites, formed in the aftermath of the original SPA collision. The impact generated a thick sheet of molten rock that gradually cooled, with different minerals crystallizing at different stages. Later collisions exposed these ancient rocks at the central peaks of large craters. Some were eventually blasted toward the Chang’e-4 landing site in Von Kármán crater by the impact that formed Finsen crater.

The second group contained considerably more magnesium and likely formed through a separate process called magnesium-suite magmatism, a type of magma activity associated with the original SPA impact. These rocks were exposed along the western ring of mountain peaks within the Apollo basin before the Chaffee S impact scattered some of them toward the Chang’e-6 landing site.

Earlier research by the HKU-led team had already uncovered evidence of prolonged volcanic activity and previously unrecognized magma activity in the region. Those studies also suggested that underground accumulations of titanium-rich minerals, known as ilmenite-bearing cumulates, helped account for the contrasting volcanic histories of the Moon’s two sides.

The latest findings suggest that the South Pole-Aitken basin formed approximately 4.34 billion years ago. Another major collision created the Apollo basin around 4.2 billion years ago, followed by the formation of the Orientale basin approximately 3.87 billion years ago. Together, these three events reveal how successive collisions reshaped the Moon’s largest impact basin over hundreds of millions of years.
