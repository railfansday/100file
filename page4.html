Lincoln City are on the hunt for a third managerial figure this calendar year following the midweek decision to part ways with joint-managers, Chris Cohen and Tom Shaw.

The duo led the Imps into their first Championship season since 1961 following Michael Skubala's summer exit to divisional rivals, Bristol City.

Lincoln coasted to last season's League One title, accumulating a staggering 103 points whilst also embarking on a 29-match unbeaten run.

But as the final month of 2026 nears, they are the lowest-placed out of the three sides who earned promotion last term, having taken just 15 points from their first 17 matches.

It remains to be seen which direction is taken by owner, Ron Fowler at the ProAmpac Stadium, but it has emerged that a previous target, now working with another Championship club, has entered the managerial frame.

Lincoln are currently outside the relegation zone by three points, with Tuesday's heavy 4-0 defeat to Charlton Athletic at The Valley, coupled with the manner of their side's performance, spelt the end for Cohen and Shaw.

The Imps have won just one of their last six Championship matches, with it now emerging through Alan Nixon - via Patreon - that Gavin Strachan has been listed as a potential candidate.

Strachan, 47, is currently on the coaching staff at West Bromwich Albion under James Morrison, with the Baggies currently within three points of the Championship summit after a 3-1 win at Bolton Wanderers.

Prior to the start of the new season, the Aberdeen-born man spent six years at Celtic working under the likes of Brendan Rodgers, Ange Postecoglou and Martin O'Neill, with the Hoops winning the last five Scottish Premiership titles on offer.

Following on from Postecoglou's exit to Tottenham Hotspur three years ago, the Australian wished to take Strachan with him to North London, but a deal could not be agreed.

The aforementioned Morrison was appointed as permanent head coach of West Brom this summer after ensuring the Black Country side's survival in difficult circumstances.

As well as retaining Matt Gill, who had been linked with a switch to Leicester City, the former Scotland international midfielder opted to install Strachan and Tony McMahon as part of a new-look coaching setup.

Given how highly Strachan is rated on the coaching circuit, Morrison's initial words following the decision read: "I’m also delighted to add Gavin Strachan to the group.

"Having a new pair of eyes from somebody with Gavin’s experience will help us massively. He has worked alongside some top managers, like Martin O’Neill, Ange Postecoglou and Brendan Rodgers, and will no doubt bring fresh ideas with him."

Prior to his spell with Celtic, Strachan also assisted Darren Ferguson in two spells at Peterborough United, as well as his stint with Doncaster Rovers.

Lisa Marei Schmidt appointed to lead Kunstmuseum Bern and Zentrum Paul Klee

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Lisa Marei Schmidt has worked at other institutions including Kunstsammlung Nordrhein-Westfalen in Düsseldorf and Berlin's Hamburger Bahnhof

Photo David von Becker/Kunstmuseum Bern – Zentrum Paul Klee

Lisa Marei Schmidt has been appointed as the new director of two major museums in Bern: Kunstmuseum Bern and Zentrum Paul Klee. The board of trustees of the foundation that oversees both institutions selected Schmidt from 97 applicants following an international search, according to a release. She will lead on artistic direction and also chair the museums' management board.

Schmidt has directed the Brücke-Museum in Berlin, home to the world’s largest collection of works by artists from the German Expressionist group Die Brücke, since 2017. During that time she has co-curated exhibitions exploring complex and sometimes dark aspects of the history of German Expressionism, such as Escape into Art? The Brücke Painters in the Nazi Period (2019) and Whose Expression? The Brücke Artists and Colonialism (2021–22). Under her direction, the museum has also strengthened its work in provenance research, restoration, art education and digitisation. The German Association of Art Critics (AICA) named the Brücke-Museum as its museum of the year for 2023.

Born in 1978, Schmidt studied art history in Marburg, Amsterdam and Berlin, and curating contemporary art at the Royal College of Art in London. Before joining the Brücke-Museum, she worked as a research trainee at Kunstsammlung Nordrhein-Westfalen and served as a curator at Berlin's Hamburger Bahnhof from 2012 to 2017. In 2020, she curated Bonzo's Dream by Swiss artist Vivian Suter, which received AICA's Special Exhibition award.

“To me, museums are places where art and people meet,” Schmidt said in a statement. "They provide space for encounters, new perspectives and dialogue across society." She said she looked forward to getting to know the teams in Bern and building on the distinctive qualities of the two museums, which have shared strategic leadership under the umbrella foundation Kunstmuseum Bern – Zentrum Paul Klee since 2015.

Schmidt will also inherit a pressing challenge at Kunstmuseum Bern. In June, voters in the canton rejected a CHF15.7m (£14.4m) planning credit, or budget allocation, for a project to renovate the museum's historic building and replace its 1983 extension, with 51.8% voting against it. The museum says its ageing buildings still urgently need repairs.

Schmidt succeeds Nina Zimmer, who led the institutions for a decade and will become director-general of Vienna's Belvedere in January 2027. The foundation’s commercial director Thomas Soraperra will serve as interim director until Schmidt arrives.

How Man United's data gurus got Leny Yoro off the hook: Stats analysis from ex-Formula One boffin proved Alexander Isak had NO chance of reaching the ball before red card - in appeal win that has left refs fuming

📅 2026-11-26  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Published: 07:49 EST, 26 November 2026 | Updated: 07:52 EST, 26 November 2026

Manchester United turned to its data gurus in a bid to categorically prove Alexander Isak had no chance of reaching the ball as part of its evidence to overturn Leny Yoro’s red card.

Yoro was sent off eight minutes into United’s trip to Anfield for a last-man foul on Isak, which referee Sam Barrott determined fit the criteria for Denial Of A Goalscoring Opportunity (DOGSO).

The young United centre-back was shown a straight red card after VAR doubled down on the day, insisting that no ‘clear and obvious error’ had been made.

United successfully appealed the decision through an independent panel and the lengths they went to for their evidence submission has now come to light.

Bosses at United included three high-definition video clips of the incident in question, which used multiple different angles as well as speeds in slow-motion and in real time for the panel to consider.

United also submitted video evidence of a DOGSO sending off earlier this season for Brighton’s Pascal Struijk against Sunderland, which was also overturned on appeal.

Leny Yoro was sent off for Man United against Liverpool but the red card was later rescinded

United showed that Isak would not have reached the ball if he'd have stayed on his feet

The most fascinating element of United’s detailed evidence pack came via its data department, led by ex-Formula One guru Michael Sansoni.

United outlined in a letter to the appeal panel that a technical analysis of the incident saw boffins at Carrington calculate the probability of Isak reaching the ball following his heavy touch prior to contact from Yoro.

The data allowed for the possibility that Isak maintained his speed and direction from the moment his legs first made contact with Yoro to assess whether he would have got to the ball before United goalkeeper Senne Lammens.

United’s analysis concluded that Isak would have been 1.8 metres shy of reaching the ball even if all optimal speed and directional factors had been achieved.

The appeals commission, however, told United that that data, however detailed, was not verifiable and so had to be treated with ‘caution’.

Considering all the elements of United’s submission, the panel unanimously agreed that it was not a red card offence and did not meet the threshold for DOGSO, immediately overturning the decision and withdrawing all punishment against Yoro.

‘We can accept referee can make a mistake. But we have VAR. What were they doing?’ a fuming Mauricio Pochettino said on Tuesday of the original Yoro decision.

Not Again: Cargo Jet Overruns Miami Airport Runway (This Time A FedEx MD-11)

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Miami International Airport (MIA) is having a bad luck streak when it comes to cargo planes being able to stay on the runway, but at least this time around there were no fatalities. Still, yikes…

At 8:25PM on Wednesday, November 25, 2026, a FedEx McDonnell Douglas MD-11 overran the runway at Miami Airport, leading the airport to be shut down for an extended period of time.

The 34-year-old MD-11 with the registration code N605FE had just performed flight FX910 from Memphis (MEM), and was coming in to land on runway 26R, which is 9,355 feet (2,851 meters) long. That’s a solidly long runway, for what it’s worth.

However, the plane wasn’t able to stop before the end of the runway, and seemed to exit the paved surface at a speed of around 50 knots, and came to a stop around 320 meters (1,000 feet) past the end of the runway, right by the airport perimeter fence.

There’s always the classic aviation adage that “any landing you can walk away from is a good one,” and if that’s the case, I guess this was a good landing.

Of course the concept of a plane overrunning a runway at Miami Airport is fresh on people’s minds, given the September 6 accident we saw with a 21 Air (Amazon Air) Boeing 767-300 that overran runway 26R. Tragically that accident claimed five lives, as the plane ran through the perimeter fence, and killed motorists. What we’ve found out about the piloting of that flight since the accident, and just how recklessly the captain was flying the plane, is astounding.

We’ll have to wait for a full investigation to know what happened (the NTSB typically releases a preliminary report within a month), but in terms of circumstances and possible contributing factors, there are a couple of things we know.

First, there’s footage showing the plane’s approach, and it makes it pretty clear that the plane touched down extremely late.

It’s also worth pointing out that weather in the region was really bad at the time of the accident. Anna Giaritelli, a reputable aviation journalist with good sources, quotes a Miami air traffic controller, who told her that “this was absolutely the most dangerous operation I’ve seen,” as much of the traffic approaching Miami had run into a “black hole of weather.”

It doesn’t seem like conditions at Miami Airport as such were that awful (compared to reports of severe turbulence in the region), but I imagine the bad weather could’ve contributed to a sense of “get-there-itis” for the pilots.

While this is of course unfortunate, the great news here is the lack of injuries or fatalities. The plane ended up coming to a stop just feet from the airport’s perimeter fence, and if it had crashed through that, the outcome could’ve been completely different, with fatalities among motorists.

It also has to be acknowledged that the pilots seemingly turned the plane a little bit left off the end of the runway, to avoid the localizer antenna. So at least the overrun was handled as well as it could be.

As 81.5 million Americans travel at least 50 miles from home for Thanksgiving, conversations around the dinner table could present a rare opportunity for people to learn more about their aging loved ones. A new class of AI startups wants to help them preserve those conversations forever.

Charlie Greene, the cofounder and CEO of Remento, first learned about the importance of recording memories when his father, Don Greene, died during the hijacking of United Airlines Flight 93 on 9/11.

A 10-year-old boy at the time of the terrorist attack, which resulted in his father’s plane crashing in Shanksville, Penn., the now 34-year-old Greene mostly has a collection of old home videos to remember him by.

So when his mother Claudette, 74, was diagnosed with stage-three lung cancer, he quickly acted to record her memories. With the intention of conducting an oral history interview, Greene searched “questions to ask a parent,” which Google morbidly autocompleted with “before they die.”

When he started asking her the questions like “How did you get to elementary school as a kid,” she lit up, surprised he was interested at all.

“The thing that blew me away about that experience was how unmorbid it felt,” he said.

Claudette is in remission, but her battle with cancer still left Greene “unhealthily fixated” on the idea of how to best preserve loved ones’ memories. In October 2023, he launched Remento, which sends older “storytellers” a weekly text or email reminder prompting them to respond to photos or questions chosen in advance by their family members, such as, “What was the hardest thing you had to overcome as a child?”

After gathering a maximum of a year’s worth of responses, the AI converts the raw transcript into a naturally flowing narrative that goes into a physical book complete with images and QR codes linking the text to the original recordings.

Greene told Fortune Thanksgiving presents a great opportunity for people to discover new memories or learn new twists on well worn stories from their relatives.

“One of the things I want for my daughter is the chance to sit next to her grandmother and hear her say ‘Believe it or not that’s me.’ Thanksgiving is a pretty good excuse to pull an old album off the shelf and make room for the two of them on the couch,” he said.

Last year, Greene secured a $300,000 investment from billionaire Mark Cuban during an episode of Shark Tank in exchange for a 10% stake in his company. He has raised a total of $4.3 million since launch.

Greene said he understands some people’s skepticism of AI, because “A lot of what AI is proposing to the world doesn’t feel actually that good to our lives right now.”

Widespread anxiety about AI replacing human workers as well as concerns over its environmental footprint have contributed to some people’s increasing skepticism toward the technology. A March study of more than 3,000 people by the nonprofit Jobs For the Future found that 38% of respondents said AI was doing more harm than good, while less than half said the technology was a net positive.

Yet Greene hasn’t gotten as much pushback about the company’s use of AI as he had expected because Remento is using the technology to provide its users with something deeply personal, he added.

“Investors want to invest in AI, because it’s an exciting place to be. But I think what we’re experiencing is people have problems and they want them solved, and they want consumer technology to be able to solve those problems,” he said.

Kindred Tales, a rival platform cofounded by Nick Hern and his wife Rebecca Hern, has seen a similar trend with their customers. The company has both AI and non-AI versions of its product.

The message the board is sending is clear. Despite Huang's extramarital affairs, including with an alleged Chinese spy and with one of his subordinates, he is extremely valuable to the company. Just how valuable? The market's reaction to Huang's reinstatement gives us a clue.

Jensen Huang. Image source: Getty Images.

It's no secret that Wall Street loves founder-led businesses, but why is that? There are several reasons. Let's discuss two of them. First, founders, who often own a substantial portion of the company, have a strong incentive to maximize shareholder value. Hired CEOs do too, to some extent. That's why equity-based compensation exists.

However, founders tend to have more skin in the game. They wouldn't just lose their job if their corporation failed, unlike many hired CEOs. They would lose a substantial portion of their wealth. Second, founders often have a much deeper knowledge of the business they created, its characteristics, risks, competitive advantages, etc. That may give them an edge in leading the business to greater heights. According to some research, founder-led companies generally outperform broader equities.

So, Wall Street's faith isn't blind -- it is backed by data. That brings us back to Huang. He helped found Nvidia in 1993 and made it one of the most valuable companies in the world, and the corporation at the center of the ongoing artificial intelligence (AI) revolution. Huang is one of the longest-serving founder-CEOs in corporate America. That counts for a lot

That's why the stock soared about 31%, gaining nearly $1 trillion in a single session, after it was announced that he was coming back. It was the largest single-day market-cap gain in history.

Huang's return reduces the uncertainty tied to Nvidia's CEO search. Before the announcement, the market didn't know whether it would take six weeks or six months, and it was hard to reliably assign probability estimates to each outcome. The market abhors uncertainty, so the news had a major calming effect for many investors. That doesn't mean that Nvidia is out of trouble.

The company still faces major investigations tied to Huang's scandals, and the man is scheduled to testify before Congress soon. Still, with Huang at the helm -- and even accounting for ongoing regulatory probes -- the stock looks attractive. The first sign of that is Nvidia's financial results. In the third quarter of its fiscal year 2027, which ended on Oct. 25, Nvidia crushed expectations.

The company's revenue soared 103% year over year to $115.8 billion, with data center revenue rising 110% compared to the year-ago period. Nvidia's non-GAAP gross margin remains exceptional for a company of this size at 74.9%, versus 73.5% in the year-ago period. Nvidia's non-GAAP earnings per share jumped 111% year over year to $2.68.

The company's AI tailwind is not slowing down much, if at all. It expects $130 billion in revenue for its fiscal year 2027 fourth quarter at the midpoint, representing a year-over-year increase of about 91%. Sure, that's a lower year-over-year revenue growth rate than in its recent third quarter, and so is Nvidia's 2028 fiscal year guidance of 75% year-over-year revenue growth.

But investors should remember two things. First, Nvidia tends to post stronger-than-expected results. It has done so more often than not for the better part of three years. So, a predicted 91% year-over-year growth rate may turn into something above 100% -- in fact, the company's Q3 2027 guidance implied its revenue would increase by about 89.5% compared to the year-ago period.

Further, a decelerating growth rate may indicate tougher year-over-year comparisons rather than slowing demand for the company's products. Nvidia clearly still has a large market to tap into, and with Huang back at the helm, it is as well-positioned as ever to do so. And even after its 31% jump, the stock is down about 36% from the all-time high reached a little over a month ago. At current levels, Nvidia stock remains a buy.

Let’s start with finance. Nearly 25 years after the euro began circulating, one might assume that it is the go-to currency for European Union firms doing business outside the bloc. The data tells a different story: In 2024, EU firms used the euro for only 46 percent of their transactions outside the EU, down just 2 percentage points from 2016, compared to a steady 41 percent invoiced in dollars. Despite long-standing calls from the European Commission for companies to switch to euros, the dollar’s prominence partly reflects its dominance of energy markets; in 2024, EU firms used dollars for 87 percent of their petroleum imports.

Next, look at European financial investments. Since U.S. President Donald Trump embraced tariffs in 2025 and then threatened ttempted to seize Greenland in early 2026, there has been talk in some EU capitals that divesting Europe’s significant holdings of U.S. Treasurys could be a way to pressure Washington. EU-based investment funds and central banks must have missed the memo: Together, they made net purchases of U.S. Treasurys totaling $56 billion between January 2025 and this August. (The data excludes Belgium, Ireland, and Luxembourg, as these countries’ hefty holdings mostly reflect their role as financial hubs.)

Despite Paris’s role as Europe’s vocal champion of strategic autonomy, French investors accounted for 61 percent of these purchases—cementing France’s status as the top EU holder of U.S. Treasurys, with $363 billion in holdings as of August.

EU appetite for U.S. public debt stems from one simple fact: At just $7 trillion, the combined stock of German, French, and EU-issued sovereign bonds is five times smaller than the stock of U.S. Treasurys. Without a greater supply of euro-denominated safe assets, EU funds have little choice but to accumulate U.S. Treasurys. This increases Europe’s exposure to threats from the United States, such as the advice of former Federal Reserve Board of Governors member Stephen Miran to withhold part of the interest that Washington pays on foreign-owned Treasurys.

These days, all eyes are on artificial intelligence, and this sector highlights the extent to which calls for European de-risking are going unanswered in the tech field. In late October, Epoch AI ranked 64 models at or above the level of OpenAI’s GPT-5. Of these, 49 were American, 14 were Chinese, and just one was European—France’s Mistral Large 4. It’s no surprise, then, that American models are becoming the default choice for European firms. According to the German tech industry association Bitkom, 76 percent of German companies using AI rely on OpenAI’s ChatGPT, up from 70 percent last year. Not a single one of the surveyed firms uses Mistral.

U.S. dominance of Western AI is unlikely to end any time soon. Epoch researchers also found that between January and June 2026, U.S.-based firms bought 78 percent of all AI semiconductors powering data centers and supporting the training of new, more powerful models. EU-based companies purchased just 5 percent of these chips during the same period, paving the way for the trans-Atlantic AI gap to widen further.

Current EU investment metrics make it hard to imagine a more positive outcome for European ambitions in other frontier sectors, such as quantum computing and biotechnology. In the field of quantum technology, data from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology shows that only four EU countries (Finland, France, Germany, and the Netherlands) have funding levels worth noting. Yet together, they accounted for just around 10 percent of global financing in 2025.

Meanwhile, the United States captured nearly two-thirds of worldwide funding for quantum technology, while Britain and Canada—both much smaller economies than the EU—are also notable players. In medical biotech, the pattern is the same: U.S. firms secured 63 percent of global venture capital between January 2015 and June 2025, nine times the 7 percent raised by EU firms, according to the European Commission.

The abysmally low levels of funding for quantum computing and biotech suggest that current European concerns about dependence on the United States for AI are only the start of a long line of similar debates. Time and again, EU firms and consumers end up buying critical technology from elsewhere that they did not finance and develop at home. As the United States’ decision in June to cut off global access to Anthropic’s Fable 5 showed, this gives Washington a simple way to deprive Europe of critical technology.

Trade is the final area in which the EU is reliant on the United States. Consider EU exports. Despite pledges to diversify, European firms have become more dependent on the U.S. market over the past decade. U.S. firms bought 21 percent of the EU’s goods exports in 2025, up from 17 percent in 2015. While a rush to stockpile European goods before the start of U.S. tariffs can partly explain the spike in EU exports to the United States last year, it does not change the long-term trend. Nor is this Europe rerouting exports that China no longer buys; at 8 percent in 2025, China’s share of EU goods exports was unchanged from 2015.

Instead, two factors help explain why European exporters have become increasingly reliant on the U.S. market. The first is pharmaceuticals. Half of the 244 billion euro rise in EU exports to the United States between 2015 and 2025 can be attributed to medicines, such as the Irish-manufactured ingredients of Eli Lilly’s blockbuster obesity drug Mounjaro. In 2025, the United States absorbed 43 percent of EU exports of drugs and medical ingredients, up from 28 percent in 2015. Booming pharmaceutical exports are good news for Europe, but shipping nearly half of them to the United States gives Washington a clear target.

The second factor is that the EU’s pledge to diversify trade ties toward middle powers is materializing only slowly. The share of EU goods exports going to 10 major middle powers, including India, Indonesia, and Turkey, remained unchanged between 2015 and 2025, at only 15 percent.

In terms of imports, the EU’s reliance on the United States is growing, too—particularly in energy and intellectual property (IP). Increased dependence on U.S. energy following Europe’s shift away from Russian supplies is familiar by now: U.S. firms now supply almost two-thirds of EU imports of liquefied natural gas, up from zero in 2015. The lesser-known story has to do with IP. Contrary to Britain, Japan, and the United States, the EU is a net importer of IP, such as software, patents, or trademarks. U.S. firms supply 80 percent of these imports, and the trade is one-sided. Eurostat documents that at 213 billion euros in 2025, the bloc’s imports of U.S. IP have grown sevenfold over the past decade. Last year, the EU bought six times as much IP from the United States as vice versa.

Failed attempts to achieve strategic autonomy, de-risking, and diversification have a precedent. In 2019, Britain, France, and Germany created INSTEX, a barter mechanism intended to maintain European trade with Iran despite U.S. secondary sanctions. INSTEX handled just one transaction before closing in 2023.

Europe’s ambition to achieve economic security stands a better chance of succeeding if Brussels gets serious about creating a euro-denominated safe asset, investing heavily in frontier technology sectors, and doubling down on signing trade deals with middle powers. Delivering on these reforms would be a winning strategy—and not just a succession of buzzwords.

Amazon workers on food stamps have tripled despite its record revenue—and it’s just the latest evidence of the new economy of shrinking labor shares

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The U.S. workforce has made the type of history it likely wishes it didn’t. Working Americans are taking home the smallest percentage of economic output, 52.8%, since the Bureau of Labor Statistics began tracking the metric in 1947.

But as the share of wealth workers take home through wages—called labor share—is shrinking, corporate profits have exploded, with the S&P 500 index gaining 600% since the beginning of the century, while wages have increased just 12.5% over the same period, adjusted for inflation.

In other words: Corporations are raking in cash, but American workers are reaping less and less of the rewards.

The consequences associated with shrinking labor shares are now becoming tangible. A recent Government Accountability Office (GAO) report found that across the 11 states sampled, Amazon, the country’s largest company by revenue, has 12,346 workers on the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) and 11,338 relying on Medicaid. That was nearly triple the number of Amazon employees in need of federal assistance compared to 2020, when GAO conducted a similar report. During that same period, Amazon saw an increase in annual profits from $11.6 billion to $77.7 billion. Amazon’s 2025 revenue soared 12% year over year, from $638 billion to a record $717 billion.

Amazon spokesperson Rachael Lighty told Fortune the conclusion drawn from the GAO report is “wrong,” and that it is misleading to look at raw numbers rather than percentages.

“When you look at the actual facts, you see that Amazon pay is among the best in the industry, regular full-time employees have access to health care from their first day—at only $5 per week with $5 copays for employee-only coverage—and 74% of our regular full-time employees are enrolled in an Amazon health insurance plan, well above the 65% private-sector take-up rate for full-time workers,” she said in a statement. “We challenge other large retailers to provide the same Day 1 comprehensive health care benefits that we do.”

Walmart and FedEx saw similar increases in the number of workers taking federal assistance, as did ride-share and delivery companies.

Kathryn Larin, director for education, workforce, and income security issues at GAO, told Fortune the data illustrates that the Americans taking advantage of social safety net programs today are overwhelmingly in the workforce, with most working full-time. The income threshold for SNAP eligibility is about 130% of the poverty line, suggesting that despite many of these workers clocking in at their jobs, they still don’t have enough money to meet their basic needs.

“What this analysis really points to is the large number of people who have very low incomes and continue to have very low income,” Larin said. “I mean, these are families that are really barely able to make ends meet, and yet they are working, and they are working a lot.”

Diane Swonk, chief economist and managing director at KPMG, recently warned of the hidden consequences of a shrinking labor share, particularly that despite economic indicators suggesting the economy is stable, most Americans are facing an ongoing affordability crisis. KPMG found in February that since 1982, corporate profits as a share of U.S. GDP increased from 8% to 15.85%. During that same period, employee compensation as a share of U.S. GDP shrank from 66.6% to 61.9%.

“This chart from my recent Economic Compass still haunts me,” Swonk said in a social media post at the time. “A friend refers to it as the ‘revolution chart,’ which [is] disturbing but telling. Inequality fuels social and economic instability.”

This trend has been about 50 years in the making, according to Anna Stansbury, an assistant professor of work and organization studies at the MIT Sloan School of Management.

Fewer workers in the U.S. are represented by unions—20.1% of U.S. workers in 1983 compared to 10.0% in 2025—giving them fewer opportunities to bargain for salaries and benefits, she noted. But more so, Stansbury blames the fissuring of the workplace, or the breakdown of the employer-employee relationship. In the past, the typical employer-employee relationship would be that of direct employment: A worker for a company does their work at the company they are employed by. For example, a large bank like Bank of America used to employ a janitor to clean its offices.

But “in more and more cases, that’s not actually people’s experience of the workplace, particularly in lower middle-income jobs,” Stansbury told Fortune.

Instead, large workplaces like retailers and banks hire gig workers or subcontractors to complete jobs once done by direct employees: Companies hire a security provider, which employs a security guard to work outside that large company’s office. Delivery drivers are contract workers, not full-time employees.

As a result, companies don’t have to provide those workers equity or benefits. If a subcontractor violates labor law, the company contracting them is not liable. In the meantime, these large companies are not only saving money on benefits, but are also getting to argue that they are increasing efficiency by not spending resources on workers whose roles are not directly driving revenue, Stansbury explained. A bank should employ bankers, not janitors, to get the most bang for its buck, the thinking goes.

Brent Neiman, a professor of economics at the University of Chicago, said he believes AI has been the key driver of diminishing labor shares. In a New York Times op-ed, he argued the technology’s productivity promises pose the greatest threat to wages.

“Anyone who has used ChatGPT can see how much work now done by people could soon be done by technology,” he wrote.

Apollo chief economist Torsten Slok has already observed a correlation between AI adoption and lowered wages. In a white paper published in July, he and economist Sania Edlich found that among 321 occupations between 2015 and 2025, jobs with high exposure to AI saw a 6.7% decline in real wage growth after 2023 (following the wide release of ChatGPT), despite no employment changes, suggesting that while AI is not displacing jobs, it is associated with compressed wages. In other words, companies are seeing increases in productivity, but are using those increases as a cover to raise prices, while keeping wages stagnant.

Stansbury has a slightly different theory about AI. While the technology could begin to have an aggregate impact on the labor market, it’s still too early to say whether today’s shrinking labor share is part of a broader economic cycle, or if it’s a secular event, she said.

Unexpected inflation spikes, like what is currently happening, are usually associated with poorer real wages. If inflation is less volatile in the coming years and wage growth recovers, today’s trend of shrinking labor share could turn out to be cyclical, Stansbury said.

On the other hand, Stansbury noted, a tightening labor market should increase labor share, and the labor market is already relatively tight. If inflation stabilizes, employment remains narrow, and the labor share increases, it will be a sign of an economic cycle completing itself. A bigger problem will be if wage growth stays low even if inflation improves and the labor market stays tight.

“If those two things happen and the labor share continues to fall,” she said, “then it would suggest that there’s actually been a secular shift, a secular acceleration in the downward trend.”

Vibe coding security: a checklist before real users sign up

Deleting a leaked API key from your code does not make it safe. Copies stay in your Git history.

If strangers are now typing their details into an app you built by describing it, this checklist is for you. It takes the risks in the order that hurts users most, with what Andrej Karpathy, Pieter Levels, Patrick McKenzie, Jeff Atwood, Arvid Kahl, Simon Willison and Joel Spolsky said about each one, every record linked to its source.

Before you invite more people, close the risks that would hurt them first. Take every API key out of the code and revoke any that leaked, confirm payments on your server and leave card data with the processor, cap what one user can trigger, and prove you can restore a backup. Then have a fresh reviewer audit the whole app, because vibe coding security comes down to the checks the AI did not run for you.

You built the app by describing what you wanted, and now strangers are typing their details into it. Being scared of a leak at this point is reasonable, not paranoid. GitHub's own description of vibe coding separates the quick prototype, where little of the result gets read or tested, from the human-led kind, where you review, test and refine. Real users move you into the second kind, whether you planned it or not.

Checked fact: It separates full vibe coding (little reading or testing of the result, suited to quick experiments and prototypes) from human-led vibe coding (you review, test and refine).

Andrej Karpathy, Vibe coding MenuGen (2025-04-27)

Karpathy wrote this in his essay on vibe coding MenuGen. The hints of greatness are real, and so is the mess you inherit once the thing matters to someone.

Read that list of risks as your checklist. The sections below take them in the order that hurts users most: keys, payments, abuse, AI features, data you cannot get back, and finally a review of the whole app.

An API key in your code works like a password for anyone who can see the code, and the bill or the breach lands on you. When you ask an AI tool to make a demo work, pasting the key straight into the file is the shortest path, so assume it may have happened and look. Principle: Accept non-zero fraud for conversion. McKenzie holds that merchants should trade off conversion rate against fraud rate rather than minimize fraud, since every anti-fraud step also reduces the customers a business can sell to.

So treat fraud control as a dial, not a wall. Start cheap: limit how many payment attempts one account or address can make in a short time, watch for bursts of small failed charges, and look at failed payments often during your first weeks. Tighten only where you see abuse, and watch your sign-ups when you do.

Principle: Design assuming users will be evil. Atwood holds that social software must be designed from day one assuming some users will be malicious, because naive designs like trackbacks eventually make the whole community suffer.

Jeff Atwood, The Day The Trackbacks Died (2025-06-04)

Kahl's user meant no harm, which is the point: you need limits for honest users too. The damage reached the login and password reset emails every other user depended on.

Principle: Never mix untrusted text with tools. Willison holds that an LLM application must never combine untrusted input such as emails or scraped pages with access to tools or private data, because any attacker text can take control of the output.

If your app reads emails, web pages or user uploads and the same model can also call tools or see private data, text written by an attacker can steer what it does.

If your app puts a model inside the product, such as a chatbot that searches user records or an assistant that sends messages, check each one against this rule. Where the model reads text you did not write, take away its tools and private data, or split the job so the part that reads the text cannot act. Test it yourself by pasting instructions into a message or a page the model will read, and see whether it obeys them.

"Don't rely on your host or anyone else to back up your important data. Do it yourself. If you aren't personally responsible for your own backups, they are effectively not happening."

Jeff Atwood, International Backup Awareness Day (2025-09-11)

A host's backup is a promise you have not checked. A copy you control is the one you can count on.

"The minimum bar for a reliable service is not that you have done a backup, but that you have done a restore ."

A backup you have never restored from is a guess. Do one restore before you invite more users and see what comes back.

What should I check first in an app built with AI?

Start with keys: make sure no API key sits in your code or in a public repository, and revoke any that did. Then check that payments are confirmed on your server, that one user cannot trigger unlimited emails or charges, and that you can restore a backup.

Revoke the key at the provider and create a new one before anything else. Removing it from the Git history is slow and often unnecessary once the old key no longer works, and GitHub secret scanning can check the whole history for others.

Is code written by AI less secure than code written by hand?

The risks are the familiar ones, such as hardcoded credentials, insecure endpoints and unvalidated input, but they are easier to miss when you did not write the code. Review and test the result yourself, and ask a fresh reviewer for evidence, not just a verdict.

Should my repository be public or private?

A public repository can be seen by everyone on the internet, so make it private unless it is meant to be open. Never upload keys either way.

Do I need to store credit card numbers to take payments?

No. A payment processor can hold the card and give you a token, and your server confirms the payment with a key the browser never sees, after checking that the amount matches.

My financial planning and my asset allocation is based on my withdrawal rate. My entire financial life is based on withdrawal rates.

I have a baseline withdrawal rate that I feel comfortable with. I select my asset allocation so that if there is a 50% market drop, my withdrawal rate stays within a certain bound. If I want to up my spending or make a large spend (gifting, DAF, setting up a trust, second home, house remodel, etc) I look at what that does to my withdrawal rate, and the corresponding effect on my asset allocation.

It is a stochastic approach. It is not perfect, but I feel comfortable with it.

An alternative is a specific budget with a LMP. I would not feel comfortable with that. It sounds deterministic, but it is not.
