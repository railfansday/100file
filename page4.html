YouTube will now guess how many people are co-viewing on TV

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

YouTube is making another big change to how views are counted on long-form videos, YouTube Shorts, and livestreams.

According to a company update, YouTube will now include "co-views" in public video view counts.

It's YouTube's estimate of how many people are watching content together on TV via a single account.

YouTube says it will include these estimated views because "viewers are now watching over 1 billion hours of YouTube on TVs every day." The company says it wants to "credit creators true reach" and "provide a more complete picture of how audiences are engaging with content."

This isn't the first recent change to how YouTube counts video views. This past summer, the platform started counting views from the moment a video plays. Previously, viewers had to watch for a specific amount of time, typically around 30 seconds for long-form videos, in order for their view to be counted in the public metric. Co-views is certainly a bit more offbeat than that, as the company is essentially inflating view counts by estimating how many people are watching together on a single device that would've previously been counted as a single view.

YouTube, however, says this new platform metric "aligns with TV industry standards, including how traditional broadcast and streaming networks estimate the total number of people exposed to content."

World of Warcraft: Forever isn't mere nostalgia bait - it's rebuilding the MMO social architecture that Blizzard spent years dismantling

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

And when I fall in love, will it be (WoW) Forever?

I don't play video games regularly anymore. Not because I've lost interest in them, but because I no longer make the time, the premium on which has seemed to exponentiate with age. I realised, however, in 2019 that I was a kind of sleeper agent: whenever I heard the opening notes of Legends of Azeroth, the original World of Warcraft login screen music, I would feel the reactivation of a latent part of my mind laid down when I was 10 years old. And so, like a form of possession, whenever Blizzard announces a new legacy iteration of the original WoW, no downpayment in time nor money is too great to prevent me from returning.

Here I am, then, at the launch of World of Warcraft: Forever, seven years after Blizzard first revived the original 2004 game with World of Warcraft Classic in 2019. Once more I set foot on Durotar, to spend as much time as I can levelling up a new Orc Warrior before I am forcibly unplugged by either my boss or, more likely, my wife, and so far it's been a welcome return to some of the frictional - but more involving and consequential - socialising that WoW's decades of updates had previously sanded down.

My relationship with Azeroth began in 2007, when I was ten years old. As soon as I inserted the CD-ROM, I knew that I was going to have to vastly increase my timeshare on the family desktop. For the next six years, school attendance, sleep, and quality family time suffered immensely as I battled to balance my new parallel existence as a member of the Orcish Horde.

It's unremarkable now, but the fact that characters created at WoW's launch are now 22 years old and can still be played in the latest iteration of the game would have been almost inconceivable to many developers at the turn of the century. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, the video game industry was overwhelmingly organised around discrete releases expected to have relatively short lives. For most games, commercial success was concentrated in a brief period surrounding their launch, after which sales declined and eventually ceased. WoW's extraordinary success, and exceedingly long half-life, demonstrated the commercial possibilities and durability of a different model: an evolving, immersive digital environment that players paid to inhabit, year after year. Rather than simply purchasing a finished product, players entered a world that would continue to grow around them. New players, after buying the game (and then its subsequent expansions) and paying for a subscription, could begin their adventures in the same world as those who had been there from the beginning, making their way through its ever-expanding geography and history.

The persistence of Azeroth has been the main driver of its abiding allure for me. I have returned to the game time after time, often just to be enveloped in an audiovisual environment that I know so intimately, and which was so formative in my life. But as the WoW universe was continually developed, the game became increasingly unrecognisable from the version I skipped school to play on Windows XP.

What I wanted from the game had also changed. In a turn that would have been completely anathema to my teenage self, I no longer cared about reaching the endgame or having the most competitive character. I stopped caring about the latest raiding content or PvP rankings. Instead, the elements of the game I had once feverishly tried to skip over, like levelling, professions and story, became, ironically, the only parts of the game that I yearned for.

As time unspooled, WoW naturally needed updates to stay fresh. This meant overhauling many of the unwieldy elements of its original design. Much of this was described as 'streamlining' the player experience, making the game less tedious by reducing downtime and, most critically, reliance on other players.

The original Azeroth emulated the real world in ways that few contemporary games do. Firstly, it was openly unbalanced, with some classes vastly stronger than others, some of which were borderline unplayable. Secondly, it required extraordinary amounts of patience and downtime to navigate. Most importantly, though, it required collaboration with others to get the most out of it. In order to access the latest content, players would have to self-organise into specifically composed groups as big as 40 players large, and schedule lengthy chunks of time each week to make progress.

Over time, much of the original social architecture that forced player collaboration was stripped from the game. Adventurers no longer needed to search for and group up with other players from their own server to complete rewarding content. Automated matchmaking systems increasingly did the work for them.

I remember many late nights when I was prevented from doing something quite modest because there were simply not enough players online, or not enough of the right classes. With my ambitions frustrated, I would often have no choice but to log off and try again in the morning. These frustrations were, I only learnt in retrospect, an important byproduct of inhabiting a world that did not exist solely for my benefit. Other people had their own schedules, priorities and ambitions, and my experience of the game depended on those contingencies.

The changes to WoW followed the trajectory of the wider online gaming industry, where players could increasingly be matched instantly with others at any time of day. These were changes that genuinely respected players' time, but the texture and immersion of the game world paid a hefty price. In making the game more convenient, Blizzard had also made it less necessary, and in some respects less possible, to participate in the social world that had once made Azeroth feel so alive.

It became increasingly clear that there was a subset of players, like me, who weren't interested in seeing an old dog learn new tricks, and just wanted to spend time with the old girl we'd raised from a pup. Even if she did walk slowly, and was a bit blind.

As the years went on, many players moved on with their lives, even if they continued to carry some indelible residue of Azeroth with them. Others created unofficial instances of the original game, known as private servers, and relived those infant years with a knowledge of the world that they could scarcely have imagined possessing when they first entered it. As these servers became increasingly popular, Blizzard, originally very reluctant, eventually launched Classic in 2019, an official revival of the game in something approaching its original 2004 form. Since then, legacy versions of WoW have become popular enough to exist alongside the modern game as a major branch of the franchise.

Re-releasing these legacy versions has been a considerable commercial success. But it also exposed a problem with the project of preservation. The original game, because it is so timeworn, has effectively been 'solved.' Players know almost everything about it, including its most obscure idiosyncrasies, and have found ingenious ways of exploiting them. The uncertainty that once made Azeroth feel so vast and mysterious has been replaced by an extraordinary collective expertise. When new content was added to the game, even the best guilds would spend weeks making no progress. During those early years, players at Azeroth's frontiers had to innovate to find creative ways of tackling novel and seemingly intractable problems.

There are clear limits to simulating a turning back of the clock. Players like me slowly realised that we didn't simply want to return to an earlier, simpler period in Azeroth. We wanted something impossible. Like the characters in Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind, we wanted to forget what we already knew so that we might experience our birth in Azeroth for the first time all over again.

WoW: Forever is an attempt to solve this problem. Like Old School RuneScape, Forever's undeniable forerunner and a considerable success in its own right, it is an expanded re-release of the original game that imagines an alternate future for Azeroth: one in which the game continues to evolve without abandoning the original texture and mechanics, and one that preserves the social architecture that made it so enriching in the first place.

As I write this, my Orc Warrior is sitting at a Campfire, a new mechanic that allows players to establish communal campsites and contribute profession-specific items that confer temporary bonuses to themselves and others. To receive a benefit from Campfires, adventurers are forced to sit together, a deliberate attempt from Blizzard to encourage interactions between players. It is too early to tell how effective this socialising mechanism will be in the long run, but nearly everyone in my experience so far has been eager to shoot the breeze together. I have already shared a number of conversations with other players, most of whom are thirty- or forty-somethings on a similar nostalgia quest as me.

I am playing the game on a laptop using a trackpad, caring very little about efficient or optimal progression. Although I suspect a substantial proportion of the player base shares my approach, not everyone has approached the game so casually. At least five players have already reached maximum level, and many others will have meticulously theory-crafted plans designed to skip straight to the endgame. This is a good thing, and the game should be able to support players with different goals. It's important that the game rewards time and effort, but I hope that the modest amount of time I am able to commit will allow me to feel like I am on my own meaningful adventure, and not a weak player who is trailing behind.

Many in the community have come to believe, perhaps wishfully, that Forever might escape the trap of vertical progression, in which each new wave of content supersedes and renders older content redundant. Instead, the promise of horizontal progression, where new content expands the range of meaningful activities and rewards available without continually raising the ceiling of character power, has been latched on to by members of the community, even though Blizzard's messaging in this regard has not been totally clear.

For players like me, the appeal of horizontal progression is that Azeroth might once again become a place to inhabit, rather than a race to finish. I want to be able to wander off, pursue some obscure profession, or spend an evening doing very little of consequence without feeling that the rest of the world has left me behind. No Pseudonymity for Woman Alleging She Was Falsely Accused of Sexual Relationship with Jeffrey Epstein, Doxed

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Education  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

From Doe v. Doe (E.D. Pa.), decided today by Judge Gerald Pappert:

Jane Doe alleges four X (formerly Twitter) users falsely accused her of having a sexual relationship with Jeffrey Epstein and doxxed her by posting on the social networking site her full name, employment and education history, phone numbers and addresses. Doe asserts claims for defamation, false light, public disclosure of private facts and aiding and abetting. She seeks to proceed under a pseudonym because she fears for her safety, along with additional reputational and professional harm. The Court denies the motion because Doe's fear of harm does not outweigh the public's interest in open litigation.

[A.] Parties to a lawsuit typically must identify themselves. Fed. R. Civ. P. 10(a). In "exceptional cases," however, parties may proceed anonymously. To do so, "a plaintiff must show both (1) a fear of severe harm, and (2) that the fear of severe harm is reasonable." Courts balance multiple factors in considering whether to let someone proceed anonymously, including (1) the extent the identity of the litigant has been kept confidential; (2) the bases upon which disclosure is feared or sought to be avoided; (3) the public interest in maintaining the confidentiality of the litigant's identity; (4) whether the issue is purely legal in nature; (5) whether the party seeking to sue anonymously has illegitimate motives; and (6) the universal level of public interest in access to the identities of the litigants. The purpose of this balancing test "is to allow a district court to determine whether a litigant has a reasonable fear of severe harm that outweighs the public's interest in open litigation." …

The first factor "does not support anonymity where the litigant's identity has never been confidential." Doe has not kept her identity confidential. After Defendants doxxed her, by posting her picture, phone numbers and prior addresses, she publicly responded by "den[ying] the connection to Epstein in the thread of Defendants' posts."

To establish fear of severe harm, Doe argues that "survivors and other named individuals have reported receiving death threats." Yet she does not allege how she will be threatened if forced to proceed under her real name, and courts reject "claims of harm or generalized fear that are found to be speculative and unsupported by evidence." As she notes, the Defendants have already shared her personal information with the public, so there is no greater risk if she proceeds under her own name.

Doe also alleges a "risk of reputational and professional devastation." But "[c]ourts in this Circuit do not recognize purely social and reputational harms, without more, as valid bases to prevail on this factor." Embarrassment or economic harm is not enough to justify proceeding anonymously either. And because her identity was made public, "litigating publicly will afford Doe the opportunity to clear [her] name" and "will not contribute further to the harm that [she] alleges has already occurred."

In assessing the public's interest, courts consider whether "other similarly situated litigants [would] be deterred from litigating claims." Doe provides no reason to believe similarly situated litigants would be deterred from bringing defamation claims due to fear of disclosing their identities. The Epstein-related cases she relies upon involve plaintiffs who Epstein sexually assaulted, not individuals falsely accused of having a relationship with him.

If a plaintiff's claims are purely legal in nature, there is an "atypically weak public interest in knowing the litigant's identity." Doe's claims that Defendants falsely identified her as someone who had a sexual relationship with Epstein are inherently fact sensitive. For example, a key issue is whether Defendants "knew or were reckless about this falsity and had no good faith basis to claim that Plaintiff was the woman" identified in the Epstein files, and resolving that depends on the nature of the facts known to Defendants.

There is no evidence that Doe has illegitimate motives to proceed anonymously, which favors anonymity. The universal level of public interest in access to the identities of litigants, however, always weighs in favor of disclosing a plaintiff's identity.

The Court does not diminish the harm Doe claims to have suffered, but she has not established how using her real name would subject her to "severe harm," separate from what she has already allegedly endured from Defendants' past actions. Any harm she may suffer from using her own name is not outweighed by the "thumb on the scale that is the universal interest in favor of open judicial proceedings." …

This is consistent, I think, with the cases generally holding that libel plaintiffs can't sue pseudonymously; see Roe v. Smith and the federal cases that it cites.

When Chipotle overhauled its rewards program in April, it made perks like free chips and guac easier to snag and added half-off entrées, as diners started to balk at high prices.

For the burrito chain, the program helps it weather inflation and keep customers in the Chipotle habit. It now counts more than 24 million active members, and they drove a third of sales in the third quarter, the highest share Chipotle has reported.

“I think in this modern world, you have to be really up to speed on what’s going on with your loyalty consumer because arguably it’s the most valuable consumer within your ecosystem,” Chipotle CEO Scott Boatwright said Tuesday at the Fortune 500 Innovation Forum in Detroit, on a panel moderated by this reporter.

Loyalty programs have become a front line in the fight for market share. The freebies get people in the door, but the bigger prize is the data: a detailed record of what customers buy and when, which lets brands tailor offers and makes it harder for shoppers to stray. Boatwright, Macy’s CEO Tony Spring, and Ally Financial CEO Michael Rhodes run very different businesses: Macy’s is No. 192 on the 2026 Fortune 500, Ally is No. 275, and Chipotle is No. 351. All three are leaning on that math.

Boatwright said the pull to trim perks is real. As a rewards database grows and inflation squeezes margins, he said, “The gravity pulls brands toward, ‘ How do I capture some margin here.” His own team brought him ideas for cutting rewards.

“We are unwilling as a brand to do that,” he said. “I said, come back to me with 10 ideas on how to increase value for the consumer and let’s have a conversation.”

That stance tracks with Chipotle’s refusal to chase traffic with deep discounts. In February, Boatwright said he didn’t want to “devalue our core offering” with McDonald’s-style deals and planned price increases below the rate of inflation. Loyalty perks let the chain hand its most frequent diners more value without cutting menu prices for everyone.

The same logic shaped Chipotle’s answer to GLP-1 weight-loss drugs: a high-protein menu with low-priced add-ons rather than markdowns. “You’ve got to have innovation that doesn’t discount the core offering, but meets a consumer need state,” Boatwright said.

Spring, who took over as CEO of Macy’s Inc. in February 2024, has been steering the 168-year-old department store operator’s turnaround. He said a loyalty program only works if shoppers believe it does.

“Loyalty is integral, I think, to the relationships people have with brands,” Spring said. “And we are constantly striving at Macy’s, Bloomingdale’s, and Bluemercury to make sure that the loyalty program has value in the eyes of the consumer, not in our eyes.”

The payoff comes when a shopper decides where to look first, at the mall or online. “If the Macy’s app or the Bloomingdale’s app is there and I already have rewards in my wallet, it’s going to encourage me to go there maybe and complete my activity,” he said.

Macy’s program is built to be easy to join, Spring said, with rewards that don’t depend on how a customer pays and bigger ones for Macy’s credit card holders. The most committed shoppers can earn perks like free merchandise, free delivery, and after-hours shopping. “If you are more committed to us, we want to provide that value to you,” he said.

Spring is also thinking about what happens to those perks when an AI agent does the shopping. Macy’s has started working with Google’s Gemini on agentic commerce, he said, and the priority is making sure reward points, gift cards, and delivery promises still work when a bot places the order.

Food, clothes, and home goods offer sensory experiences that financial services can’t match, and few people feel much affection for their bank. Ally Financial, which bills itself as the largest all-digital bank in the U.S., is trying anyway. In May, it rolled out Loyally, a fee-free rewards program open to customers regardless of their account balance.

“The question really is how can we add some real value for customers that gives them reasons they want to keep on coming back to our site and engaging with us,” Rhodes said. “We’ve literally had it for like six months now, but the early results are really encouraging.”

Ally is starting from a strong base, by Rhodes’ account. He said customer retention runs well over 90%, about 15% of new account volume comes through referrals, and 70% of customers are Gen Z or millennials.

Rhodes argued that AI search is raising the stakes on every customer interaction, because what customers and employees say about a brand feeds the engines that make recommendations. “There are people who spend 10 times what we spend,” he said of Ally’s marketing budget. “Yet in the AI search engines and unbranded inquiries in consumer banking, we show up number one.”

The harder balance is rewarding regulars while still persuading new customers to try a brand. Spring said that’s where a retailer’s customer data comes in: breaking shoppers into cohorts and targeting each one, both to drive new traffic and to “create extraordinary value for your highest, most loyal consumers.”

“It’s not a one-size-fits-all approach,” Spring said. “It needs to be very bespoke. And you have to leverage tools like, dare I say, AI.”

Boatwright said every change to Chipotle’s program aims to draw more users in at the top of the funnel, then “make it much more sticky within the funnel and get those consumers coming back more often.”

The next test may come when the customer isn’t the one searching. Boatwright said agentic AI ordering is coming whether brands are ready or not, and Chipotle has to make sure it shows up “when you ask your agent, tell me the best burrito in town.”

Newsom Proposes Mandatory Frontier AI Oversight and Shutdown Powers

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

California Governor Gavin Newsom released legislative recommendations on November 18, 2026, that would require continuous independent oversight of large frontier AI developers and let state officials order emergency shutdowns of models posing imminent catastrophic risks.

The California Government Operations Agency’s report, AI Safety in 2026 and Beyond, dated November 16, 2026, is intended to inform legislation when lawmakers return in December 2026; its recommendations are not yet enacted obligations. The plan would build on California’s requirement that large frontier developers publish safety frameworks by adding state-set baselines for safety, security and transparency throughout training, internal use and deployment.

Every large frontier developer would be subject to ongoing oversight by licensed independent verification organizations, or IVOs. Those organizations would translate the state’s standards into technical tests and update them as AI evaluation methods change. They would also receive access to models, personnel, systems, training environments and records they judge necessary, then send unredacted assessments to the state.

The Government Operations Agency would license IVOs and could revoke licenses for insufficient expertise, independence or rigor. If an assessor could not attest that a developer met the standards, the state could order corrective action after engaging with the company. Developers could challenge such determinations in court before they take effect, apart from the expedited emergency process.

A surcharge on the verification fees developers pay would help finance publicly operated CalCompute capacity. Some of that computing capacity would be reserved for independent safety and security research and public oversight, linking an industry-funded surcharge to the infrastructure needed for external scrutiny.

Each large frontier developer would maintain a Risk Containment Protocol, tested and verified on an ongoing basis by an IVO and certified as effective by a senior company official. The required capability would span compute, agents, internal access and model deployment rather than depend on a single on-off switch. Protocols would identify responsible personnel, coordinate with cloud providers and power suppliers, and establish when a model can safely be restored.

Testing would address ways shutdowns can fail, including a model copying its weights to obstruct containment or an outside actor gaining access to shutdown controls. For open-weight developers, the containment and shutdown requirements would apply only to deployments under their control because they cannot shut down copies hosted by others.

State officials could order an immediate shutdown when they have credible reason to believe there is an imminent catastrophic risk or major loss-of-control event and the developer refuses to contain the danger. The order would require the narrowest workable intervention, take effect immediately and remain in force during judicial review, although the developer could seek a temporary restraining order. It would be lifted when the danger is no longer imminent.

The reporting regime would cover attempted model-weight theft even without death or bodily injury, all internal or external loss-of-control incidents, and a new near-miss channel. The report proposes cutting the deadline for most critical incident reports from 15 days to 48 hours while retaining the 24-hour requirement for imminent catastrophic risks. Whistleblower protections would extend to former employees and contractors.

Models capable of actions that could create catastrophic risk would be treated as frontier models for incident reporting even when trained with fewer than 10^26 integer or floating-point operations. That capability-based trigger addresses the report’s concern that relying on training scale alone has left dangerous incidents outside state reporting requirements.

Companies automating AI research and development would report quarterly how their compute is divided among training, research experiments, inference for AI-research agents, safety and security work, external customers and unattributed activity, with IVOs verifying the categories. Additional disclosures would cover training-data volume, cost, subject domains and human-versus-AI provenance, as well as inference compute used to train models in reinforcement-learning environments, known rewarded misbehavior and the mitigations applied. The report says these measures would show whether AI is taking over more of its own development and whether safety investment is keeping pace.

Recursive self-improvement (RSI), whose strongest form involves an AI system independently developing its successor without human input, would be subject to standby cap authority rather than an immediate limit. Officials could impose a time-limited, periodically reviewed cap on automated AI research when it exceeds a developer’s committed thresholds or advances faster than its safeguards and evaluations, creating a material risk of catastrophic harm. The cap would be lifted after an IVO verified that adequate safeguards were in place.

The recommendations answer Newsom’s September 18, 2026 executive order on independent oversight and an AI kill switch, which requested expert proposals within two months and accelerated implementation of California’s independent-verification and AI-auditor laws. The Government Operations Agency developed the report with the California Office of Emergency Services and outside advisers Jason Goldman, Gillian Hadfield, Alondra Nelson and Rob Reich.

For developers, compliance would extend beyond publishing safety policies to continuous external scrutiny, faster incident reporting and demonstrable shutdown capabilities. The proposal leaves lawmakers with two connected decisions: how much intervention authority to enact and how to make continuous oversight operational while the licensed verification sector is still developing.

For decades, the mortgage industry has helped Americans achieve one of their biggest financial goals: buying a home and building equity over time.

It’s a model we know well. Homeowners purchase properties, make mortgage payments, refinance when it makes financial sense and gradually accumulate wealth. Much of the mortgage industry is structured around supporting that journey.

But what happens after decades of building that wealth?

As homeowners approach retirement, the financial equation shifts. Instead of focusing primarily on acquiring a home and paying down debt, the question becomes how to use the wealth they’ve accumulated to support the years ahead. Housing presents a unique opportunity but also a challenge because, for many Americans, their largest financial asset is also the place they call home.

You can sell a stock without moving. Selling a home is another matter. Trading down and moving to a less expensive home to tap home equity is both expensive and difficult. This might be why our recent research shows that the desire to remain at home continues to grow stronger with age.

In a recent Longbridge Financial, LLC (NMLS #957935) study of more than 2,000 U.S. homeowners ages 55 and older, 90% said maintaining their independence was extremely or very important to their ideal retirement. Nearly as many (88%) said the same about remaining in their homes. Both priorities ranked above paying for health care, paying off debt, long-term care and providing financial support to family members.

The desire to age in place isn’t surprising. What’s particularly revealing is how strongly that sentiment differs across age groups.

Among homeowners ages 55 to 64, 85% rated remaining in their homes as extremely or very important. That figure rose to 88% among those ages 65 to 74 and 93% among homeowners 75 and older. The importance of maintaining independence followed a similar pattern, climbing from 87% in the youngest group to 96% in the oldest.

These are differences between age groups at one point in time, rather than a measure of how individual preferences change over time. Still, the pattern points to an important finding in understanding how homeowners think about retirement.

Over decades, homeowners accumulate substantial equity through mortgage payments and increases in property values. Yet the very home that holds the wealth is also where many want to spend their later years. Its financial value tells only part of the story. Giving a homeowner a choice to move to a less expensive, and thus less attractive, home to use their home equity may not be much of a choice at all.

Consider two retired households with the same net worth. One holds most of its wealth in savings and investments. The other holds much of it in home equity.

Their balance sheets might look similar, but their financial flexibility is different. The first household may be able to draw from liquid accounts to cover an unexpected expense. The second has wealth, too, but accessing it may involve borrowing against the home or selling it altogether. Both options come with trade-offs, particularly when remaining in the home is a priority.

For an industry accustomed to measuring property values, loan balances and equity, this is an important distinction. These figures describe a homeowner’s financial position, but not necessarily how easily they can access that wealth — or what they might have to give up in the process.

The same survey reinforces why this distinction matters. Among homeowners ages 55 to 64, 73% rated paying for health care as extremely or very important to their ideal retirement. That figure climbed to 85% among those 75 and older. While these findings don’t tell us how much homeowners will spend or whether they’ll tap into their home equity, they do highlight how financial priorities can shift across different stages of life.

Mortgage finance has traditionally centered on transactions: purchasing, refinancing, servicing and eventually paying off a loan. Those functions remain essential. But paying off a mortgage doesn’t end a homeowner’s financial relationship with their home.

For some older homeowners, selling or downsizing may be the right choice. Others may have sufficient income and savings to remain in place. Still others may consider home equity loans, lines of credit or reverse mortgages, depending on their financial circumstances, eligibility and the associated costs and risks.

The opportunity for our industry extends beyond any single product. It’s about understanding the role home equity can play in supporting homeowners through retirement, while recognizing the personal value they place on remaining in their homes.

It also requires recognizing that homeowners’ priorities differ. In our study, 93% of women rated independence as extremely or very important, compared with 88% of men. Similar five-point gaps appeared for remaining at home and paying for health care. While the research doesn’t explain why these differences exist, it underscores the importance of understanding homeowners as individuals rather than treating homeowners 55 and over as a single market with identical needs and priorities.

Housing has always been both an investment and a place to live. As homeowners age, the balance between those roles deserves greater attention from the industry that helped them achieve homeownership in the first place.

We’ve spent decades helping Americans build wealth through homeownership. The next opportunity is helping them understand how that wealth can support the years ahead, without losing sight of what they value most: independence, financial flexibility and, for many, the ability to remain at home.

Flytlink covers LEO, GEO and air-to-ground (ATG) connectivity for operators, aircrew and passengers. One question sits underneath almost every story we publish: what will this actually be like on a real flight? This guide gives you the tools to answer that for yourself.

The Claim Row Stopped the Duplicate. My Agent Sent the Payment Twice Anyway.

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Yesterday I fixed my webhook duplicates with one claimable row per delivery key. A slow receiver took 2.5 seconds to answer, my sweeper ticked every second, and the same payment went out three times before the first acknowledgement landed. One row in a table, claimed atomically, and three deliveries became one. I wrote it up feeling pleased with myself. Then rudratosh left a comment that undid the whole feeling: the claim row stops the resend, but the agent still believes the step failed. Fix it quietly and the agent plans its next move from a world where no key was ever assigned, when a key exists.

He was right, and I ran it twice to find out how right. Same setup as yesterday, same slow receiver, same claim row, same key, evt-1042. The only difference between the two runs was what the planner was allowed to know.

Here is the shape of the problem. The planner watching the delivery has the patience of one second. One second after the send starts it checks for an acknowledgement, hears nothing, the receiver needs two and a half seconds, and records the step as UNKNOWN. The acknowledgement lands at about 2.5 seconds, the claim row moves to done, and the sender writes "ACK received" in its own log. All of that is true in both runs. The question is only whether the planner ever learns it before deciding what to do next.

My claim is simple, and I expect some of you to push back on it. Stopping the duplicate is the easy half of this fix. The other half is telling the planner the step succeeded, out loud, in a place it actually looks. A fix the planner never hears about is a fix that only works until the planner's next decision.

In the first run, the planner reads the claim row before planning anything. It asks for the status of evt-1042 and gets back done. The late discovery arrives as a state change it can see: UNKNOWN to SUCCEEDED, on that key, at half past three seconds into the run. Its next plan is step two, send the customer confirmation for evt-1042, and do not send evt-1042 again. The receiver logged one delivery. Zero duplicate payments.

In the second run, the planner reads nothing. The sender's log line exists, the claim row says done, but the planner never opens either one. Its belief is still UNKNOWN, the same belief it formed at one second, and it plans from that: evt-1042 must have failed, so do the step again, with a new key, evt-1043, and send the payment again. And it does. The receiver logged two deliveries, and the second one is a duplicate payment under a fresh key. Same fix, same receiver, same timings. The only difference is one read.

I ran the whole thing twice. Both runs gave the same numbers and the same plans, to the second.

The planner did nothing wrong at one second. With the information it had, UNKNOWN was the honest answer: no acknowledgement, receiver slow, step might have failed. The mistake is not the belief. The mistake is that nothing ever revisits it. A belief formed in the first second of a run is still steering the plan at the fourth second, long after the world changed underneath it, unless something goes back and checks.

This is the part people miss when they talk about retries. They argue about timeouts and intervals, the same argument as yesterday, and they treat the planner as if it watches the whole run the way a person would. It does not. It watches the moment it last looked, and then it plans. If the last look was at one second, the plan is built on a one-second-old world.

This is the finding that stung most, because the second run had the information. The sender wrote "ACK received for delivery_id=evt-1042" in its own log. The claim row sat there saying done. Both facts existed before the planner decided. Neither one mattered, because the planner never looked at either of them.

A log line is a diary entry. A state change the planner reads before deciding is feedback. The difference is not where the fact lives, it is whether the decision reads it. Every system I have built has a log full of facts nobody ever read again, and I have caught myself pointing at those logs as if their existence proved something was communicated. It proves something was written down. Those are not the same thing.

Here is the part that makes the quiet fix actively dangerous rather than merely incomplete. When the planner redoes the step, it does not reuse evt-1042. It assigns a new key, evt-1043, because from its point of view the first key belongs to a failed step. The claim row protects a key. It cannot protect a payment the planner believes was never made. The duplicate in my second run did not slip past the claim row. It walked around it, through the front door, wearing a new key.

That is why "fix it quietly when nothing downstream depends on the agent knowing" is a narrower rule than it sounds. The payment confirmation, the next step in the chain, the customer email, all of them depend on the agent knowing. The only step where the quiet fix is safe is one with no next step at all, and those are rarer than they look.

The fix for this half is embarrassingly small, which is probably why it gets skipped. Before the planner decides the next step, it reads the claim row for the key it is deciding about. One read, one status, done or not. In my run that read is the entire difference between one delivery and two, between a confirmation email and a duplicate payment.

There is a temptation to make this fancier, to build a whole notification channel, an event bus, a watcher. Resist it, at least at first. The planner already knows how to read the row. The row already holds the truth. The missing piece was never a channel. It was the habit of reading before deciding.

Two honest limits, because the test was small. First, this was a tabletop model of a planning loop on my own machine, not a real agent framework: one key, one receiver, a planner with exactly one rule, plan from the freshest state you actually read. A real agent has more inputs, but the failure is the same shape, a belief formed early and never corrected. Second, the claim row still needs a lease in real life, a crashed claimer holds its row forever, and that part is still untested here, same as yesterday.

If you run agents against webhooks, or against anything where a step can succeed slowly, you have met this bug. What did your agent do with the late success, redo the work, or carry on as if nothing had happened? And where does your planner read the state back, before it plans, or never?

The agent loop is the easy part. Rents Rise Annually for First Time in 3 Years as Multifamily Building Boom Stalls

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The median asking rent posted its first annual increase in more than three years in October, fueled by a sharp nationwide pullback in multifamily construction.

October marks the first month of year-over-year rent growth since June 2023, according to the latest Realtor.com® monthly rental report. The increase was modest, with the median asking rent rising by just $1 to $1,692, but the underlying data suggests more hikes could follow.

Larger units drove the rebound. Two-bedrooms led the way, with median asking rent rising 0.3% year over year to $1,889, followed by one-bedrooms, which edged up 0.1% to $1,566.

Meanwhile, studio rents continued to lag, falling 1.2% from October 2025 to $1,411.

"I see this as the market beginning to rebalance," Lissette Calderon, CEO of Neology Group, a Miami-based real estate development firm, tells Realtor.com. "We had an extraordinary amount of new supply delivered over the last few years, and it takes time for that inventory to be absorbed. With construction slowing and demand for rental housing remaining strong, I expect rent growth to gradually return, particularly in markets where employment and household growth continue to support housing demand."

Realtor.com senior economist Jiayi Xu confirms that last month's rent turnaround hinges on a shifting supply pipeline.

The post-pandemic multifamily construction boom of 2021–22 flooded the market with fresh rental units completed in 2023–25, keeping prices in check.

Now, a broad cooling in multifamily construction is easing downward pressure on rent, just as high mortgage rates continue to keep families in the rental market.

"The path ahead largely depends on whether new multifamily deliveries remain strong enough to outpace that demand," says Xu.

Multifamily developers remain pessimistic about current building conditions, according to the latest quarterly survey from the National Association of Home Builders (NAHB) released last week.

The NAHB's Multifamily Production Index (MPI) had a reading of 41 out of 100, down 5 points year over year. Any reading below 50 indicates that more builders view market conditions negatively than positively.

"It is not surprising that the MPI is down year over year given supply-side headwinds such as high interest rates and a rising rental vacancy rate," says NAHB Chief Economist Robert Dietz.

According to the U.S. Census Bureau's Housing Vacancy Survey, the national rental vacancy rate reached 7.6% in the third quarter, the highest level since early 2014.

Kip Lewis, chairman of NAHB’s Multifamily Council, also points to construction costs rising relative to rents, along with delays getting projects approved and connected to roads and utilities.

"As a result, many projects are taking longer to complete," says Lewis.

Echoing Lewis, Calderon says the building pace comes down to the economics of development.

"Construction costs, financing terms, insurance, and operating expenses all affect whether a project can move forward," she says. "When projected rents don’t support the total cost, securing debt and equity becomes more challenging. Developers want to build, but the numbers have to work."

A closer look at the housing data reveals just how dramatically multifamily construction has shifted over the past three years.

Between 2023 and 2025, roughly 1.5 million units in buildings with five or more units were completed, an average of 499,000 per year, up 37.7% from 2020–22.

Those newly completed units boosted the total rental stock by 3.2%, driven by the multifamily starts boom of 2021–23, when ground was broken on more than 1.45 million units.

That surge in rental construction is now fading. In the third quarter of 2026, the multifamily pipeline shrank significantly across the board, with starts averaging 403,000, down more than 9% from a year ago amid soaring financing and construction costs. Units under construction similarly pulled back, averaging 674,000, marking a year-over-year decline of 2.7%.

Completions took the biggest hit, plunging 26% from a year ago to an annualized rate of 330,000 new rental units in the third quarter as the 2023–24 cooldown in housing starts worked its way through the pipeline.

"Given the year-over-year decline in units under construction and higher construction costs, we do not expect deliveries to rebound over the next 12 months," says Xu.

That outlook aligns with NAHB’s 2027 forecast, which predicts a further decline in multifamily starts.

"A thinner pipeline could create pressure in markets where demand continues to grow, but it doesn’t necessarily mean we’re headed for a severe shortage or another sharp increase in rents," says Calderon. "That will depend on how much existing inventory remains available, local employment and household growth, and what is already under construction."

At the regional level, multifamily completions fell year over year everywhere in the third quarter due to fewer starts in 2023–24. The Northeast saw the most dramatic pullback, with finished units dropping nearly 50% from 2025 levels.

"Thinner deliveries could further push up rents in Northeast metros, especially those that have already returned to year-over-year growth, such as Philadelphia and Pittsburgh," says Xu.

The South came in a distant second, with completions falling 28% year over year, followed by the Midwest (-17.9%) and the West (-14.2%).

At the same time, units under construction fell over 11% in the Northeast and 8.8% in the South from a year ago, but increased 4.5% in the West and 12.9% in the Midwest, pointing to a split in active pipelines.

Despite the Midwest's robust building activity, rents are on the rise in several major market metros, including Chicago (+3.2%), St. Louis (+3.2%), and Minneapolis (+2.0%).

When it comes to new groundbreakings, starts in the Northeast surged a staggering 48.4% year over year, making it the only region in positive territory.

“Because projects take about two years to complete in the Northeast, that rebound is unlikely to lift deliveries in 2027," warns Xu.

By contrast, starts fell 12.4% in the West, 16.5% in the South, and 29.2% in the Midwest.

"I remain optimistic about South Florida over the long term, while recognizing that each project needs to be evaluated on its own merits," says Calderon. "For us, that means looking closely at location, the renter we’re serving, competing supply, and the total cost of delivery."

For renters in Western markets such as Los Angeles and Denver, who have seen some relief in asking rents, the slowdown in new construction could reverse that trajectory going forward.

A separate study from RentCafe broadly dovetails with the Realtor.com findings, reinforcing the narrative that the supply of rental units is shrinking nationwide.

Based on data from Yardi Matrix, a commercial real estate intelligence platform that relies on a distinct methodology from Realtor.com, the report found that new apartment construction dropped 27% in 2026, with the Northeast standing out as the sole region to see building activity rise, led by New York City.

Liverpool could be handed another opportunity to pursue Ismaïla Sarr after contract negotiations between the winger and Crystal Palace made little progress ahead of the January window.

The Senegal international was already a serious target for Liverpool during the summer, when he was keen on moving to Anfield. Palace ultimately blocked his departure, but uncertainty over his long-term future has now resurfaced.

For Liverpool, that could make Sarr a player worth revisiting if Andoni Iraola wants another proven Premier League attacker.

According to Football Insider, talks over a new Palace contract have stalled following the club’s managerial upheaval, with Sarr currently “keeping his options open” ahead of January.

Liverpool came extremely close to signing the 28-year-old during the summer. The Reds were reportedly prepared to pay around £50million, while Sarr himself wanted the move, but Palace refused to sanction his departure.

His disappointment afterwards was understandable, particularly after such a strong previous campaign.

The situation has changed again following Thomas Frank’s arrival at Selhurst Park. Palace now have no intention of selling Sarr in January, with their new manager viewing him as an important player in the club’s fight to move away from the bottom of the table.

Liverpool already know exactly what Sarr can offer.

He brings explosive pace, Premier League experience and the ability to play across the front line. More importantly, he was prepared to join Liverpool only a few months ago, removing one potential obstacle if negotiations are revived.

Palace rejected Liverpool’s £50m summer approach and subsequently moved to protect Sarr with a new contract.

The difficulty is Palace’s position. With Frank now counting on Sarr and the club facing a difficult season, convincing them to sell midway through the campaign could require Liverpool to significantly increase their summer offer.

That would be difficult to justify. Sarr would strengthen Liverpool immediately, but paying an inflated January premium for a 28-year-old is very different from taking advantage of genuine uncertainty.

The Reds should therefore stay in contact with his representatives and monitor the contract situation rather than force a deal.

If talks with Palace remain unresolved and Sarr continues to favour an Anfield switch, the opportunity Liverpool narrowly missed in the summer may eventually reopen.
