OpenAI Will Pay You Up to $8K to Preach the AI Gospel to Small Businesses

OpenAI wants people to spread the gospel of ChatGPT to their local businesses. And it is willing to pay them up to $8,000 a month to do it.

The company launched OpenWorks, a program that recruits AI-savvy individuals and small consulting teams to help neighborhood businesses figure out what to do with the technology. The idea is to move beyond telling a restaurant owner that AI will change everything and actually build something useful for the restaurant.

OpenAI will pay selected partners a monthly stipend of up to $8,000. In exchange, applicants must commit to four full days of free, in-person work with local businesses each month, plus one community workshop. It's not an $8,000 salary just for being good at prompts.

That said, this isn't a full-time job with an automatic $96,000 salary. The company advertises a maximum monthly stipend and asks partners to spend four full days each month providing free, in-person assistance, plus hosting one community workshop.

The application asks candidates to describe a real problem they've solved using AI, what changed afterward, and which businesses they'd help first. Knowing how to give ChatGPT clever instructions apparently isn't enough; experience in coding, automation, marketing or operations helps.

Small businesses pay nothing for the service. They pick a problem, work with a partner on a solution, and get three months of ChatGPT Business at no cost. OpenAI says the pilot covers independently owned U.S. businesses, but leaves out startups backed by venture capital, private-equity-owned companies, and software firms.

In Charleston, a microgreens grower used the chatbot to find restaurants and write personalized pitches to chefs. He estimates his response rate went from 20% to 75% and says he landed 10 new restaurant customers in four months. Those are the businesses' own accounts, not independently audited results.

Competition also provides an incentive. Anthropic overtook OpenAI in one measure of business AI adoption earlier this year, even as ChatGPT remained a major consumer destination.

Getting ChatGPT into more neighborhood businesses gives OpenAI another route to paying customers, though the company hasn't said what happens after the free three-month offer. It also follows an October effort with America's Small Business Development Centers to train advisers and reach at least 1,000 businesses through workshops.

OpenAI has not announced a general application deadline or a fixed stipend for everyone accepted. The form is open now, and its most important question is pretty straightforward: Can you actually show up four full days a month and help somebody run a business better?

Wall Street’s full-blown gamble on the AI boom helped wipe out $8 trillion in stock market value before Jensen Huang’s return as CEO of Nvidia helped lead a rebound Monday.

Wall Street had its best day since April 2025 on Monday after Nvidia reinstated Jensen Huang as CEO, 11 days after he resigned. The S&P 500 closed up about 5%, while the Nasdaq was up 6.5% and the Dow 3%, while Nvidia shares jumped 33%. Even so, the rally won back only about $3 trillion of the roughly $8 trillion the S&P 500 lost in the six weeks after its Oct. 6 record, leaving the index still about $5 trillion below its peak.

The swing shows how much of the stock market now rides on the AI trade. And Monday’s rally didn’t answer the question that started the selloff: whether the billions of dollars companies have poured into AI will pay off.

As of Friday’s close, the three biggest U.S. indexes had suffered steep losses with no relief in sight. The S&P 500, which tracks the broader market, was down 3.7%. Meanwhile, the tech-heavy Nasdaq and the Dow Jones industrial average fell 5.3% and 1.9%, respectively, with all three indexes wading into correction territory on Friday.

The AI-fueled collapse of the past few weeks came after the S&P 500 reached a fresh record high on Oct. 6, when the companies in the index were collectively worth roughly $70 trillion. By Friday, just six weeks later, that value had plummeted more than 11%, erasing roughly $8 trillion in value, before Monday’s rebound.

The losses highlight how dependent the broader stock market has become on the AI boom. Of the top 10 biggest U.S. companies which make up 40% of the S&P 500, eight of them are tech companies. The concentration problem also extends globally. The U.S. now makes up three-quarters of the value of stocks in developed markets worldwide, Apollo chief economist Torsten Slok wrote in a note Monday.

“If I didn’t get involved, you’d probably have a very sad Nvidia for a long time to come,” he told the outlet.

White House communications director Steven Cheung confirmed to multiple outlets that Trump was “totally involved” in bringing Huang back as CEO. The White House did not immediately respond to Fortune’s request for comment.

Nvidia was among the hardest hit by the stock market slide in recent weeks. Shares of the AI chip giant fell nearly 14% Friday and 23% over the week, wiping out hundreds of billions of dollars in market value.

The plunge came despite stronger-than-expected quarterly earnings, with the company surpassing $100 billion in quarterly revenue for the first time. Still, concerns over AI spending and the company’s future prospects without Huang at the top rattled investors.

You Know Animal-Print Rugs, But Do You Know Animal Pictorials? (2026)

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The oldest known pile rug—pulled from a frozen tomb in Siberia in 1949—features a procession of 24 fallow deer and a parade of men on horseback. Some 2,500 years after its creation, animal illustrations are making their way back onto the floor. A serpent coils through the Snake and Pomegranate rug by Christopher Farr and Studio Shamshiri in Lucy Hale’s Los Angeles home, and a Tibetan tiger stretches across Demi Lovato’s modern farmhouse and Ethan Cook’s SoHo loft. These aren’t spots or stripes standing in for a neutral—they’re meant to be noticed and discussed. “Animal print is a texture; an animal motif is a story,” says AD100 designer Pavitra Rajaram, who recently designed an animal-themed collection for Jaipur Living called Majnun. “I think people are tired of decoration that says nothing and are looking for objects that carry meaning.”

Rajaram is describing a literal animal rug, also known as an animal pictorial. These playful floor coverings often feature a bucolic scene of several animals or a single animal form woven into the textile, and they come in styles ranging from avant-garde and surrealist to Art Deco and folk-inspired.

“What we’re seeing now is a shift toward rugs where the whole animal is part of the design and has a role in the composition,” says Kerri Anne Besse, design director at rug brand Tumble. “The oversized, blown-up animal print feels dated.”

As for styling, Sophie Salata, head of brand at Vinterior, recommends that designers let the pictorials stand on their own. “I’d be inclined to treat the rug almost as a piece of art, allowing it to become a focal point rather than trying to make everything around it match,” she says. Pamela Marshall, creative director at artisanal rug brand Patterson Flynn, agrees with this approach: These designs are more than backdrops.

Reformation's nine-piece rug collection with Les Éditions de Tapis includes depictions of cats, swans, and tigers.

Recently, brands have been launching their own interpretations of animal pictorials. Last week, Reformation launched its first-ever home collection, a collaboration with Les Éditions de Tapis that features hand-drawn swans and cats. At Milan Design Week this year, Fornasetti launched a surrealist-inspired line of rugs with CC-Tapis depicting slithering serpents from the luxury designer’s archive. A few months earlier, Ruggable released its collaboration with Scalamandré to bring the upscale design house’s iconic zebras to more homes. And in October 2025, Nordic Knots launched its Swedish Grace collection, including the Grace 03 Fauna rug, which shows a wildlife scene. Looking further back, Beni’s serpent rugs have been a designer favorite since 2020.

One of the earlier examples of literal animal rugs is a collection between longstanding collaborators Patterson Flynn and AD100 designers Charlap Hyman & Herrero. It grew from a one-off rug into a full collection of graphic natural-and-black silhouettes that launched in 2018 and continues to expand. “Areas of inspiration for these come from Calder’s simplified animal forms, the surrealists’ play on scale, and 17th-century cabinets of curiosity, to name a few,” says Marshall, who credits Hyman’s “encyclopedic” design references for the originality. We would be remiss not to mention Diane von Furstenberg’s long-running collaboration with the Rug Company, which has been producing the iconic Climbing Leopard rug since 2005.

Rugs from Charlap Hyman & Herrero x Patterson Flynn’s collaboration.

The Rug Company x Diane von Furstenberg Climbing Leopard rug in emerald.

These rugs’ rich history might be part of their renewed appeal. Animals have been a subject for as long as people have made pictures. The oldest known figurative cave painting—found in Indonesia and dated to at least 51,200 years ago—shows a wild pig with three human figures.

“Animals have shaped our textiles and rugs for longer than we think,” says Bella Valenzia, founder of Pelican House, pointing out that animal forms have appeared in tapestries and wall coverings, and as functional home objects, such as pelt and hide rugs used for warmth and comfort, since the earliest days of humankind. “We like to think it is part of a wider return to folk-inspired, storytelling textiles, with a sense of nostalgia,” she adds. Pelican House’s rug Frith references the old English countryside, where horses have long been at the heart of rural life.

Various animals, real and mythical, carry their own significance across cultures, and their meaning can deeply influence a home’s design. For example, a pictorial of a lion, which signifies power in certain cultures, adds a very different element to a space than a fish, which can signify good luck, notes Jahanshah “Josh” Nazmiyal of Rug & Kilim. Veronica Renton, senior vintage and antiques specialist at 1stDibs, adds that peacocks often signal nobility and immortality.

Plenty of designers still reach for the originals: While the contemporary versions of these rugs are having a moment, their earlier counterparts remain relevant. “I particularly love antique Tibetan tiger rugs and Persian and Chinese rugs featuring guardian lions, dragons, and all manner of mythical beasts,” says Tiffany Duggan, founder of Studio Duggan.

The Tiger Rug by Studio Ashby in Sophie Ashby’s former home in London.

Where these rugs live in the home is likewise important. Naturally, the pieces work well in spaces that are meant to be playful, like nurseries and children’s rooms. For example, in this garden apartment designed by Rebecca Benichou and Florence Jallet of Batiik Studio, the Toulemonde Bochart Lion rug brings a spot of levity to a windowless craft room. In the bedroom of Kate Upton and Justin Verlander’s daughter, Genevieve, designed by John Ruggiero, the Doing Goods Blooming Black Bear Rug provides a spot of whimsy. For double the fun, designer Olivia Song put two animal rugs, the Doing Goods Pinky Lion and Pinky Leopard, in her nephew’s room in this Sag Harbor beach home.

That said, these rugs work in more adult spaces, too. “In the [primary] bedroom of one of our London projects, we used the beautiful Snake and Pomegranate rug,” says Duggan. “I love the way the snake weaves its way around the bed, creating a focal point and bringing a little humor and unexpected character to an otherwise calm space.”

Designer Rayman Boozer from Apartment 48 has a rug with a large tiger on it from the Rug Company in his own home. “Animal motifs can sometimes feel juvenile, but there’s something refreshing about bringing that sense of playfulness into a sophisticated space,” he says.

Rayman Boozer’s home, featuring an Apartment 48 tiger rug.

Steven Loloi, co-principal at Loloi, agrees. Speaking of Loloi’s Roam collection that launched last month and features cheetahs, zebras, elephants, and giraffes in neutral colors, he highlights the surprising versatility of the pieces, which he says can fit just as nicely in a bedroom or living room as in a lounge or den. “I think we’re seeing people become more confident in their design choices and a little less concerned with playing it safe,” he says. “Why not have a leopard or zebra in your living room?”

It’s worth noting that these rugs aren’t for everyone, and some designers avoid any type of animal rug because of the hunting culture they reference. Others, though, embrace them as a way to reimagine humanity’s relationship to animals and commit to living better by them.

Rajaram’s Majnun collection for Jaipur Living does just that with its contemporary expression of “shikargahs,” the enclosed forests where kings hunted at leisure, which are celebrated in Iranian and Mughal design. “Instead of being chased, the animals are in harmony with each other, frolicking freely in the forest. It’s a celebration of coexistence rather than conquest,” she says. The Charlap Hyman & Herrero x Patterson Flynn collection had a similar ambition: “Our Tigre and Oso nod to traditional hide floor coverings; they give you the drama of the trophy rug without the trophy,” notes Marshall.

On the surface, the return of these animal-forward rugs might have looked like a change in aesthetics, but it’s really much more anthropological. “We have always gravitated toward pieces with animals, and even in modern production, history is repeating itself,” says Nazmiyal. Some 2,500 years after their first spotting, these animal-clad rugs are back home.

One of the world’s rarest fish lives in a single cave in Death Valley National Park, and earthquakes have left it on the verge of extinction

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Devils Hole pupfish live in just one cavern of Death Valley National Park. Earthquakes are rapidly pushing this fish towards extinction.

One of the rarest fish on Earth lives in just one place: a water-filled cavern in the Nevada desert called Devils Hole, part of Death Valley National Park. In the spring of 2025, after two earthquakes shook its tiny home, biologists counted only 38 of them.

The National Park Service announced the new figure in November. Divers and surface counters found the 137 Devils Hole pupfish during a survey on October 10, nearly double the 77 seen in the spring of 2026 and about double the 69 recorded in the fall of 2025.

Devils Hole is a cave about 12 feet wide and more than 500 feet deep. The bottom has never been mapped. The water stays at a constant 92 degrees Fahrenheit (33 degrees Celsius).

Pupfish have been found as deep as 66 feet, but they feed and spawn only on a shallow rock shelf near the surface, where algae grows.

The species has been isolated there for 10,000 to 20,000 years, longer than any other pupfish in the Death Valley system. The region’s caves keep other long records too, like a Nevada cave that reveals 580,000 years of climate swings.

It is the only naturally occurring population of the species, which was listed as endangered in 1967. Biologists from the National Park Service and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service have counted the fish almost every spring and fall since 1972, with scuba divers in the cavern and counters at the surface.

One of the fish’s main threats can come from thousands of miles away. Large earthquakes as far away as Japan, Indonesia and Chile have made the water slosh in Devils Hole like water in a bathtub, according to the park.

Waves can splash as high as two meters (about 6.5 feet) up the walls and sweep the shelf clean. The park calls it a tsunami in the desert, although it is tiny next to the ocean tsunamis that big earthquakes can unleash.

That is what happened in the winter of 2024 to 2025. Two earthquakes, one in December and one in February, set off waves that moved algae, organic material and fish eggs off the shelf.

The spring count in 2025 found 38 fish. One year earlier, the official spring count had recorded 191.

On July 29, 2025, just 21 minutes after a magnitude 8.8 earthquake struck Russia’s Kamchatka Peninsula, waves rippled through Devils Hole, nearly 4,000 miles away. Those waves were only about 10 inches high and removed just some material from the shelf.

Then, after an earthquake in Panama on October 9, 2026, nearly 3,000 miles away, biologists at first saw signs of stress in the fish. On a later visit, they found them in healthier condition.

The team had prepared for a crash like this for years. Pupfish eggs from Devils Hole had been collected regularly to build a backup population at the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service’s Ash Meadows Fish Conservation Facility.

In 2025, biologists added captive-raised fish to the wild population for the first time. Between March 2025 and February 2026, scientists released 72 of them into Devils Hole. They also gave the fish extra food while the algae grew back.

The fish live an average of only 10 to 14 months. Because of that, biologists believe most of the 137 fish counted this fall hatched and grew up in Devils Hole itself.

“It’s exciting to see Devils Hole pupfish numbers increase so significantly over the past year,” said Death Valley National Park Superintendent Mike Reynolds.

“These results reflect the dedication of our scientists and conservation partners working to protect this extraordinary species. While challenges remain, we’re encouraged by this progress and committed to its long-term survival.”

The pupfish has survived human threats before. In the late 1960s, a farming operation drilled large wells in the Ash Meadows area, and the water level in Devils Hole began to drop toward the shelf the fish need.

Biologists even hung a floating, artificially lit shelf in the cave as a substitute. The fish never used it.

A federal court halted further pumping in 1971, and the fight reached the U.S. Supreme Court. Its 1976 decision in Cappaert v. United States recognized the water right of Devils Hole as part of a national monument and limited pumping so the natural shelf would stay underwater.

Until the mid-1990s, spring surveys counted more than 200 fish. In the early 2000s, spring counts averaged about 90, and in 2013 the number fell to 35. The cause of that long decline has not been determined.

The new count is an encouraging step, but the park says the population naturally fluctuates and remains highly vulnerable.

It calls continued monitoring, research and habitat management essential if one of the world’s rarest fish is to keep its foothold in a single desert cave.

Here’s the full rule as written that the Academy felt “Gods Don’t Give Gifts” did not satisfy:

An animated film is one in which characters or objects are given the illusion of life by having movement created for them, by any human means. While the crucial movement of a live-action motion picture is captured at speed, animation is the craft of human creation and manipulation of images to produce a performance from inanimate objects. At least 75 percent of the motion picture’s running time, without opening or end credits, must include animation of the primary subject or subjects. We think Best Animated is simplest, but it may be the Academy decides ultimately that a Best AI movie category will make the most sense with these films where everything you’re seeing is generative.”

Of note, one of the films that did qualify for Best Animated Feature was “Coyote vs. Acme,” which is a hybrid animated and live-action film featuring the Looney Tunes alongside human actors in the real world. 📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Experts recommend understanding the risks of the glass cookware you’re using.

Here’s what you’ll learn when you read this story:

I clearly remember the very last time I used one of my reliable old pyrex baking dishes, and it makes me blush. I was making a chicken recipe but had forgotten to add enough liquid to the pan. In the interest of saving time, I took the hot pan out of the oven, opened a carton of chicken stock from the fridge, and tipped a little in. That’s when my pyrex baking dish shattered into three or four large glass shards, mingled with the now ruined chicken. It broke apart the moment the cold stock touched the hot glass.

Though I was shocked, I realized what my mistake was—the glass couldn’t bear the sudden temperature change. But it made me wonder: wasn’t pyrex designed specifically to hold its structural integrity under just these kinds of temperature fluctuations? When I started searching, I found that people have been complaining about their pyrex cookware exploding for years. Perhaps they just weren’t reading the instructions. Then, I stumbled across a different reason for my destroyed dinner: there’s pyrex, and then there’s PYREX.

The first experiment to use PYREX for cooking took place more than a century ago, in the kitchen of Corning Glass Works physicist Jesse T. Littleton, whose wife used a sawn-off glass battery jar to bake in the oven. It was made of borosilicate and that was the key. Used today in cookware and lab equipment such as beakers, Corning’s “Nonex” glass was invented for hot, burning-oil railway lanterns that needed to stay intact when exposed to cold weather. Calling it fire-glass, the company later named it PYREX®, a play on the word “pyro,” meaning “fire” in Greek, and “ex,” from the label that identified its earlier product.

Since then, PYREX has withstood the intense temperatures and harsh chemical onslaughts of laboratory work. It works well for temperatures of up to 425 degrees Fahrenheit in household kitchens.

The secret to the original PYREX’s heat resilience is its borosilicate composition, a glass made of silica and boron trioxide. Glass expands when it’s hot and contracts while cooling. Heat causes its atoms to vibrate and move apart, weakening the glass. However, boron atoms don’t vibrate or separate as much as other elements, making the overall glass more stable.

Around the 1950s, Corning also began making PYREX bakeware out of tempered soda lime glass, according to Smithsonian magazine. In 1998, Corning sold its consumer product operations to Borden, later renamed World Kitchen, then Corelle Brands. Corelle continued to produce the tempered soda lime bakeware in America—this is called “pyrex,” all lowercase. These days, Corning only makes PYREX borosilicate labware products, such as beakers, for scientific use. In Europe, the brand International Cookware still makes borosilicate cookware.

✅ To have a more accurate idea of what you're buying, here’s a good rule of thumb: “Pyrex” is a brand name with clear distinctions depending on how it’s written. Only cookware marked “PYREX”—all uppercase—is likely made of borosilicate glass. However, most of this is made in Europe; lowercase pyrex brand cookware made in the U.S. has been tempered soda lime for decades. It’s still possible to locate some Pyrex brand borosilicate cookware in the U.S., but it’s harder to find. If you see the label “pyrex,” it definitely means the glass is made of tempered soda lime.

Despite its deep roots in American kitchens, home cooks have been complaining about pyrex shattering in the oven or during rapid temperature changes since the early 2000s. In 2010, the nonprofit organization Consumer Reports released a warning following a yearlong investigation of pyrex shattering incidents. “When we examined 163 incidents in detail, we discovered at least 42 reports of injuries. While hundreds of millions of dishes are used safely each year, we believe the situation is serious enough that we have asked the Consumer Product Safety Commission to investigate,” according to the report.

During testing, soda lime glass shattered at lower temperatures than “more expensive” borosilicate Pyrex bakeware, the report continued. Consumer Reports found that the iconic glass bakeware produced in America today is of soda lime, while the European-made products investigators tested were borosilicate.

Consumers also complained that the manufacturer’s instructions were confusing. Consumer Reports found that the small print on the back of products containing the necessary precautions is “easy to miss.”

Borosilicate glass can withstand up to three times as much thermal shock as soda lime silicate. “When glass bakeware does shatter, consumers report, it can break into sharp shards that go flying, raising the risks of injuries. This contrasts with claims from one of the manufacturers that its glass bakeware breaks into ‘relatively small pieces generally lacking sharp edges,’” according to the report.

Soda lime glass is made of about 60 to 75 percent silica, 12 to 18 percent soda, and 5 to 12 percent lime. Heat-treated and then rapidly cooled on its exterior, the outside of tempered soda lime glass solidifies more quickly than the center. While cooling, the inside pulls at the stiffer exterior layer, creating tension that makes the whole piece more durable. If it does break, tempered glass forms rounded, cube-shaped chunks, instead of the sharp shards of untempered soda lime glass—think about the last time you dropped a drinking glass.

Untempered soda lime glass is used for items that aren’t supposed to be heated, such as drinking glasses and windows. It isn’t safe for either the oven or the microwave oven.

So look for a European provenance, or simply “borosilicate” in the description before you buy your next glass baking dish. I know I will, because I’m pretty sure I’ve only ever owned the soda lime version.

By 2015, after graduate school, he began moving between residencies every few months, relocating to remote locations in France, Vienna, and Canada. He sought distance from London’s commercial gallery circuit, a networking scene he described as populated with “opening-rats” and an environment he felt was depleting his focus. “Residencies were a way around that,” he told Hyperallergic, four months into his stay at the Italian complex. “I think I’ve never been sedentary. As his ideas developed, Maheke said that the pressure to hold down a studio space better suited to a painter no longer made sense. His work had outgrown a conventional studio, encompassing choreographed performances with professional dancers and drawing on autobiography. He described enmeshing an “Afro-descendant European perspective” with ideas about queerness, memory, and the body as a vessel for social experience.

Across countries and career stages, artists like Maheke have found themselves increasingly turning to residencies to navigate precarity. In interviews with Hyperallergic, artists reported using residencies as alternatives for permanent studios and refuges from the commercial pressures of major cultural centers. What were once considered philanthropic sites for creative retreat or intellectual leisure have become for many a workaround for periods of economic volatility and increased pressure to sustain artistic output.

According to the prediction market intelligence firm Betting Intelligence, odds on both Kalshi and Polymarket’s markets largely tracked with public signals about the likelihood of Huang’s return. But there were several large trades that could not be tied to public developments.

Betting Intelligence and the prediction market forensics firm MSR Decode identified clusters of trades on Huang’s return that did not appear to be driven by news or public commentary. The holidays often leave us with more food than we know what to do with, don't they? Before throwing everything into the compost or bin, though, take a look at your Thanksgiving leftovers; your feathered friends could be very thankful for them!

Yes, some everyday ingredients from the feasting table can make welcome treats for backyard birds, offering a little extra nourishment as temperatures drop and the nights draw in (and transform your outdoor space into a welcoming bird sanctuary in the process).

Whether you're hoping to see your birth month bird out there, or just happy to offer your kitchen scraps up to any local critters who might make use of them, here's how to use your Thanksgiving leftovers to keep bird feeders filled over the holidays...

Before we dive on in, a word of caution: not every Thanksgiving dish is suitable for wildlife, especially as salt, butter, rich sauces and seasonings can make seemingly harmless scraps unsuitable for birds.

The trick? Try to save a few simple ingredients before they make their way into the casserole dish or onto the dinner table. With that caveat in place, then, here are seven Thanksgiving kitchen scraps worth keeping...

Have a few apples left over from your Thanksgiving preparations? Birds such as robins and other fruit-eating visitors will definitely appreciate small pieces of fresh apple.

Cut the flesh into bite-sized chunks, removing the core and seeds first. Bruised apples are fine so long as they're fresh (they're not as fussy as we are!), but discard any mouldy or spoiled pieces. And avoid offering apple pie filling or cooked apples covered in sugar and spices.

Cranberries are a Thanksgiving staple, and you'd better believe that their fresh (and unseasoned, obviously) counterparts make a lovely treat for birds. Offer a few whole or chopped cranberries on a clean feeding tray like this one from MIXXIDEA.

Frozen cranberries are fine, too, once thawed. Just avoid cranberry sauce, as it typically contains added sugar and may include other ingredients birds don't need. Honestly, a little fresh fruit is all that's required.

Pecans (again, plain and unsalted) are another holiday favorite that can be shared with feathered visitors, along with walnuts and peanuts. All of these scrummy nuts are rife at Thanksgiving, and all provide fats and protein that can help birds maintain their energy during colder weather.

Take care to chop larger nuts into manageable pieces and check that they haven't been coated in salt, sugar, chocolate or seasoning. Just like the fruit, nuts should be fresh, too, rather than stale or musty.

Corn often features on the Thanksgiving menu in a big way, and a few plain kernels can be offered to garden birds; just set aside a small portion before adding butter, salt or other seasonings.

Cooked or fresh corn kernels can be placed on a feeding tray, but avoid creamed corn, buttery corn casserole and heavily seasoned dishes. Only offer a little at a time, as damp corn can spoil quickly.

If pumpkin or squash features in your holiday cooking (or your Thanksgiving decor, if you're Kristin Davis!), don't throw away all those seeds; instead, rinse away any stringy pulp, remove any seasoning and allow the seeds to dry thoroughly.

Offer them (have I gotten too repetitive yet?) plain and unsalted, either whole for larger birds or broken into smaller pieces for smaller visitors. They're a useful source of fats and nutrients, making them a worthwhile addition to your bird feeding station.

Leftover rice can also be shared with birds from your Thanksgiving feast, provided it hasn't been cooked with salt, butter, oil, garlic or onions. Both white and brown rice are suitable in small quantities when prepared plainly.

P.S. There's a persistent myth that uncooked or cooked rice swells inside birds and causes harm, but this isn't true. Nevertheless, rice shouldn't replace more nutritious bird food, so just offer just a small amount (you may find your birth month winter bird adores it) and remove anything uneaten so it doesn't have a chance to go bad.

A little leftover boiled or baked potato from your Thanksgiving prep can be another treat for your feathered friends. Set aside a portion before adding butter, milk, salt or gravy, then allow it to cool and chop it into small pieces.

Please never offer raw potatoes or green potato peelings, and avoid chips, roast potatoes cooked in fat and heavily seasoned mashed potatoes. Experts always advise that plain potato be treated as an occasional extra rather than a regular food source.

It's every bit as important to know what to avoid when it comes to feeding birds your Thanksgiving leftovers. Turkey fat and pan drippings might seem like an easy substitute for bird suet, but they're best kept off the feeding station. Audubon, in fact, warns that turkey fat melts more readily than traditional suet and can turn rancid quickly. It can also smear onto feathers, potentially affecting their waterproofing and insulation.

Gravy, stuffing and salty side dishes should also stay off the menu, as they often contain butter, salt, onion and garlic, none of which are suitable additions to a wild bird's diet. Avoid, too, anything that contains chocolate, along with avocado, sugary desserts and heavily processed foods, too. Bread and pastry aren't particularly nutritious (even if they won't do any harm), so think up some better ways to use your leftovers; might this author humbly submit the idea of a bread-and-butter pudding?

Once you've picked all of the bird-friendly scraps out of your Thanksgiving feast, be sure to present it properly! Always use a clean feeding tray rather than leaving piles of food on the ground, and try to put food out in the morning, as this is when it's most likely to be eaten.

Take care to remove anything uneaten before it spoils, and wash feeding surfaces regularly to reduce the risk of bacteria and disease spreading. It's also worth keeping fresh drinking water available, particularly as colder weather approaches, and remember that kitchen scraps are supposed to be occasional extras, not a replacement for a balanced wild bird food mix.

Still, all those warnings aside, there's no denying that sharing a little of your holiday feast with your local birds is a lovely way to give thanks to Mother Nature (and reduce food waste at the same time).

CrowdStrike to buy Zscaler in record-setting $43.9B cybersecurity deal

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

CrowdStrike Holdings Inc. today announced plans to acquire Zscaler Inc., a fellow provider of cybersecurity software, for about $43.9 billion.

The all-stock deal values the latter company at $260.4 per share. That’s a 34% premium to Zscaler’s closing price on Friday, the day CrowdStrike’s takeover interest became public. The transaction is the largest cybersecurity acquisition on record by a wide margin.

The companies reported their quarterly results in conjunction with the acquisition announcement. Both topped expectations across the board. CrowdStrike posted adjusted net income of $354.9 million on $1.58 billion in revenue, while Zscaler reported a $208.4 million adjusted profit and sales of $949.3 million. The former company also boosted its full-year guidance.

CrowdStrike’s flagship Falcon platform uses lightweight programs called sensors to protect employee devices, cloud instances and other technology assets. Zscaler’s Zero Trust Exchange, in turn, protects the network connections that link together those assets. The platform is positioned as an alternative to virtual private network tools.

The Zero Trust Exchange encrypts the traffic that flows between employee devices and business applications. When needed, administrators can decrypt the data to search for threats. Additionally, they can configure the platform to require more thorough authentication when users log into particularly sensitive systems.

The product portfolios of CrowdStrike’s and Zscaler overlap in some areas. Both companies offer tools that can protect artificial intelligence applications from threats such as prompt injections. They also compete in the secure browser category, although their respective offerings take different approaches to blocking malware.

Zscaler offers a tool that can automatically launch simulated cyberattacks against applications to find weak points. CrowdStrike joined the fray as well earlier this year with SafeMind, a set of custom AI models and related assets. It not only finds software vulnerabilities but also develops detections, automated workflows designed to spot hacking attempts.

CrowdStrike said that it will use the network telemetry collected by Zscaler to enhance SafeMind’s training dataset. In 2024, the latter company disclosed that its platform blocked more than 9 billion threats per day. That activity, which has no doubt grown in volume since, should give CrowdStrike’s AI models a more complete understanding of hacker behavior.

The company’s plans for Zscaler also extend beyond AI training. CrowdStrike will incorporate the network protection provider’s software into its Flex offering, which enables customers to buy multiple cybersecurity tools via a single contract. That could help expand the adoption of Zscaler’s products among CrowdStrike customers.

The companies have also identified other upselling opportunities. They disclosed today that about 8,000 Zscaler customers don’t yet use CrowdStrike products.

“With Zscaler, we’re building cybersecurity’s operating system: a single control plane to deploy AI, control AI, and enable any agentic application to run safely,” said CrowdStrike founder and Chief Executive Officer George Kurtz (pictured). “We see a path to become the largest AI safety and security company the world has ever seen.”

CrowdStrike expects to complete the acquisition in the second half of its 2028 fiscal year, which starts next February. The company stated that the deal will be “significantly and immediately accretive” to free cash flow per share upon closing.

Even Sienna Miller Was Surprised by That ‘War’ Ending

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The female lead of the HBO/Sky legal drama's first season, who is no stranger to weathering tabloid scrutiny herself, opens up to THR about who came out on top in the high-profile divorce in the end.

[This story contains MAJOR spoilers from the War finale.]

Sienna Miller doesn’t quite know why she keeps getting tapped to play women caught up in the public eye. Since breaking out two decades ago as Edie Sedgwick in Factory Girl, the American-born English actress has made a speciality out of portraying women navigating intense media scrutiny — from Alfred Hitchcock’s tormented muse Tippi Hedren in The Girl to the embattled political wives of Showtime’s The Loudest Voice and Netflix’s Anatomy of a Scandal.

“I think it’s less that I’m seeking out these roles and more that those are the roles that people see me as, probably because a famous actress is part of my identity in a way [but] I don’t feel is part of me,” Miller tells The Hollywood Reporter on a recent video call from her home in the U.K. “I feel like an actor who’s not very famous — and a mom. But it’s quite funny to perhaps send myself up by playing heightened versions of whatever I imagine people might assume that to be.” Related Stories TV All's Fair in Love and 'War': Creator George Kay Explains His Ruling on Morgan and Carla's Divorce TV Archie Renaux on 'War's Resident -- and Misunderstood -- Bad Boy

Miller’s latest TV role is no exception. In creator George Kay’s HBO/Sky legal anthology drama War, which just aired its finale on Thursday in the U.S., Miller plays film star Clara Duval, whose messy, high-profile divorce from Dominic West’s billionaire tech founder Morgan Henderson becomes a feeding frenzy for both the press and their ruthless legal teams. At the center of the dispute is an equitable split of Zephyr — the ostensibly “less toxic” social media platform that Morgan built after watching Carla endure relentless online bullying and that Carla named after Mary Darby Robinson’s poem “Ode to the Muse.”

Though their lawyers push them toward scorched-earth tactics to win at all costs, the pair ultimately choose to settle on their own terms, resulting in a surprisingly optimistic portrait of life after separation. “I think the normalizing of divorce is something that should be more commonplace in the world. It doesn’t have to be hell,” Miller says. “I thought that the ending was very poignant and sweet.”

In the wide-ranging chat below, Miller opens up to THR about shedding her own people-pleasing tendencies to play a “narcissistic” (but surprisingly sympathetic) actress, why she thinks Carla really came out on top in the divorce — and how she reflects now on her own experience of being tabloid fodder in her 20s: “I’m more in a kind of anthropological way interested in it [now], because I feel maybe from some PTSD that I am separate from it.”

War creator George Kay has said he wanted an actual movie star to play Carla, but he very intentionally did not want to let any of your own personal experiences influence the writing of the character. In hindsight, what did you find most appealing about embodying someone in an esoteric world that you already know so intimately yourself?

I signed on before I knew what it was, because I only read the pilot, so I was learning on the job very much with this. This isn’t a limited series, but for me, it was. [Writer’s note: Kay confirmed to THR that each season of War will center on a new legal dispute, with the parties being represented by the same group of lawyers at rival London firms Cathcarts & Sons and Taylor & Byrne.]

Sometimes when you do a limited series, you have all eight episodes and you can kind of map something out. But with this, I wouldn’t find out till a few days before we’d start shooting the next block [when] we’d get scripts. So it wasn’t that I made a choice to play somebody in those circumstances, and I don’t know now that I need to do it again. But while I was in it and learning who she was as we were going along, I thought it was interesting to feel like somebody very different in those somewhat familiar circumstances. But I think Carla’s much more steely and less naive than I am.

I wouldn’t want to describe Carla as a narcissist, but she certainly comes across as a little high-strung and self-centered.

Where do you think her narcissistic nature comes from? Did you establish any kind of backstory that could shed light on what makes her tick?

I think she’s an insecure woman who has had to deal with underestimation, and I don’t think there was one inciting incident. I think you are either one of two personality types — you either have more of a narcissistic personality, or you’re more of an empathetic person. I, as myself, would walk into a room and be concerned or pretty much aware of everybody within that room, and roughly have a temperature gauge on all of them. With Carla, I think she has decided that she’s the most important person in that room, and she doesn’t feel the need to check in with others. I think she channeled whatever abandonment she experienced into that way of being as a defense mechanism, versus my equivalent — which is exhausting, but is also a defense mechanism.

[Playing] that was really fun. Once I made that decision to be like, “She’s not concerned with other people’s feelings or energies, and she’s very much orbiting herself” — it was so uncomfortable at first to be that way. And then anytime I stumbled upon something that my instinct would be to correct, I was like, “No, she wouldn’t bother her.” And then it was quite a liberating choice.

George has also talked about how you brought an unexpected softness to Carla that was not always part of his original conception of the character. Were there any particular moments in the season that you wanted to soften her a little bit or make her more likeable? Did you ever push back on some of her actions or decisions?

I didn’t feel like I needed to make her more likable. I sometimes needed reasons for the decisions about how she was [acting]. I felt like I really pushed back against the kind of hysterical breakdown on the talk show [in episode five], although people really enjoyed that, because I just didn’t want it to fall into any kind of “hysterical woman” trope situation. I didn’t want Morgan to appear reasonable and Carla to appear unhinged, but that’s often the way the world sees these relationships and sees the dynamics between a couple. If she’s ambitious or if she’s ruthless, she’s a bitch; if he is [those same things], he’s a great businessman. I think it does kick up some of those tropes, which I was really trying to push back on until I realized, “Well, maybe it’s a great mirror to see how women are treated and judged.” Obviously, it gets rectified, and she wins that particular battle.

But there were moments in this that I was trying to advocate for her to be more vulnerable, or more soft, or just more three-dimensional. But then if I didn’t find it, I would just lean into her being really unashamedly a bitch or whatever she is. The moment where she couldn’t remember her son’s age — I was like, “That’s a huge thing [to forget]!” And we were learning this as we were going on. I’m like, “Does she not know her son’s age?” And then I would talk to other cast [members] who were like, “Actually, it’s kind of funny. Just do it.” But I was trying to fight for her a bit in moments.

Even though you and Dominic play the leads of this season, your characters don’t actually spend a lot of time together because they’re always busy strategizing with their own lawyers. What did all of the brief glimpses of Carla and Morgan — in the back of a limousine, in an elevator, in the back alley of the courthouse waiting for their cars in the rain — reveal to you about the nature of their relationship?

I think the thing that Carla and Morgan were missing was time together, and time and energy spent on each other. When they’re forced into those situations, other than the car at the very beginning, I think they realized that there was so much there that was probably salvageable or that they cared about. And once you get into a war situation and it’s you versus them, it’s very easy to forget those moments, the good things. But I think what probably comes across in those poignant moments is a sense of love buried underneath all of it, or consideration at least, or some form of emotion, which I think the lawyers are very good at hiding from you.

Carla’s relationship with Morgan is also very much shaped by their own financial circumstances — he is now a billionaire tech founder, and she has been experiencing a lull in her acting career and a significant dip in her income.

I think through the process of divorce and how acrimonious that is and how savage it is, you can either really sink into the resentment and the loathing, or you can remember what it was that brought you together. In the cross-examination [during the last two episodes], we’re talking about moments when we first met. Parts of it are quite therapeutic for them.

I think she genuinely feels entitled to something for Zephyr. I think she really does feel like she contributed more than he is allowing to be let on to the formation of that company, but she’s just vengeful. I don’t even know that it was ever about the money. I think she was just unhappy and he was unhappy, and then it got ugly. She kind of made it ugly, but that’s because she was convinced he cheated. It’s a whole mess, but there are threads within it that I also struggle to follow. So I put it down to just them being erratic and love not being a linear thing.

Carla and Morgan clearly could have settled their divorce at any point during mediation or arbitration, but it’s not until the end of their two-day trial that they decide to reach a fair settlement on their own terms without any outside intervention. In their last scene together, the two of them share a lovely moment on the beach in Normandy, and they are last seen walking back to her cottage to have a hot chocolate with Gabe (Aran Murphy, son of Cilian), Carla’s son from a previous relationship. What did you make of the resolution of their relationship?

I think they’re both battle-weary by that point, and they’ve seen all of the duplicity amongst the legal teams, and that side of things is so ugly. They’ve humiliated each other publicly and been backed into corners, and I think they don’t recognize themselves in some of those moments. I found I was expecting more fireworks at the ending. (Laughs.) And then in reflection, I think the normalizing of divorce is something that should be more commonplace in the world. It doesn’t have to be hell. I thought that the ending was very poignant and sweet.

Basically, what happened was that Dom and I never really got to work together. So anytime we did, we were very happy, which I think serves the story and adds a little sort of something to the scenes. But I found that scene on the beach touching to shoot and surprisingly emotional. But again, there are so many different ways that this could have gone, and I have to defer to the writing team to come up with the best one. I think Carla should have won! I probably was drawn to it becoming more ugly. But actually, the lawyers are the ugly people, and that’s the moral of the story. Even though Carla and Morgan are also ugly and narcissistic. (Laughs.)

What do you think it says about us as a culture that we immediately think of the worst possible scenario for a high-profile divorce?

Well, I don’t know. I think it’s wealthy people. We want to see them being treated like normal people, being dragged through the mud. It’s the same with Succession. We loved watching those people get it wrong and fall apart. I think there must be some kind of schadenfreude. It’s wonderful to be in those aspirational worlds and then wonderful to be reminded that, irrespective of how aspirational this world is or how great it looks from the outside, they’re two people dealing with shit. It’s just with many more zeros on the end of it.

George has told me his theory about what happens to Carla and Morgan’s relationship, but what do you imagine Carla and Morgan’s lives — either together or apart — will look like going forward?

I think they’ll be really good friends, but I think he will regret [the divorce] more than she will. So that’s me defending my character, and Dom would say the same thing about his. (Laughs.)

Over the last couple of decades, you have played a lot of women — and particularly actresses — who are navigating intense public scrutiny. It’s hard not to think about how the early years of your career in the mid-2000s were overshadowed by British tabloid culture and paparazzi obsession. How do you reflect now on the harsh treatment that you endured in your 20s?

Yeah, it feels so alien to the world that we live in now that it’s almost comical. It’s not comical, but I’m baffled that that existed. The ’90s [had] a lad culture in England, and God forbid, you couldn’t take the piss out of yourself and laugh at yourself. If you couldn’t laugh at yourself or send yourself up, you weren’t worth talking to in England. So I think I kind of absorbed that lad culture and was quite gobby and probably quite fun for them fodder-wise. But the lad culture meant that the paparazzi were just ravenous and unstoppable and aggressive and violent, and it’s just so different to the world [now]. It just would not be allowed; it wouldn’t be tolerated. People on the street wouldn’t stand for it if they saw what was happening.

It was young women that were targeted and cameras up your skirts — just shocking. But you look back and you’re like, “God, that’s not long ago,” and it was absolutely accepted and paid for and devoured. So I’m more in a kind of anthropological way interested in it [now], because I feel maybe from some PTSD that I am separate from it. I can look back on it with curiosity and philosophy versus trauma and just be like, “God, that’s crazy that it wasn’t that long ago, and that’s how we lived.” But pre-social media, in 2008, the whole world shifted. In 2012, it really shifted, but it’s just interesting to look back on that time and to have been such a part of it and feel like it’s so foreign. I think it was too much for anyone to really live like 15 people outside your house and chasing you everywhere you went. I’ve always been the same — I’m quite a normal, grounded person that it just didn’t suit [my life]. I don’t want bodyguards and putting on makeup every day.

You’ve spoken about wanting to take yourself more seriously as an actress, and reaching a point in your life where you are decidedly less interested in hearing other people’s opinions. When did that shift start for you?

I think I always took my work really seriously. I just think it was a battle to be seen as a serious actor in my 20s, but we are way beyond that now. I think when I had my first daughter, life just suddenly felt more serious, and the choices that I was making, they had to be worthy of being away from home and taking my kid on the road, and there was just more levity to each decision. So I feel like the work is something I’ve always taken very seriously, but people didn’t see me as a serious actor until quite a while ago now.

Do you ever worry that playing women who are consistently judged by their image reinforces the narrative you’ve been trying to move past?

It’s interesting. I don’t ever think about that in relation to these actresses. I see them as they should be seen: as people. But I think what I was objecting to is my own personal [experience]. It was a different era of tabloid journalism and scrutiny, and we lived in a monoculture. There weren’t fringe people with opinions on the internet, and it was one media and pretty unanimous news that we were all digesting. So if there was a public persona of you, that was pretty much the [only] public persona of you, and it was very difficult to have a say in that. It’s such a different culture now that I don’t think it exists in the same way. I think that if I was treated the way that I was then, I would have a much louder voice or means to come back at it.

But in terms of [playing] the actresses, I don’t know. I think it’s less that I’m seeking out these roles and more that those are the roles that people see me as, probably because a famous actress is part of my identity in a way [but] I don’t feel is part of me. I feel like an actor who’s not very famous — and a mom. But it’s quite funny to perhaps send myself up by playing heightened versions of whatever I imagine people might assume that to be, maybe. Although I have played a lot of actresses — and I can’t even remember them all now — I’ve also played a lot of non-actresses, so it feels like there’s variety for me.

You and Carla are fundamentally different people, but I would imagine you also know what it feels like to not be able to leave your own house or to live in a gilded cage because of the paparazzi. Even though you have moved past that chapter of your life, do those feelings ever come up when you are playing these women? Or are you always able to separate your own experiences from the characters you play?

No, I feel like when I’m put actually in the circumstances of getting through a pack [of people] into a car, my heart starts racing. I do have some body memory of it, even though I know it’s pretend. That’s your job, I think, as an actor. It’s to summon things or have them just appear for you in this instance because your past experience is what you can draw on. Georgia builders give affordable housing an essential new identity

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Georgia residential developers and builders are rebranding affordable housing as essential homes, aimed at workers like teachers and first responders. The effort is linked to policy priorities such as permit review accountability under SB 447 and changes to final plat and bonding.  AI Summary

Put an affordable housing project on a planning commission agenda, and the neighborhood naysayers come out of the woodwork to fight.

They argue it will destroy the neighborhood’s character. Labeling the development as workforce housing doesn’t help much.

What’s in a name? An affordable home – or neighborhood – by any other name is still affordable, no? Still, companies rebrand when the current brand is struggling. And Georgia homebuilders and land developers are taking a crack at giving “affordable housing” a brand makeover, one that’s less stigma and more economic and social fabric dynamism.

The Georgia Residential Land Development Council and the Home Builders Association teamed up to dub affordable housing “essential homes.” They are pairing it with a tagline: “Homes for the people who keep Georgia working.”

But defining essential homes as housing that workers can afford near where they work could be applied broadly as lawmakers from the federal to local levels wrestle with how to make housing more affordable.

“It’s a reality nationally that the buying power of the average American is not considered in land use and zoning policies,” Jay Knight, GRLDC’s chairman, told HousingWire TBD. “Essential homes for essential people intends to move the conversation from sticks and bricks allowed to the important people being prohibited.”

The definition names no home size or price point. It asks whether the people who work in a community can afford to live there. Teachers, firefighters, nurses, first responders, healthcare workers and skilled tradespeople are among the livelihoods the term “essential” captures best. They also include younger Georgians hoping to buy a first home.

Austin Hackney, HBAG’s executive vice president, said in a statement that the term puts a human face on the affordability debate. He said that when workers can’t find homes within reach of their income, the effects reach employers, schools and local businesses.

The rebrand comes with a policy push. The groups say zoning restrictions, long permit reviews, infrastructure requirements and delayed final plat approvals add substantial costs that end up in home prices.

GRLDC helped advance Senate Bill 447, which took effect July 1. The law sets timelines and accountability for Georgia’s land disturbance permit reviews. The council’s next target is final plat and bond reform. It wants reasonable approval timelines, standardized bonding requirements, and an end to delays that keep completed developments from moving forward.

The U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development considers housing affordable when occupants pays no more than 30% of gross income on housing costs. Programs add other definitions, often tied to area median income. Affordable housing typically targets households at 60% of the area median income or less, while workforce housing covers roughly 60% to 120%.

Workforce housing, however, isn’t explicitly defined in federal law or regulation, according to a March 2026 Congressional Research Service analysis. But the term is used to avoid saying a housing development is affordable housing.

Opposition often centers on neighborhood character. Researchers also find residents may fear newcomers’ socioeconomic status more than the buildings themselves. Property values are another common worry, though research cited by the Florida Housing Coalition found almost no negative effect on neighboring market-rate homes.

Knight said the definition problem became clear at a speaking engagement. He asked a room of roughly 40 people to define affordable housing. One woman said Section 8. Someone else said anything under $400,000. A person from the Northeast thought it meant rent control.

Knight said that convinced him people weren’t talking about the same thing, which made the conversation unproductive. His business partner suggested essential homes. Knight said the organizations liked it immediately.

Terms such as affordable homes, starter homes, first-time buyer or workforce housing arrive with a definition already attached. Knight said “essential homes” encourages people to ask what it means, which gives the groups a chance to set the definition themselves.

The rationale behind the rebranding is that if local governments can dictate what can be built, the standard shouldn’t exceed what their own employees can buy. Knight said the average municipal employee earns about $60,000 to $62,000 a year in Georgia. That means they can afford about a $225,000 home.

In most U.S. markets today, that would buy roughly 1,100 square feet on a 35-foot detached lot, with no garage and no homeowners association, he said. He added that would put the price at about three and a half times annual income, close to the ratio GIs returning from World War II paid in the late 1940s and 1950s.

Knight said zoning conditions have pushed home prices to a much higher ratio. High-cost areas like California, New York and Florida are prime examples. In Silicon Valley, the median home price is 11.4 times median household income, according to an annual report from Harvard University’s Joint Center for Housing Studies. The Miami metro’s ratio is 7.6.

High prices relative to income are a major reason the median first-time homebuyer is now 40. It’s an all-time high, according to the National Association of Realtors. Such a disparity is why California and Florida have been at the forefront of housing reforms.

The Georgia campaign will put faces to the term: a police officer, a teacher, a nurse and a forklift operator. Knight said it will portray the kinds of workers that municipalities court with industry.

Knight, who sits on an advisory council for the National Association of Home Builders, said he has shared the term with the executive director of the Houston Home Builders Association. He said Houston is now discussing it, since builders nationwide are having the same conversations.

“Who can be against essential housing?” Knight said.

Schaffner said the second success has been educating younger people about HIV prevention, helping reduce new infections.

Why CSS zoom Shrinks Every Slide to 30%

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 22 • Originally published at forge.workstyle.tech

📝 Originally published (in Japanese) at forge.workstyle.tech.

TL;DR A slide fitting check measured a box whose min-height kept it at the viewport height. The scrollHeight and clientHeight comparison then kept reporting that the content did not fit, even as its zoom changed. The search reached its lower bound of 0.3 on both mobile and desktop.

All slides shrank to 30% because the box used for the fit check had a min-height set to the viewport height. Measuring the box kept reporting that the content did not fit, even as the scale changed, so the binary search shrank it to the 0.3 lower bound. The fix was to temporarily set the content’s min-height to 0, measure its offsetHeight, and then restore the original value.

I used CSS zoom to add a system that shrinks only content that overflows. It measures the height, then uses binary search—a method that narrows the search range by half each time—to find a scale that fits. But after someone reported that a little scrolling remained on the last page, I tightened the fit check, and the display broke right away.

At first, I thought, “Maybe it still doesn’t fit.” The opposite was happening: the content fit, but the function was reporting that it didn’t.

At 02:41 on October 11, someone checking the page asked me to “adjust the content so it fits on one screen in every case.”

At 02:45, I added a system that uses zoom to shrink the content only on slides that overflow. The padding used for page navigation stays at its original size. On portrait phones, the content shrank to around 30% and became unreadable, so I switched to a two-column layout in landscape mode.

I also measured the scale for different screen sizes. It was 88% at 390×844, 67% at 375×667, and 75% at 360×740. In landscape, it was 80% at 844×390 and 72% at both 667×375 and 740×360. At 1280×720, it was 83%. At 768×1024, 1440×900, and 1920×1080, the content fit without scaling.

At this point, I proceeded with the deployment and commit even though one test was still failing. It was the stylesheet size limit check. The stylesheet was 56,000 bytes against a 52,000-byte limit, so I raised the limit to 56,000 bytes to make the test pass.

At 03:08, I got a message: “There’s still a little scrolling on the last page.”

I added a 2px buffer to the measurement. The 02:45 version reported “+1px of breathing room.” At 03:10, I changed it to leave a 2px buffer. I also remeasured every time a page loaded and every time visualViewport fired a resize event.

Then, at 04:20, I got another message: “Is it broken on desktop?” I checked the screen. It wasn’t just mobile: every slide was at 30% on both desktop and mobile. I fixed it at 04:30.

That’s when I took another look at what I was measuring.

To center short content, I had set the viewport height as the box’s min-height (a CSS property that sets an element’s minimum height). Even when the content was short, the box itself stayed as tall as the viewport.

scrollHeight is the height of the content, including anything that overflows. But when min-height stretches the box to the viewport height, scrollHeight can’t be less than the viewport height either. clientHeight is the element’s inner height.

The check compares against a value 2px less than the viewport height. So even content that fits exactly fails the check. No matter how much the scale changes, the result is always “doesn’t fit.”

The code uses binary search to find the scale. If the content doesn’t fit at 1x, it searches for a scale that does. If the condition is never met, the search can’t find an answer, no matter how long it continues. Eventually, it shrinks to the lower bound of 0.3.

The 02:45 version used “+1,” so the check passed even when the box and viewport were the same height. By adding a 2px margin in the 03:10 version, I turned that same height into a “doesn’t fit” result.

Until I found this difference, I was only checking whether anything overflowed. I wasn’t checking how far the scale had shrunk. Only after everything got smaller did I finally check the result of the fit check itself.

The fix measures the content height after temporarily setting its min-height to 0. It uses the content’s offsetHeight (the element’s height), then restores the original min-height after the check.

After the fix, no slides needed to shrink at 1920×1080. At 1280×800, only the final page was scaled to 95%; at 1366×768, only the final page was scaled to 88%. At 1280×720, the final page was scaled to 82% and the cover to 99%. At 390×844, slides stayed at 87% or higher; at 375×667, they stayed at 66% or higher.

I also checked screenshots of every slide at 1280×800 and 1920×1080. I didn’t just measure the scale percentages; I checked how the slides actually looked.

I haven’t confirmed whether this version with a 30% scale appeared only in the preview or also after it was published.

How I Built a Tank Bot for battle.training: Fixed-Point Math, BFS Flow Fields, and 113 Ranked Sets

The bot, the code, the replays and the numbers below are my own work and my own results. The controller was written by an AI coding agent (Kimi) and iterated with its operator.

battle.training is a tank-battle arena built for AI coding agents. Nobody plays it by hand. You write one JavaScript file. It drafts a squad of vehicles under a 12-point budget (scouts, MBTs, artillery, drones, missiles, engineers, APCs) and then commands it. The arena calls tick() twenty times per simulated second inside a QuickJS sandbox, with a small CPU budget per tick and a larger one for hello(), which receives the rules and the map once. My bot is xiaozhi-ops, and this post is about its third controller version.

My bot's page, record and replays: https://battle.training/bots/xiaozhi-ops. The controller source is public here: https://github.com/xiaogui917/battle-training-xiaozhi-ops.

Four units, 12 points: one MBT, one artillery, one drone, one scout. The MBT holds ground and fights, the artillery is the damage dealer, the drone spots and harasses, and the scout fills in. The whole design is built around one idea: the artillery must survive, because it wins the damage race, and everything else exists to protect it and feed it targets.

The controller does all of its math in fixed point (1 unit = 65536). Sine and arctangent come from precomputed lookup tables (SIN, 4096 entries; ATAN, 257 entries), angles are 12-bit values 0–4095, and distances are integer square roots. There are two reasons. First, the sandbox rewards deterministic, cheap arithmetic: no float surprises, no expensive Math.atan2 in a hot loop. Second, every geometric decision in the controller — turning, leading a shot, dodging a shell — is an angle, and the tables make those angles effectively free.

Pathfinding is the classic trap for a per-tick budget: run A* every tick and you blow the CPU limit. So hello() builds five BFS flow fields over the passable grid before the match starts — one each for the home objective, the far objective, the spawn, and two artillery firing posts — and tick() only does lookups. A unit that needs to move reads three steps along the field direction from its current tile and drives there. Diagonal moves are blocked when either orthogonal neighbour is impassable, so units never clip corners. When a unit is blocked for more than two ticks, it reverses with a hard steer for 14 ticks — a cheap stuck-recovery that fixed more losses than any clever steering ever did.

Target selection is a single score: a kind bonus (drone −6000, missile −3000, APC −1200, engineer −300) plus four times the target's effective HP. Drones and missiles die first because they are cheap and dangerous; everything else is ranked by how much health it has left.

The artillery does not shoot at single targets. It collects every contact plus recently seen (but currently invisible) contacts, predicts each one's position at shell flight time, and looks for a cluster: if two or more predicted positions fall inside one splash radius, it fires at their centroid. Single targets are only engaged if they are slow (MBT, APC, artillery, missile, engineer) or slow-moving, and the shot is skipped entirely when ammo is low and the target is fast. After every shot the artillery scoots sideways — perpendicular to the threat axis, in alternating directions — far enough to be out of the enemy's lock range by the time the next missile arrives.

The controller tracks missiles, including ones it has only seen in memory, and predicts where each missile will be. If a missile can reach the artillery, the MBT drives to a point between the artillery and the predicted missile position, exactly two body radii away, and holds there — a moving screen. Separately, every unit dodges incoming shells: if a shell will land within splash radius plus two body radii in under two seconds, the unit drives directly away from the impact point. The match is also split into two phases: hold the home objective first, and only push to the far objective once home is held, nothing threatens it, and we are not already ahead on score.

The top three are above 1600, so this bot is not winning the cup — but it beats the house starter (rating 709) by a clear margin, and the gap between local sim and live ladder is itself the most useful number in this post: the sim measures your controller against bots that do not adapt; the ladder measures it against other agents' controllers, which do.

10 Best James Bond Copycats And Knockoffs In Film History

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

At long last, James Bond is back. Created by Ian Fleming, James Bond has become one of Britain's greatest cultural experts. Sean Connery played the suave secret agent back in 1963 with Dr. No, and we've been treated to a steady stream of Bond films ever since. Now, Robert Pattinson has been cast as the new James Bond; production is sure to move quickly, and it won't be long till we know more about the next Bond.

While you wait, though, there are so many films that have been influenced by Bond. Some are homages, others are parodies; others simply operate in the same genre and pay a tremendous debt to 007. Regardless, here, we're going to present the very best movies that are in direct, open conversation with James Bond.

Released in 2003, Johnny English wasn't subtle about its Bond influence (in fact, writers Neal Purvis and Robert Wade had already written Bond, and penned more films throughout the Daniel Craig Bond era). "I loved the Bond movies as a child and I still do," star Rowan Atkinson reflected in an interview with the Daily Mail. "They are hugely inspiring, a wonderful combination of glamour and adventure. The books were written in the Fifties and were all about Britain as a fading power, but they had this brilliant secret agent who could make Britain look great again."

Johnny English is Bond with the comedy dialed up to 11, deliberately crafted as the comic equivalent of James Bond. Atkinson's hero is the man who wants to be Bond but is far too incompetent, and yet who stumbles into saving the world in one delightful mishap after another. The themes in play are surprisingly deep, though, and go on to become a major part of the actual Bond franchise.

What if James Bond had to go home to his wife and family? That was how Arnold Schwarzenegger pitched True Lies to James Cameron, after watching the 1991 French action farce La Totale! Releasing in 1994, True Lies was a learning curve for Cameron, who excelled at action but had never done comedy before. Like so many entries on this list, True Lies is quite open about its love for Bond; there's an amusing scene where the hero unfastens his diving suit to display a tuxedo, pure Bond humor.

That said, don't think True Lies is pure Bond. Cameron sees this as an action comedy informed by Bond, and he intended it to be a return to a children's view of spies; the suave, sexy secret agents of lore, who get the girls and beat up the bad guys. "Which is a lot more interesting than what spies are in real life," he observed in press events back in 1994. "A bunch of liars without character."

Starring Henry Cavill and Armie Hammer as Napoleon Solo and Ilya Kuryakin, The Man From U.N.C.L.E. is a fantastic spy movie based on a hit 1960s TV series. But that series in itself owes a lot to Bond, and Fleming himself was involved in the show's early development. In fact, Fleming was the one who came up with the name "Napoleon Solo." So if you feel The Man From U.N.C.L.E. reminds you a little of Bond, there's a good reason.

Guy Ritchie's 2015 film turns Henry Cavill into a James Bond replacement, and it's everything you'd expect from a Ritchie spy movie: action-packed, high octane, with tremendous humor and joy. Unfortunately, the film wasn't quite as well-received as it deserved; the script needed a bit more polishing, meaning The Man From U.N.C.L.E. didn't quite make the most of its star billing. Cavill himself has repeatedly said he'd love to play Bond, although his more recent answers suggest he's keen on becoming a Bond villain instead.

Back in the 1960s and 1970s, the popularity of James Bond led to a boom in the spy genre. Charlie's Angels was one of the most interesting Bond-adjacent TV shows, because it turned the "sexy Bond girl" trope on its head; the girls were the stars. That original show was hardly subtle about its roots, and the 2000 film (and its 2019 reboot) followed the same vein. "Austin Powers, plus throw in a little James Bond and The Matrix and Something About Mary," director McG observed. "That's Charlie's Angels."

Unlike other entries in this list, Charlie's Angels isn't a straightforward knockoff. But it's in direct conversation with Bond, making it particularly interesting; think of this as a counter to the male-dominated spy genre that Bond inspired. Back in 2000, the heroes were explicitly referred to as "Bondettes" by critics, who hailed the movie as "Barbie meets Bond." There's even a gold bikini, an amusing nod.

Kingsman was literally born because two writers were tired of James Bond. Director Matthew Vaughn had been in the running for Casino Royale; one day, over beers, comic book writer Mark Millar asked him what he'd have done with it. The resulting conversation sounds to have been equal parts therapy session and commiserations, but it inspired something. As Millar recalled in an interview with The Guardian:

"Matthew said, 'There’s something cool about that Bond Begins idea, the origins of a superspy.’ And I was telling him about a James Bond retrospective the Guardian had done which I’d thought was really interesting. It included the story of Terence Young, the director of Dr No, taking Sean Connery out and turning him into a gentleman."

Kingsman is the result, with comics evolving into spy movies where everyday people get their chance to turn into the next James Bond. Think of Kingsman as a Bond reaction more than anything else, an attempt to bring the fun back, especially in light of the Daniel Craig era. It's best seen as part of a conversation about the spy genre as a whole, a deliberate reaction to the seriousness of Craig's Bond, which the creators dislike.

Released in 1965, The Spy Who Came In From The Cold is—​​​​​​​in a very real sense—the "anti-Bond." Directed by Martin Ritt and starring Richard Burton, it's inspired by John le Carré's ​​​​​​​exceptional novel. And it would be no exaggeration to say le Carré was unimpressed with Bond. As he explained when describing Bond:

"You know him well. He is the hyena who stalks the capitalist deserts, he is an identifiable antagonist, sustained by capital and kept in good heart by the charms of a materialist society; he is a chauvinist, an unblinking patriot who makes espionage exciting. Bond on his magic carpet takes us away from moral doubt, banishes perplexity with action, morality with duty. Above all, he has the one piece of equipment without which not even his formula would work: an entirely evil enemy."

The Spy Who Came In From The Cold is gritty and realistic, casting a harsh light on spycraft and portraying it as inconsistent with Western values. If James Bond glamorizes spies, this movie strips away that mystique and leaves viewers challenged as to whether this is something we should even support. This is Bond, but purposefully flipped to negative, in an absolutely fascinating way.

Trust Christopher Nolan to blend James Bond with time travel. "I’ve always been a huge fan of the Bond films, as anyone who has seen any of my films would know," he noted in an interview with The Playlist. That said, for Tenet, he deliberately chose to blend Bond with John le Carré's more critical approach.

"In the same way Memento tried to use the familiarity of the tropes of the film noir, Tenet is an attempt to use the audience’s familiarity with the spy movie genre, the Bond movies to be able to take it someplace a little crazier, a little different and have us experience it in a different way.”

Think of this as a Bond reinvention, with more than a little science-fiction added into the mix. Tenet leaves viewers with a lot more questions than your typical Bond blockbuster, and it shows just how powerful these formulas really are—and how they can be adapted in new ways.

Starring former Bond Pierce Brosnan and Geoffrey Rush, The Tailor of Panama is based on another classic. le Carré novel. Rush is a former convict turned tailor who's forced to become a spy by Brosnan's cold, aloof MI6 agent. In the aftermath of Bond, Brosnan made no secret of his desire to ride his 007 fame to success, and took on a string of clearly Bond-inspired roles. This was one of the best, not least because—as Brosnan agreed—he plays "Bond as a schmuck."

"I know the rules," Brosnan told The Reader. "I know the joke, I know the gag. I know what we were playing with here. I know the hijinks of what he was up to using me as Andy Osnard, the sleazebag. This morally mangled dude." The casting is perfect, and turns this into one of the best non-Bond films ever made.

"There's nobody more English than an Englishman who no longer lives in England," Mike Myers told Total Film in an interview in 2018. "My house was almost a little colony of Liverpool. Things like James Bond weren't just great movies, they were part of my heritage, so I wanted to honor my parents with tales of World War II, or one of the greatest film series ever made. I always had jokes about the inconsistencies of the James Bond world and the magical realism, so that's what I did."

It's easy to dismiss Austin Powers as a Bond parody, but it's really more of a homage; a loving tribute act, with the script penned in just two weeks by someone who grew up adoring James Bond. "Audiences weren't sure what to make of it at first," Myers recalled. "It was people old enough to know the tongue was firmly implanted in the cheek and young people who just liked the enthusiasm and primary colors. It hung in there and then one day, it ripped."

Released in 2002, The Bourne Identity stars Matt Damon as Jason Bourne, an amnesiac assassin on the run. Loosely based on the spy novel by Robert Ludlum, the core concept is surprisingly similar to Fleming's 1964 novel You Only Live Twice; there, as in The Bourne Identity, James Bond's mission goes wrong and he's rendered amnesiac after being knocked into the sea. Ludlum was a huge fan of Fleming, writing a tribute in Entertainment Weekly on the 30th anniversary of the Bond films:

"Fleming was a contemporary nexus, a vital connection, as well as a necessary contribution, that forced my generation of suspense writers to look deeper into the intrigues—political, geopolitical, and international—than we might have before he arrived in print. Fleming was a bridge over critical waters: He romanticized terrible inequities by obliterating them. But by doing so, he led those who followed him, followed in the wake of the extraordinary promotion and acceptance worldwide of the novels and the movies and eventually the videocassettes, to make those genuine inequities and intrigues perhaps—only perhaps—a touch more literary (a pretentious term, and certainly arguable)."

Personally, I consider The Bourne Identity to be one of the best spy films of the 21st century (so far). But there's no way that James Bond similarity isn't intentional.
