Sylvester Stallone’s Role In I Play Rocky Casting Confirmed By Director

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

I Play Rocky director Peter Farrelly reveals the role Sylvester Stallone played in the movie's casting.

The biopic about the making of Rocky features Anthony Ippolito playing both Stallone and the titular character. In terms of his involvement with the movie, Stallone previously said he was "blindsided" and "shocked to read [about] it. I have zero to do with it. Since I lived it, I thought I might be able to participate and give them some insight." Farrelly later shared that a script was sent to the original Rocky star and writer, who gave his blessing to proceed with the project.

In an interview with The Ankler, Farrelly has now explained that "when I met with Sylvester Stallone to get his blessing, he had one request. He goes, 'If you could, I'd like you to hire a relative unknown because that's what I was.' And I think that was part of the charm of the character is that nobody really knew him before."

At the time, Farrelly and his creative team had started discussing who should be cast, but the official process had not begun yet. Then "Anthony [Ippolito] heard about it, tracked down the script, put himself on tape in his grandmother's basement in Long Island, did three scenes, including 'the thing' scene at the end when he's at the door, and I sent it to Rick Montgomery, my casting agent. He sent it to me. I was like, that's it. It's over."

Ippolito starred in Adam Sandler's critically panned 2015 movie Pixels and played Al Pacino in Paramount+'s 2022 miniseries The Offer, but he was a relative unknown before being cast as Rocky. As for how Farrelly knew he was the right choice, he reflects on how "people say, 'How'd you know? What gave you the courage to take a chance on him?' I was like, 'I wasn't taking a chance. It was fully formed.'"

Farrelly continues, "This kid came in and he's a genius. He knew the difference between Stallone and Rocky, and there's a subtle difference . . . Anthony Ippolito didn't do a caricature of either. When you're looking at him as Stallone, it's like he is Stallone."

While Stallone was not directly involved in the casting process for I Play Rocky, his advice was taken into account and honored by casting Ippolito, with this being the 27-year-old actor's most high-profile role yet. Per Farrelly's explanation, the selection was about more than Stallone's advice, though, as it had more to do with Ippolito being able to seamlessly play both the real-life artist and the iconic fictional character he brought to life.

Following its premiere at the 2026 Toronto International Film Festival, the movie has received an 87% Tomatometer score based on 62 reviews. In ScreenRant's I Play Rocky review, George Bate gave it a rating of nine out of 10 stars. In terms of Ippolito's performance, he wrote that it "produces the kind of awards-worthy performance that actors make careers out of; there is more heart and humor in a single scene than most movies possess in their total runtime; and, perhaps most surprising of all, Farrelly creates a film that somehow matches the inspiration fostered by watching Rocky."

George Benson Feted by Wyclef Jean & More at Jazz Foundation Fundraiser: ‘Just One Way to Give Back’

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Patrice Rushen was also among the honorees, while guests included Herb Alpert and legendary songwriter Mike Stoller.

Ten-time Grammy Award winner George Benson is the 2026 recipient of the Tommy LiPuma Award for Recording Excellence. The honor was presented by the Jazz Foundation of America (JFA) during its fundraising event at Vibrato Grill Jazz in Los Angeles on Sunday (Nov. 1).

The evening’s additional lifetime achievement honorees included four esteemed musicians saluted together as Titans of Jazz: pianist/NEA Jazz Master Patrice Rushen (also known for her 1982 R&B/dance hit “Forget Me Nots”), saxophonist Azar Lawrence (whose credits include Elvin Jones and Miles Davis), bassist Henry “The Skipper” Franklin (Roy Ayers, Hugh Masekela) and drummer Roy McCurdy (Cannonball Adderley, Ella Fitzgerald). Also honored for her longtime support of the music community was philanthropist/Vibrato managing partner Eden Alpert, who received the Quincy Jones Love & Peace Humanitarian Award from Jones’ daughter Martina Jones. Music icon Jones, who died in 2024, was an honorary founder of the JFA.

Noting Alpert’s commitment to JFA by “hosting benefits at Vibrato over the past decade,” JFA executive director Joe Petrucelli said, “Through her philanthropic work and leadership, Eden has provided opportunities for veteran and emerging musicians alike while contributing to the vitality of the live music community in Los Angeles.”

Legendary songwriter Mike Stoller of Leiber & Stoller presented Benson’s award. “I first heard of George as a wonderful jazz guitar player,” Stoller said in part. “Then I found out he could do other things. He later made four songwriters very happy —  Barry Mann and wife Cynthia Weil, my late partner Jerry Lieber and me — as we were thrilled to hear a version of a song we wrote as performed by him, and he knocked us down. The song was ‘On Broadway.’ [This award] is so deserved.”

During his acceptance speech, Benson name-checked several people who played pivotal roles in his career. In addition to Stoller, those people included Herb Alpert, frontman of famed band Tijuana Brass and co-founder of A&M Records — who helped Benson “buy my first house” — as well as late acclaimed producer Tommy LiPuma, who helmed Benson’s game-changing 1976 album Breezin’.

“Tonight is an incredible night for a lot of reasons,” said Benson. “I’m sharing it with people who love the same things that I love. And thanks for contributions to the auction because I know what it’s going to do for some very deserving people who gave us a lot of the wonderful music that we’ve enjoyed for many years. This is just one way to give some of that back.”

Grammy-winning drummer Steve Jordan, who currently plays with The Rolling Stones, served as the event’s artistic director. Accompanied by a band of top-notch musicians, Jordan saluted Benson through a rousing set of the latter’s classics: “Breezin’” and “This Masquerade” featuring guitarist Isaiah Sharkey, “Turn Your Love Around” and “Give Me the Night” sung by Aloe Blacc and “On Broadway” with Grammy winner Wyclef Jean. NEA Jazz Master Carmen Lundy led the lineup of the evening’s performers with her opening set, followed by the Titans of Jazz.

Rounding out the evening was a live auction. Among the items up for bid were an autographed Ibanez George Benson Signature Line guitar with case (sold for $6,500) and “On Broadway” sheet music as performed by Benson and signed by Stoller ($2,000).

Proceeds from the evening benefit the JFA’s Musicians’ Emergency Fund, which provides housing assistance, healthcare, disaster relief and employment opportunities. It’s all in keeping with the organization’s 37 years-and-counting mission: “Honoring jazz, blues & roots … one musician at a time.”

Karol G Sweeps Latin Airplay’s Top 3 With ‘No Me Arrepiento de Sentir Tanto’ Tracks

"Thank you to everyone who listens to these songs, makes them their own, and takes them to places we sometimes never even imagined."

Sr. Charts & Data Analyst (Latin & Billboard Español)

Karol G makes history becoming the first artist on Billboard’s 32-year-old Latin Airplay chart to hold Nos. 1, 2 and 3 simultaneously with songs from one album, on the chart dated Nov. 7. Further, she’s just the third act, and only woman, to hold the entire top three, following Bad Bunny and J Balvin, who both did it in 2019 — but not with songs from the same album.

“It brings me so much happiness to see how music continues to connect with people in this way,” Karol G shares with Billboard. “Having three songs occupy the top three spots on the Latin Airplay chart is something that thrills me and fills me with pride — but above all, it makes me feel deeply grateful.”

As “BbY WOW” is back on top of the overall Latin Airplay chart, Karol sweeps the top three. The track, with Judeline and rusowsky, returns to No. 1 for a third week, generating 11.1 million audience impressions in the United States during the Oct. 23-29 tracking week, up 29%, according to Luminate. All three songs come from her album No Me Arrepiento de Sentir Tanto, released in August, which spent 11 weeks at No. 1 on Top Latin Albums.

“Karol is in a special moment where different songs are connecting with different audiences at the same time,” Jose Cedeño, EVP & GM of Interscope Capitol Miami, tells Billboard. “Each record has its own story and momentum, and our job is to support what fans and radio are responding to. We don’t see these songs as competing with each other. There’s real demand for all three, and seeing them at No. 1, No. 2 and No. 3 on Latin Airplay speaks to that connection and the range of her music.”

“BbY WOW” trades places with “Ahí,” with Drake, which dips to No. 2 after one week in charge, with 8.3 million impressions, down 10%. Meanwhile, “Alguien Que Te Amaba,” climbs to a new No. 3 high, up 31% to 7.6 million impressions.

“Behind every song, there is a story, a great deal of hard work, and an incredible team that makes it possible for the music to reach so far,” says Karol. “Thank you to everyone who listens to these songs, makes them their own, and takes them to places we sometimes never even imagined.”

While Karol G goes 1-2-3 on Latin Airplay with songs from the same album, Bad Bunny and J Balvin tripled up atop the list for a week each in August and September 2019, respectively.

On the August 3, 2019-dated ranking, Benito was credited on all three top spots. Starting with “Callaita,” from his album Un Verano Sin Ti, the song surged 7-1 for his first and only week in charge. The No. 2 spot belonged to “Que Pretendes,” with J Balvin, from their collaborative album, OASIS. Standalone cut “Soltera,” with Lunay and Daddy Yankee, rounded out the top three at No. 3.

J Balvin took over the top three on the chart dated Sept. 28, 2019. “Que Pretendes,” with Benito, led at No. 1. “No Me Conoce,” from Jhayco’s album Famouz, also with Bunny, rose to No. 2, and “China,” the all-star collaboration by Anuel AA, Daddy Yankee, Karol G, Balvin and Ozuna, came in at No. 3.

Elsewhere, “BbY WOW” adds an 11th week at No. 1 on the multi-metric Hot Latin Songs chart — the second-longest command in 2026 (behind Bad Bunny’s 31-week reign this year — 72 overall — through “DtMF”).

Villarreal 0-6 Paris Saint-Germain: Fabian scores twice as clinical champions make history

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Syndicated reporting provided by Stats Perform and reviewed by the SoccerNews editorial team.

Fabian Ruiz scored twice as Paris Saint-Germain made history in their 6-0 battering of Villarreal that saw them stay perfect through four matches of the Champions League.

The defending champions were four goals to the good inside just 17 first-half minutes at Estadio de la Ceramica on Tuesday. Indeed, the four goals they scored after 16 minutes and two seconds is the fastest a team has ever scored four goals in the competition's history.

Joao Neves' clever flick got the ball rolling, before Desire Doue and Senny Mayulu benefited from two poor Peter Gulacsi clearances, and Fabian hammered in a fourth.

Fabian was on target again five minutes after the restart and teenage defender David Boly bagged off the bench as Luis Enrique's side made it 12 points from a possible 12.

Neves got PSG up and running, ingeniously picking out the top corner inside the six-yard box when Villarreal failed to clear their lines at a ninth-minute corner.

Gulacsi made a hash of a routine clearance that led to Doue rolling into the bottom-left corner after being teed up by Maghnes Akliouche, and another poor moment from the Villarreal goalkeeper eventually led to Mayulu pouncing with an effort he drilled into the floor after Neves' effort was blocked.

By the time Fabian slashed a bouncing ball into the bottom-left corner in the 17th minute, it was over as a contest, though the Spaniard and Doue almost stretched the lead before half-time.

Fabian had a wonderful second early in the second half, brilliantly taking down Vitinha's long pass, cutting inside Renato Veiga and flicking past Gulacsi.

There was time for another, as Neves picked out Boly on the right of the area, and the 17-year-old smashed his effort high past Gulacsi via a deflection off Veiga before wheeling away in delight at his first Champions League goal.

Binance's investigation does not establish that an AI model autonomously planned or executed the theft. Rather, the attackers exploited an internal AI tool as part of a broader campaign involving stolen credentials, compromised developer software, and social engineering.

In June, a Binance engineer downloaded a tampered software installer while attempting to install a popular AI coding tool. Binance detected the suspicious activity, replaced the device and rotated associated credentials, although information about the employee and colleagues had already been exposed.

In September, another engineer fell victim to a fake recruitment operation involving staged job interviews and a malicious coding assignment. The malware compromised the employee's computer and gave the attackers access, which they later used to move deeper into Binance's systems.

The FBI has attributed the broader attack to North Korea, whose hacking operations have increasingly targeted software developers and cryptocurrency businesses.

The Binance incident follows months of warnings from cybersecurity researchers that artificial intelligence is expanding both hackers' capabilities and the systems available for exploitation.

In February, Google's Mandiant reported that North Korean hackers targeting the cryptocurrency industry had incorporated AI-assisted techniques into their operations.

The researchers documented an intrusion involving a fake video conference, malicious software, and a suspected AI-generated video impersonating a corporate executive. They could not independently confirm the deepfake in that particular incident but identified the group's use of AI tools for reconnaissance and social engineering.

By September, Google's Threat Intelligence Group reported that attackers were moving beyond using AI to generate malicious content toward automated operations that could execute multiple stages of an intrusion.

In one case, hackers compromised a cloud resource and organized an AI-assisted mass credential-harvesting campaign in less than six hours. Google also identified attackers attempting to manipulate AI coding assistants and security scanners during software supply-chain compromises.

The findings closely resemble one aspect of the Binance breach: developers and the AI-powered tools they depend on are becoming targets attackers use to reach sensitive systems.

Separately, AI developer Anthropic disclosed in September that criminals and suspected state-sponsored operators had used its Claude models to conduct reconnaissance, develop exploitation tools, and automate data theft.

Some operations involved AI agents carrying out multiple stages of an attack with limited human supervision, reducing the technical expertise and manpower traditionally required.

The exchange is now seeking support from the same industry whose technologies were featured in the attack.

Binance said it is working to establish a security research fund and develop targeted initiatives with leading AI companies, blockchain industry participants and cybersecurity firms.

The proposed collaboration will focus on strengthening AI security in blockchain environments, although the exchange has not disclosed the fund's proposed size, participating companies or launch date.

It also plans to expand spending on AI-enabled threat detection, endpoint protection, developer-environment security and behavioral anomaly monitoring.

The exchange said it has already rebuilt the affected internal AI tool and wallet infrastructure, replaced compromised employee devices and strengthened access controls.

Binance has also commissioned an independent cybersecurity review to validate its investigation and identify additional safeguards.

The company said approximately $2.85 billion of the $6.34 billion in affected digital assets has been frozen or intercepted, while 98% of impacted token assets had been replenished as of Oct. 31.

The independent review has yet to establish the full extent of the weaknesses that allowed attackers to exploit the exchange's internal AI environment. Its findings will inform the security changes Binance plans to implement as it develops the proposed industry fund.

Where to Find the Small Worlds Most Photographers Walk Past

Peter Baumgarten was looking for centimeter-tall mushrooms when he spotted a cluster of orange caps on a rotting log. A few warm, rainy days had encouraged mushrooms to emerge in Canada’s McLean’s Park, a forest on Ontario’s Manitoulin Island where he regularly walks his dog. At six feet two inches tall, Baumgarten could easily walk past mushrooms that small if he weren’t specifically looking for them.

Full disclosureThis article was brought to you by OM SYSTEM.

Peter Baumgarten is just one of a number of OM SYSTEM ambassadors who specialize in macro photography and shared with PetaPixel their favorite places to look for small subjects and the encounters that keep them returning time and time again. For Lisa and Tom Cuchara, that includes the meadows and woodland around Little River in southern Maine. Chris McGinnis searches the rainforests of Costa Rica and Panama, while also expanding his macro photography portfolio in Maine and his own backyard.

Their photographs show the miniature world that can turn up on a rotting log or a garden stem. Baumgarten gets low on the forest floor to photograph mushrooms and the slugs that feed on them. The Cucharas and McGinnis have both made some of their favorite finds after dark, the Cucharas in a garden in the Northeast and McGinnis at a pond in Panama.

In one of Baumgarten’s favorite macro photographs, a garden slug stretches along rotting wood beneath a cluster of orange mushrooms, its head raised toward one of the caps.

“What excited me the most was the garden slug getting ready to chow down, which isn’t a common shot,” he says.

Baumgarten takes many of his macro shots at a park close to home. McLean’s Park has several kilometers of trails through mixed forest, which he uses for hiking, biking, cross-country skiing, snowshoeing, and photography.

“It is about a 15-minute drive from my home,” he says. “I go there several times a week to walk my dog. As a mature forest there are plenty of fallen trees and branches that make for the perfect environment for a bunch of different types of mushrooms.”

“What I love about the park is the variety of photographs I can take there,” he notes. “One day I might photograph a person walking their dog in autumn along a leaf-covered trail, with tall trees and yellow foliage all around. Another day, I might be down among the fallen logs and branches photographing mushrooms.”

Baumgarten likes to shoot most of his mushroom macro photos below eye level. “I love capturing them from underneath,” he explains. “For me, the gill structure is perhaps the most interesting and photogenic part of the mushroom. I want a soft background that doesn’t pull focus from my subject. By shooting from just below the mushroom I can usually include the more distant, lush greenery of the forest.”

“While mushrooms don’t move, when I’m photographing a slug in the low light of the forest, I have to be aware that even its slow movement can cause blur,” he continues. “Shooting handheld, I might use a shutter speed of around 1/100 second to keep it sharp, raising the ISO if I need to.”

Bumping up the ISO, the camera’s sensitivity to light, lets him shoot at a faster shutter speed when the light on the forest floor is dim. With a mushroom, he can usually keep the ISO low and slow the shutter down instead. The image stabilization in his OM SYSTEM OM-3 lets him handhold exposures as long as half a second, even while using in-camera focus stacking, which combines the sharp areas of photographs focused at different distances to keep more of a close-up subject in focus.

“I capture mushrooms in a very similar way to how I photograph wildlife,” Baumgarten says. “For many wildlife shots I prefer to use my long lens. This gives me great close-up images of my subject. But a great close-up is not always the best way to showcase the animal. Showing the animal in its environment helps me tell more of the story about where it lives. That’s when I turn to using a wider lens. I will often do the same when photographing mushrooms.”

Mushrooms can appear quickly and may not stay in good condition for long, so Baumgarten recommends watching the weather to help decide when to go looking.

“Mushrooms need warm, moist conditions, so I recommend exploring one or two days after a good rain,” he advises. “I have had great success from late spring to late autumn. I recommend avoiding bright sunny days. The dappled sunlight on the forest floor can work for some shots, but often it is more of a hindrance than a help.”

A subject that stays put gives photographers new to macro more time to experiment with focus and composition.

“Photographing mushrooms is a great way to get into macro shooting,” he adds. “I love the geometry of most mushrooms and the infinite patience they have. I’ve never had a mushroom run away from me as I sneak up on it.”

Those quiet searches are also part of why he keeps returning to the forest.

“I often turn to macro photography to get me out of a rut,” Baumgarten says. “I primarily shoot landscapes and wildlife and over the years there have been many stretches of time where I just couldn’t capture shots that I was happy with. It’s at these times, when I feel a bit down, that I grab my macro lens and head into the forest for a bit of exploration and de-stressing. I slow down, observe carefully, and invariably find something in the macro world to photograph. And the best part is, I don’t have to go far. Macro subjects are everywhere, and a local park or garden can be a great place to start looking. I’m lucky to have McLean’s Park just a short drive from home.”

For Lisa and Tom Cuchara, some of the strangest subjects turn up after dark.

“One evening I headed into the garden to look for tiny nighttime wonders,” Lisa Cuchara recalls. “Imagine my surprise when I found a bright green hornworm sporting what looked like dozens of tiny white marshmallows. Those little white blobs weren’t eggs or fungus, though. They were the silken cocoons of tiny parasitoid wasps. The wasp larvae had developed inside the living caterpillar before coming out to spin their cocoons. It was creepy and fascinating at the same time.”

Gardeners don’t like hornworms, since a hungry one can make a massive amount of tomato foliage disappear overnight. The wasps, most likely a Cotesia species, are another matter.

“This was a perfect example of nature’s own pest control,” Lisa explains. “Unlike regular parasites, which generally don’t kill their hosts, parasitoids kill the animal they develop in. These tiny wasps help keep hornworm numbers down, which makes them real allies for gardeners.”

“When I saw this scene, I knew that the challenge was getting the detail of both the caterpillar and those wonderfully wonky white cocoons while working handheld in the dark,” Lisa recalls. “Even at f/11, there just isn’t enough depth of field to get everything sharp in a single exposure.”

An aperture of f/11 is a small lens opening that would normally extend depth of field, the zone of a photograph that looks sharp. In-camera focus stacking, used with the Cucharas’ OM-1 Mark II and the M.Zuiko Digital ED 90mm F3.5 Macro IS PRO lens, got around the problem.

“I was able to shoot a focus-stacked image handheld, without setting up a tripod or disturbing my subject,” Lisa notes.

For insects, the Cucharas use a small flash fitted with a diffuser.

“A bare flash gives you harsh highlights and deep shadows, especially on an insect’s shiny exoskeleton,” the Cucharas explain. “Using a diffuser turns that small flash into a much larger, softer light compared to a tiny subject. The flash also helps freeze movement, whether it’s a slight breeze moving a leaf or an insect shifting position.”

“Without a flashlight and a willingness to explore the garden after sunset, I might have walked right past an extraordinary biological story unfolding just inches away,” Lisa adds.

On other outings, the Cucharas have photographed a moth laying eggs at night and a crab spider grabbing an unsuspecting bumblebee. They’ve also watched a caterpillar that had just shed its outer skin, known as a molt.

“A black swallowtail caterpillar will eat its own molt for energy,” they add. “Those spontaneous moments of seeing something so unique are just one reason we enjoy macro photography so much.”

The Cucharas have turned their own yard into a wildlife habitat, replacing most of the lawn with the goal of mainly native plants grown without pesticides or herbicides. They also lead macro and close-up photography retreats at Little River in North Berwick, Maine.

“We love places where you can move just a few feet and find a whole different world,” they say.

Insects are cold-blooded, so how active they are depends on the temperature and conditions around them.

“Pick a rainy, drizzly, or overcast day,” the Cucharas advise. “Insects like to hang out under the leaves and they’re less likely to move. And get there early, because a lot of insects are cold and slow in the early hours, which gives you more time to photograph them.”

“Slow down and look under every leaf,” they continue. “Don’t rush from flower to flower; pick a small area and explore it from a few angles. You start to see a jumping spider on a stalk, holes in a leaf from insects eating, or a curled leaf with a caterpillar inside.”

“If you’re just starting to practice macro photography, a stationary flower with some personality, pollen, or water droplets is a wonderful starting point,” the Cucharas note. “A flower past its prime, a crooked stem, a curled petal, or an oddly shaped mushroom can be a far more compelling story than a flawless blossom. And learn to notice the background, because it’s just as important as the subject.”

“At one of our retreats, six groups rotated through my session to photograph a swallowtail chrysalis on its host plant,” Lisa recalls. “It gave me a chance to talk about fennel and other host plants, and about how to photograph it without disturbing the spot or making it more obvious to predators. No photograph is worth possible harm to the subject, including insects and the plants they’re on.”

Lisa also says that it’s not necessary to have a large piece of land to start creating your own macro photography sanctuary. “I always recommend planting more native and host plants everywhere, even if it’s just a few planters at your condo. Native plants are what attract the insects that look fascinating in a photograph.”

Even with their own garden, the Cucharas still head out to other spots for macro photography. However, unlike static landscape overlooks, macro photography is always delivering something new. “We often visit the same places again and again, and we always come home with different photographs,” the Cucharas explain. “Flowers open and fade, insect species change from month to month, and the light and weather change. Macro photography might be the best genre for photographers who are always looking to shoot, and learn, something new.”

Chris McGinnis has captured some of his favorite macro photographs after dark, in the rainforests of Costa Rica and Panama.

McGinnis says that one of the most interesting subjects in the Central American rainforest is the red-eyed tree frog. “With these frogs, I am constantly surprised with the variety of moments I am able to capture them in, and maybe my favorite moment was on a trip to Panama with fellow OM SYSTEM ambassador Lee Hoy,” McGinnis remembers. “We had some workshop clients with us, and they faded one by one and went off to sleep, but Lee and I stayed out shooting. We were on this little pond, looking for frogs, and we’d found and photographed several. All of a sudden Lee says, ‘Red-eyed tree frogs in amplexus.’ I said, ‘What? What do you mean?’, not knowing what amplexus meant. Lee pointed to a branch and there were two red-eyed tree frogs with the male on top of the female. I’ve photographed hundreds of these frogs before, but I had never seen them do this. The shot was a unique addition to my portfolio, and I learned a new word in the process.”

According to McGinnis, red-eyed tree frogs spend the day higher up in the trees and come down toward the water as the sun goes down, which is why he looks for them at night. But whether it’s day or night, he often chooses to shoot macro photographs with a non-macro lens.

“That night with the frogs was a time when I could have used my M.Zuiko Digital ED 90mm F3.5 Macro IS PRO,” he recalls. “I wanted to be up really close, but I wanted a wider view so the story was the frogs in their environment, as that can make a more interesting shot.”

Instead, he had the M.Zuiko Digital ED 8-25mm F4.0 PRO on his camera that night. It’s a wide-angle zoom that can focus close enough to fill the frame with a frog while still showing the branch the pair was sitting on.

“I don’t think I could ever get tired of searching for red-eyed tree frogs,” he adds. “It’s an addiction for me. I find one, I photograph it until it jumps away or I’ve exhausted every angle I can get. Then I could go back to my room and go to sleep, or I could try to find another one. And I have a really hard time walking away, because that next one might be something I’ve never seen before, like capturing two red-eyed tree frogs in amplexus.”

“The biggest challenge of the rainforest at night is getting lost,” McGinnis warns. “So I always get a guide, as there is incredible value in going out with someone who knows their way around the rainforest. Not to mention the danger hiding on the ground and in the trees. Central America has lots of venomous snakes. You grab the wrong thing, you step in the wrong place, and you could be in trouble. A local guide knows how to keep you from accidentally running into one of them.”

Rain is a given in a rainforest. “Photographing in the rain is challenging because it can be harder to find subjects or keep your clothing dry, but luckily I never have to worry about my camera gear in the rain, which eliminates one of the challenges,” he notes. With IP53-rated weather sealing, his OM-1 Mark II can keep shooting through snow, dust, and rain. “I’ve been shooting days on end in pouring, torrential rain in Central America, and I’ve never had a problem with my gear.”

When McGinnis isn’t finding small subjects in the Central American rainforest, he also helps lead multi-day macro retreats with Lisa and Tom Cuchara and other OM SYSTEM ambassadors, on fellow ambassador Steve Morello’s property in southern Maine, as well as other locations. One of his favorite subjects on Morello’s property is dragonflies.

“If you find a dragonfly as the sun’s coming up, they’re not warm yet,” McGinnis advises. “They are cold and they might have dew on their wings, making it harder for them to fly. They’re almost like sitting ducks at that time, and much easier to photograph than in the heat of the day, when they’re busy hunting.”

He also searches his own backyard after dark, where wolf spiders and other hunting spiders give themselves away. Their eyes have a reflective layer that bounces a flashlight beam straight back, a glint known as eyeshine.

“In my own backyard, I love to photograph spiders and praying mantises after dark,” he continues. “Spiders hunt at night, and they are easier to find than most people realize. If you hold your flashlight up to the side of your head and scan the grass about 10 to 15 feet ahead of you, the eyeballs of a spider will flicker back at you. In the pitch black, all you’re looking for is those little diamonds in the grass. Having the flashlight right to the side of your eye keeps your line of sight in line with the beam of the flashlight.”

Some of McGinnis’s favorite discoveries don’t happen until he’s back at his computer. “I was trying to get this mantis to cooperate, and I ended up with tons of shots of it in different poses.”

“As I was reviewing them, I noticed a little something else in the shot that was invisible to my naked eye,” he continues. “That mite on its head is very, very tiny, just one or two millimeters. That’s actually happened to me many times, and finding those tiny surprises when I zoom in on the computer is one of the best things about macro photography.”

McGinnis says this is a great way for both beginners and seasoned macro photography veterans to practice and capture unique photos. “Go outside with a flashlight and scan the yard, and you’re bound to find something. No matter where you live, especially in the summertime, go out after dark with your camera and a light. On a nice, warm summer night, it’s just really fun. I’m exploring this small world, and through that exploration, new things reveal themselves. That’s what keeps me going back. Even if it’s my backyard, a local park, or a tropical rainforest, I’m always discovering new things and finding interesting stories in the small world that most other photographers walk by.”

Is Palantir Stock a Buy After its Latest Earnings Report?

Revenue nearly doubled, but a slowdown in contract growth has investors on edge.

I've liked Palantir Technologies (PLTR -1.65%) stock for a long time and even predicted earlier this year that it would reach $220 before the end of 2026.

Unfortunately, after the company's third-quarter earnings report, it appears that prediction won't come true. But I'm still holding my shares.

Despite another quarter of outstanding revenue growth, Palantir shares fell more than 5% following the company's Q3 report and are now 16% below their all-time high. Investors appeared concerned about a slowdown in the company's total contract value (TCV), which increased just 6% year over year after jumping 49% in the second quarter.

Does that mean Palantir's remarkable growth story is starting to lose steam? Not necessarily.

Palantir's third-quarter revenue was $2.31 billion, up 96% from a year earlier. U.S. commercial revenue grew 148% to $984 million, while U.S. government revenue increased 96% to $953 million. For the first time, Palantir generated more revenue from its U.S. commercial customers than from its U.S. government business.

I've long argued that Palantir's Artificial Intelligence Platform (AIP) is revolutionary because it helps companies put AI to work in their actual operations. Rather than simply generating text or answering questions, AIP allows businesses to connect AI models with their existing data and use them to make decisions and improve efficiency.

And the results suggest that businesses are increasingly willing to pay for those capabilities. Palantir's U.S. commercial customer count increased 29% year over year to 686, while the company closed 212 deals worth at least $1 million during the quarter.

The company also raised its full-year revenue guidance to between $8.53 billion and $8.534 billion, representing approximately 91% growth.

Those aren't the numbers of a company whose business is losing momentum.

There's a legitimate reason investors are paying attention to Palantir's contract numbers. Total contract value measures the potential value of contracts signed during a period, including certain contract options. It's an important indicator of future business, although not all of that value represents guaranteed revenue.

Palantir's third-quarter TCV reached $2.92 billion, increasing just 6% year over year. That was a sharp slowdown from the 49% growth reported in the second quarter.

CFO Dave Glazer explained on the company's earnings call that Palantir signed several large contracts that were shorter than usual as customers began adopting its new Sovereign AI capabilities. The company expects those agreements to grow in size and duration as customers expand their use of the technology.

That makes sense, but it's still something investors should watch. Shorter contracts can reduce reported TCV even when customers are spending more on Palantir's software.

Meanwhile, Palantir's total remaining deal value increased 55% year over year to $13.4 billion, suggesting its existing business continues to expand.

Still, Palantir's premium valuation leaves little room for disappointment. The stock has a forward price-to-earnings ratio of 108 times and a forward price-to-sales multiple of 51 times. If contract growth remains sluggish, investors may begin questioning whether the company's extraordinary revenue growth can continue.

I've been bullish on Palantir for years, and its transition toward a larger commercial business is one of the biggest reasons.

Election cake is a sweet civic tradition dating back to 1771. The annual Election Cake Bake-Off at Connecticut's Old State House in Harford, CT, has continued the election cake celebration for more than 250 years. Barbara Glassman Dell hide caption

Election day can be described in many ways: stressful, overwhelming, anxiety-inducing — and the list goes on.

But in Hartford, Connecticut, it's a piece of cake — specifically a mutli-tiered red, white and blue patriotic cake decorated with an American flag, stars and a bald eagle made entirely of frosting.

This masterpiece won the annual Election Cake Bake-Off at Connecticut's Old State House in Harford, CT. Nicole Sousa, the events and outreach manager at the Old State House, told Morning Edition about the Bake Off and rich tradition behind it.

Election cake first appeared in Hartford, in 1771, when the northeast United States was still a collection of 13 original colonies. Connecticut was a pioneer in early self-government, and it was one of the only regions who could elect their own officials.

"It was definitely something that Connecticut, as a colony and then later as a state, celebrated and really felt attached to," Sousa said.

So, election day was joyous. It was marked with parties, parades and community-wide celebrations — often involving cake.

But election cakes looked nothing like they do now. At that time, the iconic cakes resembled a tough, fruit cake, often soaked in alcohol.

The winner of this year's winner of the Connecticut Election Cake of 2026 was a mother-daughter duo led by Sophia Grestini of Amy's Cakes LLC in Cheshire, CT. Barbara Glassman Dell hide caption

Plus, this cake definitely couldn't fit in your modern fridge. In the eighteenth century, Sousa said the cakes were made using 30 quarts of flour, ten pounds of butter, 14 pounds of sugar, 12 pounds of raisins, three dozen eggs and over a quart of wine or brandy. Sousa said the massive cakes were baked to feed huge crowds of celebrating voters.

NASA astronauts voted in the 2026 midterms from space. Here’s how.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Election Day reached beyond Earth this year.

Three NASA astronauts aboard the International Space Station cast ballots in the 2026 midterm elections, with astronaut Anil Menon sharing a look at his orbital voting setup on Tuesday, Nov. 3. His polling place? The same small compartment where he sleeps.

In a video posted to X, Menon showed how he used his computer to complete an electronic ballot and send it to Harris County, Texas. Fellow astronauts Jessica Watkins and Luke Delaney also voted from the station.

"We filled out an electronic ballot and sent it securely to the election officials," Menon wrote.

So how does a vote get from orbit to an election office? It takes a few more stops than a trip to the mailbox.

According to NASA, astronauts can request an absentee ballot using a Federal Post Card Application. Once completed aboard the station, their electronic ballot travels through the agency’s Near Space Network, which also carries other communications between the station and Earth.

The ballot passes through NASA's Tracking and Data Relay Satellite System to a ground antenna in New Mexico, then heads to Mission Control in Houston before reaching the appropriate county clerk. NASA says the ballot is encrypted and accessible only to the astronaut and the clerk.

This isn't a new Election Day workaround. Texas lawmakers cleared the way for astronauts to vote from orbit in 1997. That year, NASA astronaut David Wolf became the first American to do so while aboard Russia’s Mir space station.

Nearly three decades later, the space station's sleeping quarters are still pulling double duty for democracy.

Suno Says Jason Isbell Lawsuit Over Artist ‘Identities’ Must Be Dismissed: ‘Nothing Unlawful About That’

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The AI music company has filed its first major response to the novel lawsuit, which accuses it of violating musicians' likeness rights.

Suno is asking a judge to throw out a lawsuit claiming the AI music firm stole the artistic identities of Jason Isbell and other stars, arguing there’s “nothing unlawful” about seeking to replicate the “vibe” of a particular artist.

Isbell sued in August, accusing Suno of allowing users to “conjure up a beloved musician’s identity” by entering their name as a prompt in its AI song generator. It was a novel case centered on an artist’s likeness rights, unlike earlier litigation against AI labs over the use of copyrighted music to train models.

But in Suno’s first major response on Tuesday (Nov. 3), its lawyers say there is “nothing unlawful” about merely using an artist’s name to describe music, any more than it’s illegal for a human artist to say “let’s go for something here with more of a Jason Isbell vibe.”

“When people describe styles of music, they often use artists’ names as shorthand for the genre they perform in,” Suno’s lawyers write in a motion obtained and first reported by Billboard. “This case turns on the premise that the law somehow condemns that universal practice. In reality it does not; the First Amendment would not allow it to.”

Suno’s motion, which asks the judge to dismiss the case immediately, argues that Isbell’s lawsuit attacks “ubiquitous methods of communicating ideas” and would hurt the “creative and cultural ecosystem” if allowed to proceed.

An attorney for Isbell did not immediately return a request for comment on Tuesday.

Isbell and other artists sued in August, accusing Suno of violating artists’ likeness rights (also known as publicity rights) by encoding their entire musical personas as tokens in its AI model. The case was filed as a class action, seeking to potentially represent “millions” of other artists: “The platform is built to identify musicians by name and generate identity-evocative output on command. This is its product.”

Suno was already facing a slew of lawsuits from major music companies, who claim the company violated their federal copyrights by ingesting millions of songs to train their models. The new case was different, accusing Suno of breaching state laws in New York, California, Tennessee and elsewhere that protect a person’s name, image and likeness from commercial exploitation.

Suno says it doesn’t actually allow users to prompt its AI model with specific artist names. When users do enter artist names, the platform says it translates that request into “descriptive musical characteristics,” which can then be used to direct the model to generate a song. A prompt of “Duran Duran,” for example, might get redirected into one of “New wave, Synth-pop, Pop rock.”

Isbell’s lawsuit claims those safeguards are faulty and can be duped by merely using spaces, such as Taylor Swift with “t a y l o r s w i f t” or Bad Bunny with “b a d b u n n y.” Even when a name is translated into musical styles, Isbell says the model clearly still exploits an artist’s identity: “Suno built and trained a model to index musicians by name. It encoded musicians’ identities into its AI model to create its commercial product without consent.”

But in Tuesday’s response, Suno says those accusations are legally deeply flawed. Simply using the name of a real person as a “shorthand to convey information about the genre that they want to create in,” Suno’s attorneys argue, is not the kind of thing that has historically violated state-law likeness rights.

“The law does not prevent people from using names to find or describe information, nor does it prevent building a tool capable of recognizing those references and acting on the information they supply,” Suno’s lawyers write, arguing that a ruling to the contrary would violate the First Amendment’s freedom of speech.

Even if likeness rights worked that way, Suno says the case would still be doomed by federal “preemption,” a legal doctrine that bars state-level claims that interfere with existing federal laws. Allowing artists to sue to assert their state-level likeness rights in such a way, Suno says, would overlap too much with federal copyright law’s existing protections for creative works.

“Congress made the considered policy choice not to confer intellectual property protection on an artistic style, precisely to allow subsequent creators to build on the prior works of a genre,” Suno says. “[This lawsuit] would create a shadow state law IP regime … fundamentally conflicting with the federal regime that has been in place for more than two centuries.”

Scientists discover a hidden pattern in the sun’s chaotic magnetic field

The solar wind's magnetic field twists one way at one scale and the other way at a smaller one, and the pattern drifts outward.

Stir milk into coffee and the swirls that follow have no plan. The stream of charged particles flowing from the sun is turbulent, too, with magnetic fields twisting through the chaos.

Scientists have long struggled to find order in these turbulent magnetic fields.

The twist has a pattern. In measurements from NASA’s Parker Solar Probe, the field turns one way at one size of ripple and the opposite way at ripples twice as small.

The pair of opposite twists sits right where the gas stops behaving like a fluid and starts behaving like a crowd of separate particles.

Masatomi Iizawa of TU Braunschweig led the analysis with Nobumitsu Yokoi of the University of Tokyo and colleagues in ten countries. Among them is Stuart Bale of UC Berkeley, who leads the instrument that took the readings.

“We see a clear structure in the spectrum, even though the turbulence represents unstructured motions,” said Iizawa, the paper’s first author.

A magnetic field in moving gas points somewhere, and it also turns.

As a ripple passes a detector, the field direction rotates, either clockwise or counterclockwise with respect to the gas’s direction of travel.

Physicists call that sense of rotation magnetic helicity. In the big, slow ripples of the solar wind, it has looked like nothing but noise since the 1980s.

Clockwise and counterclockwise turned up in roughly equal measure, which is what randomness looks like.

Sharp peaks near the smallest ripples have shown up before, in data from earlier spacecraft.

Following one as the gas traveled was the part no one could do, because older missions sampled too slowly to catch ripples that fast.

Parker Solar Probe samples fast enough. The team used its magnetometer readings from late September to late November 2021, an hour at a time.

Over those weeks the spacecraft swung between 0.1 and 0.78 times the Earth-sun distance.

Close to the sun, at a tenth of that distance, the spectrum held two spikes rather than noise.

One spike sat at 10 ripples a second and carried the twist one way. The other sat at 20 ripples a second and carried it the other way.

The two peaks revealed an organized pattern that random fluctuations alone could not readily explain.

Two opposite twists sitting side by side is what plasma physicists call a bihelical structure, and until now it was something theory predicted and data rarely showed.

Comparing measurements at different distances from the sun, the researchers found that both peaks shifted toward slower frequencies, corresponding to larger structures.

The drift held from 0.1 out to about 0.4 of the Earth-sun distance.

Gas expands as it travels, so some drift was expected. Simple expansion predicts a particular shape to it, with the frequency falling in step with distance.

The measured drift fell off exponentially instead, far faster than expansion accounts for.

The researchers then checked the other obvious explanation. If the twists were made and maintained by local conditions, their position should track the local scales set by protons in the gas.

Scaled against those, the peaks slid right past them.

The team calls that behavior nonlocal. The findings suggest that interactions across different scales, rather than local plasma conditions alone, may be driving the changes.

Turbulence normally hands energy down a ladder, from big swirls to small ones, until the smallest turn into heat. The same ladder turns the tiny vortices seen on the sun’s own surface.

One recent theory holds that at the boundary between fluid behavior and particle behavior, the twist itself jams that ladder and forces energy back up to larger scales.

A pile-up of magnetic twist at scales bigger than the local proton scales is exactly what the jam would produce.

The team’s observation fits that prediction, and it arrives as a measurement rather than a simulation.

Yokoi said the result matters for plasma physics and astrophysics alike.

Understanding this turbulence could eventually help scientists improve models of the solar wind and its effects on Earth’s magnetic environment.

Past roughly half the Earth-sun distance, the two spikes faded out.

The probe’s orbit stays within a few degrees of the plane the planets share, and around there it keeps crossing the current sheet dividing the sun’s magnetic field in two.

Crossing that sheet may scramble the structure, or the fade may just be where the spacecraft happened to be.

One spacecraft measures one point, so the twist it records is a slice of the full three-dimensional quantity.

Turning ripple timings into ripple sizes rests on an assumption about how fast the gas carries them past. The team puts the possible error there at a factor of about 2.5.

Nobody could watch the energy move, either. That would take readings of the gas’s speed as fast as the magnetic ones, and particle instruments can’t scan that quickly in gas this thin.

No one has caught these structures forming, or followed one outward in a single parcel of gas.

Iizawa’s team suggests Europe’s Solar Orbiter, which flies above and below the planets’ plane, could settle whether the fade is real or an accident of one orbit.

The full study was published in the journal The Astrophysical Journal Letters.

Horror thrillers are a special kind of horror that needs to grip you in the plot right from the very beginning. It is essential to set up the story, build the suspense, and attract the audience's attention right from the opening scene in all genres of cinema. Horror particularly needs this, resulting in some of our favorite opening moments of all time.

The opening scene, when done right, can fill the viewers in on what they can expect and enjoy the absolute wonder they're about to experience for the rest of the story. Sometimes, the opening surpasses the rest of the film even. Horror has no short list of the most compelling opening scenes, but the best ones truly come from when horror meets thriller. No better genres mix suspense, atmosphere, and bone-chilling dread than these two when combined. They've resulted in some of the best openings horror has to offer. From one nail-biting sequence to the next, here are the ten best first scenes in horror-thriller history.

Ari Aster established himself as the king of thematic horror thanks to his 2019 cult classic Midsommar. This eerie slow-burner is the modern equivalent of Robin Hardy's The Wicker Man, combining slow-moving tension and shocking rug pullers. But unlike its iconic inspiration, Midsommar hits the ground running with one hell of a brutal opening tragedy.

The film starts with the protagonist, Dani (Florence Pugh), beginning her spiraling descent into grief and madness when her mentally struggling sister kills her parents and herself via carbon monoxide poisoning. It's a gun-punching opener highlighted by a slow, quiet first few moments followed by an intense transition to a graphic mass murder, accompanied by haunting music. This scene perfectly sets up the unexpected brutality of Aster's unique film.

The pinnacle of horror in the late 2010s, Jordan Peele's remarkable filmmaking debut, Get Out, is the modern masterpiece that so many films since have continued to reach for greatness. A thriller that constantly keeps you on the edge of your seat, its greatest strengths come from its ambiguity and unconventional storytelling. Hardly anywhere does this shine more than in its eerie and rather strange opener.

Instead of a gruesome kill or frightening introduction of an antagonist, it opens with an ordinary Black man (Lakeith Stanfield) walking down a dark street when he's kidnapped by a mysterious man in a knight's helmet. It doesn't establish the plot, and we don't even know who this young man is; we just witness a bizarre kidnapping that we don't get an answer for until much later in the story. Though this might seem unusual, it perfectly grips you into a one-of-a-kind psychological story that will surely leave you speechless by the end.

Easily one of the most paranoia-inducing horror films in history, this incredible nail-biter changed the game with its unique premise and unrelenting suspense, making viewers scared that anything terrible could happen at any moment. And it all starts with a highly tense opening scene, which has become cemented in horror history.

When high schooler Alex Browning (Devon Sawa) experiences a premonition of a horrific disaster before his class trip's plane takes off, he freaks out and escapes the departure along with a lucky few shortly before the plane actually explodes. This opener has become synonymous with tension building. It's perfectly set up through the use of subtle ominous foreshadowing and eerie leitmotifs, and the terrifying punch comes when Alex's vision comes true. It's a well-crafted, successful opener that jump-started this iconic horror franchise with a legendary boost.

Most of the incidents in the series are verifiable, though when there’s ambiguity, Watkins tends to lean into the legend — as with the famous incident involving Cassius and the casting of his Olympic gold medal into the Ohio River. The series has points it wants to make and it makes them with very little subtlety, but Muhammad Ali was a figure so big that he can withstand some heavy-handedness.

Watkins is intrigued by the things that make Clay special — and equally by the things that make him dangerous and, in turn, endanger his opportunities to claim a title belt. He juxtaposes Clay’s rise with the developing civil rights movement, monitoring the pace of progress, which means something different to each character. What feels like progress to Sonny Liston — who’s treated like an animal in the fields of Arkansas and then by the media and even Clay as he becomes a title contender — isn’t the same as what feels like progress to Clay Senior, who had aspirations to be a legitimate artist and instead paints billboards. And it isn’t the same as what feels like progress to Malcolm X and Elijah Muhammad (Hill Harper), who see in Cassius Clay the most visible spokesman the Nation would ever have.

The eight-episode length of the series gives every character motivations and complications, whether it’s Cassius’ determination spawned by the murder of Emmett Till or his mother’s concern from having seen boxer Sam Baroudi killed in the ring in 1948. There are gaps in some of the characterizations, with the women feeling especially archetypal and Rudy reduced to a devoted sidekick, but it’s generally a benefit to build out a world around Ali.

Still, it’s Best’s show, and he nails the two biggest challenges in this mammoth role. He builds up Cassius’ voice from soft-spoken Kentucky lilt to a poetry-spewing trumpet, embodying the braggadocious icon viewers think they know, while showing the man who existed at the dining room table or in a pew at a house of worship. This is a Cassius Clay who lived in the spotlight and a man who existed in private as well.

Best’s physicality is perhaps even more impressive, whether it’s reproducing expressions and posture from famous Ali snapshots or navigating the ring convincingly in the series’ fight sequences. There have been decent boxing shows in the past, like FX’s Lights Out, but Watkins and subsequent directors deliver more and better fight scenes than I’ve ever seen in the medium. Unlike Hulu’s uninspired Mike Tyson scripted series, the fight scenes here stay fairly close to the historical record, so if you watch The Greatest and then watch YouTube videos of the notorious London showdown with Henry Cooper or the first fight against Liston, the reproduction and the truth line up well. Much of that is due to Best’s emulation of his character’s fancy footwork, pawing jab and viciously timed attacks.

Some of the supporting casting is initially off-putting, with several actors who are, and come across as, significantly older than the characters they’re playing. Oddly, if you stick with it, those choices work.

Amin Joseph is 46, playing Sonny Liston, who was in his early-to-mid 30s, but Liston was an old 33, having spent years in prison after his rough and impoverished beginnings. Joseph makes Liston into a flawed but tragic figure, ferocious in the ring but eager to find acceptance outside of it. A scene with Liston flailing in an effort to humanize himself for the pubic by telling a joke during a talk show appearance is one of the series’ highlights.

Michael Ealy is 55, playing a man who was killed at 39, but the Malcolm X we meet here is a Malcolm X at a crossroads, deservedly worn beyond his years. The extra gap in age between Ealy and Best helps to illustrate the gap in maturity between the two men, one just starting his public life and one soon to have it ended.

Hardwick makes Cassius Senior volatile but wounded, with Gourrier centering Odessa’s fierce devotion and pride. Parham has a strong physical presence, but the show doesn’t quite get Rudy, even denying him the chapter of his own boxing career that started at the same time. Perhaps that will get worked into future seasons.

And I hope there will be future seasons of The Greatest, because this was only one chapter in a life filled with even more potent journeys.

Based on the confidence of this round of episodes, I’d say Watkins might be able to get two seasons out of Ali’s Vietnam protest, his exile from boxing and his first loss (with Joe Frazier taking on the secondary parallel role); another full season leading up to and including the Rumble in the Jungle (with George Foreman as the parallel foil); and then a final season on the closing chapters of his career, from the Thrilla in Manila on (perhaps making more use out of Regen Wilson’s Howard Cosell).

In an ideal world, Watkins would be able to take a few between seasons, just to let Best age up a little and continue what should be a well-deserved breakout.

Stuck in years-long lines for grid connections and new turbines, some AI data centers are making their own electricity with converted jet engines, the New York Times reported Tuesday.

The engines, pulled from retired aircraft and rebuilt to burn natural gas, aren’t anyone’s idea of a permanent power plant. But they’re another sign of how far power supply has fallen behind the AI buildout.

For companies stuck in those lines, the main draw of converted jet engines is how quickly the engines can be up and running. “It’s all about getting power as fast as possible,” Rystad Energy analyst Audun Martinsen told the Times. With jet-powered units, “they can have a data center running in 24 months,” he said.

Hooking up 100 megawatts to the grid in Northern Virginia, by comparison, takes about seven years, according to the International Energy Agency. Most power capacity is expected to come on by 2031, an executive at Mitsubishi Power, one of the world’s top makers of power generation equipment told Bloomberg last week.

In some cases, these delays are already costing some data center operators business. Last month, Microsoft pulled out as the anchor customer for Nscale’s data center in Loughton, England, after the local utility said it couldn’t deliver enough power until the early-to-mid 2030s.

Elon Musk’s xAI is among the companies already using the engines, having installed eight converted jet-engine turbines in Mississippi to help power its Colossus 2 data center in Memphis, the Times reported, citing state permits and satellite imagery. The units come from ProEnergy, which rebuilds a GE engine commonly found on Boeing jets.

In 2025, OpenAI followed Musk’s lead with gas turbines at its first Stargate site in Abilene, Texas. Crusoe, a Denver-based startup that builds AI data centers, is constructing that Abilene campus and ordered 13 of ProEnergy’s converted units in April.

In September, Crusoe walked away from a $1.25 billion deal to buy 29 natural gas turbines from Boom Supersonic, a startup developing a supersonic passenger jet that has also begun selling a version of that jet’s engine as a power plant for data centers. A Crusoe spokesperson told TechCrunch at the time that the company still plans to use turbines.

Currently the Trump administration is adding to the pressure by encouraging data centers to generate their own electricity.

Converted jet engines come with trade-offs, though: They’re less efficient and pollute more than turbines designed for conventional gas plants and it takes more of them to make the same amount of power, according to the Times. That may ultimately make converted jet engines a short-term play with some experts saying that efficiency and environmental concerns may grow in importance in a year or two.

The Origins of ‘Elf’: How Will Ferrell Came to Replace Chris Farley in the Christmas Classic

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

In an exclusive excerpt from his new oral history, comedian Andy Richter — who has a small part in the movie — retraces the unlikely birth of the 2003 holiday hit.

The following is excerpted from Elf Unwrapped: Behind the Scenes of a Modern Christmas Classic – Andy Richter‘s Oral History of the Making of Elf the Film That Became a Holiday Classic, by Andy Richter (who had a small a role in the hit 2003 comedy) with Aaron Kino.

The story of Elf begins like most Christmas tales do — with a Jewish kid from Philadelphia. In 1993, an aspiring screenwriter named David Berenbaum moved from Philly to L.A. with dreams of making it big. He had no connections, no experience, and for the most part, no clue how to make it in “the biz.” But what he did have was an overactive imagination and a brain brimming with fanciful ideas. David was a cockeyed dreamer, as many of us are when we land in Hollywood, but David just felt completely out of his element. Compared to the East Coast, L.A. is just . . . weird. No one seems to have a real job here, but they all talk like they’re showbiz big shots. People act like they’re your friend but then look past you mid conversation, just in case someone better walks by. David was lonely, especially when the holidays rolled around — which are even weirder in L.A. Suddenly, it was an unholy eight- five degrees around Christmastime! To cope, he rented a bunch of Christmas movies on VHS so he could watch them over and over. He binged classics like It’s a Wonderful Life, Miracle on 34th Street, Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer — anything to feel a bit more Christmassy. Feeling inspired, he sat down to write his first Christmas film: a screwball romantic comedy called Christmas in New Jersey, which centered on two people having the worst days of their lives, who decide to completely start over as if they had never been born (very It’s a Wonderful Life coded).

Fortunately for him, that script actually got optioned — which is when a production company or studio pays you a little bit of money for the “option” of one day making the movie and then paying you a lot of money. Unfortunately, 99 percent of optioned projects never get made, and that was the case with Christmas in New Jersey. The good news, though, is that it opened up a ton of doors for David. Impressed by the quality of his writing, the film executives he met with kept asking him, “Okay, what’s next?” “Next??” David thought. “Damn, I better come up with something else!” Luckily, he was still in that Christmas frame of mind when he started working on his next big idea: the script that would eventually become Elf.

When David first started writing Elf in 1995, he was writing on spec. In Hollywood terms, “spec” means “speculative” — in other words, no one was paying him to write this script, so he was doing the work for free, hoping that he’d be able to sell it down the line. And though he loved a lot of the holiday movies he’d been bingeing, it was ultimately a 1964 Rankin Bass TV special that really inspired this particular project.

For some background: Rankin Bass was this quirky little production company that basically dominated Christmas TV programming for a couple of decades. If you’re a fan of Elf (and holiday movies in general), you’ll probably recognize their stuff right away. They specialized in stop-motion specials, the kind where the puppets move one frame at a time. Their most famous production, Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer, has been on TV every December since LBJ was in the White House. But they also made Frosty the Snowman, Santa Claus Is Comin’ to Town, and The Year Without a Santa Claus — basically all those herky-jerky yet very magical holiday staples that feel like fever dreams nowadays but absolutely shaped everybody’s idea of what Christmas looks and sounds like.

Rankin Bass’s rendition of Rudolph the Red-Nosed Reindeer (adapted from a 1939 booklet published by the department store Montgomery Ward) follows the time-honored tale of Rudolph, a poor reindeer who gets mocked by all the other reindeer because of his glowing and very shiny red nose. After those mean bullies cast him out, he stumbles across a fellow misfit named Hermey, an elf who longs to be a dentist. Rejected by their peers at Santa’s workshop, they set out on an adventure, stumbling upon the Island of Misfit Toys and running into now-classic characters like the Abominable Snow Monster along the way. When they finally return home, Rudolph ends up saving Christmas by using his nose to guide Santa’s sleigh through a terrible snowstorm, proving that what made Rudolph different is exactly what made him special.

David knew what it felt like to be a misfit; after all, it wasn’t that long ago that he’d been working in a lingerie store, sweating in eighty-five-degree heat at Christmas. Essentially, he saw himself in Rudolph and wanted to create a character of his own that followed a similar sort of hero’s journey. Rudolph’s innocence also reminded David of Josh Baskin, Tom Hanks’s character in Big: a somewhat whimsical weirdo who doesn’t really fit in. Thanks to his wish being granted by a magical fortune-telling machine, Josh is quite literally bigger than all his peers, and this is also what sparked the idea of a giant (well, human-sized) elf named Buddy.

So, at this point, David has an idea with a great hook: a human who is raised as an elf in the North Pole. It’s a funny concept, it’s packed with potential for physical humor, and it also has this “fish out of water with a Christmas twist” angle that hasn’t really been done before. But as excited as he was in the moment, David knew he needed to flesh it out wayyyy more. He didn’t want it to just be a one-note gag. Plus, after watching all those Christmas movies, he realized the ones that really stuck around — the ones that reached the pantheon — had a little something extra.

In the original drafts of the script, when Buddy left the North Pole in search of his human family, both his mother and father were still alive. This presented a few problems — namely that it required a whole lot more explaining. It seems believable that a man might not know he had a kid, but what had Mom been doing for the past thirty years? Searching for her missing baby? Or just going on with her life, sipping margs by the pool and pretending nothing happened? Either way, it doesn’t exactly scream “jolly good fun.”

So David decided to follow in the footsteps of Cinderella and Snow White and pretty much every Disney movie and Grimm fairy tale ever — and killed off Buddy’s mom. Focusing the script around Buddy’s relationship with his dad made a lot of sense story-wise, but even more than that, it was a deeply personal choice for David. His dad had passed away when he was young, and this idea of Buddy searching for his father really resonated with him.

Ultimately, it was this emotional yet endearing through line that elevated Elf from a funny premise or silly sketch into a full-blown movie with the potential for real staying power. Before, the drafts felt a lot more jokey, but with that last piece in place, the script finally transformed into what he’d wanted it to be all along: a holiday comedy with a whole lot of heart.

DAVID BERENBAUM, SCREENWRITER: I just went back to what would be the most personal connection for me — my dad’s passing — so that’s why I made that choice. Once I found that, the writing became a whole lot easier and I had something that I thought was good. Plus, I thought, comedically, it’d be funny for Buddy to go, “I’m here, Dad!”

In 1996, after writing many (many, many, many) drafts of the script, David sold his screenplay for millions of dollars, and it was smooth sailing right into production.

Of course that’s not what happened. Instead, the film got stuck in “development hell,” the term for projects in Hollywood that languish with production companies and studios for years and never actually get the green light.

First, Elf was optioned by the Motion Picture Company of America (aka MPCA), a production company known for making critically acclaimed classics like the Academy Award–nominated Ulee’s Gold (starring Peter Fonda) and Beverly Hills Chihuahua 2 (starring George Lopez). It was while MPCA held the reins that the very first actor was attached (or in Hollywood speak, “verbally agreed”) to play Buddy: the one and only Chris Farley, fresh off Saturday Night Live. Once he was on board, David began refining the script with Chris in mind, and, at several points, was even scheduled to sit down with Chris himself so they could go over the new material. Alas, that meeting never materialized, and after a year, MPCA’s exclusive option period expired, relegating the what-if of Chris Farley starring in Elf to Hollywood legend.

Then, another production company optioned Elf, but after that endeavor didn’t work out either, the revised script earned David acceptance into the prestigious Disney Writing Program. This program brings in a couple of writers each year and has them rewrite whatever is in production at the time. It’s a trial by fire—Disney-style—but it’s also an incredible opportunity. David spent two years there, working on anything and everything they threw his way; in fact, one film he wrote there — The Haunted Mansion, starring Eddie Murphy—actually came out a few weeks after Elf. During this time, Disney also had the opportunity to make Elf, but alas, once again, for the innumerable and inscrutable reasons that most Hollywood projects die, it never came to fruition.

Luckily, the wait to bring Elf to life would soon be over, once a fairy godmother in the form of another SNL legend came along.

The people who eventually brought Elf to the big screen came from wildly different corners of Hollywood, from nobodies to legends, executives to improvisers, Comedy Central to Saturday Night Live. It was a jolly band of misfits, and the strange alchemy that united them — part design, part luck — would end up paying incredible dividends.

Cocreated by a group of Chicago improv comedians (including Stephen Colbert and future Elf scene-stealer Amy Sedaris), the show was an offbeat, crass send-up of after-school specials — those preachy ’90s TV movies that aired right when kids got home from school and taught very obvious lessons about very serious issues. Strangers with Candy set out to offend audiences everywhere — and it did so brilliantly. There’s this truly unhinged episode in the third and final season in which Amy’s character discovers that she has Native American heritage and tries to learn more about her “roots.” I make a cameo in this episode, as does a tall, goofy up-and-coming SNL cast member named Will Ferrell. This was actually the first time Will and I worked on the same project. But more importantly (at least for the tale at hand), Strangers with Candy was produced by Comedy Central executive Kent Alterman, who would also go on to play a pivotal role in the Elf saga. A second wave of attacks on supermarket refrigeration systems across Europe this week has heightened concerns about the broader "cold chain's" vulnerability to similar attacks, given that supermarkets aren't the only businesses that rely on cold storage.

As in mid-October, an unknown threat actor appears to have gained access to refrigeration controllers at multiple European supermarkets and switched the systems to defrost mode, raising temperatures to 50 degrees Celsius (122 degrees Fahrenheit) and causing food spoilage.

Reporting by the BBC, Reuters, CNBC, and other outlets suggest the latest attacks were smaller in scope compared to the first wave in October that impacted more than 700 retail and grocery stores primarily in France. A smaller number of stores in Belgium, Germany, the Netherlands, Portugal, Poland and Spain were also caught up in the October attacks.

BBC reported the new attacks as impacting "dozens of supermarkets" in seven European countries including Belgium, Luxembourg, Estonia, Ireland, Sweden and Finland. Spokespersons from several supermarket chains like France's Carrefour, Belgium's Ahold Delhaize and Colruyt Group, and Sweden's Coop chains confirmed varying but relatively limited impacts from the new attacks.

In a Nov. 2 statement, Carrefour said a cyberattack targeting its refrigeration systems had impacted 15 out of 700 stores in Belgium. In an undated statement, US-based Copeland, which manufactures technologies for regulating commercial refrigeration systems, alluded to a cybersecurity incident affecting refrigeration systems in France, which it said related to a discontinued generation of supervisory controllers.

No individual or group has claimed responsibility for either the first or the second wave of attacks, and the motivation behind it remains a mystery. European authorities are currently investigating the attacks.

Researchers, including those at France's CERT, have linked the hacks so far to Internet-connected refrigeration controllers made by Denmark-based Danfoss, Italy-based Dixell, and Copeland. Organizations using these controllers often connect them to the Internet to enable remote maintenance and support, but in doing so also potentially expose them to unauthorized access and remote attack by threat actors.

Risks include unpatched vulnerabilities in these technologies that attackers can exploit, like three in Danfoss' AK-SM 800A controllers that researchers at Claroty disclosed this August and another two they reported the same month in Copeland controllers. Weak configurations and poor security hygiene are also other major problems.

"Misconfigurations and systemic hygiene failures represent the vast majority of the threat surface," says John Gallagher, vice president at operational technology (OT) and Internet of Things (IoT) security vendor Viakoo. "While unpatched vulnerabilities exist in older firmware, they are rendered dangerous only because basic architectural hygiene was skipped, especially [when] making them Internet-facing."

Common mistakes include widespread use of default passwords, vendor-shared service codes, or static credentials across hundreds of locations makes brute-forcing trivial, Gallagher adds.

The weaknesses are not confined to supermarket refrigeration systems. The same combination of Internet connectivity, remote access, and inadequate security practices can put temperature-controlled infrastructure across other critical sectors at risk, according to security experts.

Sean Tufts, field chief technology officer (CTO) at Claroty, points out that "pharmaceutical storage and distribution, food distribution centers, and industrial facilities all rely on connected operational technology to maintain precise environmental conditions."

The level of exposure will vary depending on how these systems are deployed, connected, and secured. But many organizations that rely on remotely accessible refrigeration or temperature-control equipment face similar challenges around asset visibility, third-party access, and securing legacy systems, Tufts says. "The same types of weaknesses can exist across different industries, even if the equipment and operational requirements differ."

The potential consequences of a compromise, says Tufts, can vary by sector.

"A disruption to pharmaceutical storage could compromise temperature-sensitive medicines, while an incident affecting food distribution could lead to spoilage and supply chain disruptions," he notes. "Industrial environments may face additional operational consequences depending on the materials and processes involved."

The supermarket attacks are a reminder that refrigeration and temperature-control systems are part of essential operations and not just back-of-house equipment, Tufts adds. Organizations should identify these assets and know how they connect to the Internet and third parties.

"Where remote access is necessary, use a VPN, strong authentication, or a centralized secure-access tool with session tracking." Changing default passwords, keeping firmware current, and segmenting are also recommended security practices, he says.

Gallagher says that while it's unclear how the attacks on the commercial refrigeration systems played out, the most straightforward theory is the threat actor likely compiled a target list by scanning the public Internet for specific controller fingerprints, such as those tied to Danfoss, Dixell, and Copeland. They then likely deployed an automated script across that target list to exploit known web vulnerabilities or push default credentials, triggering the defrost mode simultaneously.

Gallagher says the same attacks that worked on commercial refrigeration systems can potentially work across almost any form of OT and IoT systems. That's because these systems often are managed outside of IT, lack automated controls to keep firmware and passwords updated, and have porous network borders. "The growth over the last three years in operational ransomware and in cyberattacks causing physical damage shows that these attacks are on the rise and not specific to any one kind of system."
