Star Trek: Voyager Officially Revisits the Series' Best Two-Parter in a Brand-New Way

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

One of the best two-episode arcs in Star Trek: Voyager, if not the entire Star Trek franchise, is the "Year of Hell" duology. The story from the show's fourth season pitted the crew of Voyager against a unique threat that tested their perseverance more than any previous enemy had.

Now, new DLC for survival game Star Trek: Voyager - Across the Unknown​​​​​​​ will revisit the iconic storyline. Players won't just be able to relive "Year of Hell," however, they'll be able to change the course of history.​​​​​​​

Daedalic Entertainment has announced that the fourth Expansion Pass DLC for Across the Unknown will be inspired by "Year of Hell". The announcement describes the DLC as "a self-contained mini-campaign of three new sectors." The DLC will be released on Dec. 9. As in the show​​​​​​​, the game will pit players against the Krenim captain Annorax as he wields a weapon that allows him to alter history and destroy entire species. As a standalone expansion, Year of Hell won't affect any of players' main-game save files​​​​​​​.

Unlike the base game, which sees the Voyager trying to rebuild​​​​​​​ and survive with few resources, Year of Hell will invert the traditional gameplay experience. Players will start with a fully repaired and fully equipped ship​​​​​​​ before encountering the Krenim. While damaged rooms will be repairable, lost decks will cause players to lose every room, and players can lose up to five decks​​​​​​​. Crew members can also die, leaving the Voyager crew scrambling to stay alive​​​​​​​. Daedalic Entertainment says that the goal of the DLC "isn't to finish with a perfect ship. It's to see how many of your crew are still standing after twelve months."

If players want to follow the plot of the "Year of Hell" episodes, that is absolutely possible, but Daedalic​​​​​​​ has given players another option. While Captain Janeway can make alliances with a number of friendly ships, she can also choose to join Annorax. "Help Annorax repair the timeline, once he agrees to stop erasing civilizations. Whether he keeps that promise is another matter. Your senior officers may not share your enthusiasm, and their disapproval grows as the months pass."

Depending on which path players take​​​​​​​, the other inhabitants of the sectors will have shifting opinions of the Voyager and act accordingly. The base game is required, but those who own the Expansion Pass will automatically receive Year of Hell when it is released. In the meantime, you can rewatch the "Year of Hell" episodes​​​​​​​ to prepare for your own fight against Annorax.

LayerZero locks early ZRO deposits behind validators users cannot choose

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

LayerZero’s launch validators will share the voting power of early ZRO deposits, with withdrawals locked until 2027.

LayerZero named Kakao Pay and ZAN among the first validators for its Zero blockchain on Nov. 25 and said Four Pillars would run validator operations on behalf of Samsung Card.

The operators are slated to help confirm blocks when Zero opens to the public, a launch planned for Nov. 30.

The appointments show who will exercise some of the voting weight tied to early deposits of ZRO, the token that backs validator votes on Zero.

Under LayerZero’s staking terms, the portal automatically spreads early stake equally across the initial validators. Holders have no say in which validators receive it and no way to withdraw early.

LayerZero says its launch validators will span Asia, Europe and North America. Its Nov. 25 announcement names the Asia-Pacific participants and identifies Four Pillars as the operator for Samsung Card.

Validators check proofs that transactions were processed correctly and vote on blocks. A validator’s vote carries more weight when more ZRO is staked behind it, according to LayerZero’s staking guide.

The project’s February technical paper describes a design in which operators can validate without putting up their own tokens, and the network selects its consensus committee in proportion to stake.

A holder who disagrees with a validator during the lock has no option in the portal to redirect the stake or exit the position, and the lock covers the staked tokens and any rewards.

LayerZero expects unstaking to open by March 31, 2027, and says that date may change. Once unstaking opens, it can take up to seven days, so moving ZRO off Zero to another chain may require a later upgrade.

LayerZero describes a 1% annual issuance target as the basis for the reward pool, and the terms set an initial validator commission of 10% on rewards. A holder’s share depends on how much ZRO they stake, how long they stake it, and how much others commit.

Any rewards compound into the locked position, and the staking guide says validators will set their own commission after the initial period.

Until unstaking opens, stakers can track their balances, but the selected validator set exercises the consensus votes backed by those deposits. The public launch is the next chance to see which of the announced validators come online.

Every seven years, European leaders gather in Brussels for what is arguably the most consequential negotiation cycle in the European Union. As German Chancellor Friedrich Merz put it upon arrival, it is about "the future of the European Union."

This is not a typical EU summit. The leaders of the 27 member states are confronting a question that will shape all angles of EU politics for years to come: How much money should the EU have, who should pay for it, and what should it be spent on?

It is the essence of EU summit diplomacy: a painstaking exercise in give-and-take, bargaining and compromise.

The European Commission laid down the opening bid over the summer. The proposal was ambitious. Brussels wants an EU budget that reflects a rapidly-changing world: more spending on defence, innovation, research, digital technologies and competitiveness. At the same time, that inevitably means pressure on some of the traditional pillars of EU spending, particularly agricultural subsidies and parts of the bloc's regional development programmes.

Since then, every member has arrived with a shopping list of demands.

Governments have pushed back against cuts, demanded new priorities, sought exemptions and fought for national interests. There have though also been others who are pushing FOR a reduction in spending, including Merz.

The original proposal from Commission President Ursula von der Leyen has already undergone numerous adjustments as negotiators attempted to accommodate competing demands.

The result is an extraordinarily complex puzzle.

At the centre of it all stands European Council President Antonio Costa. He will be shuttling between bilateral meetings with leaders, gather small groups behind closed doors and attempt to construct a package that gives everybody something they can take home and present as a victory.

For Costa, the stakes are high. Delivering an agreement would be a major test of his authority and reputation as the EU's chief broker. Especially as he was not made top negotiator for the EU in the peace talks on Russia's war in Ukraine. Diplomats expressed confidence in his fairness and negotiating skills before the summit, viewing him as someone capable of building trust among leaders who have differing priorities.

Yet nobody in Brussels expects an easy breakthrough. Many officials acknowledge that the pressure on member states may not yet be intense enough to force the final concessions required for a deal. Leaders are still defending red lines rather than crossing them.

So, this summit will highly likely end without a grand agreement. Leaders will probably decide to take stock of the latest negotiating proposals and return home to assess them in detail. There will be a new — and perhaps final — round on the next summit in December, a few days before Christmas.

As leaders grapple with long-term budgets, across Europe, young people are rising up. They're demanding better jobs, more funding for education and lower rents. The problems they are protesting are complex and often specific to their home country.

But there’s also a common message: Young people feel pessimistic about their economic future. A look at the data shows they could be right.

8 Smartest Characters In The Lord Of The Rings Movies, Ranked By Intelligence

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

In The Lord of the Rings, J.R.R. Tolkien presents what we would call "intelligence" in quite a nuanced way. It's important to realize Tolkien was no fan of the modern idea of "intelligence." As he wrote in one letter, "I have never yet heard of an 'intelligence' test that was not specially suited to select nitwits (bright perky little nitwits possibly, but not always even so). But what would you: they are invented by nitwits (or some by impostors)."

Tolkien prized carefulness of thought, not quick-thinking. He believed in the value of knowledge, but that knowledge had to be digested and interpreted. In truth, I don't think Tolkien actually believed in the pursuit of intelligence at all; rather, as a Catholic, he honored something that his faith would probably call "wisdom." All that makes it fascinating to look at The Lord of the Rings through the lens of intelligence, though, because it helps us to see the world as Tolkien did—and to learn from him.

Grima Wormtongue is one of Tolkien's most underestimated characters, in large part because Peter Jackson's movies diminish Wormtongue quite significantly. Jackson presents Wormtongue more as a channel of Saruman's power than a mastermind in his own right. When Gandalf frees Théoden from Wormtongue's influence, there's a magical backlash that knocks Saruman off his feet. But that idea is entirely absent in the books themselves.

In the movies, you see hints of Wormtongue's true nature. He's ruthless, cunning, a manipulative mastermind who can figure out any way to spin a crisis to his advantage. Grima Wormtongue is the consummate modern politician, the spin doctor if you like. It's not a coincidence that Tolkien portrays him as the villain whose intelligence—deployed so selfishly—almost brings down an entire kingdom. With Wormtongue, we immediately see that intelligence is a very dangerous thing if you're lacking in character.

It's easy to criticize Denethor. A ruler of Gondor, he is slow to respond to Gandalf's appeals, simply because his intelligence is stronger than the strength of his convictions. He's right in his estimation of Mordor's forces, and he correctly understands the strategic importance of Minas Tirith. He's something of a political mastermind in his own right, understanding the relationships between different countries. I like to think of the Bismarck of Lord of the Rings.

But again, notice Denethor's weaknesses. It's possible he was a little too intelligent; he believes he could filter the facts, and thus discounted the possibility he was being manipulated. Emotions increasingly compromised his judgment—and, again, he's unaware of it. Tolkien's characters are moral ones, and Denethor is a warning to the world that intelligence without morality and conviction can and will lead you astray. Especially if others are there to manipulate you.

It would be so easy to miss Smaug on this list. We tend to focus on Smaug as one of The Lord of the Rings' most powerful villains, but the Great Dragon displays a great deal of what we would call intelligence. He's surprisingly strong when it comes to understanding and interpreting others, as seen in the riddles he sets. Those demonstrate a remarkable depth of strategic thinking, deductive reasoning, and perception.

Tolkien reveals Smaug's intelligence in so many subtle ways. His manner of speaking is more complex than other characters, showing an intuitive grasp of metaphor and demonstrating quite sophisticated rhetorical techniques. He swiftly identifies Bilbo's psychological weaknesses, manipulating them with skill. But again, note that this interpersonal intelligence is flawed because of Smaug's character. The Great Dragon isn't necessarily a warning against the excess of power, but rather of trickery and cunning.

As physically powerful as the elves may be in The Lord of the Rings, they are also notable for their intelligence and wisdom (although the latter is often presented as flawed, compromised by excessive nostalgia). Elrond is thousands of years old, and he's been part of so many critical events on Middle-earth; you can get a sense of how important he is in The Rings of Power, where a younger Elrond is a key character in a story loosely based on Tolkien's notes.

Importantly, though, Elrond's historical knowledge informs his analytical reasoning. He's able to place the present danger in its historical context, and understand both the risks and the best strategies. Remarkably, he is willing to listen to different perspectives; he even sets aside the traditional conflict between Dwarves and Elves, allowing every member of the Fellowship a voice. But again, note that he's a little too conservative, a little too cautious. He has to be heeded, but sometimes overruled.

I suspect some readers will disagree with my placing Galadriel above Elrond. But the comparison between the two is striking; like Elrond, she has accumulated a vast amount of knowledge and experience, beyond anything mortals can ever imagine. What matters, though, is that Galadriel demonstrates what we would now call emotional intelligence. She sees into the souls of others, understanding their characters and motivations.

She is also strikingly humble. Notice Galadriel's reaction when Frodo offers her the One Ring; she knows exactly what it would do to her, and she wants nothing to do with it. Galadriel's perception is relatively untouched by ego, meaning she moves beyond mere intelligence. She's the first character on this list to truly demonstrate wisdom.

In pure intellectual ability, Saruman is almost unparalleled in The Lord of the Rings. It helps that, like the Elves, he draws on a vast reservoir of experience; but his experience is even less limited than theirs because wizards are essentially angels in Tolkien's mythology. Saruman is a scholar and a gifted researcher, an academic who asks the questions no one else dares ask. He notices the sweeping patterns of history, the decline of magic, and the potential of industrialization.

But, of course, Saruman is limited because of his own ego. He sees patterns, and believes he can master them. He looks on others with disdain, viewing them as a means to the end he seeks to accomplish. What's more, because the pattern of progress is visible, he believes he should master it and determine it. Saruman's intelligence is second to his ambition, demonstrating once again that character comes first in The Lord of the Rings.

Think of Gandalf and Saruman as two sides of the same coin. Gandalf's natural intelligence is roughly equal to Saruman's, who perhaps has the edge on him in terms of overall analytical ability. But with Gandalf, intelligence is tempered both by conviction and humility. Gandalf recognizes the patterns of history, but refuses to ally with things he believes are wrong. His morality gives him a different starting point, if you like, meaning the outcome is very different.

Time and again, Gandalf demonstrates that his intelligence is subordinate to his humility. A machine learning engineer builds and operates models and ML pipelines. An AI engineer usually builds applications and systems that use models, retrieval, agents or other AI services. An AI automation engineer connects existing models, business data and software tools into controlled workflows. The titles overlap, so choose by the work described: model ownership, product/application ownership, or business-process ownership.

Ask what the engineer is primarily responsible for keeping correct. A machine learning engineer owns the path from data to a trained, served and monitored model. An AI engineer owns a product or application that uses AI capabilities. An AI automation engineer owns a business workflow in which AI is one decision-making component among databases, SaaS tools, rules and human approvals.

These boundaries are practical, not official. A small company may call one person an AI engineer while expecting all three kinds of work. A larger organisation may split model training, AI product development and business integration across separate teams.

Accessible text equivalent: ML engineers centre the model lifecycle; AI engineers centre an AI-enabled application; AI automation engineers centre an end-to-end business process with integrations and human control.

A machine learning engineer turns data and modelling work into a reliable production capability. Current Google Cloud and AWS role guides include data preparation, model or foundation-model development, training, serving, pipeline orchestration, monitoring, security and ongoing improvement.

This path fits people who enjoy mathematics, experiments, data quality and infrastructure as much as model output. Typical projects include demand forecasting, recommendation, classification, computer vision, model fine-tuning or a reusable inference service. The engineer must explain metrics, data leakage, generalisation, drift and the operational cost of serving.

In this comparison, AI engineer means the application-focused role. The engineer selects or integrates models, builds retrieval and agent logic, connects the system to a product, evaluates task behaviour and operates the application. Microsoft’s current AI-103 profile names planning, generative and agentic solutions, information extraction, security and cross-functional collaboration.

The role still needs software engineering. A model call without authentication, schemas, tests, permissions, monitoring or failure handling is not a finished application. Some employers use “AI engineer” as an umbrella that includes ML engineering, so the vacancy must confirm the boundary.

An AI automation engineer begins with a business process: lead intake, document handling, reporting, knowledge access, customer requests or internal operations. The engineer connects triggers, models, databases and business applications, then adds rules, approvals, logs and recovery.

The model should not control permissions or consequential decisions. External messages, payments, access changes and sensitive actions remain behind deterministic rules or human approval. Almost a decade later, the wallet is active again. And its owner’s reported acquisition price makes the transaction particularly interesting.

According to Lookonchain, the whale originally acquired 23,412 ZEC at an average price of approximately $215, representing an investment of just over $5 million.

The latest transactions account for roughly two-thirds of that original position, with at least 7,750 ZEC transferred to a Kraken deposit address.

At the reported transfer valuation of approximately $723 per ZEC, those 15,750 coins are worth around $8 million more than their estimated acquisition cost. That would represent a return of approximately 236% if the investor sold them at that price.

What makes this wallet fascinating, however, is what happened between those two valuations.

Zcash spent much of 2024 and early 2025 trading around $16 to $20 before staging a remarkable comeback. At $20 per coin, the whale’s original investment would have been worth less than $470,000, a paper loss of more than 90%, assuming the position remained intact.

Holding an asset through a temporary market correction is one thing. Holding it through years of declining relevance, while newer cryptocurrencies attract developers, investors and attention, is something else entirely.

Whatever the owner’s reasons for keeping the coins untouched, the decision has aged considerably better than it appeared to during Zcash’s quieter years.

There is something almost ironic about the fact that investors are following the movements of a whale holding one of crypto’s best-known privacy coins.

Unlike networks where transaction privacy is mandatory, Zcash supports both transparent and shielded transactions. Transparent addresses allow observers to examine balances and transfers, while shielded transactions use zero-knowledge cryptography to conceal transaction details.

That distinction explains how blockchain monitoring platforms can identify certain large ZEC movements despite the cryptocurrency’s privacy capabilities.

It also explains why the available information tells only part of the story.

Lookonchain can identify an apparent exchange deposit and estimate its value. Establishing who controls the wallet, whether ownership changed privately during those nine years or what the investor intends to do next is considerably harder.

Even the reported acquisition price is an estimate rather than a verified record of the owner’s personal trading history.

That is an important limitation of on-chain analysis, particularly when dealing with a network explicitly designed to give users greater control over their financial visibility.

The renewed enthusiasm surrounding Zcash has attracted a different kind of investor from the one buying ZEC in 2017.

Corporate treasury strategies have emerged, institutional investors have taken an interest in privacy-focused assets, and the debate over financial surveillance has given Zcash a fresh investment narrative.

NewsBTC previously reported that Winklevoss Capital backed the launch of a dedicated Zcash treasury strategy, illustrating how exposure to the cryptocurrency has begun extending beyond traditional crypto wallets.

But the revival of an old cryptocurrency also comes with an interesting complication: investors who have spent years underwater finally have an opportunity to recover their capital or take profits.

That does not necessarily make the Kraken deposit bearish. A transfer to an exchange is not proof of a completed sale, and one wallet cannot establish a broader trend among Zcash holders.

Still, the transaction is worth watching. If more early investors start moving dormant coins onto exchanges, Zcash’s new buyers may find themselves absorbing supply from an entirely different generation of holders.

Nine years ago, someone reportedly committed more than $5 million to the idea of private digital money. Today, the blockchain offers a glimpse of what that investment has become.

Whether the owner has finished holding Zcash is a question the blockchain alone cannot yet answer.

Edited By: Vanni Gibertini

Last week, ICAO Headquarters in Montréal hosted the Seventh Worldwide Air Transport Conference, where Member States and industry stakeholders gathered to set global policy priorities on aviation economic regulation, infrastructure financing, resilience, consumer protection and sustainable growth.

Over 1,500 delegates from 154 Member States and organizations attended the week-long event thirteen years after the Sixth Conference in 2013. They discussed high-level topics in commercial aviation: modernizing economic and legal frameworks, investing in infrastructure and skilled workforces, and ensuring that innovation keeps pace with rapidly changing technologies and global demands.

“Broad agreement was reached on the need for harmonized, risk-based, and proportionate economic regulation that facilitates the adoption of emerging technologies such as artificial intelligence, automation, sustainable aviation fuels, and advanced air mobility solutions,” ICAO said in a press release during the Conference.

Participants agreed on the value of frameworks that support innovation and investment for economic development. They stressed this especially for smaller landlocked States, which depend heavily on air connectivity for growth.

The Conference supported modernizing air navigation infrastructure in line with ICAO’s Global Air Navigation Plan. It also supported liberalizing air services agreements without making liberalization conditional on infrastructure development, since new air links fostered by more liberal agreements are seen as key to economic growth.

However, all initiatives should be flexible enough to address local circumstances and should not burden developing States. ICAO’s statement continued: “The Conference also reaffirmed that international standards are key to building effective, market-enabling policies and avoiding fragmentation that could impede cross-border air services. Delegates pointed to ICAO’s Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation (CORSIA) as a successful example of this approach.”

Participants were asked to express their views on the documentation proposing various initiatives with regards to consumer protection legislations that have become an integral part of the legal framework for all air operators.

At the Sixth Worldwide Air Transport Conference in March 2013, ICAO was asked to develop Core Principles on consumer protection. The final document, adopted by the ICAO Council in 2015, contains provisions on:

The report for the Seventh Conference invites States to modernize their consumer-protection frameworks, keeping them evidence-based, proportionate, accessible and responsive to the changing realities of commercial air transport. It also says legislation should establish clear responsibilities for carriers, intermediaries and other providers regarding information, complaints and disruption assistance.

ICAO is expected to review its Core Principles while preserving national and regional flexibility. It is also expected to assess whether existing policies adequately cover online intermediaries and other overlooked situations.

Many Member States cautioned against the ICAO Air Passenger Handbook proposed by IATA, because producing and maintaining a single repository covering many different national laws would be too costly.

The Conference asked ICAO to “examine the need for harmonized, risk-based guidance on the use of AI in aviation, including common terminology and meaningful human oversight.”

The rapid development of AI and its unknown effects on aviation operations is a topic Member States will need to monitor. ICAO will coordinate and facilitate international exchange, while States will oversee implementation to ensure that the technologies adopted are secure, interoperable and consistent with cybersecurity and data-protection objectives.

ICAO will handle AI within its existing structure, as the proposal to create an ad hoc unit was not supported.

During the final two days of the Conference, ICAO held an Extraordinary Session of its Assembly to elect the four additional Council members required by the entry into force of the 2013 protocol amending the Convention on International Civil Aviation. The Council is the Organization’s permanent governing body, accountable to the Assembly and responsible for setting priorities and implementing its directions. Its membership increased from 36 to 40, and there are no permanent seats.

Six candidates competed for the four new seats: Ethiopia, Indonesia, Kazakhstan, Oman, Thailand and Türkiye. In the vote on the final day, Kazakhstan and Thailand were not elected, and the other four States won seats.

Welcome, once again, to PWNED, where we’re thankful every time someone leaves a password sticky note on a monitor or fails to delete a terminated employee’s admin account. Our story comes courtesy of Da Li, who serves as global alliance manager at Info2Soft, a company that provides data protection and recovery software. At one point, a partner called him into a case where a Chinese vehicle parts manufacturer fell victim to a ransomware gang. Unfortunately, its backup was compromised too.

The manufacturer had religiously backed up its manufacturing execution system (MES) and SAP orders, inventory, and finance systems every night. There was even a green dashboard that showed the backup process had succeeded, and someone checked that every morning.

However, one Friday evening, crims broke into the network and found out that the backup server was joined to the same Windows domain as the rest of the network. The hackers found a plaintext password for the backup service account in a maintenance script on a file share and discovered that someone had used the very same password for a domain admin account.

So the first thing the crooks did was delete every restore point on that backup server. Then they started encrypting the production servers to ransom them.

“Every backup job had succeeded for three years,” Li said. “The failure was that the thing meant to save them could be reached with the same keys as the thing being attacked.”

In a post early Thursday, Zakura recommended users migrate any ZEC still in Sapling to Ironwood. These shielded pools allow users to hold and transfer ZEC without publicly revealing transaction amounts or sender and recipient addresses.

"The time to prepare for a cryptographic breakthrough is before it happens," the team wrote.

While practical breaking of the pairing-based cryptography used by Sapling remains highly speculative, Zakura also recommended a network upgrade to disable payments into Sapling. Roughly 400,000 ZEC, or approximately 2% of supply, remains in the pool, according to Zakura, worth roughly $291 million at current prices. Zakura said most wallets support both Sapling and Ironwood, and the impact should be minimal, with miners accounting for much of the activity left in Sapling.

An earlier Zcash forum proposal in April had suggested waiting a year after a community decision to make Sapling withdrawal-only. At the time, the proposal put its balance at 600,000 ZEC, down from 1.1 million at the start of 2024. Zakura did not suggest a new deadline in its Thursday post.

Zakura pointed to a reported proof of the Riemann hypothesis, which concerns the distribution of prime numbers, and unsubstantiated rumors that AI labs had also made advances in integer factorization, the process of splitting large numbers into their prime factors. The difficulty of that task underpins RSA encryption, which is rarely used in cryptocurrencies, according to the team. Zakura said some math can be used for attacks on both RSA and pairing-based cryptography, so advances in one could help with the other, but the reported Riemann proof and the rumors do not show that either can be broken in practice.

"Practical pairing breaks are highly speculative," Zakura said, adding that transparent transactions and Ironwood do not rely on factoring or pairings.

Nevertheless, a hypothetical break in Sapling's pairing-based proof system would allow forged proofs, Zakura said, putting the funds in the pool at risk. The same cryptography does not secure users' private keys or their cryptographic records of shielded funds, so the team said developers could replace the proof system without replacing those records. However, Zcash would have to freeze Sapling before an attack compromises its state and users could then later recover their funds using a new ECDLP-based or hash-based SNARK, Zakura said.

A chip can contain hidden vulnerabilities or malicious modifications that users cannot easily detect. Even a secure wallet application cannot guarantee safety if the hardware underneath it has been compromised. This has happened recently with Ledger, and Ledger is not the only one, with Trezor and other hardware wallets being recently compromised.

“Not your silicon, not your keys.” – Vitalik put the problem bluntly.

The phrase extends crypto’s self-custody mantra into the physical computing layer. Owning private keys might not mean much if users cannot trust the hardware handling them.

This is why Vitalik argues that “the hardware layer must be open for participation and tinkering too.” He wants hardware designs to become accessible to developers, researchers, and independent auditors, allowing them to examine and modify components rather than blindly trusting manufacturers.

This could eventually create opportunities for Ethereum hardware focused on security, cryptographic computing, and decentralized infrastructure. Ethereum validators, hardware wallets, and zero-knowledge proof systems all depend on computing components that users need to trust.

Nvidia built its dominance by turning GPUs into the backbone of artificial intelligence. Its hardware, software ecosystem, and CUDA platform have made competition increasingly difficult.

But Vitalik’s vision displays a different approach. Instead of chasing the fastest processors, Ethereum hardware could prioritize openness, verifiability, and security.

Vitalik acknowledges that open-source hardware faces performance limitations. Advanced chip manufacturing remains expensive and tightly controlled, leaving open designs behind the industry’s most sophisticated commercial processors.

Yet that trade-off might not matter for every workload. Vitalik writes that “Secure hardware may be 10x less efficient or more” while still being sufficient for many computing needs. That’s a striking contrast to an industry obsessed with squeezing more performance from increasingly expensive chips.

Specialized silicon designed for cryptographic operations, privacy-preserving computing, secure data processing, or decentralized networks could benefit from open designs. Developers could modify the architecture, researchers could inspect the circuitry, and manufacturers could compete to produce chips from publicly available specifications.

Ethereum already has an extensive developer community experimenting with cryptography and distributed computing. Extending that culture into silicon could give the ecosystem a foothold in hardware without immediately challenging Nvidia’s AI infrastructure.

The ambition could be less about building another GPU giant and more about creating a competing hardware philosophy.

That could eventually extend into personal computers, servers, IoT devices, and secure computing infrastructure. Hardware designed around transparency could become valuable as AI, financial services, and sensitive personal data move into increasingly powerful machines.

Nvidia’s dominance isn’t going to disappear overnight. But Ethereum has never needed to follow the traditional technology industry’s playbook.

The eminent evolutionary biologist and modeller John Maynard Smith was once asked about Karl Popper and the philosophy of science. His answer was not flattering. His impression was that scientists who take the philosophy of science seriously, and allow their research to be influenced by philosophical preconceptions, are "far more likely to do themselves harm than good". He loved reading philosophy of science and was always ready to argue about it, he added, but he did not believe that one should allow oneself to be influenced by it when actually thinking about science.

This is why I've recently published an academic article on the topic. These are quite radical words, and though Maynard Smith's generalisation is untenable, there is an important insight to be extracted.

If modellers approach the philosophical literature on models with the goal of figuring out what models are and what a good model is, they might easily find themselves lured into naive monistic views of models that fail to represent the full epistemic richness of modelling practices, thereby turning them into worse scientists than they could have otherwise been.

This could happen in several ways. For instance, models with epistemic functions not recognised by narrow monist accounts might be discarded. Consider, for example, models with weak predictive power, but great generality and insight into complex systems.

Perhaps Maynard Smith's worry is most strongly illustrated in economics, where Milton Friedman and Karl Popper are taken to have the "last word" in any methodological debate, and are frequently invoked to both defend and criticise the work of modellers. They are perhaps paradigm cases of monist thinking. Friedman argued that the only relevant desideratum in science concerns predictive power, while Popper argued that what matters is falsifiability.

These accounts of science have received extensive criticism over the last decades. Indeed, the philosopher Alex Rosenberg argued that Friedman's naive picture single-handedly justified the existence of philosophy of economics as a distinct profession. Economists, however, are unlikely to read much if any work on methodology beyond Friedman and Popper. As the economist Dani Rodrik notes, economic methodology is relegated to specialised journals and, more importantly, is not really being taught in graduate school.

The result is that economists seem to believe that their science operates according to a simple form of falsificationism, a picture of science that, most contemporary philosophers of science would agree, both misrepresents scientific practice and provides the wrong ideal of how science ought to function.

The obvious counter to Maynard Smith's concern is that it is not an argument against the philosophy of science, but rather against bad philosophy of science, which limits, rather than fosters, insights into the richness of the epistemic tools available to scientists.

Philosophers are not innocent here. Some debates in the philosophy of science rest on such monist motivations to seek univocal accounts of laws, explanation, species, functions, preferences, and many more. As Peter Godfrey-Smith emphasised, one of the hazards of philosophy is the temptation to come up with theories that are "too broad and sweeping". This hazard has not been avoided in the philosophical literature on models. Many philosophers of science seem to be under the impression that once we figure out what "models" are and how they work, we can finally get a handle on all the other pressing questions in the philosophy of science. I argue that this way of thinking about models is grounded in a mistake.

A philosophy of science worth reading takes the plurality, diversity, and richness of models and modelling practices seriously. It does not hand scientists a single criterion for good science.

Economists, Rodrik argues, need a better story about the kind of science they practise, and some economists have recently engaged with philosophical questions themselves, seeking contact with philosophers of economics in the process. Philosophers of science, in turn, should turn more of their attention toward both the social sciences and the philosophy of the social sciences. Their lack of engagement with this rich literature is a historical omission that may have provided a misguided understanding of how science works.

Should scientists ignore philosophers? They should ignore those who tell them that there is only one way of doing science well.

The three-person panel’s written reasons were published on Thursday and stated: “The commission members were unanimous that, taking the foul committed by LY (Yoro) out of the scenario, AI (Isak) would not have had an obvious goalscoring opportunity.

“Despite the fact that AI had reached the ball initially, his touch was heavy to the extent that he had in fact lost control of the ball – that control had, in effect, passed to the advancing MUFC goalkeeper who in the view of the commission would have been able to gather or smother the ball before AI could get to it and shoot at goal.

“To the mind of the commission, the referee had made an ‘obvious error’ in deciding that AI (Isak) would have had an obvious goalscoring opportunity.”

Webb, the chief refereeing officer at Pro Ref, told the Match Officials: Mic’d Up programme which aired on Tuesday, shortly after confirmation the ban had been removed: “There’s a question mark I guess around whether or not he (Isak) would get onto that ball before Lammens. “The VAR checks to see whether that decision made on the field was clearly and obviously incorrect, and formed the opinion that it wasn’t.

“I think by and large, most people would see that as a correct decision, with Isak moving quickly on towards that ball. The alternative view is that Lammens might get there first, but I think it’s a little bit debatable. (It’s) certainly not clearly wrong.”

The FA’s independent panel, which was made up of former Blackburn and Middlesbrough winger Stuart Ripley, former Sheffield United and Notts County forward Tony Agana and ex-Walsall goalkeeper Mick Kearns, disagreed.

The reasons confirm that United submitted technical information to the panel which concluded Isak would have been, at best, 1.8 metres short of the ball even if Yoro had not made the challenge.

To add to the confusion, the Key Match Incidents (KMI) Panel is reported by the Daily Mail to have backed Barrott’s call and the decision of the VAR John Brooks not to recommend a review.

8 Modern Thriller Books To Read Before Kicking the Bucket

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

There's plenty of adrenaline to be found in modern thriller books. With anxiety, crime, and social tensions seemingly at an all-time high, these novels keep readers up past midnight, tapping into some of society's biggest fears. Whether it’s fractured relationships or institutional corruption, thriller books translate these tensions into pages.

Whether you're into psychological mind games or constant plot twists, there's something for every kind of thriller fan. From an international murder conspiracy to a desperate attempt to prove someone's loyalty to both sides of a war, here are modern thriller books to read before kicking the bucket.

Shutter Island is proof that books can be better than their movie adaptations. Dennis Lehane's novel follows U.S. Marshal Teddy Daniels and his new partner, Chuck Aule, who are sent to a remote island to investigate a patient's disappearance from a hospital for the criminally insane. The staff's strange behavior makes their investigation anything but routine, and a storm soon leaves them with no way off the island.

Unlike the movie, the novel allows Teddy's descent into madness to progress more organically. The limited point of view makes his increasingly questionable thoughts and assumptions feel convincing, and that's all thanks to Lehane's ability to hide Teddy's real experiences with eloquent prose. With the hospital staff constantly making him question his own judgment, readers are left just as uncertain as Teddy about what is real and what isn't.

Viet Thanh Nguyen's The Sympathizer explores the Vietnam War through the eyes of someone living in a constant state of in-betweens. A half-Vietnamese, half-French communist spy, the novel's unnamed narrator is forced to flee Saigon for Los Angeles. Despite having to rebuild his life in a new country, he secretly keeps tabs on South Vietnamese military officials living in exile.

It's a never-ending losing game in The Sympathizer, with the narrator unable to please either side without betraying the other. He is constantly caught between his political beliefs, personal identity, and the need to stay alive. No matter where he goes, he cannot simply leave his past behind. The Sympathizer explores this constant duality between two identities, two loyalties, and two countries, showing just how difficult it is to start over when his own allegiances keep pulling him in opposite directions.

BookTok sensations do not always deserve their stellar reputation, but Alex Michaelides' The Silent Patient is a cautionary tale of what it means to be a silenced woman, quite literally. Alicia Berenson seems to have the perfect life as a successful painter married to Gabriel, until she shoots him five times. After his death, she stops speaking and is admitted to a psychiatric facility. Six years later, psychotherapist Theo Faber takes on her case.

Michaelides complicates the story by bringing in two near-convincing perspectives. Theo's investigation and Alicia's diary entries reveal different sides of the events surrounding Gabriel's murder, though neither account can be trusted entirely. With nobody telling the whole truth, readers must decide whose version to believe: the accused murderer, or a man convinced he has her figured out.

Lucy Foley's The Guest List is essentially Agatha Christie meets Knives Out. Magazine publisher Jules Keegan and television personality Will Slater gather their handpicked guests for a glamorous wedding on a remote Irish island. But the celebrations take a grim turn when a storm cuts off the island from the mainland. Before the couple can even say their "I dos," Will is found stabbed to death, leaving everyone a potential suspect.

Unlike most murder mysteries, The Guest List doesn't exactly make readers feel sorry for the victim. The guests' motives trace back to an incident from years earlier, one that could ruin Will's reputation (before he died). There's something almost satisfyingly karmic about his tragic fate. But then again, no one wants to be on the same stranded island as an unknown killer.

Toni Collette and Kit Harington are shooting a new Miss Marple and 20th Century has several more Christies in development, so the drawing room is busier than it has been in decades. Here is which of them you are, and the case for yours being the best.

Order, method, and a quiet confidence that you are the cleverest person present. Poirot is a retired Belgian police officer who solves murders by sitting still and thinking, and who is vain enough to make the reveal a performance every single time.

People tell you things because they cannot imagine you being a threat. Marple solves murders by comparing everybody to somebody she once knew in St Mary Mead, which sounds like a joke until you notice it works every time, because human beings are not as original as they think.

You have far too many ideas and roughly a fifth of them are excellent. Ariadne Oliver is a successful crime novelist who is sick of her famous foreign detective and keeps saying so, which is Christie writing about Christie and getting away with it completely.

You are steady, decent and the reason the difficult genius has anybody at all. Hastings narrates the early Poirot novels, misses most of the clues, and is the only character in the books that Poirot is unambiguously fond of, which tells you what he is really for.

Anyone who enjoyed Stephen King's Misery should give Taylor Adams' novel a read. No Exit follows college student Darby Thorne, who gets stranded at an isolated highway rest stop during a Colorado blizzard. With four strangers for company, Darby is horrified to discover a kidnapped nine-year-old girl locked inside a van outside. The worst part is that the kidnapper is one of the four strangers sharing the rest stop with her.

Darby is just someone who happens to be in the wrong place at the wrong time, which makes her situation all the more dire. With no one she can trust, she has to rely on her limited abilities to stay alive and save the child. It's a nerve-wracking survival thriller, but also a disturbing reminder that situations like these aren't just fictional scenarios and can happen to anyone, particularly women who find themselves in vulnerable situations.

Stieg Larsson's The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo follows disgraced journalist Mikael Blomkvist, who accepts a job from wealthy businessman Henrik Vanger to investigate the disappearance of his niece, Harriet, who vanished during a family gathering on the Vanger family's remote island four decades earlier. Working alongside hacker Lisbeth Salander, Mikael soon realizes that there's much more to the case.

Often considered a quintessential Nordic noir, The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo is dark, bleak, and shows little mercy towards its flawed characters. Mikael and Lisbeth are far from conventional heroes, and neither has had an easy life. In many ways, they're both just trying to survive. But as they uncover the truth behind Harriet's disappearance and the dangers surrounding it, they find a new purpose: fighting back, even if it means breaking the law.

Adapted into an HBO limited series, Sharp Objects follows journalist Camille Preaker, who reluctantly returns to her Missouri hometown to investigate the murder of a young girl and the disappearance of another. Forced to live with her estranged mother, controlling stepfather, and rebellious half-sister Amma, Camille must also confront memories of her late sister Marian. As the investigation takes a darker turn, Camille begins to suspect that the case is closer to home than she would like.

Gillian Flynn might be best known for her work Gone Girl, but Sharp Objects raises an important warning about the dangers of unaddressed intergenerational trauma. Through Camille's strained family relationships, the series explores how abuse doesn't just affect one person; it creates a hostile environment where trauma gets passed down, damages relationships, and breeds new forms of violence.

Few works of art are as iconic as Leonardo da Vinci's Mona Lisa, so it's no surprise that Dan Brown uses it as the starting point for The Da Vinci Code. After Louvre curator Jacques Saunière is found murdered, Harvard professor Robert Langdon becomes involved in an investigation that takes him through hidden messages, famous paintings, and the mystery of the Holy Grail.

Brown isn't simply interested in telling a murder mystery. He also plays with historical accounts, particularly those surrounding Christianity, by exploring what might happen if widely accepted beliefs were challenged. Some readers may find this approach uncomfortable, but it makes the novel all the more interesting. Brown takes complicated subjects like art, religion, and ancient history and turns them into a puzzle that makes readers want to crack open a history book for fun.
