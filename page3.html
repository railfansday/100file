Stale links on UK public sector websites direct visitors to gambling dens

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Consultancy finds 93 routes to casino promotions across 26 orgs, with defunct enterprise partnership domains repurposed

UK public sector websites are directing visitors to gambling promotions through old links to defunct local enterprise partnerships (LEPs), research by digital consultancy Digital Royalty has found.

The consultancy identified 93 links across 79 pages on websites belonging to 26 public bodies.

LEPs brought together councils and businesses to promote economic growth in England under a coalition government initiative launched in 2010. Central government funding ended in April 2024, with their core functions transferred to local and combined authorities.

Among other things, Digital Royalty builds and looks after websites, which means chasing down old links. In August, it found that a link on Surrey County Council's website led to a former LEP address now displaying casino content. It went looking for more.

Digital Royalty founder Alex De Sousa said: "Slow is usually a sign of caution and diligence, but 31 months after the LEPs wound up, it's crossed into inefficient and dangerous."

The consultancy offered The Register four examples of websites run by the Department for Digital, Culture, Media and Sport (DCMS), Hull City Council, Parliament, and the Local Government Association. One example is a government page about the Digital Skills Partnership, originally published under an earlier incarnation of DCMS.

The page includes a link that appears to be for the Humber Digital Skills Partnership within the Humber LEP website, but in fact takes users to a site advertising "Independent Non GamStop Casinos UK – New Casino Sites Outside GamStop." GamStop is the self-exclusion scheme designed to help people restrict their online gambling, which makes the links more concerning than ordinary website clutter.

The same link appears on the Hull City Council website under a list of Joint Committees, Partnerships and Trusts where it appears as Local Economic Partnership (Humber LEP).

An old page for the Parliament website detailing the Department for Business, Energy & Industrial Strategy committee's Industrial Strategy report includes a footnote (135) that appears to be for the Business Central funding finder service, but takes the user to the "Highest RTP Non GamStop Casinos for UK Players."

The Local Government Association's (LGA) sustainability and waste route map included a link to the former York and North Yorkshire LEP website. The destination initially appeared legitimate but contained links promoting crypto casinos. The LGA now appears to have removed the link.

All the organizations mentioned have been contacted by The Register to offer them the opportunity to comment.

Digital Royalty checked former web addresses associated with about 70 LEPs and growth hubs, then used a backlink index to identify roughly 3,000 incoming links from public sector sites. Most were simply outdated rather than leading to gambling content.

The findings are not exhaustive: the index may miss pages, and the research did not cover other types of defunct public body whose domains may have been repurposed. ®

8 Modern Thriller Books To Read Before Kicking the Bucket

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

There's plenty of adrenaline to be found in modern thriller books. With anxiety, crime, and social tensions seemingly at an all-time high, these novels keep readers up past midnight, tapping into some of society's biggest fears. Whether it’s fractured relationships or institutional corruption, thriller books translate these tensions into pages.

Whether you're into psychological mind games or constant plot twists, there's something for every kind of thriller fan. From an international murder conspiracy to a desperate attempt to prove someone's loyalty to both sides of a war, here are modern thriller books to read before kicking the bucket.

Shutter Island is proof that books can be better than their movie adaptations. Dennis Lehane's novel follows U.S. Marshal Teddy Daniels and his new partner, Chuck Aule, who are sent to a remote island to investigate a patient's disappearance from a hospital for the criminally insane. The staff's strange behavior makes their investigation anything but routine, and a storm soon leaves them with no way off the island.

Unlike the movie, the novel allows Teddy's descent into madness to progress more organically. The limited point of view makes his increasingly questionable thoughts and assumptions feel convincing, and that's all thanks to Lehane's ability to hide Teddy's real experiences with eloquent prose. With the hospital staff constantly making him question his own judgment, readers are left just as uncertain as Teddy about what is real and what isn't.

Viet Thanh Nguyen's The Sympathizer explores the Vietnam War through the eyes of someone living in a constant state of in-betweens. A half-Vietnamese, half-French communist spy, the novel's unnamed narrator is forced to flee Saigon for Los Angeles. Despite having to rebuild his life in a new country, he secretly keeps tabs on South Vietnamese military officials living in exile.

It's a never-ending losing game in The Sympathizer, with the narrator unable to please either side without betraying the other. He is constantly caught between his political beliefs, personal identity, and the need to stay alive. No matter where he goes, he cannot simply leave his past behind. The Sympathizer explores this constant duality between two identities, two loyalties, and two countries, showing just how difficult it is to start over when his own allegiances keep pulling him in opposite directions.

BookTok sensations do not always deserve their stellar reputation, but Alex Michaelides' The Silent Patient is a cautionary tale of what it means to be a silenced woman, quite literally. Alicia Berenson seems to have the perfect life as a successful painter married to Gabriel, until she shoots him five times. After his death, she stops speaking and is admitted to a psychiatric facility. Six years later, psychotherapist Theo Faber takes on her case.

Michaelides complicates the story by bringing in two near-convincing perspectives. Theo's investigation and Alicia's diary entries reveal different sides of the events surrounding Gabriel's murder, though neither account can be trusted entirely. With nobody telling the whole truth, readers must decide whose version to believe: the accused murderer, or a man convinced he has her figured out.

Lucy Foley's The Guest List is essentially Agatha Christie meets Knives Out. Magazine publisher Jules Keegan and television personality Will Slater gather their handpicked guests for a glamorous wedding on a remote Irish island. But the celebrations take a grim turn when a storm cuts off the island from the mainland. Before the couple can even say their "I dos," Will is found stabbed to death, leaving everyone a potential suspect.

Unlike most murder mysteries, The Guest List doesn't exactly make readers feel sorry for the victim. The guests' motives trace back to an incident from years earlier, one that could ruin Will's reputation (before he died). There's something almost satisfyingly karmic about his tragic fate. But then again, no one wants to be on the same stranded island as an unknown killer.

Toni Collette and Kit Harington are shooting a new Miss Marple and 20th Century has several more Christies in development, so the drawing room is busier than it has been in decades. Here is which of them you are, and the case for yours being the best.

Order, method, and a quiet confidence that you are the cleverest person present. Poirot is a retired Belgian police officer who solves murders by sitting still and thinking, and who is vain enough to make the reveal a performance every single time.

People tell you things because they cannot imagine you being a threat. Marple solves murders by comparing everybody to somebody she once knew in St Mary Mead, which sounds like a joke until you notice it works every time, because human beings are not as original as they think.

You have far too many ideas and roughly a fifth of them are excellent. Ariadne Oliver is a successful crime novelist who is sick of her famous foreign detective and keeps saying so, which is Christie writing about Christie and getting away with it completely.

You are steady, decent and the reason the difficult genius has anybody at all. Hastings narrates the early Poirot novels, misses most of the clues, and is the only character in the books that Poirot is unambiguously fond of, which tells you what he is really for.

Anyone who enjoyed Stephen King's Misery should give Taylor Adams' novel a read. No Exit follows college student Darby Thorne, who gets stranded at an isolated highway rest stop during a Colorado blizzard. With four strangers for company, Darby is horrified to discover a kidnapped nine-year-old girl locked inside a van outside. The worst part is that the kidnapper is one of the four strangers sharing the rest stop with her.

Darby is just someone who happens to be in the wrong place at the wrong time, which makes her situation all the more dire. With no one she can trust, she has to rely on her limited abilities to stay alive and save the child. It's a nerve-wracking survival thriller, but also a disturbing reminder that situations like these aren't just fictional scenarios and can happen to anyone, particularly women who find themselves in vulnerable situations.

Stieg Larsson's The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo follows disgraced journalist Mikael Blomkvist, who accepts a job from wealthy businessman Henrik Vanger to investigate the disappearance of his niece, Harriet, who vanished during a family gathering on the Vanger family's remote island four decades earlier. Working alongside hacker Lisbeth Salander, Mikael soon realizes that there's much more to the case.

Often considered a quintessential Nordic noir, The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo is dark, bleak, and shows little mercy towards its flawed characters. Mikael and Lisbeth are far from conventional heroes, and neither has had an easy life. In many ways, they're both just trying to survive. But as they uncover the truth behind Harriet's disappearance and the dangers surrounding it, they find a new purpose: fighting back, even if it means breaking the law.

Adapted into an HBO limited series, Sharp Objects follows journalist Camille Preaker, who reluctantly returns to her Missouri hometown to investigate the murder of a young girl and the disappearance of another. Forced to live with her estranged mother, controlling stepfather, and rebellious half-sister Amma, Camille must also confront memories of her late sister Marian. As the investigation takes a darker turn, Camille begins to suspect that the case is closer to home than she would like.

Gillian Flynn might be best known for her work Gone Girl, but Sharp Objects raises an important warning about the dangers of unaddressed intergenerational trauma. Through Camille's strained family relationships, the series explores how abuse doesn't just affect one person; it creates a hostile environment where trauma gets passed down, damages relationships, and breeds new forms of violence.

Few works of art are as iconic as Leonardo da Vinci's Mona Lisa, so it's no surprise that Dan Brown uses it as the starting point for The Da Vinci Code. After Louvre curator Jacques Saunière is found murdered, Harvard professor Robert Langdon becomes involved in an investigation that takes him through hidden messages, famous paintings, and the mystery of the Holy Grail.

Brown isn't simply interested in telling a murder mystery. He also plays with historical accounts, particularly those surrounding Christianity, by exploring what might happen if widely accepted beliefs were challenged. Some readers may find this approach uncomfortable, but it makes the novel all the more interesting. Brown takes complicated subjects like art, religion, and ancient history and turns them into a puzzle that makes readers want to crack open a history book for fun.

Three Ancient Cosmic Collisions May Explain Why the Moon Has Two Faces

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists have narrowed down when three massive collisions reshaped the Moon, bringing them closer to understanding why its Earth-facing side looks so different from its far side.

Billions of years ago, a collision powerful enough to melt enormous quantities of lunar rock created the Moon’s largest impact basin. Subsequent collisions broke apart and scattered some of that ancient material across the surface, leaving behind a geological record that scientists are now piecing together.

Located on the Moon’s far side, the South Pole-Aitken (SPA) basin is one of the oldest and largest impact structures in the solar system. Understanding its history could help explain why the Moon’s near and far sides are so different, what lies beneath its surface, and when major collisions occurred during its early evolution.

An international research team led by Professor Guochun Zhao, Mok Sau-King, a professor in the Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences (DEPS) at the University of Hong Kong (HKU), and Dr. Yuqi Qian, the study’s first author, has identified two types of magnesium-rich rocks with remarkably different origins. Published in Science Advances, the research also establishes a clearer timeline of the major impacts that shaped the region.

For decades, the basin remained largely unexplored. That changed in 2019, when Chang’e-4 completed the first successful landing on the Moon’s far side, followed in 2024 by Chang’e-6, which brought the first rock samples from this largely unexplored region back to Earth.

Using Chang’e-6 samples allocated to HKU DEPS and surface measurements from both missions, the researchers obtained the first direct geological evidence from the two landing sites for reconstructing the basin’s impact history. Satellite observations and numerical simulations helped them trace the origins of rocks across the wider basin.

One group of rocks, known as moderately magnesium-rich norites, formed in the aftermath of the original SPA collision. The impact generated a thick sheet of molten rock that gradually cooled, with different minerals crystallizing at different stages. Later collisions exposed these ancient rocks at the central peaks of large craters. Some were eventually blasted toward the Chang’e-4 landing site in Von Kármán crater by the impact that formed Finsen crater.

The second group contained considerably more magnesium and likely formed through a separate process called magnesium-suite magmatism, a type of magma activity associated with the original SPA impact. These rocks were exposed along the western ring of mountain peaks within the Apollo basin before the Chaffee S impact scattered some of them toward the Chang’e-6 landing site.

Earlier research by the HKU-led team had already uncovered evidence of prolonged volcanic activity and previously unrecognized magma activity in the region. Those studies also suggested that underground accumulations of titanium-rich minerals, known as ilmenite-bearing cumulates, helped account for the contrasting volcanic histories of the Moon’s two sides.

The latest findings suggest that the South Pole-Aitken basin formed approximately 4.34 billion years ago. Another major collision created the Apollo basin around 4.2 billion years ago, followed by the formation of the Orientale basin approximately 3.87 billion years ago. Together, these three events reveal how successive collisions reshaped the Moon’s largest impact basin over hundreds of millions of years.

From the CD changer to the iPod and aux jack, the decade saw audio options shift—for better or for worse.

These days, each of us is in our own little world. We’ve forfeited a largely shared reality and replaced it with an almost infinite series of more private universes. And I’m going to be honest with you: Results have been mixed. The seeds of our collective solitude were planted in the decades before, and the worst of it wouldn’t hit us until the years after, but 2000–09 was the era when the monoculture really began to fracture and fragment into microcultures. The great American mainstream was dwindling in the Aughts, a narrative expressed through our music. New technology changed the way people across the world listened. And as Americans, we in particular experienced this historic shift through our car stereos.

This story originally appeared in Volume 38 of Road & Track.

Once we shook off Y2K anxiety, the new millennium began as normal. Fueled by boy bands, blockbuster hip-hop, and, somehow, Limp Bizkit, the recording industry was flush in 2000. What was big was huge, and whether you liked the songs or not, you knew them. My main musical memory of the year 2000 took place in a rental car, on a drive up to the mountains with my roommates. The mid-size sedan had a mere cassette player, so we made do with the radio. On that October afternoon, some station played the comeback single from a band that hadn’t released a studio album in three years: U2’s “Beautiful Day.” We turned it up, we nodded along, and we were impressed that a band could sound this good despite having members who were now 40. Best of all, we shared the experience with however many strangers were also tuned in to this frequency.

As the Aughts progressed, I found myself sliding into my car’s CD slot fewer store-bought discs and more homemade mixes I burned using my iBook after downloading music from iTunes. It was easy, it was cheap at 99 cents per song, and it was as frictionless as a click on the touchpad. I recently found a box of these discs in my garage, and each one is packed with music I have not thought about since I put them there. These early-­Aughts songs left me no physical album covers to remember them by; the playlists were not yet saved for posterity on streaming services. They vanished.

Then there was the iPod. Other MP3 players predated it, but the iPod was sleek and sexy and held up to 1000 songs, which I swear sounded like a lot in 2001. The forward-thinking among us might carry a special cord in our bag in case we found ourselves in a car with an auxiliary input. Traditional radio stations suffered as we ditched the DJs and did their job for free.

Satellite radio launched an effort to fill the void—XM in 2001, Sirius in 2002, then SiriusXM in 2008, with hundreds of hyperspecific-genre stations on the digital dial. Satellite radio broke us in to the concept of paying a monthly fee to listen to music we don’t own, and Spotify and Apple Music would run with that idea in the decades ahead. Now we all have access to virtually every piece of music ever recorded, but who is guiding us through it? It’s chaos. I find myself longing for those days before endless choices, before Bluetooth or aux cables, when I’d trust a stranger to play something for me while I was driving. When I’d be on a road trip and an unknown station would fuzz in and play a country song and a public service announcement, when a human being in a studio said something that streaked on radio waves across the desert air, and we were connected, until the station fuzzed out.

Of course, I can still do that. But the convenience culture that has continued to creep in since the Aughts has conditioned me to pull out my phone, wirelessly connect it to the car, and put on a mix or a podcast. I actually have to remind myself that those days were real, when the radio was once our go-to.

I miss those times. The new stuff from U2 is pretty good, though.

As darkness falls, Saturn will glow at first magnitude, shining with a sedate, yellow-white light and appearing noticeably brighter than the star Fomalhaut, about 40 degrees (the equivalent of four fist-widths) to its lower right.

Warning: This article contains spoilers for "The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth."

It simply wouldn't be a Quentin Tarantino movie if it didn't double down on itself. After wowing audiences everywhere in 2019 with "Once Upon a Time in Hollywood," the quirky and uber-talented filmmaker returned to the scene of the crime (so to speak) once more with his long-awaited sequel/spin-off film, "The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth." This time, the buddy comedy act with Leonardo DiCaprio's Rick Dalton and Brad Pitt's Cliff has been dropped, focusing solely on the latter as the stuntman embarks on his latest round of mischief. Though Tarantino stepped aside from his directing duties to make room for David Fincher, well, let's just say that the script is as purely QT as it gets.

That quickly becomes evident as we're dropped back into this alternate-history world of 1970s Los Angeles, and there's one recurring gag that references a certain controversy from before. You see, despite so much acclaim from critics and fans alike, "Once Upon a Time" drew the ire of many with its irreverent treatment of martial arts legend Bruce Lee, portrayed by Mike Moh in the film. Even Lee's family weighed in on the matter, objecting to how easily Cliff puts the arrogant Lee in his place during a fight scene on the set of a film. Not surprisingly, "Cliff Booth" returns to this fraught topic on several occasions, and it certainly feels like a meta-textual jab at the real-world backlash from before.

Was this in poor taste, or is Tarantino merely poking fun with a self-deprecating running joke? Moviegoers will undoubtedly have strong takes on either side. Here's where we land on our second go-around with this little debate.

You know, there's something to be said for addressing an uncomfortable matter head-on instead of dancing around it — or even ignoring it altogether. Quentin Tarantino has never been one to shy away from a fight, a trait he happens to share with washed-up stuntman Cliff Booth. As a master cinephile, a brilliant bartender, a hit with the ladies, and a ruthless killer all in one, the power fantasy of it all isn't exactly hard to see in "The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth." Perhaps that's what made the Bruce Lee moment in "Once Upon a Time in Hollywood" stand out all the more (a fight that almost ended very differently). It's one thing for Cliff to be the coolest person alive in his world, and quite another for him to intrude rudely upon our own.

So how does "Cliff Booth" address this elephant in the room? Although the incident is brought up on a number of occasions in passing, Yahya Abdul-Mateen II's buzzy actor Rodrick "Hot Rod" Harrison is positively fixated on it once he meets Cliff — a fact that also seems to catch the eye of talent manager Karen Crosby (Elizabeth Debicki). Intriguingly, Cliff himself almost seems embarrassed by the attention, perhaps self-reflexively brushing it off as the brash actions of a young man. Maybe, like Tarantino himself, he's a little older and a little wiser and less apt to repeat the same mistakes.

Less generously, the fact that this fight is emphasized so much in "Cliff Booth" could make the exact opposite point. It's treated almost like a legend or a myth in this world, giving Cliff a dangerous sense of aura and mystique that belies his charming appearance.

Either way, this is just one ingredient tossed into a much larger stew. "The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth" may struggle to reach the transcendent heights of "Once Upon a Time in Hollywood," but isn't that part of its point, too? "What is Cliff Booth without Rick Dalton?" Quentin Tarantino's script seems to be asking. Not dissimilarly, is a Tarantino movie with David Fincher at the wheel really a Tarantino movie anymore? Who can say how much ownership any of us have over our own art — or our lives in general?

Perhaps that's why "Cliff Booth" feels so much colder and mean-spirited than its predecessor. Although both films build to violent climaxes, the one in "Hollywood" is shot to be much more over-the-top and darkly funny than the one here. Before, Rick Dalton and Cliff were fighting to preserve a very specific way of life — one that the Manson family threatened to overturn. Here, Cliff very easily could've avoided the bloodshed with killers Eddie (Matt Groove) and Fernando Verdusco (JB Tadena), but he pursues this anyway out of ... ego? Principle? A sense of right and wrong? Cliff is a man of both simplicity and contradictions, so perhaps it's only fitting that the filmmaking style reflects this through Fincher's very different approach, as well.

That's where the repeated jabs about the Bruce Lee fight come in, as a moment that Cliff has no choice but to own. "Cliff Booth" is a movie about our attempts to define our own stories — whether it be himself, the troublemaking TV star Charlie Peters (Scott Caan), or Karen. And maybe Bruce Lee, too.

By pursuing access to the U.S. capital markets, FutureMain aims to strengthen its international business foundation and accelerate the adoption of its industrial AI technologies.

A vibration too faint for the human ear to detect. A temperature variation that is difficult to observe directly. An unusual pattern hidden within operational data.

The global artificial intelligence industry is advancing rapidly, with companies competing to develop increasingly capable models and applications.

Yet applying AI effectively in real industrial environments remains a fundamentally different challenge.

A factory cannot be fully understood through information learned from the internet alone.

Similar vibration patterns may indicate entirely different mechanical problems depending on equipment design, operating conditions and the physical mechanisms involved.

For plant operators, an alarm indicating that something is abnormal is rarely sufficient to justify stopping a critical production line.

What matters is understanding not only what is wrong, but why it is happening and what should be done next.

The newly discovered Titian self-portrait

“Both Rubens and his erstwhile assistant Van Dyck venerated Titian as the very prince of painters.” So writes the Titian expert Peter Humfrey in the catalogue for Dickinson’s impressive new exhibition, Titian, Rubens, Van Dyck, which opened last night and runs until 18th December at 58 Jermyn Street, London.

Simon Dickinson and his son Milo have brought together two paintings by Titian, two by Rubens and nine by Van Dyck for the show, all from private collections and most for sale, priced between around £400,000 and over £3m. At the heart of the show is the relationship between the three painters, principally the influence of Titian on the two younger, Flemish painters.

The exhibition, one of the most ambitious staged by the gallery in its 33-year history, is accompanied by an extensive catalogue including essays from Humfrey alongside the Belgian art historians Koenraad Jonckheere, a professor in Northern Renaissance and Baroque art at Ghent University, and Katlijne Van der Stighelen, a Rubens expert.

While works by Titian, Rubens and Van Dyck are “very, very difficult to find”, Milo Dickinson says, there are still some at large thanks to all three being extremely prolific. “Titian was this great role model to Rubens and then Van Dyck, both as a man who was a successful artist throughout his life, courted by major patrons, but also as a painter: the colours he used, the compositions he devised,” Dickinson says. Rubens, for instance, “spent several months just copying paintings by Titian in Madrid, which had been bought by Phillip II of Spain.” As for Van Dyck, the sketchbook he took around Italy when he was a young man is full of annotated studies of Titians.

The exhibition includes several recent rediscoveries, most made after cleaning, with the catalogue outlining the arguments in favour of the attributions made by various experts.

Peter Paul Rubens, Study of the young Anthony van Dyck (around 1616-7)

Among them is what is thought to be a late self-portrait by Titian, variously dated by scholars to between 1546-47 to 1560. The attribution has been long debated by scholars, Milo Dickinson says. Everett Fahy, the American art historian and former director of the Frick who died in 2018, first proposed the attribution to Titian in 2009 when the painting emerged—filthy—from a French private collection. The oil on canvas, small at 44cm by 33cm, was sold for $69,850 at Sotheby's New York in 2024, catalogued as “attributed to Titian”. It has subsequently been cleaned, X-rayed and further studied. Now, Dickinson says, “various scholars—Peter Humfrey, Nicholas Penny and Ian Kennedy—have written on it and debated the dating of it because [if the attribution is correct] it would make it one of only three accepted self-portraits by Titian”, alongside those in the Gemäldegalerie in Berlin and that in the Prado, Madrid. The painting, which has been in a UK private collection since 2024, is now priced at £1.5m.

Another find is a Rubens head study which is now thought to depict a young Anthony van Dyck, based on dating, the sitter’s distinctive features and comparison with van Dyck’s self-portraits. The oil on panel, which measures 43cm by 35cm, was bought by the current owner at Sotheby’s Paris last November as part of the sale of the collection of the British property billionaire Manny Davidson. Catalogued as a Head Study of a Boy by Rubens, it sold for €744,000. With fitting circularity, Davidson had bought the painting as “attributed to Rubens” from Simon Dickinson in 2007, snapping it up after this attribution was questioned by the vetting committee at Tefaf Maastricht—Simon Dickinson spent several years trying to prove it was by Rubens.

The study had been thought to date to around 1614, when Van Dyck would have been only 14 or 15 years old, younger than the boy depicted here with his wispy stubble. But the Louvre, which holds the other half of this double head study, has recently argued that its sketch probably dates to 1616-17. “That got us thinking,” Dickinson says. “Once you've seen it cleaned, you realise the hair colour, the distinct shape of the face, the lips, the shape of the nose, they're totally concurrent with what Van Dyck looked like in his self-portraits.” He adds that Jonckheere and Van der Stighelen support the identity of the sitter as being van Dyck, alongside other scholars. The head study is for sale, price undisclosed.

Brunaille equestrian study by van Dyck (around 1629-32)

Yet another post-clean rediscovery is a study by van Dyck for the equestrian portrait of Albert de Ligne, Prince of Arenberg and Barbançon (around 1629-32) which hangs in Holkham Hall, Norfolk. It is one of several similar brunaille studies, one of which is the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. Another of these sketches, presumed to be the original, had been in the De Craecker collection in the 19th century but had since gone missing. “Then this one appeared in an American private collection and it was pretty clear, upon seeing it cleaned, that this was the original and the other versions were copies done in Van Dyck's studio by assistants.” Dickinson says.

He adds that “the sheer quality, the way that he uses highlights, the abbreviated forms” in this version are superior when compared to the others. The pentimenti (revisions) in this, typical of Van Dyck, have also become clearly visible over time, Dickinson says, particularly the adjusted position of the horses head and front legs. The American collector, who bought it in 2014, is now selling the sketch through Dickinson, with an asking price in the region of £500,000.
