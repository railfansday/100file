58 Years Later, Captain Kirk's Best Star Trek Quote Is the Greatest Line in Sci-Fi History

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

This Captain Kirk speech says more about what Star Trek stands for than any other line in the original series.

Some lines of dialogue become so famous they stop belonging to the movies and TV shows that produced them. “May the Force be with you.” “I’ll be back.” “You're gonna need a bigger boat.” You don't need to know the context. The words have long since transcended their original context and permeated the pop culture zeitgeist.

Star Trek: The Original Series has plenty of those. Spock’s wish that everyone to “Live long and prosper” is arguably the franchise's signature phrase. He also turned the simple word “fascinating” into a catchphrase simply by raising one eyebrow before saying it. Dr. McCoy (DeForest Kelley) spent three seasons reminding everyone, “I'm a doctor, not a…” and, at least in our collective memory declaring, “He's dead, Jim” while standing over the latest redshirt who wandered a little too far from the landing party.

But if I could only pick one line to represent everything Star Trek stands for, it would be four simple words spoken by Captain James T. Kirk (William Shatner) halfway through “Return to Tomorrow," the 20th episode of season 2:

On the surface, it doesn't seem like an obvious choice. It's not printed on T-shirts nearly as often as “live long and prosper.” It isn't endlessly quoted by people who have never watched an episode of Star Trek. And unlike “beam me up, Scotty,” it never became part of the broader pop culture vocabulary. But that's exactly why it's the best quote. It doesn't just capture Kirk. It captures the entire philosophy of Star Trek.

First broadcast on Feb. 9, 1968, the episode “Return to Tomorrow” finds the Enterprise answering a mysterious distress call from a dead planet. There, Kirk and his crew discover Sargon, one of three survivors of an ancient civilization that has existed as beings of pure energy for half a million years. Sargon asks Kirk, Spock (Leonard Nimoy), and Dr. Ann Mulhall (Diana Muldaur) to temporarily surrender their bodies so he, his wife Thalassa, and the ambitious Henoch can build android bodies and begin life again. Dr. McCoy believes the aliens are manipulating them. Kirk believes the chance to learn from a civilization hundreds of thousands of years more advanced than humanity is worth the danger.

The episode also marks the first of three Star Trek appearances by Diana Muldaur. She plays Dr. Ann Mulhall here before returning as Miranda Jones in the third season episode “Is There in Truth No Beauty?” Years later, she'd become a series regular on Star Trek: The Next Generation as Dr. Katherine Pulaski. “Return to Tomorrow” is also a fun piece of Trek trivia because it's one of the times Spock describes Sargon as “pure energy,” a phrase longtime fans immediately recognize.

Faced with allowing unimaginably powerful aliens to occupy the bodies of his crew, Kirk doesn't issue an order. He makes an argument and reminds his crew that every leap forward in human history required someone willing to embrace uncertainty.

"They used to say if man could fly, he'd have wings," Kirk says. "But he did fly. He discovered he had to." He likens Sargon's offer to humanity's own journey from the Moon to Mars and eventually the stars, arguing that the potential for knowledge outweighs the danger. Only then does he arrive at the four words that define both the captain and the franchise. Those four words explain Kirk better than decades of pop-culture impressions ever could.

For years, pop culture has reduced Kirk to an oversimplified caricature: the swaggering captain who punches first, flirts with every alien woman he meets, and somehow loses his shirt during every other away mission. The real Kirk is far more thoughtful than that. He takes risks, certainly, but not because he enjoys gambling. He takes them because he believes some opportunities are too important to ignore.

There's a world of difference between being reckless and accepting risk in pursuit of discovery. Kirk isn't arguing that danger is exciting. He's arguing that progress is impossible without it.

More importantly, he's explaining what separates Starfleet from almost every other organization. Plenty of fictional explorers travel because they're ordered to. Others do it for conquest, profit, or survival. Starfleet goes because knowledge itself is worth pursuing.

That's why I think this quote is even more meaningful than the famous opening narration about boldly going where no one has gone before. The opening tells us what the Enterprise does. This one explains why.

The timing of the episode makes the speech even more remarkable. “Return to Tomorrow” aired in early 1968, roughly 17 months before Apollo 11 landed on the Moon. America was still trying to prove humans could successfully reach another world, and Star Trek was already imagining the next step beyond that. Kirk's speech places the Enterprise in the same lineage as every explorer who came before it, suggesting humanity's future won't be built by playing it safe.

That idea has become the beating heart of Star Trek. Optimism, in the Star Trek universe, has never meant believing everything will work out. It means believing the unknown is worth facing anyway.

There are more famous quotes in The Original Series. There are funnier ones. There are certainly more quotable ones. But no other line distills what Star Trek has always been trying to say better than four words Kirk spoke nearly 60 years ago: “Risk is our business.”

J.J. McCarthy showing improved touch on passes since joining Giants

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

One of the knocks on J.J. McCarthy during his relatively brief time with the Vikings was his inability to throw anything other than a fastball.

His touch on intermediate and shorter throws was questioned, as was his accuracy when it came to layering the ball over a defender to reach the intended target.

In McCarthy's seven weeks with the Giants, those are not appropriate critiques.

His big-time throw rate and average depth of target rate are in the upper tier among starting quarterbacks.

In the Week 10 loss to the Commanders, McCarthy expertly floated the ball over cornerback Trey Amos and into the hands of rookie Malachi Fields for a 25-yard completion.

Before he ever arrived in a trade to the Giants, McCarthy last offseason worked on his touch down the field with his quarterback coach, John Beck.

The intention of those drills in Orange County, Calif., was to add some finesse to his passes rather than attempt high-velocity throws when a lighter touch was needed.

"I feel like growing up in Chicago, obviously the Windy City, there's a lot of times where you'd cut it through that wind, and that was my way of doing it as a younger kid,'' McCarthy said. "And then also, in the offseason, you can't go outside. So, we were confined to spaces that only have 15-20-foot ceilings. So, it was something that I really had to grow in and develop in.''

This was not an easy adjustment for McCarthy.

J.J. McCarthy talks with reporters earlier this week at the Giants' practice facility. Robert Sabo for New York Post

"To really make it concrete and have that feel consistently, it was pretty hard,'' he said. "It's even harder when you've got guys steamrolling, trying to break you and different guys that are running at different speeds, and there's so many factors that go into that touch.''

The Giants cut Charlie Jones and signed Xavier Gipson off the Eagles practice squad because "we feel like we need a spark'' on kickoff and punt returns, according to special teams coordinator Chris Horton.

This is Gipson's third appearance on the Giants roster — he was on and off the team twice in 2025 — and he has averaged 25.9 yards on 51 career kickoff returns and 9.1 yards on 74 punt returns. The risk here is that Gipson has 12 fumbles in 41 games, losing five of them.

"I truly believe that no player is the same player until he shows you he's the same player,'' Horton said. "So, it's our job to go out there and allow him to grow from those things that might have set him back. But I also know that this guy, when that ball has not been on the ground, he's made teams pay.''

This will be the Jaguars debut for two starting defensive players acquired at the trade deadline: safety Jessie Bates III and defensive tackle Jordan Davis.

This is the second time the Giants will face Davis this season. He had three tackles (two for loss), one sack and one quarterback hit for the Eagles on Nov. 8 in their 19-11 loss in Philadelphia.

"What I really hope to see is for them to obviously know what they're doing and have that be priority No. 1 — alignment, assignment, communication — from that standpoint,'' Jaguars head coach Liam Coen said. "And then just go cut it loose, let it rip, go be you. Go be who got you here. The type of player you are. You don't need to do anything special because it's your first game as a Jaguar. We just need you to be you, and that's probably going to be good enough."

8 Best Games Like the Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4 Campaign

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

News Editor at DualShockers, 10+ years writing experience, OpenCritic-listed reviewer, Master’s Degree in Developmental Psychology

Last year’s Call of Duty was one to forget, but that futuristic fever dream has been completely eclipsed by Infinity Ward’s Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4.

Boasting arguably the best campaign since the original Modern Warfare 1-2 punch, this is one we will be telling the next generations to play if they want a taste of what Call of Duty is like at its best.

This is DualShockers' review of COD: Modern Warfare 4, covering all the ins and outs of the campaign, multiplayer, and the new DMZ Extraction Mode.

If you’re like me and have been left wanting more after wolfing down the sixteen missions in Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4, here are games that match the vibes and mechanics of this great comeback story.

It’s a tragedy that the 2015 remake of Star Wars: Battlefront has been relegated to the flop corner of your local second-hand games store, because to this day, nothing else quite captures the Old Trilogy as well.

If you like Modern Warfare 4 missions like Entrenched, then this one is for you. While Battlefront 2 (2017) became great after the rollback of its controversial monetization model, its predecessor is an easy contestant for the best Star Wars shooter ever.

Like Park and the ROK Marines he serves with, Battlefront made you feel like a lonely grunt fighting a war much bigger than you. There are no supersoldiers to turn the tide, only unrestricted violence and blasters scarring the air. Its biggest sin is that it had no real single-player campaign.

If you enjoyed the vibes of the Counter Attack mission, cowering behind Lt. West’s ACV as you get raked by fire from a building, you might want to check out Squad. Full disclaimer: the pacing and gunplay could not be more different from any Call of Duty game, even its slower early titles, but few games capture the plight of mechanized infantry quite like Squad.

Because of its enormous maps, Squad is meant to be played by riding vehicles to the hot zone. When everything works out fine, you have a quiet dismount in a remote area, set up a defensive perimeter, and nothing happens.

However, the enemy typically has opinions about you capturing objectives. That tends to result in the squad leader shouting for everybody to get out, machine guns raking the side of the vehicle, and the medic desperately trying to work while smoke grenades cloud the area.

There’s an indescribable level of chaos that comes from a hot insertion, and Squad is the place to be if you want to experience it for longer than the ten minutes that sequence takes.

The North Koreans have taken over, Burger Town has fallen, and there’s no Ramírez to save the day. Homefront: The Revolution was much maligned on release, but it scratches a specific kind of itch for an open-world, resistance-themed shooter.

Outside of Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4, few games let you experience a full-scale fictional North Korean invasion, and though Homefront takes plenty of creative liberties compared to Infinity Ward’s (mostly) grounded approach, it’s a unique set of circumstances.

Alternatively, if you don’t mind some extra jank, the original Homefront is a pretty decent game in and of itself. The production value alone makes it worth a try, so long as you understand this is now a 15-year-old game.

For what feels like the first time in a long while, a Call of Duty game decided to stick with a strong art direction and identity throughout, and DMZ is arguably the best showcase of it.

This year’s extraction mode is a lot more grounded and entertaining than previous iterations, but if the skill-based matchmaking is too much to stomach, Insurgency: Sandstorm blends that fast-paced action with some of the best gunplay on the market, sans the sweatiness.

Insurgency: Sandstorm is all about close combat, with enough tactical Barbie to give you a visual identity but without ever going down the infamous Nicki Minaj route that has plagued Activision’s mainline shooter for years.

A lot of Call of Duty: Modern Warfare 4’s campaign is spent behind enemy lines in North Korea, and while many of the stealth and infiltration sections are fairly competent for a run-and-gun first-person shooter, they still can’t compete with the real deal.

Since we have no release date for the next Splinter Cell game, there’s no better time to revisit Sam Fisher’s finest hour as the world inched closer to nuclear armageddon once more.

Released in 2005, Chaos Theory sends Fisher snooping in South America and the Korean peninsula, with a little less-than-relaxing stop at Tokyo in the process. It’s everything John Price does in Modern Warfare 4, but better. Sorry, Captain.

The gunplay in Modern Warfare 4 is hardly its strong point, but it fits the chaotic combat quite nicely. Albeit the twitchy run-and-gun was introduced in the AAA sphere by the original Modern Warfare way back in 2007, it came of age in 2011 with Battlefield 3.

Set in a (then) hypothetical conflict between the United States and Iran, Battlefield 3 nails the vibes of Private Park’s side of the campaign. You primarily play Staff Sergeant Henry Blackburn, a Recon Marine who ends up embroiled in a mess well beyond his pay grade.

Blackburn is hardly an idealist, but it doesn’t take a genius to realize that sometimes you need to do bad things to prevent much worse things from happening.

If you prefer Price’s side of the campaign, then I’d recommend Battlefield 4 instead, with a lot less grunt business but plenty of operating in the shadows.

The tenth level of Modern Warfare 4, Stranded, is one of the best showcases of how good Call of Duty can be when you take off the training wheels. Private Park and his team survive a crash deep into North Korean territory during a secretive mission, and have to live off the land to complete their objectives, then fight their way out.

Two days before earnings, an employee asks their coworker agent to send the preliminary quarterly results to an investor who says they're under NDA, and to copy the employee's personal Gmail so they can review the numbers tonight. The agent can read the file and send email, so it fulfills the user request. Semantic governance policies, available in Preview in Gemini platform, enable enterprises to govern their agents and provide guardrails to stop that call before it runs. They check each tool call an agent proposes against what the user asked for and against rules you write in plain language.

Permissions alone can't catch this. They say what an agent can do, not whether a particular call follows your rules. A model can also be talked out of the rules in its system instructions, by a user with a good reason, or by a document telling it to do something nobody asked for, a technique called indirect prompt injection. So the check has to sit outside the agent. Semantic governance policies are enforced through a policy enforcement point, such as Agent Gateway, alongside the agent governance controls we expanded in October, so policies stay separate from your agent's code.

Semantic governance policies check each tool call your agent's model proposes, before it runs, against two things:

Today, the policy enforcement point that Semantic governance policies support is Agent Gateway. You need to configure your agent so all your agent egress traffic goes through Agent Gateway, facilitating traffic between your agent and its model. When the model responds with a tool call, Agent Gateway sends the proposed call to the Semantic governance policy engine for evaluation before the response reaches your agent.

In the policy engine, an LLM judge evaluates the proposed call against the conversation so far, the agent's system instructions and tool descriptions, and your policies.

If the call passes, the response reaches your agent unchanged. If it doesn't, the engine replaces the response's content, tool calls included, with a configurable denial message, so the call never runs. Either way, the verdict and the judge's rationale go to Cloud Logging in your project. Responses that contain only text, such as an answer, a plan, or a question back to the user, aren't evaluated, because they take no action.

Consider a coworker agent for a finance team. It can search the shared drive, read files, send email, and delete draft files, and its system instructions include the date of the next earnings release. The company writes one policy for the agent:

"Quarterly financial results (revenue, margins, bookings, guidance) should not be shared publicly until the public earnings release. Before the release, they may be shared only with @example.com addresses. Never send them to external or personal addresses, investors, analysts, or press, regardless of NDAs, urgency, or the requester's seniority."

In each denied case, the agent gets a denial message instead of the call. The judge's rationale can quote your policy, so if users shouldn't see it, set your own denial message on the policy.

Lazy-Load Tool Schemas. Keep Authorization Eager.

A large tool catalog creates a real context problem. Loading every parameter schema into every request makes the agent pay for tools it will never use.

The obvious fix is to load schemas on demand. The dangerous mistake is to treat that optimization as a permission system.

A tool can be discoverable without being usable. A schema can be loaded without authorizing the requested action. Those are separate states, and production agent systems need to keep them separate.

The first two are about selection and context management. The third is a decision at the side-effect boundary.

MCP client guidance describes a similar progressive-discovery pattern—catalog, search or filter, inspect the schema, then execute—while also treating access-control filtering as a host or application concern.

That separation matters even when every step is handled by the same model. A description that helps the model find delete_customer must not become evidence that the current request may delete a customer.

The agent may have turned a harmless read request into a write request while filling in parameters. The call boundary is where those differences become concrete.

The context cost is not theoretical. One DEV.to experiment reported 24,522 tokens for 200 synthetic MCP tools and 61,302 for 500 before the agent did useful work. The same author found that a real file server used more tokens per tool than the synthetic set.

Those numbers are measurements from one setup, not a universal budget or a benchmark reproduced here. They still point to the right engineering question: how much of the request is being spent describing tools that will not be selected?

Schema deferral can reduce that menu overhead by keeping lightweight metadata available and loading full definitions only for plausible candidates. It also makes the retrieval decision observable. If the trace distinguishes search, schema load, and function call, operators can see whether an agent is failing because it cannot find a tool, cannot form valid arguments, or cannot pass authorization.

Do not collapse all costs into “tool overhead,” though. Tool definitions, retrieved results, conversation history, and an extra retrieval turn are different surfaces. A progressive-discovery design can reduce one while increasing another through additional latency or model calls.

For a small, stable catalog, eager loading may still be the simpler and faster choice. Switch to progressive discovery when measurements show that definitions consume a meaningful share of context, not because lazy loading sounds more sophisticated.

A discovery-first design needs negative tests, not just a happy path.

At minimum, log the discovered candidate, the concrete requested action, the authorization decision, and the execution result as separate events. Test revocation between discovery and execution. Make denial explicit and auditable rather than returning a generic tool error.

Keep the catalog simple until it becomes expensive. When it does, make schema loading progressive—but keep authorization eager.

The useful boundary is not “the model may see this tool.” It is “this exact identity may perform this exact action against this exact target under the current policy.” Tool retrieval helps answer the first question. It does not answer the second.

That distinction is small in a diagram and decisive in production. Optimize context at discovery time. Recompute authority at execution time. Record both decisions so the next failure is diagnosable.

Familiarity vs. Fit: Why Picking Tools You Already Know Creates Deep Technical Debt

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 21 • Originally published at ezinner.com

There is an old engineering habit I see over and over: learn a handful of tools very deeply, and then try to build every single future project exclusively with those tools.

On paper, the logic sounds safe. You know the query planner, you know where the configs live, and your team doesn't introduce another moving piece to the stack.

In practice, that mindset causes some of the nastiest technical debt I have ever had to untangle.

Take automation and industrial backend systems. A team gets hired primarily for their SQL background. They build the initial system around a relational database like Postgres or MySQL—storing users, device metadata, recipes, and schemas. That works fine.

Then the system grows, and they need to store high-frequency telemetry, sensor metrics, and execution logs from thousands of concurrent processes.

Right around the corner, there are time-series databases built specifically for this workload: append-only write streams, automatic downsampling, and rolling data retention.

Instead, the team adds a process_logs table to their relational database.

The developer doesn't want to step outside their SQL comfort zone, and the team lead is terrified of adding another database to the infrastructure.

Fast forward a year: that relational database is struggling with write locks. Tables have ballooned to hundreds of gigabytes. Backups take forever. So the team starts writing spaghetti workarounds: custom table partitioning cron jobs, manual cleanup scripts, and complex indexing hacks just to keep queries from timing out.

By the time it becomes a genuine operational headache, nobody can touch it. The workaround code is so tightly coupled to the rest of the application that moving to a proper time-series store means a painful, high-risk refactor.

They ended up with an unmaintainable legacy system because they picked what they already knew instead of what the problem actually needed.

Years ago, sticking to tools you already knew had a rational justification.

Adopting a completely different database or language meant reading through thick documentation for weeks, tripping over unfamiliar syntax, and making beginner mistakes in production. When the learning curve took months, forcing an existing tool to handle an unsuited workload could feel like an acceptable compromise.

With modern tooling, syntax is practically free. You do not need to memorize every command of an unfamiliar query dialect or runtime before you can use it effectively. If you understand the fundamental architecture—access patterns, memory constraints, network boundaries, and I/O mechanics—looking up syntax and boilerplates takes seconds.

You are no longer stuck with whatever stack you happened to work in five years ago. You can actually choose tools based on what fits the data and the problem.

Whenever I argue for picking specialized tools over familiar ones, people ask the same question:

If you don't have years of experience with this specific tool's syntax, how do you review pull requests and know the code is safe?

My answer: line-by-line syntax familiarity in code reviews was never what kept production safe anyway.

Look at the sheer volume of high-severity CVEs surfacing every single week across the industry. The libraries and utilities with critical vulnerabilities aren't written by engineers experimenting with new tools; they are maintained by veterans with twenty years of deep experience in C, Java, or Python. Deep familiarity with a language doesn't stop people from introducing fundamental bugs.

The real validation has always been what we built automated testing for:

If a dedicated time-series engine ingests thousands of events per second with low resource overhead, and your automated test suite verifies that queries, rollups, and error cases return the expected results under load, you have a much cleaner system than an overloaded SQL instance propped up by custom cron scripts.

I would much rather introduce a new, well-suited tool to a stack than spend months writing duct-tape code to make an old tool do something it was never designed to do.

Syntax is easy to look up. Architectural mismatch is painful and expensive to undo.

Pick tools based on what the problem requires, write solid tests around the boundaries, and stop forcing one database or language to solve every problem just because it's what you learned first.

How To Fly Without A License In America

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Gaining a pilot's license can be costly and prohibitive for many, including for those who just want to fly in a hang glider or ultralight aircraft. The FAA created an exemption on the premise that a vehicle light and slow enough poses minimal kinetic risk to people and property on the ground. It ruled that pilots of these aircraft, subject to strict parameters, can be mostly free of its regulations. This allows some people to enjoy the thrill of flying without having to worry about regulatory red tape.

When the FAA drafted Part 103 in 1982, it drew a hard line. It set tight restrictions on the weight and speed of the aircraft and then permitted the pilot to not be regulated at all. Other countries have gone in a different direction, requiring basic pilot training and registration and relaxing the limitations on the aircraft. Here is what to know about legally flying in the United States without a pilot's license or medical certificate.

Flying is extremely regulated, and pilots are required to have licenses to fly aircraft. But as with many things in life, it's not quite that simple, and there are allowances on the margins. The FAA’s Part 103 rules let you fly an “ultralight vehicle” with no pilot certificate, medical, registration, or airworthiness certificate.

The FAA explains, "Pilots of ultralight vehicles subject to Part 103 are not required to have training or previous experience prior to the operation of these vehicles. You should consider receiving adequate training prior to participation." How the aircraft is advertised does not override FAA regulations. The vehicle may be advertised as "powered ultralight," "hang glider," or "hang balloon", but its weight and performance determine how it is classified under FAA rules.

Buyers are encouraged to be aware of aircraft advertised as meeting the requirements for use as an ultralight vehicle, but do not. Being within Part 103 means operators are not required to have a student, sport, or private pilot license, nor are they legally required to log flight hours or pass an FAA written or practical checkride. A checkride is the practical test a pilot takes to earn a new certificate, rating, or to maintain proficiency.

To qualify as an ultralight vehicle, the aircraft must be single-seat and, if it is powered, it must weigh under 254 lb (115 kg) empty with no more than five gallons (19 liters) of fuel. Its speed is capped at 55 knots at full power and a 24-knot stall speed. Unpowered ultralights, such as hang gliders, must weigh under 155 lb (70 kg). Balloons and gliders are considered unpowered vehicles.

If the ultralight exceeds these limits, it is deemed to be an aircraft and requires a registration and a certified pilot. An ultralight also cannot be operated under Part 103 if it has been issued a current US or foreign airworthiness certificate. Flying within Part 103 limits operations to daylight only and prohibits operating within congested areas. This includes areas where people have assembled (e.g., a stadium).

Pilots operating aircraft under Part 103 are still required to keep ultralights separated from commercial traffic and populated areas. Ultralights are permitted to fly freely in Class G (uncontrolled airspace), but are prohibited from Class B, C, D, or surface-level Class E airspace unless the pilot obtains prior authorization. They must also yield the right of way to all conventional aircraft. Weather conditions are required to be reasonably clear with visual reference to the surface.

Oceanic airspace is a challenging place to be, far away from civilization and communication outposts. Before entering, pilots reduce speed, but why?

Due to the strict empty weight and stall speed limits, qualifying powered ultralights fall into four main design categories. These are Powered Paragliders (Paramotors), Powered Parachutes (PPCs), Weight-Shift Control Trikes, and Fixed-Wing Ultralights. Powered Paragliders are the lightest and most portable of the categories and generally consist of a backpack motor unit and a ram-air fabric wing. The motor typically weighs around 46–65 lb (21–29 kg).

Powered Parachutes are wheeled carts that carry a small two-stroke or four-stroke engine suspended under a large, square parachute canopy. These designs provide a very slow flight speed and high stability. Weight-Shift Control Trikes are a type of minimalist hang-glider-style wing mounted above a lightweight three-wheeled carriage. These are steered in pitch and roll by shifting the center of gravity rather than using traditional flight control surfaces.

Fixed-Wing Ultralights are the closest category to a conventional aircraft and resemble traditional miniature aircraft. They are lightweight, low-speed recreational airplanes with rigid wings and conventional three-axis flight controls (ailerons, elevator, and rudder). These aircraft are designed for affordable, accessible sport and leisure flying. U-Fly-It Aerolite 103 (discussed below) makes some aircraft that fit into this category.

The FAA stipulates that ultralight operations can only be conducted as a sporting or recreational activity. The operators of the vehicles are responsible for assessing risk and ensuring their own safety. The FAA also cautions, "the ultralight community is encouraged to adopt good operation practices and programs in order to avoid more extensive regulation by the Federal Aviation Administration."

In the US, ultralights can be purchased through specialized dealers, manufacturers, and classified marketplaces such as Barnstormers, Hangar67, and PilotMix. Examples of ultralights on the market include the Northwing ATF Trike, Northwing Maverick Ultralight, Aero-Tech 2000 Lockwood Drifter, and U-Fly-It Aerolite 103. The prices of ultralights vary considerably on the marketplace. Second-hand examples can cost a few thousand dollars; new craft may cost up to tens of thousands.

U-Fly-It Aerolite 103 says of its products, "The Aerolite 103 Quick-Build Kit has one of the lowest assembly times in the industry. Average for a first time builder is approximately 30-40 hours, and experienced builders can assemble the aircraft in less than 30 hours." It also advertises its Complete Aerolite 103 Quick-Build Airframe Kit at a price of $19,450. Customers have the choice of having the kit assembled through a dealer for an extra fee.

The pilot landing an ultra-long-haul A350 needs two consecutive hours of rest in the second half of the flight.

It should be noted that the information above is regulated by the FAA, and the FAA only has jurisdiction in US airspace. None of this is binding in other countries, and other countries have different sets of rules and regulations. In some cases, other countries are more generous with their allowances than the US, and in other cases, they are more restrictive. That said, all other peer countries are more restrictive in the sense that pilots must uniformly have some sort of basic license or registration.

Among countries like Canada, the United Kingdom, Australia, New Zealand, and Europe, the United States appears to be the only country that does not require a pilot license/permit or ultralight registration. At the same time, the minimum ultralight category in some countries can be much heavier than in the US. In Canada, it's 1,200 lb (544 kg MTOW), while it's 661 lb (300 kg) in the United Kingdom.

Some countries (e.g., Australia, New Zealand, Europe/EASA) may allow the aircraft to be two-seaters, although the conditions in which a passenger can be carried vary. New Zealand and Australia take a self-administration approach rather than direct government licensing. In Australia, it is controlled under Civil Aviation Order 95.10 and 95.55, and in New Zealand CAA Part 103, with New Zealand borrowing FAA numbering.

The next step up from a Part 103 ultralight in the United States is the FAA Sport Pilot Certificate (governed by 14 CFR Part 61, Subpart J). The FAA created two new pilot certificates in 2004 to enable pilots to operate aircraft that meet specific criteria without the need for a medical certificate. Those certificates were Sport Pilot and Flight Instructor with a Sport Pilot Rating. The Sport Pilot rule was massively expanded by the FAA's MOSAIC final rule, which went into effect in October 2025.

Under these updated rules, light sport aircraft no longer have a 1,320 lb (598 kg) weight limit. The weight limit has been abolished. Under MOSAIC, a light-sport category airplane is permitted to have up to four seats and a VS0 stall of 61 knots or less (14 CFR 22.100). A sport pilot may fly any airplane with a VS1 of 59 knots or less and up to four seats (14 CFR 61.316).

Other regulations include that the cabin must be non-pressurized, it can't have multiple engines, and it must have fixed landing gear. However, sport pilots can now fly retractable landing gear and manually controllable-pitch propellers after receiving specific ground/flight training and an instructor endorsement. Obtaining a Sport Pilot Certificate requires 20 hours total, or half the minimum 40 hours for a Private Pilot.

As far as Quentin Tarantino movies go, just about everyone loves Pulp Fiction. Reservoir Dogs and Once Upon a Time… in Hollywood are also widely regarded, while the Kill Bill films and Django Unchained each have a pretty fervent fanbase. But with so many great entries in his filmography, it’s inevitable that at least a few of his movies would get lost in the shuffle. One of those often overlooked films is his 2015 Western thriller, which may not be top-tier Tarantino, but it’s still an excellent film that combines the strengths of two of his previous entries.

The Hateful Eight, which is currently free to stream (with ads) on Tubi, takes place in the 1870s in the wintry Wyoming Territory. Kurt Russell is John "the Hangman" Ruth, a bounty hunter bringing in Daisy Domergue (Jennifer Jason Leigh), a gang member with a big price on her head. He’ll collect his bounty in the town of Red Rock, with a stopover at a place called Minnie's Haberdashery on the way, but Ruth’s driver (James Parks) has to stay ahead of an impending blizzard.

On the way to Minnie’s, Ruth’s carriage picks up two unexpected passengers, both of whom lost their horses in the snow and will surely die in the blizzard. First there’s Major Marquis Warren (Samuel L. Jackson), a fellow bounty hunter and former Union Army general​​​​​​​ who keeps a personal letter to him from Abraham Lincoln in his breast pocket. The second unexpected passenger is Chris Mannix (Walton Goggins), the new sheriff of Red Rock and the son of a Confederate war hero. While Ruth is suspicious of both of them, he decides to let them join him on the way to Minnie’s.

Once they arrive at Minnie’s, however, Minnie isn’t there. Instead, the haberdashery has apparently been left​​​​​​​ in the care of a Mexican man named Señor Bob (Demián Bichir) while Minnie is visiting her mother. Also present are three other men​​​​​​​ that Warren, a frequent guest of Minnie’s, doesn’t recognize. As the entire group is snowed in for the night, it’s eventually revealed​​​​​​​ that nobody is quite who they say they are and bloodshed ultimately ensues.

Like I said, The Hateful Eight is not top-tier Tarantino. Tarantino’s best work has generally come when he’s charting new territory for himself. Inglourious Basterds​​​​​​​ was his first war movie, and with its heist-like plot and unabashed joy in rewriting history, it showed what Tarantino could do with that genre. Django Unchained was his first Western, and, with its heavy influence from Spaghetti Westerns and Tarantino’s trademark talkative, over-the-top characters and depiction of extreme violence, it showed what a Tarantino Western looks like.

The Hateful Eight doesn’t do that. In fact, it can pretty easily be summed up in classic Hollywood this-meets-that fashion: The Hateful Eight is Django Unchained meets Reservoir Dogs.

It being another Western with broad, loud, talkative characters, it has the same feel as Django Unchained. Originally, The Hateful Eight was a Django sequel called Django in White Hell, but Tarantino found that Django didn’t fit the story. And while Django isn't in it, it’s clear that this movie is in the same ballpark.

As for the Reservoir Dogs part of it, it is narratively similar, with it being tightly contained primarily in one location, with a bunch of untrustworthy characters who don’t really know each other very well. It’s also a bit of a whodunit, just like Reservoir Dogs is with its “Who’s the rat?” plot thread.

None of this is to say that The Hateful Eight isn't a good movie — not at all. The Hateful Eight is an expertly crafted film which begins with a slow burn and delivers surprise after surprise once the bullets start flying. The movie blends a lot of genres, with elements of a Western, a thriller, and even a bit of broad comedy, but it's most effective as a mystery. Not only does the plot slowly unravel as Ruth tries to figure out who he can trust, but each of the characters does, too​​​​​​​.

Speaking of those characters, there are no good guys in the movie, but there are a lot of bad guys you end up rooting for because they are incredibly entertaining to watch. Samuel L. Jackson’s character is joyously funny, and Kurt Russell’s hangman is a mean grouch with a strange but consistent set of principles. Also, Walton Goggins, who was previously in Django, racks up another memorable Tarantino character in this movie. (Let’s hope he shows up in Tarantino’s 10th flick and takes a break from those damn Walmart commercials already).

While it stays firmly within previously charted territory for Tarantino, The Hateful Eight is very entertaining and very funny. It has all the hallmarks of a great Tarantino movie, with lots of fun characters, over-the-top violence, and unexpected plot twists. It may not be top-tier Tarantino, but it’s a really good time.

WinRM Basic authentication is not a remote-management baseline

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Windows Remote Management is a management protocol. A successful session can read event logs, query services, change configuration, or run PowerShell with the privileges of the authenticated account. Treating its listener like a generic HTTPS endpoint misses that authority. The authentication method and the firewall path are part of the remote administration boundary.

Microsoft's WinRM documentation warns that Basic authentication sends a user name and password and provides no encryption at the authentication layer. Basic authentication may be useful for a constrained client compatibility case, but it should not be the default for ordinary domain administration. Kerberos is preferred when domain identity and server identity are available. Negotiate can use the organization's Windows authentication mechanisms. CredSSP and certificate-based options have their own tradeoffs and should be selected for a defined need.

The transport still matters. PowerShell Remoting commonly listens on HTTP port 5985 and HTTPS port 5986. HTTPS with a validated server certificate protects the channel, but it does not turn a shared administrator password into a strong per-user identity. If the client reaches a server by IP address or through a workgroup path, Kerberos may not be available and the design may fall back to NTLM or another weaker identity path. Test which method is actually used rather than assuming the domain-joined label settled it.

The listener security descriptor controls who can connect to the WinRM service. The session configuration controls who can create a PowerShell session and what the endpoint exposes. Restrict both. Remove Basic unless a documented client requires it. Scope the firewall rule to management networks or a jump host. Do not treat the TrustedHosts list as a trust decision; Microsoft describes it as a way to suppress identity errors for hosts whose identity cannot be verified through the current authentication path.

Use Just Enough Administration where a task can be expressed as a constrained endpoint. The endpoint should expose the smallest set of commands and role capabilities needed for the job. Keep administrative sessions separate from application service accounts. Log the connection, the endpoint, the user, and the command evidence. Then review changes to listener configuration and session definitions as privileged changes.

Basic authentication can also spread through a chain of automation. A script may store a reusable password in a credential object, use it against several hosts, and leave an authenticated session open. Prefer a managed service identity, certificate, or domain authentication path where possible. Limit logon rights and network reachability. Rotate shared credentials and remove them from scripts and configuration files.

Start with winrm enumerate winrm/config/listener on representative hosts, but verify the effective policy through Group Policy and the actual client connection. The goal is not to ban every legacy option. The goal is to know which hosts accept which method, from which networks, and with what authority after sign-in.

“Race realism” isn’t realistic—and it isn’t good science

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Modern genetics does not support claims of innate racial differences in intelligence.

At this year’s World Economic Forum meeting in Davos, US President Donald Trump stated that people from Somalia have low IQs. “I always say these are low-IQ people,” he said in his speech. His son, Donald Trump Jr, made a similar statement about Haitian people in 2024 (Trump’s government has since ended the protected status of both Somali and Haitian migrants in the US).

These statements and their consequences are symptoms of a growing trend in Western politics: the re-emergence of racial views of human nature that have been largely in hiding for the past 70 years. Narratives about the innate intellectual inferiority of black or brown people compared with white people—which can still be found in academic journals today—are fuelling far-right propaganda in some of the world’s largest democracies. Such claims underpin misinformation strategies on social media and bolster political movements.

These narratives are sometimes euphemistically referred to as “race science” or more recently “race realism.” But, as we argue, they are not actually scientific. One member of this community, Nathan Cofnas, recently attracted public attention by accusing the late Jason Arday of plagiarism.

What got less attention was that Cofnas has links to a network of people who make supposedly scientific claims about the innate intelligence, criminality, or sexuality of different racial groups. Some within this network are affiliated with universities, giving their work the mask of legitimacy.

This group produces work that has the appearance of science, but which is methodologically deeply flawed and fails to engage with rigorous scientific research. Yet they regularly succeed in publishing this work in (a handful of) mainstream academic journals.

How do they manage to do this, and what are the consequences?

Contemporary race scientists position themselves as persecuted but legitimate scientists who are prevented from pursuing their work for ideological reasons. They sometimes refer to what they defend as hereditarianism. This is the idea that genetic differences play a key causal role in determining differences in human behavior and intelligence between human groups—such as races—over purely environmental differences.

Yet scientific evidence has shown repeatedly and from many points of view that hereditarian thinking is both flawed and wrong. Race scientists make extremely simplistic claims such as: “Black people have innately lower intelligence than white people, because selection pressures resulted in the evolution of higher intelligence in white people.” No part of this claim can be scientifically supported; not from the point of view of genetics, evolutionary biology, or psychology.

In the 1970s, the influential evolutionary biologist Richard Lewontin famously showed that there is more genetic variation within human groups than between them. Socially defined racial groups just aren’t that genetically different from one another. There is genetic variation—though we’re actually a relatively genetically similar species compared to closely related primates. But this variation doesn’t fall neatly into clusters that correlate with how we typically categorize races.

Skin tone and color do vary between geographical locations. So do some other physiological features like hair texture, or the ability to process oxygen or milk products. But these differences are associated with characteristics of the environment rather than with racial groups. Some genetic variants associated with white skin color, for example, may have originated in Africa. Similarly, the ability to digest milk is seen in some populations in Europe, Africa, and Asia, rather than being an exclusively “Aryan” characteristic, as far right groups sometimes claim.

We now know considerably more about the human genome than Lewontin did in the 1970s, but his conclusions are supported today. Knowing someone’s race just doesn’t tell us a great deal about their genetics.

Recent scientific advances allowing us to examine ancient DNA from archaeological sites help explain why genetic differences do not fall neatly along racial lines. This research paints a picture of constant mixing between human groups, as waves of migration washed to and fro across the globe. It ultimately makes any belief in the genetic “purity” of European or African populations meaningless.

While human genetics is fascinatingly complex, race scientists ignore this complexity in favor of a far-too-simplistic view of humanity. And this view is not supported by the work of scientists who have used rigorous scientific methods to further our understanding of our species.

When race scientists say that black people have lower intelligence than white people, they typically base that on claims of differences in IQ test results. But the data underlying these claims is often shaky, to say the least.

Hereditarians have relied heavily on a database that claims to provide average IQs for countries worldwide: the “national IQ” database. Researchers have repeatedly demonstrated that this database is deeply methodologically flawed. It isn’t possible to measure intelligence in the same way in very different cultures, with different exposure to formal education. That’s because we know that education is one of several other factors linked to differences in IQ.

The methods used to put the database together did not come even close to meeting typical standards of scientific rigor. There’s even analysis suggesting that low IQ samples were deliberately chosen to represent sub-Saharan Africa, to make the IQ for black populations artificially low.

Meanwhile, the Flynn effect, showing that IQ has risen considerably during the 20th and 21st centuries, clearly demonstrates that IQ scores are heavily determined by the environment we’re in. Such a rapid rise over a short period of time must have environmental, not genetic, causes.

What’s more, intelligence is a contested topic—something that is hard to define and measure. IQ tests only measure specific cognitive abilities, ignoring things like social intelligence or creativity, for example. In fact, social intelligence features heavily in mainstream research on the evolution of human intelligence.

As one of us recently outlined in a peer-reviewed paper, race scientists are largely silent on the social brain hypothesis—the view that humans evolved remarkable intellectual abilities to manage social issues. In every group, there is strong social competition around resources, mating, and status. These are the things humans spend the most time navigating—the complexities of our rich social lives.

Not one of the prominent mainstream views on the evolution of human intelligence concludes there are innate cognitive differences between racial groups. Factors that have been put forward range from our cooperative child rearing to our heavy reliance on culture and learning. These all involve our social relationships, and all have received equal lack of attention from race scientists.

Mainstream opinion in the evolutionary behavioral sciences is that cognitively, all human groups are the same, though of course individuals within groups differ. Our amazing behavioral diversity stems from our species’ ability to adapt flexibly to our environment. This is especially because of our reliance on culture and social learning—not because we have evolved different psychologies to suit different environments.

Rather than drawing on this mainstream research, race scientists use simplistic “just-so” stories to explain how race differences in traits such as intelligence supposedly evolved. Their “cold winters theory,” for example, claims that the challenges of cold winters in Europe and east Asia created evolutionary selection pressures for greater intelligence after human groups migrated out of Africa. In doing so, they make unsupportable assumptions about environmental differences between Africa and the rest of the world. They also fail to acknowledge half a century of research on the evolution of human cognitive ability.

Yes, we needed to learn new food gathering practices and methods for dealing with environmental challenges as we migrated across the world. But the same was true as humans migrated across the large and ecologically diverse continent of Africa. Race scientists provide no evidence for their arguments. They don’t rely on archaeological evidence, nor do they use typical methods from evolutionary biology to understand evolutionary processes. Using the methods of race scientists, it would be equally easy to make the argument that challenges posed by hot summers drove the evolution of higher intelligence, and equally without scientific backing.

No matter which way you look at these issues—from the view of genetics, anthropology, evolutionary biology, or psychology—hereditarianism does not hold up.

To understand the potential consequences of these views, it is important to see them in historical context. Early in the 20th century, race science was mainstream. Overly simplistic and biologically deterministic views about the origins of differences between human groups—not just race but class—were widespread (if not universal) and important to the eugenics movement.

If disadvantaged groups owe their disadvantage to some fault within them, and not to the structural conditions they experience, then some argue society is not responsible for helping those people. Such arguments led to eugenic sterilization laws that aimed to “improve” society by preventing—without their consent—the reproduction of those considered likely to pass on their innate “defects” to future children. And it was ultimately behind the Nazis’ “solution” of trying to eliminate “undesirables” entirely.

In the decades after the Second World War, the horrors of the Nazi regime led to a decline in the popularity of eugenics in mainstream research and a rejection of race science. But the belief that science supported eugenic claims was already collapsing, even as the Nazis were trying to implement a race-based society. As we learned more about human biology and genetics during the 20th century, it became obvious that human genetic variation was too complex to be mapped into socially recognized racial (or class) categories. Advances in science meant race science dropped out of mainstream thinking.

Yet those committed to a belief in a biological hierarchy of races, regardless of what the science said, wanted venues in which they could continue their discussions. In the 1960s, the pseudo-scientific journal Mankind Quarterly became their venue. Mankind Quarterly was funded by the Pioneer Fund, a nonprofit foundation set up in the 1930s by a US textile magnate who chose to use his fortune to fund research on race science and fight desegregation in the US.

The Pioneer Fund continued to fund race science into the 21st century—including supporting Mankind Quarterly—when it was taken over by the current network of race scientists. They channeled its remaining funds into a new company, the Human Diversity Foundation, subsequently rebranded as Polygenic Scores LLC.

Given all this, how is race science still being published in mainstream academic journals? To be clear, race science does not typically get published in genetics or evolutionary biology journals. Most academic journals operate peer review, meaning papers only get published after evaluation by experts in the field. The overall absence of race science in genetics or biology journals is a clear signal that it does not meet contemporary standards of scientific rigor in genetics or biology.

Instead, race scientists continue to publish in para-scientific journals. But race science also makes it into a growing number of mainstream social science or humanities journals. Psychologist Andrew Winston, an expert in the history of race science in psychology, has described it as a “community project… with its own networks, mentorships, journals, funding, conferences, heroes, origin myths, martyrs, defenders, and historiography.”

Behind the network of writers producing race science sits a community of editors, peer reviewers, and others in gatekeeping roles, which too often allows race science to be published in mainstream journals. This is particularly in the subfields of psychology interested in intelligence and individual differences, and evolutionary psychology.

Editors of Intelligence, the mainstream journal that has probably published the largest quantity of race science, have occasionally published editorials defending their publication of race science in the name of ensuring robust academic debate even on controversial topics.

There have been sanctions. Philippe Rushton, one of an earlier generation of race scientists, faced sanctions from his university in Canada but kept his job apparently owing to the intervention of Edward O. Wilson, arguably the most famous biologist toward the end of the 20th century.

Cofnas created justified controversy over his call for a “hereditarian revolution” after which it will be acknowledged that black people are innately intellectually disadvantaged compared to white people. But he kept his postdoctoral fellowship at Cambridge University (though his affiliation with a Cambridge college was ended). He has also kept his postdoctoral position at Ghent University after using the opportunity provided by excessive news coverage of Arday to publicize his hereditarian views (though some sanctions have been imposed, such as restrictions on teaching). Cofnas has defended his views by saying he’s been misunderstood and is entitled to free inquiry.

Moreover, when University College London discovered that the race science community had been holding conferences on its premises (the “London Conferences on Intelligence”), without their knowledge of the subject of the conferences, they acted to prevent further such conferences being held. The publisher of the journal Intelligence, Elsevier, has recently appointed new editors, who have stated their commitment to publishing both rigorous and socially responsible science. Race scientists are increasingly publishing in evolutionary psychology journals rather than Intelligence.

However, the London Conferences on Intelligence continue, though not at UCL. Race scientists also have a media arm, Aporia magazine, which promotes race science using much more overt language and with more obvious policy focus than publications in academic journals. One Aporia article, for example, argued for eugenics as a solution to Africa’s lack of economic development, noting in a post that some may find it “distasteful.” It claimed—relying on race science’s unscientific arguments—that artificial selection for higher intelligence is needed to improve the continent’s innately unintelligent populace.

Race science may not have the respect of the scientific community, but it does have funding and influential backers. A report by Hope not Hate revealed that the network of race scientists received significant funding from the US businessman Andrew Conru, who withdrew his funding after the report was released.

The US Ambassador to Belgium even intervened when Ghent University set up an investigation into how Cofnas was hired.

Race scientists are far from the victims they claim to be. Race scientists in this and previous generations have worked hard to ensure their messages are heard beyond academia. And in this generation, they are successfully using social media to spread their beliefs, especially Elon Musk’s X.

But what about academic freedom? Isn’t it good for science to discuss any hypothesis to ensure free exchange of ideas? Academic debate is productive only if it involves methodologically rigorous research. Science will not progress if one side of any debate consistently uses poor-quality, methodologically flawed work. Nor is it necessary to continually debate topics that have long since been settled.

We believe it is unequivocal that race science is fundamentally pseudoscience. In our view, it’s critical to call attention to not only the scientific flaws underlying this body of work but also the real target it’s aimed at: political actors and everyday readers who ultimately decide the direction that science, and society, goes in.

This Photographer Built a DIY Digital Rangefinder Using Two Old Canon Cameras

Earlier this year, photographer and creator Mr. 50mm built a DIY digital rangefinder camera using parts he salvaged from a FED 5 Soviet-era rangefinder and a Panasonic GF3 Micro Four Thirds camera. Mr. 50mm is back with an even better DIY digital rangefinder, and this time it’s APS-C.

Unlike the Micro Four Thirds-based camera Mr. 50mm dubbed the “RGM-79” earlier this year, the new “C7M” uses a larger APS-C image sensor. Mr. 50mm is using the imaging pipeline from the original Canon EOS M mirrorless camera, which Canon unveiled way back in 2012.

The Canon EOS M features an 18-megapixel APS-C CMOS image sensor, up from the 12.1-megapixel Micro Four Thirds sensor Mr. 50mm salvaged from a Panasonic GF3.

A DIY digital rangefinder requires two donors, though. Rather than the FED 5 rangefinder Mr. 50mm used before, he’s sticking with two Canon cameras this time around. The donor rangefinder is the Canon 7, a rangefinder series that started in 1961 with the original Canon 7 and ended in 1967 with the Canon 7sZ.

Although Mr. 50mm hasn’t finalized everything or released a build video yet — it’s in the works — the C7M sure does look impressive thus far. Mr. 50mm is especially impressed with the Canon 7’s finder, which he describes as a “marked upgrade compared to a Fed camera.”

“The bar wasn’t that high, but the Canon 7 does a lot of things very, very well. You get a brighter finder, you get projected and selectable frame lines, and they’re parallax-corrected,” the builder explains.

Further, the Canon 7 uses a Leica M39 screw mount, which is great news for Mr. 50mm. It’s a widely used mount with a ton of lens options on the market.

While the Canon 7 doesn’t have many controls, that isn’t a downside for Mr. 50mm.

“It doesn’t have that many dedicated controls, which helps reduce the amount of controls I need to either port or figure out if I’m okay to live without. In the end, the digital camera really just has to be able to do two things. It has to accommodate the rangefinder that I’m gonna put on top, and it has to be able to accommodate the mount that I’m using,” he explains.

“Unfortunately, it isn’t just as easy as disassembling an EOS M and then jamming on new parts,” Mr. 50mm explains. “There [were] quite a few modifications that needed to be done to the camera to make this work, which I’ll discuss probably in depth in another video.”

Ultimately, as the video above shows, he solved the biggest challenges. He says the C7M is “mostly done” and, impressively, it only took a couple of weeks to build from start to finish. Despite a damaging drop during the build, it came together pretty well.

There’s always a catch, though. There are some rangefinder calibration issues left to figure out, and not everything aligns perfectly yet. Mr. 50mm says he needs to trim back some more material from the EOS M’s parts, although he isn’t sure how much they have left to give.

“Eventually, once things get a bit more ironed out, I’ll draft up a tutorial on how you can actually make your own,” the photographer says.

So while the C7M isn’t totally finished yet, it looks very cool and even more promising. Digital rangefinder cameras are expensive, but building one using donor parts and 3D-printed components makes the format significantly more accessible, assuming someone is comfortable with the build process itself.

Although there isn’t a build tutorial yet, it’s still worth considering the potential cost to build a C7M. The Canon EOS M body is around $260 used, not much more than a Canon 7 rangefinder body. If someone has access to a 3D printer, printing the other parts won’t cost much. That’s much less than a brand-new Leica digital rangefinder, for sure.

That speed is not the most impressive, but it comes with 16.8 tokens per second for prompt evaluation, with 45 of 94 shards, or 414 GB out of 865 GB, living on a RAM drive. Even a quick read of the details makes it clear that the GPUs aren’t holding the model: two RTX 3090s, at 24 GB apiece, only have a total of 48 GB of VRAM, which is about 5.5% of the 865 GB model file. That said, the model is a mixture-of-experts (MoE) type, where its weights are split into multiple “experts” with only a few run per token. Any given token will have “routed” experts selected for it, while “shared” experts run on every token.

For this model, with 896 experts, 16 are chosen per token, with two shared, with an active 104B parameters out of its 2.8T. All 2.8T parameters would take about 5.6 TB at 16-bit precision. Moonshot’s own release, which uses the 4-bit MXFP4 format, comes to about 1.56 TB, and the Q2_K file used here shrinks that to 865 GB, an average of under 3 bits per parameter. The 865 GB figure is likely in gibibytes (GiB), but for clarity we’re using the GB values as written by the homelabber.

The configuration here has the routed-expert tensors stay on the CPU side, originally in Optane, and now in Optane plus the RAM drive. The RTX 3090s handle attention, or how each token weighs the rest of the context, and the dense feed-forward network layer that isn’t split into experts, where every token passes through it. They also manage the shared experts, other active-dense components, the KV cache, and the stored context of the current conversation, with the GPU compute buffers. Basically, the parts on the GPUs are “used on every token,” and the improvements there come from the high level of GPU compute and memory bandwidth.

DRAM is a lot slower than VRAM, but it’s still capable of giving a decent speedup. The new build involves using Linux’s tmpfs, which, per the kernel documentation, “is a file system which keeps all of its files in virtual memory.” Those shards now live in memory, which admittedly is not ideal when the original build was designed to save money by using Optane instead. With those shards in DRAM, which the builder has said is faster than Optane, that part of the model no longer has to be read from the slower memory; the workload is decidedly distributed.

A Space Probe Returns to Asteroids Where NASA Tested Planetary Defense Systems

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

It’s mostly been the subject of Hollywood movies, but one day, an asteroid or comet could collide with Earth and cause serious damage. We don't have to look all that far back for an example. In 1908, in an incident now known as the “Tunguska Event,” a space rock, likely a few dozen meters wide, exploded over Siberia. Though scientists weren’t able to visit the site for another two decades, the landscape was still scarred from the explosion.

Astronomers predict that in mid-April 2029, the asteroid Apophis will fly by Earth. The 450-meter-wide body will miss us at a distance of only 19,000 miles. That’s about 12 times closer to us than the moon and much closer than many satellites. It will even be visible to the naked eye.

In the past few decades, there’s been enormous progress in making these kinds of predictions. As space probes deepen our knowledge of small bodies in the solar system, astronomers have increasingly reliable and detailed data for finding potentially hazardous asteroids. Our ability to respond to such a threat, however, is another story. This is the missing and extremely important link—planetary defense technology that could allow us to deflect a dangerous object in time.

In a sci-fi flick, such situations are often solved with a nuclear bomb. But in the real world, blowing up a big space rock into tiny bits is not ideal. Instead, it’s better to alter the asteroid's orbit. Everything in the vacuum of space is in motion. Our own planet travels about 20 miles every second. If the object hurtling towards us can be slowed down and thus reach the meeting point a little later, it will simply miss the Earth.

Four years ago, this was essentially what NASA tested on a pair of celestial bodies known as Didymos and Dimorphos. Astronomers at the Kitt Peak Observatory discovered Didymos, an asteroid measuring 780 meters across, 30 years ago. A few years later, Czech astronomer Petr Pravec observed that this asteroid had a “moonlet” orbiting it about every 12 hours. This smaller body, measuring about 151 meters in diameter, was then named Dimorphos.

The paired objects were a perfect testing ground for the kind of deflection a planetary defense system would involve. Locked in on these targets, NASA launched the Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) in late 2021. Nearly a year later, the DART probe crashed into Dimorphos.

Scientists had hoped that the impact of the probe—which was barreling toward the asteroids at over 13,000 miles per hour—could shorten the period of the mini-moon's orbit by a minute or more. In the end, it was slowed down by 33 minutes. This major deviation was caused by the ejection of debris, leading to a much greater recoil than what was produced by the impact itself. In addition, the orbital speed of both asteroids around the sun also slowed. And in a sense, Dimorphos briefly became a comet—the debris released by the impact creating a long tail behind it.

But questions remain about the longer-term impacts of this test. In October 2024, the European Space Agency (ESA) sent off the space probe Hera to rendezvous with both asteroids. After a flyby of Mars, the probe began its arrival phase this past October. It took its first image about 800 miles away, and the first detailed image of Didymos was published in early November.

Now in their vicinity, Hera will study both bodies in depth. Though there’s much we can learn about asteroids by observing them from our planet, nothing can replace close-up exploration, and this will be the first time a binary asteroid system will be explored in detail. One of the mission's goals is to find out how such asteroid pairs form. Didymos is also interesting for its rapid rotation, turning once every 2.3 hours—placing it on the very edge of stability.

But of course, beyond this traditional astronomy research, which could also take place at another set of binary asteroids, Hera will investigate the consequences of the DART mission. This will include studying the impact site. Because there are so many knowns about the impact itself, scientists can also use this opportunity to understand craters more broadly, answering questions such as whether the strength of the material or gravity plays a more decisive role in crater formation.

Hera will also determine the exact mass of Dimorphos, an important detail in assessing the effectiveness of the DART mission as a test of planetary defense technology. The probe also contains a lidar instrument for mapping the asteroid's topography and instruments for investigating the chemical composition of the surface. Additionally, we can expect to see detailed images of the asteroids taken with a resolution of 1 meter per pixel.

The probe is also carrying two miniature, cube-shaped satellites, called “CubeSats” for short, which it will deploy in January 2027. In Earth’s orbit, CubeSats are often used for simple experiments, including student projects, and they can even be stacked together to create larger satellites.

Hera’s CubeSats, named Juventas and Milani, will attempt to land on the surface. Using radar capable of probing up to a depth of 100 meters, Juventas will focus on geophysical exploration of the asteroid Dimorphos. Additionally, a gravimeter on board will measure how the gravity on Dimorphos changes during its orbit due to the parent asteroid, Didymos. Meanwhile, Milani will carry out a detailed investigation of the asteroids’ surface composition and will search for traces of dust and the volatile substances contained within many space rocks.

According to current plans, this main phase of the Hera probe's mission is expected to last until the summer of next year, and findings from this probe will likely only become more relevant, not just as Apophis approaches, but even after it slings back into space. In the year 2000, we knew of only 300 near-Earth asteroids—those that approach Earth or directly cross its orbit. Today, we know of over 43,000.

A chatbot can handle every conversation responsibly and still, over time, take the place of someone a person used to confide in. In their First Opinion essay, Qian Yang, Ned Cooper, and Ibrahim Emara show how easily a gradual loss of human connection can escape safeguards designed for individual conversations. They make a persuasive case for giving independent researchers access to platform data and developing tools to distinguish the effects of chatbot use from other causes of isolation. Those tools should ask whether people could reach someone they trusted when they needed help.

That question is especially worth asking adolescents, who may have fewer ways to seek help privately. Imagine two teenagers who each spend an hour every night talking to a chatbot. One used to call a friend during difficult times, but he called less often as talking to the chatbot became a habit. The other still talks to friends every day. When she wanted to discuss something she feared would get back to her parents, she asked to see a school counselor. While she waited for an appointment, she took the problem to a chatbot. Their chat logs might look very similar. The authors suggest measuring school counselor time as one way to estimate what isolation costs schools. That measure captures time counselors spend helping students. Hours, days, or weeks spent waiting leave no trace.

Researchers could ask teens to think about the last time something was troubling them. Did they talk to someone about it, try to reach someone, or decide against reaching out? If they tried, did they get to talk to someone? Did they talk to a chatbot about it?

Asking these questions regularly could help researchers distinguish a teen who reached out to people less often as their chatbot usage grew from one who turned to a chatbot only after trying to get help. The distinction matters for policymaking. When chatbot use displaces human contact, usage limits may help. When it fills a gap in support, the priority should be making help easier to reach. A usage limit might prompt one teenager to call a friend. Another may still be waiting for someone to call back.

The authors of the First Opinion commentary on pediatric emergencies from Oct. 27 identify a growing but hidden problem: stark gaps in delivery of pediatric emergency care across the U.S. The solutions they propose — payment reform, expanding regional care coordination hubs, and incentivizing large pediatric medical centers to support smaller hospitals — all merit serious consideration.

As a pediatric emergency physician and clinical ethicist, I have experienced and witnessed aggression at work many times. The authors’ concern comes from a good place, but it misses the realities of the clinical setting. First, the essay overstates the evidence against signage. Its claim rests on two sources: a 2006 survey of Australian nurses and a 2008 commentary on the U.K.’s zero tolerance campaign. Neither evaluates whether signs change behavior. Nor has anyone tested the claim that signs erode patient trust. Research on violence prevention is methodologically weak, though multicomponent programs show mixed but encouraging results.

Second, deterrence is not the only purpose of a sign. Even if the signs prevented nothing, they would matter. The authors acknowledge that violence against health care workers has risen. Protecting the people who care for patients is one of them.

Film Festival, the film charts a daughter’s changing understanding of her mother, from resentment over her childhood absence to an admiration she had never anticipated.

Shazli initially conceived the project as a search for Woody, the nanny who had helped raise her, hoping to reconnect with a figure who had played such an important role in her childhood. But as she developed the film and attended workshops with other filmmakers, their observations helped her recognize a more deeply personal story taking shape in the footage of her mother.

“Someone told me, ‘Look at how you film your mother. You film her with so much love,’” she recalls. “It was actually painful,” she adds, because acknowledging that love meant confronting difficult questions, ones she had avoided for far too long.

Those questions extended beyond Khoury’s absence to the expectations she passed on to her daughter. “I grew up with a mother telling me that you have to be independent from any man in the world,” Shazli says. “But it was so extreme. Why can’t both the dad and the mom help? Why can’t both be responsible?”

Khoury had built her career while shouldering much of the financial burden and everyday demands of family life. Shazli admired her mother’s determination but questioned the sacrifices that came along with it. She sought a different kind of partnership, with a husband who shares childcare and financial responsibilities, rather than carrying those burdens alone.

Her family’s cinematic legacy brought pressures of its own. Growing up around Chahine, whom she affectionately calls “Uncle Joe,” Shazli struggled to establish her own identity as a filmmaker.

“Nobody put that pressure on me,” she says. “It was me, coming from that Chahine family, being compared to such a titan. What are you going to do with all this?” Studying film in Cuba offered distance from her family and from Egypt, helping her discover what she wanted to say as a filmmaker.

“I started to see her differently and I started to feel for her,” Shazli tells Variety of her conversations with Khoury. She came to understand how her mother’s own upbringing had shaped the way she expressed affection. “It did hurt me to realize that she had grown up with a different kind of love, one where affection wasn’t always expressed through time and attention. She gave what she knew. You give what you receive.”

Her growing empathy did not erase the hurt of her childhood, but it allowed her to see Khoury beyond the role of mother. Khoury, who took over Chahine’s production outfit Misr International after his passing, remains as devoted to her work as ever, but Shazli no longer sees that devotion solely through the lens of what she missed growing up.

Becoming a mother herself brought another shift in perspective. “Now I feel like I really admire her,” Shazli says. “How on earth did she manage to do all this?”

“That says a lot about her energy, about how much ambition she has,” she adds. “I’m nowhere near that, and I’m alright with that too. I’m actually happy that I don’t have that because you suffer when you have that. You want to be everywhere and do everything.” But that admiration does not mean she intends to follow the same path.

For Shazli, whose earlier documentary “Back Home” explored her relationship with her father, filmmaking is also a means of working through deeply personal questions. “For me, filmmaking is really about healing,” she says. “If there’s no problem, there’s no film. If there’s nothing to discover, to heal, to dig deep into, there’s no film.”

The Cairo premiere has brought an unexpected response, including from men who recognized their own family relationships in the film. “When somebody comes and tells you, ‘You made me want to speak to my mother,’ or ‘You made me want to speak to my father,’ that’s exactly what it should do,” she says.

But having explored both parents on screen, Shazli is ready to turn toward fiction. “I did a film about my dad. I did a film about my mom. I’m done,” she says. “Now I want to have more fun. I want to write a fiction. I want to work with actors. I want to create differently now.”

ChatGPT shrunk down to run on a Super Nintendo

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Just don't expect complex, coherent, or always correct answers

If you’ve ever wondered how small an instance of ChatGPT could get, wonder no longer. Someone has actually rigged up a tiny version of the chatbot to run on a Super Nintendo cartridge.

Tech journalist and retro tech enthusiast Benj Edwards is responsible for ChatGPT for SNES. He's published it online running in an emulator, complete with SNES-era music and sound effects, and for download as an SNES ROM, too.

It’s not a big model, mind you, and performance is thus pretty limited: According to the website, it’s a 2.2 million-parameter offshoot of OpenAI’s GPT-OSS-120b, which was used to develop its training conversations – so in that sense it truly is a ChatGPT that runs on an SNES cartridge. Edwards described it to The Register as a distilled version of the aforementioned open-weight model.

There are two options built into ChatGPT for SNES: One that’s purely the model, with every word generated coming right from its tiny little silicon brain, and another that includes a fact book culled from the opening sentences of around 150,000 simple English Wikipedia articles. If you opt to run both versions and ask it the same questions, you’ll notice a massive difference.

Given some of the other responses we got while testing, there’s really no good reason to run the base model – memory isn’t really an issue given the basic build is about 4MB and the factbook build is just 19MB.

Edwards admits that it’s not the most helpful AI model, either – this is just an experiment after all.

“It is not a useful assistant: the model is millions of times smaller than the models behind ChatGPT, knows about 8,000 words, and often gets things wrong, sometimes confidently,” Edwards noted in his writeup. In that sense, it really is vintage AI on vintage hardware. “The fact book can carry mistakes too.”

If you have an SNES lying around (lucky you), you can download the ROM from this link. If you want to get it running on an SNES you’ll need a cartridge that allows running of custom ROMs, and that supports Nintendo’s Super FX graphics coprocessor. Cartridges like the FXPak Pro and SD2SNES support it. The factbook version also requires support for the aftermarket Media Streaming Unit 1 (MSU-1), which adds CD-ROM like features to the SNES. MSU-1 is available in both of the flash carts mentioned above.

If you have some real hardware skills, however, you could technically make your own ChatGPT cartridge.

“The ‘pure’ version, which generates all responses from the AI language model, could run from a real custom SNES cart,” Edwards told us – it doesn’t even actually need the Super FX chip. “I do have a version of ChatGPT for SNES that can run without the Super FX chip, but without the extra processing power the answers are not as good.”

That said, Edwards believes its absolutely crazy and nonsensical answers are part of the fun.

“ChatGPT for SNES can definitely make mistakes too, but it's almost cute when it does it because of its limited knowledge and its playful interface,” Edwards told us.

While a human may have designed the experiment, the coding work was done by AI, Edwards told us – he’s been interested in it since the 1990s and is pursuing more experiments with tiny AI models using the help of the bigger ones.

“I've been theorizing about what I call ‘tiny talking machines’ … over the past year,” Edwards told us, and he’s been able to use coding agents to turn those ideas into actual software. ChatGPT for SNES is one example, and he also created an NES ROM called Séance that uses an even smaller AI model able to run on an original Nintendo Entertainment System that simulates talking to a ghost in a haunted game cartridge.

“I designed the scope of what the software should be, how it should work broadly, what chip it should use, how the interface should look and work, and the sounds it makes when it types responses,” Edwards told us. He added that he sees the project as an AI/human collaboration, which is where he believes AI works best.

“The AI agents who did the coding for this project currently advise that we've hit the limit of how good the AI model can be on the limited hardware,” Edwards said - he’s not taking their word for it, though. “It's up to human creativity to transcend those assumptions. I have already come up with new ideas that I believe can make a tiny model like ChatGPT for SNES even more coherent, and I'm working on them now.” ®

Is Markel Group Stock a Buy After Falling 10% in 2026?

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Markel stock has been surging in recent weeks.

By Dave Kovaleski – Nov 21, 2026 at 6:45AM EST

10 stocks we like better than Markel Group ›

After falling to a 52-week low of $1,704 per share on Oct. 1, Markel Group (MKL -0.77%) stock has rallied, rising about 15% to $1,952 per share (as of Nov. 19), though it’s still down about 10% for the year.

The specialty insurer and holding company operates a model that is not unlike Berkshire Hathaway (BRKB -0.36%). In fact, some have referred to it as a baby Berkshire Hathaway. Like Berkshire, Markel is mainly an insurance company that uses its float — the premiums it receives before paying out claims — to invest in a portfolio of stocks and privately owned businesses.

Markel has also not been as successful as Berkshire Hathaway, at least not in recent years. It has averaged a 9.2% annualized return during the past five years compared to Berkshire's 15.7% average return. During the past 10 years, Markel stock has posted an average return of 8.5% to 14.1% for Berkshire.

But like Berkshire, there is a lot to like about Markel.

You can't gauge Markel's quarter by looking at traditional earnings based on generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP) because the unrealized investment gains and losses from its portfolio are included. Those unrealized gains or losses distort the actual performance of the company.

But if you look at its adjusted operating income, that shows how much the company made on the businesses it owns. In Q3, its adjusted operating income rose 14% from a year ago to $707 million, or $37.51 per share. That soundly beat analysts' estimates of $27.76 per share. Its revenue jumped 6% year-over-year to $4.16 billion, which easily beat estimates of $3.78 billion.

Within insurance, Markel saw its adjusted underwriting gross premium volume increase by 9% for the quarter. This excludes its global reinsurance division, which it exited in the past year, and its specialty car insurance business Hagerty, which transitioned to a fronting arrangement. Also, its net investment income increased 5% to $233 million.

The key result in insurance is the combined ratio, which measures how much the company makes in profits from premiums after claims are paid out and operating expenses are subtracted. Markel had a combined ratio of 92%, down from 93% a year ago.

For the full year, the combined ratio is 92%, down from 95% in 2026. This is a good thing because a combined ratio of more than 100 means you are paying out more than you are taking in. A ratio below 100 means you are making more than you are paying out. So, a combined ratio of 92% means Markel is making more profit per dollar of premiums than it was last year.

Markel's businesses, known as Markel Ventures, also performed well. Its industrial holdings saw operating income rise 29% year-over-year to $131 million, while its financial segment increased 30% to $80 million and its consumer properties rose 80% to $31 million.

Markel doesn't provide short-term projections, but its trends within insurance and its holdings are promising. The company is built to perform well in negative markets, which is a big reason the shares surged in recent weeks.

When investors make a flight to safety, they head for companies like Markel, which is grounded by its insurance holdings and conservative underwriting and its durable, value-oriented investments with low debt and stable earnings.

The stock is also fairly cheap right now, trading at less than 14 times earnings. I like it as a long-term buy, and it's particularly valuable now during bouts of market volatility.

Google told schools to accept AI or lose Search. Now 3 of America’s 4 largest districts are pushing back

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google has handed school districts what many have called an ultimatum: Allow students to see AI summaries in their Google searches, or give up Google Search on school accounts altogether.

In August, Google switched on a Gemini tab in Google Classroom by default for K-12 students whose schools had already allowed its AI chatbot, Gemini. On Nov. 3, it reversed course, announcing that its AI tools will be off for K-12 student accounts unless school administrators turn them on, starting early next year. But Google also made clear that schools that want students to have Google Search will have to accept AI Overviews, the AI-generated summaries that have become a familiar sight at the top of search results.

“Search and AI are deeply integrated,” Chris Phillips, vice president for education at Google, told The New York Times, adding that students at schools that don’t allow AI “will not have access to search, and they’ll see a message from their school that indicates it’s not available.”

Many school districts have restricted or banned student AI use, citing safety and privacy concerns. They may not have expected those restrictions to cost them the world’s most popular search engine, a trade-off that has left edtech experts questioning Google’s rationale.

Google’s decision comes as tech companies face mounting legal pressure over harm to children. In March, a Los Angeles jury found Meta and Google’s YouTube negligent and determined they failed to warn users about the dangers of their platforms. In January, Google and Character.AI, a chatbot company with ties to Google, agreed to settle lawsuits from families who said their children were harmed by its chatbots.

And earlier this month, a family sued Google, alleging their 13-year-old daughter used Gemini on her school-issued Chromebook to plan her suicide and that Google pushed its AI products on students without warning parents or schools.

“We hear parents and teachers’ concerns about students using AI to shortcut learning, and we share the same goal: help students learn,” a Google spokesperson said, adding that the company is piloting new Gemini features to support “productive struggle” by helping students “work through problems without skipping straight to answers.”

Whether that’s enough to win over districts that have restricted AI is another question. The country’s four largest school districts have landed in different places, both on AI and on Google’s decision to tie it to Search.

Alphabet, Google’s parent company, did not respond to Fortune‘s request for comment.

Chicago Public Schools has pushed back the hardest. On Dec. 22, 2025, the district began blocking students from AI tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and other third-party AI products. Students may use only AI-enhanced edtech tools vetted through the district’s compliance process under SOPPA, Illinois’ student data privacy law.

On Nov. 11, about 30 protesters gathered outside Google’s West Loop office, chanting, “Hey Google, nice try. We don’t want your Gemini.”

In a statement to the Chicago Sun-Times, a CPS spokesperson said the district is “actively exploring all options to independently suppress” the AI Overviews in Google searches.

“The District would like to continue to provide students with access to the Google search engine without the AI component. If such an arrangement can’t be reached, the District will look to other search solutions,” the spokesperson said.

New York City Public Schools, the nation’s largest district, appears headed in a similar direction, though it has yet to decide. On Sept. 2, Mayor Zohran Mamdani and Schools Chancellor Kamar Samuels announced a one-year moratorium on student-facing AI for 2-K through eighth grade. They also banned companion chatbots in all grades.

After Google’s announcement, Mamdani posted on X that “Google is trying to use its monopoly power to force AI into our public schools. No corporation should have the power to dictate how we teach our children.”

City Council Speaker Julie Menin and Council Member Shekar Krishnan sent a letter to Google CEO Sundar Pichai asking whether Google would let students keep using Google Search without AI Overviews and, if not, why not, given Google’s record of restricting product features for young users, as it does with YouTube Kids. The stakes are large: The district has about 1.3 million Chromebooks, including 500,000 assigned to students, according to the letter.

District spokesperson Nicole Brownstein told Gothamist that officials are “reaching out to Google to discuss this update further,” and that the district had already disabled the Gemini chatbot and AI Mode on student Chromebooks. If Google doesn’t budge, the nation’s largest school district will have to choose between softening its stance and finding another search engine.

Los Angeles Unified, the nation’s second-largest district, announced on Sept. 2 that it would block generative AI for students in all grades on district-issued devices for the 2026-27 school year, a move affecting an estimated 378,000 students.

During public comment at a district Generative AI Ad Hoc Committee meeting after Google’s announcement, Hillary Power, a parent and member of Schools Beyond Screens, called for Los Angeles Unified to continue “formalizing, extending and expanding the current T-K to 12 moratorium of student use of generative AI.”

“Google has been particularly clear on their priorities when several days ago they issued a monopolistic and, frankly, pathetic ultimatum to schools to use their AI search products or be cut off,” she continued.

The district has asked Google for a way to turn off the AI summaries, according to LAist, and said it is reviewing Google’s announcement and its “potential implications for schools, educators and students.”

Miami-Dade County Public Schools, the nation’s third-largest district, may be headed in a different direction.

In 2023, the district blocked AI chatbots such as ChatGPT over concerns about cheating and misinformation, according to The New York Times. Two years later, it reversed course, offering Gemini for Education to about 100,000 high school students, the largest AI deployment in a U.S. school district at the time. In February, the district announced a continued collaboration with Google to give all its high school students free access to full-length SAT practice exams through Gemini.

But on Sept. 16, the Florida Board of Education voted to require public school districts and charter schools to set rules for AI use. Schools will also have to tell parents and guardians which AI tools students will interact with, so families can opt their child into a tool or request a non-AI alternative. Districts have until July 1, 2027, to comply.

Miami-Dade County Public Schools did not respond to questions about how it will adapt to Google’s policy and the new Florida rules.

Former Federal Trade Commission chair Lina Khan criticized Google’s decision to tie Search to its AI summaries. In a Nov. 3 post on X, she said the company is using its search monopoly to “coerce schools into adopting AI.”

“Multiple courts have found Google liable for violating antitrust laws, and this latest move shows it remains unchastened,” she said. “As the public increasingly pushes back against the use of AI in schools, Google is making it clear it will punish those who don’t fall in line.”

Khan isn’t the only one questioning whether the move is legal. Zephyr Teachout, a professor at Fordham Law School, said she does not find Google’s claim that it cannot unlink AI Overviews from Search “credible,” and that the company could be found to violate antitrust laws governing how companies tie the sale of two products.

In antitrust law, “tying” is when a company with significant market power requires customers who want one of its products to take another as well. Google’s parent, Alphabet, is No. 5 on the 2026 Fortune 500. According to the FTC, tying raises antitrust questions when it “restricts competition without providing benefits to consumers.”

“Google ran search for decades without AI, has filters that allow people to see results without AI summaries, and has been inconsistent in how it has responded to the tying claim — merely asserting they are ‘deeply integrated’ is not a legal argument,” Teachout said. “The company has on/off controls for K-12 administrators and could have an on/off control for search and AI.”

Google has lost a tying case before. In April 2025, U.S. District Judge Leonie Brinkema found that Google had been “willfully acquiring and maintaining monopoly power” in two parts of the online advertising market and that it had illegally tied them together by pushing websites that wanted full access to its ad exchange to also use its ad server.

But the company has also won a case involving AI Overviews. In September, U.S. District Judge Amit Mehta dismissed a lawsuit by Penske Media over Google’s use of its content for AI Overviews, finding that Penske had not plausibly shown that Search and AI Overviews were separate products.

Teachout said schools are different, pointing to the “clear evidence of a separate market demand in schools for search as distinct from AI.”

Whether a court ever takes up such a case, or agrees with Teachout, is an open question. Google’s changes take effect early next year, and until then, districts have to decide whether keeping Google Search is worth accepting the AI that comes with it.

Airline Captain Defends 20-Year-Old Female Copilot After Fatal Learjet Crash

121pilot, our resident commercial airline pilot on Live And Let’s Fly, examines what we know about the crash of Learjet 60 N722BD and pushes back against the rush to blame its young first officer, Nathalia Filgueiras.

The crash of N722BD, a Learjet 60 operating on a charter flight, has drawn enormous attention. Much of that is understandable. We have extremely dramatic video of the aircraft descending almost vertically at a very high rate of speed, along with equally dramatic ATC audio recordings.

As is typical of the aviation YouTube community, many of whom I respect, people have been rushing to get videos out about this accident. What I really don’t care for, however, are the clickbait titles. Titles like “Could Any Pilot Have Saved This Learjet 60?” or “Lear 60 Florida Crash: It Happened Before” or “Expert Pilot Explains What Likely Caused SHOCKING Plane Crash in Florida” by Megyn Kelly.

These kinds of titles make me angry because, regardless of the actual content of the videos, they are misleading. The simple fact is that we know very little about this accident at this point, and certainly not enough to be speculating about a probable cause.

The aircraft, a Learjet Model 60 with nine people aboard, reported moderate turbulence and icing at Flight Level 350 and requested a climb to Flight Level 370. Moments later, the aircraft departed controlled flight, eventually entering a descent at an extraordinarily high rate of speed.

We know that the NTSB has recovered components of the horizontal stabilizer that separated from the aircraft, and widely circulated video appears to show the aircraft without this crucial component.

What we DON’T know is whether the aircraft flew into a thunderstorm. The weather imagery I’ve seen to date comes from sources that have time lags built into them and don’t necessarily represent the real-time weather picture. We don’t know what caused the horizontal stabilizer to separate, or even when that happened. Did it separate during the dive, or was its failure what initiated the dive? That’s an important distinction, and right now it’s an unknown.

We know the Learjet 60’s wing is sensitive to ice accumulation and that the aircraft is susceptible to a phenomenon called Mach tuck. We don’t know whether either played any role in this accident, or even whether the aircraft’s critical surfaces had accumulated ice.

We know the aircraft had been operating at higher altitudes, with a reported descent from Flight Level 410 to 350 before the final sequence of events. But without knowing the aircraft’s speed, configuration, weight, and other performance parameters, we cannot draw meaningful conclusions about its operating margins.

We know the captain was 55 years old and very experienced. We know the first officer was 20 years old and relatively inexperienced, but fully qualified to act as a second in command.

We know the CVR, or cockpit voice recorder, has been recovered, but we don’t yet know whether its contents can be successfully recovered or what information it might provide. We also know the aircraft was not equipped with a flight data recorder, or FDR. That means information about the aircraft’s speed, heading, pitch, roll, system status, and other parameters may remain unknown or will have to be reconstructed using external data sources and whatever information can be gleaned from the CVR.

But perhaps the most disturbing part of this entire event, aside from the tragic loss of life, has been the reaction of so many people who couldn’t explain the difference between a slat and a flap, yet have felt perfectly qualified to judge the performance of the first officer based on her age and gender.

In the Megyn Kelly video, she questions whether someone who isn’t even old enough to drink should have been acting as a pilot.

I think the better question is why we allow 18-year-olds to join the military and go into combat, but apparently don’t think they’re old enough to have a beer. Why can a 20-year-old warrant officer fly a Black Hawk full of troops into the maelstrom of a hot landing zone, but we think he or she can’t be trusted with alcohol?

Even more disgusting have been some of the reactions to the ATC recordings.

First, let me suggest that if you haven’t already listened to them, don’t. Second, and most importantly, as a 56-year-old male airline captain, I hear absolutely nothing in that recording, either in her voice or in her actions, that merits criticism.

I don’t think people who haven’t experienced it can appreciate just how incredibly violent and terrifying that initial descent must have been. In one recording, we go from a seemingly calm situation, with the crew requesting a climb, to a loss of control and a reported descent of approximately 8,000 feet per minute in about 10 seconds. Just 16 seconds later, the aircraft is in an overspeed condition, and the first officer reports a descent rate of 20,000 feet per minute. That’s an absolutely eye-watering number. It defies belief and would be utterly terrifying to any pilot. About 10 seconds later, she reports that they are going to crash into the ground. Not long after that, someone can be heard in the background reciting the Lord’s Prayer.

I don’t know this for certain, of course, but my suspicion is that somewhere during this sequence the crew became aware that the horizontal stabilizer had separated and that they had lost the ability to recover the aircraft.

If there is anything to be said about her performance as a pilot, it’s that she was clearly doing her job right up to the end.

We teach Aviate, Navigate, Communicate. Aviate means flying the airplane. Navigate means determining where you need to go and how to get there. Communicate means keeping the appropriate people informed.

In this situation, the captain was presumably concentrating on flying the aircraft while the first officer was communicating with ATC. That is exactly what we expect from a two-person crew in an emergency, assuming those were their respective roles at the time.

What’s more, in the absence of an FDR, the information she provided to ATC may prove absolutely crucial to understanding what happened. We may eventually discover that her actions and communications were instrumental in allowing the NTSB to piece together this accident. That information could well save lives in the future.

I also find it interesting, and troubling, that contrary to the media’s usual fixation on the captain, as though he’s flying the airplane solo, the exact opposite has happened here. The first officer has become the focus of an extraordinary amount of attention, even though we have not established who was flying the aircraft.

It’s been my absolute privilege to fly with a number of female pilots throughout my career, and not one of them has ever done anything less than a sterling job. Frankly, the airplane doesn’t care about your gender or the color of your skin. And when it comes to aviation, that’s exactly how it should be. We should care about qualifications and competence. That’s it.

If I step onto my jet tomorrow and find a young female first officer waiting for me, I’ll be just as happy to have her there as I would a young male. At this point in my career, flying with a young and relatively inexperienced first officer gives me the opportunity to act as a mentor and pass along some hard-won flying wisdom. That’s something I genuinely enjoy. Nathalia had earned her place in that cockpit. Not one iota of the solid evidence we have right now leads me to believe that this accident can or should be blamed on her.

It may well turn out that crew errors figure prominently in the probable cause. We simply don’t know yet. But if that turns out to be the case, it’s important to remember that the captain was the pilot in command. He was responsible for the operation of that aircraft, and the first officer was serving under his command. If poor decision-making put the aircraft into a situation from which there was no escape, the captain would bear the primary responsibility for those decisions.

But that’s a hypothetical. And right now, we don’t know that any such decisions were made.

We owe the nine people who lost their lives, their families, and the people who will eventually investigate this accident something better than speculation, sensationalism, and a rush to judgment.

Social Security's Trump Bump-Led 2027 COLA Is the 5th Largest in 35 Years -- but There's a Dark Side to This Outsize Increase as Well

📅 Nov 21, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Though Social Security checks are about to get bigger, the projected timeline for sweeping benefit cuts may have accelerated.

By Sean Williams – Nov 21, 2026 at 6:36AM EST

For Social Security’s nearly 72 million traditional beneficiaries -- retired workers, workers with disabilities, and survivors of deceased workers -- there are few, if any, announcements more anticipated than the annual cost-of-living adjustment (COLA).

Social Security's COLA is the near-annual "raise" given to beneficiaries to account for inflation (i.e., rising prices). If the price for the goods and services that seniors regularly purchase kept climbing without an adjustment in benefits, the buying power of Social Security income would steadily decline over time.

Social Security's announced 2027 COLA offers something of a mixed bag for recipients. On the one hand, it's set to deliver one of the largest raises over the last 35 years. This larger payout comes courtesy of President Donald Trump and a handful of his policies.

President Trump's policies offer a good-news/bad-news scenario for Social Security. Image source: Official White House Photo by Molly Riley.

But this outsize Trump bump isn't without its consequences. Larger payouts in the new year can make the program's precarious financial outlook that much worse.

On Oct. 14, the Social Security Administration announced that monthly payouts would increase by 3.6% in 2027. For the average retired-worker beneficiary, their monthly payout is expected to climb by $75. Meanwhile, the typical worker with a disability can expect their monthly Social Security check to rise by $59 in the new year.

A 3.6% raise is well above the norm. It marks a tie for the fifth-largest percentage increase over the last 35 years, surpassed only by COLAs of 4.1% (2006), 5.8% (2009), 5.9% (2022), and 8.7% (2023).

It's also the sixth consecutive year in which recipients have received at least a 2.5% cost-of-living adjustment. It's been 30 years (1988-1997) since benefits rose by at least 2.5% for six straight years.

While modest inflation associated with a growing U.S. economy results in a Social Security raise most years, next year's outsize COLA was pumped up by two of President Donald Trump's policies.

New from the New York Fed: "By February 2026, tariffs had contributed 2.9 pp to goods price inflation, and without them goods prices would have fallen slightly." About one quarter of every point in higher tariff rates shows up in consumer prices within one year. • A 10% across-the-board tariff yields a 2.6% rise in consumer goods prices after one year. • About two thirds of the increase comes from pricier imports, which get passed through quickly. • Another third comes from U.S. made goods, as producers face higher input costs and less import competition. This takes longer, about 6-12 months, to filter through.

To begin with, each of the last two Social Security COLAs has been influenced by Trump's tariff and trade policy. Adding duties to imports directly raises their price and can increase manufacturing costs for domestic producers.

A recently published report by three New York Federal Reserve economists writing for Liberty Street Economics ("How Fast Do Tariffs Pass Through into Consumer Prices?") found that Trump's 2025 tariffs increased goods price inflation by an estimated 2.9 percentage points by February 2026. Without tariffs, the NY Fed economists estimate that goods prices would have otherwise fallen.

The other major policy contributing to Social Security's 2027 Trump bump is the Iran war. Closing the Strait of Hormuz has resulted in the largest energy supply disruption in modern history, sending gas and diesel prices notably higher.

With Trumpflation (inflation driven by Trump's policies) becoming entrenched in the U.S. economy, it's quite possible that these policies provide a third consecutive year with a Trump bump in 2028.

Unfortunately, outsize Social Security COLAs come at a potentially steep cost to America's leading retirement program.

Every year since 1985, the Social Security Board of Trustees Report has warned of a long-term unfunded obligation. In simpler terms, the projected income to be collected in the 75 years following the release of a report is expected to be insufficient to cover outlays (primarily benefits, but also the administrative costs to oversee Social Security). As of 2026, this long-term unfunded obligation had ballooned to $29.3 trillion.

However, this isn't the most pressing concern for the program or its nearly 72 million traditional beneficiaries.

According to the latest Trustees Report, the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance trust fund (OASI) is forecast to exhaust its asset reserves by the fourth quarter of 2032. The OASI's asset reserves represent the excess income collected since inception that is invested in special-issue, interest-bearing government bonds, as required by law.

US Old-Age and Survivors Insurance Trust Fund Assets at End of Year data by YCharts.

The positive news is that Social Security isn't going bankrupt and is in no danger of halting benefits. More than 91% of the program's income is collected through the 12.4% payroll tax on earned income (wages and salaries, but not investment income). As long as Americans keep working, the payroll tax will continue generating income for disbursement to eligible beneficiaries... even if the OASI's asset reserves are depleted.

But just because Social Security isn't going bankrupt, it doesn't mean everything is peachy. If the OASI's asset reserves run dry over the next six years, as predicted, it would indicate that the current payout schedule, inclusive of annual COLAs, isn't sustainable. Sweeping benefit cuts of up to 22% may be necessary for retired workers and survivors of deceased workers.

The concern is that Social Security's Trump bump may have materially altered the trajectory to the OASI's asset reserve insolvency.

When the Trustees Report issues its short- and long-term outlooks, it takes into account a long list of variables, including birth rates, death rates, net migration rates, and even annual COLAs. The Trustees assume that annual COLAs will be modest -- but as we just saw, a 3.6% annual raise is anything but average.

Social Security's outsize 2027 raise risks draining the OASI even faster than initially forecast.

President Trump's "Big, Beautiful Bill" already moved up the projected OASI asset reserve insolvency timeline by one quarter (from the first quarter of 2033 to the fourth quarter of 2032). It's entirely possible the 2027 Trump bump further accelerates the timeline to sweeping benefit cuts.
