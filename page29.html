We Now Know What Robert Pattinson Was Doing When the James Bond Casting News Broke

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

All thanks to filmmaker Robert Eggers, who directed the star in 2019's 'The Lighthouse.'

It’s been a whirlwind 48 hours for Robert Pattinson.

The Hollywood Reporter heard from sources that the British star only found out he’d been tapped to succeed Daniel Craig as James Bond on Sunday morning, when Denis Villeneuve — who’s helming the next Bond movie — called him. Producer Amy Pascal was said to inform the other candidates.

Within a day, the whole world knew too. Amazon MGM Studios, which bought the creative rights to the Bond franchise from producer siblings Michael G. Wilson and Barbara Broccoli just last year, released the news via the character’s official social media accounts. Just before 7 a.m. PT on Monday, a tuxedo-wearing Pattinson emerged from the shadows, gun in hand. His announcement post has accrued over 1.2 million likes on X at the time of writing. Thanks to Robert Eggers, who directed Pattinson in his 2019 period horror-fantasy The Lighthouse, we now know what the next Bond was up to when that news became public and sent the internet into a tailspin. During an on-camera interview with IGN, Eggers was asked if, while Pattinson’s character killed a seagull during the making of The Lighthouse, he ever stopped to consider: “There goes the next James Bond.”

“No, but I’m not surprised that it panned out the way that it did,” replied the Nosferatu filmmaker. “And also, crazily enough, he was watching a screening of Werwulf when the news broke, and I was just texting with him about it this morning,” he said, laughing.

Werwulf, set for release on Dec. 25 in the U.S., stars Eggers regulars Aaron Taylor-Johnson, Lily-Rose Depp, Willem Dafoe and Ralph Ineson, and is set to chronicle a cursed farmer’s transformation into a monstrous creature in 13th-century England. Coincidentally, Taylor-Johnson was among the rumored Bond contenders for a long time.

So what did our new Bond think of Werwulf? “Oh, he loved it, of course,” added Eggers.

Pattinson, who this year alone has starred in The Drama, The Odyssey, Primetime and still has Dune 3 to come, said on Monday through an Amazon MGM Studios press release: “James Bond is one of the most legendary characters in cinema, and to be entrusted with 007 is the honor of a lifetime. I’m deeply grateful to Denis, Amy, David, Tanya and everyone at Amazon MGM Studios for their belief in me. To say this role is a dream come true is an understatement. I couldn’t be more excited about this partnership and the work ahead with this fantastic team.”

He won a months-long race for the role, beating fellow screen-testers Callum Turner, Josh O’Connor and Jack Lowden. Villeneuve, who recently worked with Pattinson on the final Dune outing, is directing a script from Peaky Blinders mastermind Steven Knight.

The 40-year-old actor is also already the face of DC Studio’s Batman. THR took a deep dive into why his casting is the smart but safe choice as the Bond franchise enters a new era. Fan reaction to the announcement is here — and some are suggesting Pattinson’s starring role in Twilight predicted his future Bond role.

In fact, Pattinson might have even manifested it. In 2012, during the height of his Twilight fame, the actor was asked in an interview if he could be playing 007 in 10 years’ time. “Yeah, definitely,” he replied, “but more like 20 years. […] I think there’d be nothing worse than, like, ‘Let’s get a fresh-faced Bond!’ That would be the worst idea in the world. After Daniel Craig, you have to have some baggage. It would be ridiculous to reinvent [him] as some young posh kid. Or, if [he] was, I’d have to get so tortured in the first few scenes. I’d have to do the first film with one arm or something.”

Nvidia lost its CEO and its crown, but the sell-off could be a gift for patient investors.

The bigger they are, the harder they fall. That adage is playing out before investors' eyes with Nvidia (NVDA -4.12%).

Throughout most of the past year, Nvidia ranked as the world's largest company by market cap. However, the chip stock plunged more than 30% below its previous high, forfeiting its top spot to Apple (AAPL -0.98%). Nvidia's shares now trade below $170 for the first time in nearly eight months.

But buying Nvidia on the dip has proven a smart strategy in the past. The stock could be a screaming buy yet again.

It's important to understand that while Nvidia's stock has pulled back sharply, its underlying business is booming. In the second quarter of fiscal 2027, the company's revenue more than doubled year over year to $96.2 billion. Its earnings skyrocketed 128%.

Nvidia is scheduled to announce its third-quarter results after the market close on Tuesday, Nov. 17, 2026. There's no reason to anticipate a dramatic slowdown. Wall Street expects the company to post year-over-year revenue growth of roughly 91% in Q3, with earnings up 90%.

The demand for Nvidia's GPUs remains strong. Its Blackwell architecture has been a resounding success -- and led in every category on two industry AI benchmarks.

The multi-rack AI supercomputer platform Vera Rubin began shipping in August 2026. Nvidia expects Vera Rubin to have the fastest product ramp in the company's history.

Meanwhile, Nvidia's trailing 12-month price-to-earnings ratio of 20.6 is at its lowest level since early 2019. Its valuation is even more attractive with growth factored in. Nvidia's forward earnings multiple is 10.3. Its price-to-earnings-to-growth (PEG) ratio, which is based on analysts' five-year earnings growth projections, is a super-low 0.22.

You might have noticed that I haven't yet mentioned the reason why Nvidia's stock has plunged recently. However, there's no way to completely ignore the elephant in the room: Jensen Huang's abrupt departure as CEO following allegations of two affairs, one with a person accused of being a Chinese spy and another with an Nvidia employee.

Huang's unexpected exit certainly leaves a void at Nvidia. He co-founded the company in 1993. He has been the visionary who led Nvidia to jaw-dropping growth.

But I don't think that Huang's absence means that Nvidia is doomed. For one thing, he didn't single-handedly drive the company's product vision. Nvidia has plenty of highly experienced and capable leaders who will ensure its technology advances, including longtime CFO and interim CEO Colette Kress.

Don't rule out the possibility that Huang could return, either. The Financial Times reported that he could attempt a comeback as Nvidia's CEO and might even try to get the White House to lobby on his behalf.

Maybe Huang will become Nvidia's CEO again; maybe not. Either way, the current sell-off looks like a great buying opportunity for patient investors.

First, what is Backpack crypto? Why is it exploding? Backpack is suddenly becoming one of the market’s hottest stories after securing a major regulatory breakthrough in the United States. Its native BP token surged 30% in 24 hours as traders rushed to price in the exchange’s latest expansion. The Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) has effectively opened the door for Backpack to challenge established crypto derivatives platforms on American soil.

The approval gives Backpack the regulatory foundation to operate a US derivatives exchange and clearinghouse. Its ambitions extend beyond simply offering another place to trade Bitcoin and Ethereum. The company wants to combine leveraged crypto trading, collateral management, and global liquidity under one regulated platform.

The development also arrives as competition heats up over who can deliver the next generation of crypto trading infrastructure.

Founded by Armani Ferrante, Backpack started as a Solana-focused crypto wallet and exchange. It has since expanded into securities, tokenization, and derivatives. The company then reports more than $450 billion in cumulative trading volume and customers across over 150 countries.

Its latest breakthrough involves two separate approvals from the CFTC. The Designated Contract Market (DCM) registration allows Backpack to operate a regulated derivatives exchange.

Meanwhile, its Derivatives Clearing Organization (DCO) registration provides the infrastructure needed to manage collateral, margin, and settlement obligations. Together, these approvals establish the foundation for Backpack to offer leveraged crypto perpetual futures in the United States.

The proposed platform will support 24/7 trading, crypto and fiat collateral, cross-margining, and access to Backpack’s global order book.

That last feature could prove particularly important. Rather than isolating American traders inside a separate liquidity pool, Backpack wants to connect US customers with its international trading ecosystem.

BP responded immediately. The token climbed by more than 30% over 24 hours to $1.10, pushing its market cap higher as trading activity accelerated. The rally ties to growing expectations that Backpack could become a serious competitor in the American derivatives market.

The regulatory breakthrough also strengthens its position against offshore platforms that have dominated leveraged crypto trading for years.

Backpack’s approval exposes an interesting opportunity across the wider crypto market. Bitcoin remains the industry’s largest liquidity pool, yet its base layer was never designed for sophisticated smart contracts, high-speed decentralized trading or complex margin products.

That creates an infrastructure gap developers are racing to fill. Solana demonstrated how quickly decentralized applications can execute transactions. Bitcoin, meanwhile, offers a much deeper pool of established capital.

Bringing those strengths together could unlock an entirely different market for Bitcoin-denominated trading.

Projects exploring this direction, Bitcoin Hyper ($HYPER), which combines Bitcoin Layer 2 infrastructure with Solana Virtual Machine technology. Its architecture aims to let users bridge BTC into a faster environment supporting smart contracts and decentralized applications.

The potential goes beyond faster transfers. Bitcoin liquidity could eventually support decentralized spot markets, margin trading, and perpetual futures without forcing traders to abandon Bitcoin for another blockchain ecosystem.

Bitcoin Hyper has already attracted more than $33.2 million through its presale, exceeding Backpack’s previously disclosed $17 million Series A funding round. Its token currently sells for just $0.0137, while its staking program advertises rewards of up to 35% APY.

With its November 26 launch target approaching, the presale is entering its final stretch. The combination of Bitcoin liquidity and Solana-style execution is attracting attention as developers look for ways to bring more sophisticated financial applications to Bitcoin.

Backpack now has something many offshore competitors cannot easily replicate: a regulatory pathway into the American derivatives market. The company still needs to turn its approvals into actual trading activity, but the potential market is enormous.

American traders represent an important source of institutional and retail liquidity. Bringing them into a unified order book could strengthen Backpack’s competitive position while attracting market makers and professional trading firms.

BP’s 30% rally shows investors are already anticipating that expansion, and Backpack’s regulatory breakthrough could mark the beginning of a much larger shift in crypto trading.

The race is no longer just about building another exchange. It is about connecting deeper liquidity, faster execution, and increasingly sophisticated financial products. Bitcoin’s enormous capital base makes that opportunity particularly difficult to ignore.

Astronaut Loads 35mm Film Into Analog Camera While in Space

Astronaut Loads 35mm Film Into Analog Camera While in Space

German astronaut Rabea Rogge flew on the Fram2 spaceflight mission in April 2025, bringing her trusty second-hand analog camera.

Rogge is an Arctic roboticist, currently studying for a PhD at the Norwegian University of Science and Technology in the Department of Marine Technology. She is also Germany’s first-ever female astronaut.

Last week, Rogge shared a video on Instagram as she loaded up a roll of 35mm film while onboard the SpaceX Crew Dragon Resilience spacecraft.

While many celluloid shooters have encountered difficulties loading film on terra firma, Rogge deftly loaded a roll of Kodak Portra 160 into the camera that she calls a “sturdy companion.”

A post shared by Rabea Rogge (@rabearogge)

“Analog pictures from space,” Rogge captions the post. “My old second-hand analog camera has been a sturdy companion on all my expeditions from under -20°C [-4°F] in the high Arctic to the humid climate at sea around the equator. On Fram2 it got to launch on a rocket as well – and it still lives on so far!”

PetaPixel identified Rogge’s camera as a Minolta X-700 with a 45mm lens attached. The X-700 was the flagship SLR for the now-defunct Japanese company for the best part of 20 years. In 1982, it won the European “Camera of the Year” award, and it is still popular in the second-hand market today.

With the film loaded in the camera, Rogge was afforded stunning views of Earth thanks to the Crew Dragon’s 360-degree cupola. At 2,000 square inches, it was the largest single window ever flown to space. Rogge, along with her crewmates Chun Wang, Jannicke Mikkelsen, and Eric Philips, were also the first people to ever orbit over Earth’s north and south poles.

“Can’t imagine the face of the people from the film lab when they scanned your roll,” comments Thibaut Everaerts on Instagram.

Rogge’s camera is far from the first Minolta to ever fly from space; in fact, the first American to ever orbit the Earth, John Glenn, took a Minolta Hi-Matic (repackaged as an Ansco Autoset) with him into space.

Despite buying the automatic camera for just $40 from a drugstore in Cocoa Beach, Florida, NASA engineers modified the camera with a pistol grip so that Glenn could use the camera while wearing his bulky astronaut gloves.

The photos that Glenn took in 1962 and their resulting popularity are credited with helping NASA realize how important it was to document Earth from space.

President Trump’s 48-hour trip will begin with a meeting with Southeast Asian leaders in Manila. He will later meet Xi Jinping of China and Vladimir Putin of Russia in Shenzhen, a Chinese economic hub.

President Trump on Tuesday began a whirlwind Asia trip in which he will meet Xi Jinping of China and other world leaders, with an unpredictable agenda that could include the future of American tariffs, nuclear arms control and U.S. restrictions on advanced artificial intelligence chips.

Mr. Trump landed in Manila, where he is scheduled to meet with President Ferdinand R. Marcos Jr. of the Philippines and attend a gathering of Southeast Asian leaders. After less than 24 hours there, he will head to the APEC economic summit in Shenzhen, China, where all eyes will be on his expected meeting with Mr. Xi and President Vladimir V. Putin of Russia.

The brief itinerary appears to reflect Mr. Trump’s preferences. He dislikes big summits where he often is not the sole subject of attention. And he enjoys meeting with leaders of powerful nations — especially Mr. Xi and Mr. Putin, two authoritarian leaders he often talks about in admiring terms.

Mr. Trump will be meeting Mr. Xi for the third time this year, and a three-way meeting between China, Russia and the United States would be the first of its kind. Each leader will arrive at the session, planned for Thursday, with a different agenda, and some analysts have compared it to Yalta, where the leaders of the United States, Britain and the Soviet Union divided up postwar Europe in 1945.

That analogy has limits: No one thinks these three leaders, powerful as they are, are there to redefine the borders of any continent. But their very presence at one table would underscore they have major sway over the shape of the next world order. And for Mr. Trump, who loves the symbolism of a dramatic photo op, that may be enough.

On Tuesday, Mr. Trump will catch the end of a dayslong meeting of the Association of Southeast Asian Nations.

He isn’t just any latecomer: He is the one who issued tariffs on countries across the region, even on allies — bolstering the feeling in Asia that the United States is not a reliable partner, but rather one that treasures its commercial interests over its security interests. Southeast Asian leaders will be looking for any signs that the Republican defeat in the midterm elections may have humbled Mr. Trump or made him reconsider his policies.

Mr. Trump will also meet with leaders from the so-called “Quad” — the United States, India, Japan and Australia. All three partners are smarting from high energy prices triggered by the Iran war, a conflict Mr. Trump never discussed with them before launching it in late February.

Before leaving for China on Wednesday, Mr. Trump will be hosted by Mr. Marcos, who has been contending with Chinese challenges to islands that both Beijing and Manila claim.

In Shenzhen — the heart of China’s robotics and A.I. industries — Mr. Trump will pursue détente with Mr. Xi. He needs to extend a soon-expiring trade truce that limited tariffs on Chinese goods entering the United States in return for a Chinese commitment not to cut off rare earths and magnets.

Then comes the main event: the meeting with Mr. Xi and Mr. Putin.

“We are going to be talking about denuclearization, a tough subject because it is very hard for any of us to say we’ll get rid of our nukes,’’ Mr. Trump told reporters before leaving Washington, referring to the agenda of the trilateral meeting. “We will talk about Iran a little,” he said, insisting the situation was in “very good shape.”

Mr. Trump also said he would talk about Ukraine, on which Mr. Putin’s aides are engaging in talks with the U.S. and Ukrainian officials, but also renewing demands that NATO pull back its troops to where they were before the alliance expanded toward Russia.

On nuclear weapons, Chinese officials have made clear they are not interested in talks until their arsenal is roughly the size of the American one. And on the Iran war, analysts say it seems unlikely that the three-way meeting would make any progress — at a moment when Russia is happy to see energy prices remain high, and China is eager to see them rapidly decline.

Whale snot helps scientists sequence an entire genome

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 17, 2026 5:00 AM EST

Whale vomit can be turned into expensive perfume. Whale carcasses will sink to the bottom and become marine life habitats. Whale pee nourishes the entire ocean. But how about whale snot? The microbes and other biological material emitted from their blowholes when the giant cetaceans breathe has helped scientists sequence an entire whale genome: Take that whale vomit. The findings are detailed in a study published today in the journal Molecular Ecology Resources.

Whole genomes are crucial for animal conservation. With genomic data, researchers can measure population health, to see how much genetic diversity and inbreeding is present. It can also help identify individual animals for monitoring, help determine an animal’s sex, and estimate the population’s size.

To obtain genetic data from whales, marine biologists historically have had to conduct a skin biopsy. They’ll take a dart, stick it into the whale’s blubber, and take a small sample of the skin and blubber. However, during sampling, boats must get close to the whales, which can disturb them. In some locations and for some endangered species, getting close enough to take a biopsy isn’t permitted or possible.

That’s where whale snot—or blow—comes in. For this study, a team from Scotland’s University of St. Andrews, the University of Copenhagen in Denmark, the North Coast Cetacean Society, and Fisheries and Oceans Canada used several small drones off the coast of British Columbia. The drones were equipped with sterile petri dishes to collect the samples.

The team flew the drone through the exhaled breaths of one North pacific humpback whale (Megaptera novaeangliae kuzira) at a time, but still sampled several individual whales. The humpbacks generally took one to six breaths every time they surfaced—covering the drone and the dishes in whale snot.

The team collected 58 blow samples from 26 individual humpback whales. They also took repeated samples from the same whales, collected in different encounters and across two years to match the genomes. They then cross-referenced the genomes collected from the whale blow with genomes sequenced from traditional blubber biopsies taken from the same animals.

To keep the DNA from mixing, the team targeted the whale most upwind in the pod, since the blow downwind might mix with the blow from the whales up ahead. Resting whales were never sampled, and the team stopped the drone flights if a whale showed any reaction. More than one third of samples were collected by flying the drone directly from land—no boat required.

The DNA was sequenced to produce two types of genomes. Low-coverage nuclear genomes, which are inherited from both parents, and high-coverage mitochondrial genomes, which are inherited from the mother only.

The team found that the genetic diversity estimated from the blow samples was very similar to the estimates taken from traditional biopsies. Fortunately, the humpback population in the study shows little evidence of recent inbreeding. There is a small proportion of the genome that has long stretches of identical DNA, but the team believes that most likely reflects distant shared ancestry or past population bottlenecks.

According to the team, this research marks one of the first whole genome datasets for a group of North Pacific humpback whales. Previously, only one North Pacific humpback whole genome was publicly available.

“We will use these blow-derived genomes to not only assess population structure and health, but also assess kinship and family ties among humpback whales foraging in northern British Columbia,” Dr. Éadin O’Mahony, a study co-author and marine mammal ecologist who was at the University of St. Andrews at the time of the study, said in a statement. “The aim is to understand how their social bonds form: Are the whales that forage together family, or long-term friends?”

As expected, the North Pacific humpback whales were genetically distinct from their counterparts living in the North Atlantic. This confirms that data taken from whale blow works as well from data taken from conventional biopsies. The drone-captured genetic analyses also correctly determined every whale’s sex, and revealed three previously undescribed mitochondrial DNA variants, which could help show how some of these whales are related. The blow from whales swimming in groups also did not have any sign of contamination from neighboring whales.

About 16 percent of the DNA in each blow sample were also primarily from the microbes living in the whale’s airways. According to the team, this indicates that a single breath could tell us about the individual whale and its respiratory microbiome, opening up a potential new window into cetacean health.

That’s a whole lot of data from just a little bit of snot.

Modern physics rests on two frameworks: general relativity and quantum mechanics. General relativity describes very large things, like stars; quantum mechanics describes very small things, such as atoms. Both are astonishingly good at predicting how the universe behaves, but they’re mathematically incompatible. Finding a theory that unites them—“quantum gravity”—is one of the field’s biggest goals. Black holes are a natural testing ground, because they’re one of the few areas where both relativity and quantum mechanics matter—they pack immense mass into a very small space.

One of the most useful tools for studying quantum gravity is called holographic duality. The real universe has three dimensions of space and one of time, which relativity combines into four-dimensional spacetime. But physicists studying quantum gravity often work with simpler model universes that they can understand in more detail.

The basic idea of holographic duality is that some of these theoretical universes can be described in two ways: as a complete universe with gravity and spacetime, and in quantum terms, with no gravity at all, “written” on the universe’s outer boundary. Just as a real hologram encodes a 3D image on a 2D plane, the quantum model encodes all the information of the universe in one dimension fewer (a universe with four dimensions of space, for instance, can be fully described by a quantum theory with three). This lets physicists turn hard questions about gravity into questions about quantum physics, which are sometimes easier to answer.

The first problem Claude solved concerns what happens when you heat up one of these theoretical universes. At low temperatures, the universe fills up with a gas of particles. Above a certain temperature, a black hole forms instead. In 1983, Stephen Hawking and Don Page showed that this switch is abrupt: it’s a phase transition, similar to how water suddenly boils at 100°C. But Hawking and Page’s calculation relies on general relativity, which holds only when the universe is vast, compared with the tiny scale at which that description breaks down. As the theoretical universe shrinks toward that smaller scale, spacetime loses any definite shape and becomes blurry, and nobody knows how to do the calculation on the gravity side of the duality. On the quantum side, though, the same situation can be calculated, at least in principle.

In 1999, physicists found that there was a phase transition in that regime as well. But they didn’t know whether it was abrupt, like Hawking and Page’s, or smooth. In 2003, a group of physicists set out the calculation that would settle the question, but it was extremely complex and was never completed. Claude did it by running a small research group of copies of itself, in which it worked out almost every piece of the calculation independently and checked its math against known results. It showed that the phase transition is abrupt. The same sudden switch appears at both extremes, suggesting that black hole formation isn’t just a feature of relativity but is actually built into the underlying quantum theory.

The second problem concerns a simplified theoretical universe with two dimensions of space instead of three. This universe is straightforward enough to study in detail, but still has black holes and other mysteries similar to those of our own universe. Even in this simpler universe, physicists don’t know how to create a full theory of quantum gravity, so they approach it from the other direction: rather than building a single, correct theory, they try to rule out wrong ones by showing that they are internally inconsistent. This method is called bootstrapping.

Since 2009, physicists have used a version of this method to set a ceiling on how heavy the lightest black hole in such a universe could be. Any theory that breaks the ceiling is ruled out, and each lowering of the ceiling narrows the search. Results from numerical calculations hinted at where the best possible ceiling lay, but they couldn’t prove it, and the best proven ceiling was well short of what the numerical results suggested.

It doesn’t identify the correct theory, but it may point physicists in the right direction.

Neither result describes our own universe directly, and neither has practical applications, but both contribute to a long-running theoretical effort to understand how quantum mechanics and relativity fit together.

To understand the first problem, we need to start with something much more familiar: a walk through New York City.

Imagine that you’re heading from Penn Station to the High Line entrance on West 30th Street and 10th Avenue, and there are two equally good routes. You could walk west along West 33rd Street and turn south, or head south first and then walk west along West 30th Street. Either way, you have to pick one. In the quantum world, though, that’s not true: a particle can take both routes simultaneously.

This is most clearly demonstrated in the famous double-slit experiment. In this test, you fire particles, one at a time, at a barrier cut with two narrow slits, and record where, on a screen behind it, each one lands. Each particle lands at a particular point on the screen, as a particle should. But as you fire more of them, they build up a pattern of stripes that would only be possible if each one had passed through both slits, like a wave.

To predict what a quantum particle will do more generally, physicists have to add up every path it could possibly take, known as its possible histories. Not every history counts equally; some contribute far more to the sum than others. It’s an extravagant and mysterious way to describe how a single particle behaves, but it’s also one of the most successful ideas in physics.

This approach, known as the “sum over histories,” is well established for particles and anything made of them. But what about gravity? Einstein showed that gravity is a consequence of curved spacetime: objects with mass bend space and time around them, and we feel that bending as gravity. In a quantum theory of gravity, therefore, what gets summed over is the shape of spacetime itself, rather than just the paths of particles moving through it. To make predictions in a quantum theory of gravity, we would have to add up every shape that the universe could possibly take, and nobody yet knows how to do that. Much of modern quantum gravity research is an attempt to work it out.

Hawking and Page found a case where this sum is simple enough to handle but still interesting. To explain Hawking and Page’s work, we need to imagine a different kind of universe, called anti-de Sitter spacetime. Imagine you’re standing in a giant bowl. No matter how hard you throw an object, it will always roll back to the place you threw it from.

Our universe isn’t like that. The difference comes down to a quantity called the cosmological constant, which describes the energy of empty space. In our universe, this energy is positive, which makes space expand ever faster. In anti-de Sitter spacetime, the energy is negative, so everything is pulled back to where it started. Hawking and Page chose this universe for their calculation because of how it behaves when it heats up: the bowl keeps heat in so it can stay at a steady temperature.

At any given temperature, two histories matter far more than all the others. Here, a “history” means an entire version of the universe: a particular shape of spacetime, along with everything in it. In one, the bowl is filled with an ordinary gas of particles—including gravitons, the particles believed to carry gravity. In the other, it contains a black hole. Whichever contributes more to the sum is, in effect, what we would observe—it “wins.” At low temperatures, the gas history wins, and the universe is full of gas; at high temperatures, the black-hole history wins, and the universe contains a black hole. At one particular temperature—which physicists now call the Hawking-Page temperature—the universe switches abruptly from one to the other. This is the phase transition.

It’s a rare case where we can see two very different shapes of spacetime competing in the sum over histories, and can say which one wins.

This is where the holographic principle enters the story. In the 1970s, Jacob Bekenstein and Hawking found that the amount of information a black hole can hold is proportional to its surface area, not to its volume. That’s very different from ordinary matter, where every bit of volume holds roughly the same amount of information.

In the early 1990s, Gerard ’t Hooft and Leonard Susskind proposed that this idea applies to gravity in general—that everything inside a region of space can be fully described by the information on its boundary, just as a flat hologram can display a three-dimensional image. In 1997, Juan Maldacena formalized the idea further. Gravity inside the anti-de Sitter bowl, he argued, is equivalent to an ordinary quantum theory of particles, with no gravity at all, existing on the rim of the bowl (the universe’s outer boundary). These are the gravity side and the quantum side of the duality we described in the introduction—they’re two languages for describing the same underlying reality.

In the case Claude studied, the quantum theory on the rim is called N=4 super Yang-Mills theory. It’s related to the theories that describe how particles interact in the real universe, but it has extra symmetries that make it much easier for physicists to work with. (Claude has worked with this theory before, calculating how particles interact within it to a new level of precision.) Since the gravity side predicts two histories competing with each other, the obvious question is: does N=4 super Yang-Mills theory (the quantum side) show the same thing?

For now, physicists can analyze this theory in detail only when its particles barely interact with each other, a condition known as weak coupling. This is the opposite end of the spectrum from where Hawking and Page’s gravitational calculation works. Weak coupling on the quantum side corresponds to the small, blurry universe on the gravity side. So there was no guarantee that any of Hawking and Page’s findings would hold there.

Remarkably, they do. Bo Sundborg in 1999, and after him Ofer Aharony, Joseph Marsano, Shiraz Minwalla, Kyriakos Papadodimas, and Mark Van Raamsdonk, showed that even at weak coupling, the theory on the rim goes through a transition as it heats up. This is its counterpart of the switch from gas to black hole inside the bowl. But it was still unknown whether this transition is abrupt, like Hawking and Page’s, or smooth and gradual.

When particles barely interact, physicists have to calculate how they behave one step at a time. They start with a simplified version of the theory in which particles don’t interact at all. Then they add a series of corrections, each more precise and harder to compute than the last. Physicists call these steps loops. In 2003, Aharony and his colleagues realized that whether the phase transition was an abrupt jump, as Hawking and Page predicted, or gradual, hinged on a single number that appeared only three loops in. Computing that number involves many thousands of terms, known as diagrams—far too many to work through by hand. In 2005, the same group completed the calculation for a simpler cousin of the theory and found an abrupt transition. But the version for N=4 super Yang-Mills, more than 10 times larger, was never completed.

The scale of the computational problem, while daunting, wasn’t the only obstacle to completing the calculation. There was a conceptual issue, as well: naively adding up all the parts of the calculation gives an infinite answer, and those infinities have to be tamed carefully to extract a meaningful result.

To solve the problem, Claude ran a small research group of agents, all copies of itself. Almost every piece of the calculation was done twice, by agents acting independently. For extra consistency checks, parts of the calculation could also be compared to previous results in the literature.

The result Claude found is an abrupt jump as the temperature rises, exactly analogous to what Hawking and Page had found using the sum over spacetime histories. The emerging picture is very simple: the transition is abrupt at both weak and strong coupling—the blurry and the smooth ends, in the gravity description. The most natural possibility is that it stays abrupt everywhere in between.

This is a beautiful outcome. It shows that a calculation using the sum over histories in gravity qualitatively matches the behavior of an ordinary quantum theory with no gravity at all.

Many open questions remain. The abrupt transition Claude found is very much like the one we see whenever we boil water. One interesting feature of abrupt transitions is that the losing side of the competition can survive for a while: for instance, water can be superheated above 100°C without boiling, as you could see for yourself if you were to put a cup of extremely pure water in the microwave. Something similar happens here. Above the Hawking-Page temperature, the hot gas of gravitons and other particles can survive for a long time in a superheated state. But at a higher temperature it can no longer survive: this is another transition, the Hagedorn transition, which is a characteristic signature of string theory. On the gravity side, that transition has many interesting properties of its own, and it would be valuable to check in detail whether they also appear in N=4 super Yang-Mills theory.

Another open question concerns the other possible histories of spacetime. Hawking and Page considered three: one in which their model universe is filled with gas, one that contains a large black hole, and one containing a smaller black hole that never comes to dominate the sum over histories. In principle, there are many more possible histories. As far as anyone knows, none of them ever dominates. But given certain changes to the starting conditions—if the universe is set spinning, for instance, or given a fixed amount of energy or charge—then some of these histories do take over. Working out exactly which ones win, and when, is an interesting problem for the future.

As we saw earlier, one useful model universe has just two dimensions of space and one of time: it’s called “3D gravity” because it describes gravity in three spacetime dimensions. According to the principle of holographic duality, 3D gravity inside the anti-de Sitter bowl is equivalent to an ordinary quantum theory living on the bowl’s rim.

As a model, 3D gravity is much simpler than the real thing, but it still allows for black holes and many of the same mysteries. It provides a useful laboratory for studying the sum over spacetime histories. It’s also a good place to test the idea that spacetime isn’t a basic ingredient of the universe, but emerges from something deeper. In general relativity, black holes look smooth. But in quantum gravity, if you zoom in to the smallest scales—the Planck length, around 10-35 meters, where quantum effects on gravity become impossible to ignore—black holes are made of an enormous number of tiny, individual building blocks. Physicists believe the same is true in our own, four-dimensional universe, but the 3D version is much easier to analyze with holographic duality.

Claude’s results build on a method called the bootstrap, a general strategy for solving quantum theories. Counterintuitively, the strategy doesn’t try to solve a theory directly. For 3D gravity, that isn’t even possible, because nobody knows how to define the theory precisely. Instead, the bootstrap rules theories out by showing that they contradict themselves. Physicists scan over all possible quantum theories and try to eliminate them one by one.

Remarkably, in many cases this method makes it possible to pinpoint a single quantum theory and calculate precisely how it behaves. The requirements of relativity and quantum mechanics are so demanding, and so delicately balanced, that together they rule out every alternative. In 2012, the bootstrap was used to solve a quantum field theory called the 3D critical Ising model, a simple model of magnetism that also describes water at the point where liquid and vapor become indistinguishable. Since then, it has been applied to many different quantum systems.

In 2009, Simeon Hellerman introduced a technique called the modular bootstrap, which applies the bootstrap method to 3D gravity. Its goal is to rule out inconsistent theories of quantum gravity in three spacetime dimensions. By combining it with other bootstrap methods, physicists hope to eventually eliminate every inconsistent theory. Whatever is left must be consistent. There could be no consistent theories, or just one, or whole families of them. Any of these outcomes would be interesting, because it would help us understand what spacetime looks like at the Planck scale in this simplified universe, and give us hints about the Planck scale in our own.

Here’s how the modular bootstrap works. 3D gravity in anti-de Sitter spacetime has a quantity called the central charge (c), which roughly counts how many microscopic building blocks the universe is made of. When general relativity holds, c is very large, because large black holes contain an enormous number of building blocks. A second quantity, M₁, is the mass of the lightest black hole. To run the bootstrap, physicists scan over every possible theory, with every possible value of c and M₁, and look for inconsistencies.

To understand how that search works, we need to go back to the sum over spacetime histories. In 3D gravity, that means adding up different 3D shapes of spacetime. They can be simple, like a ball, or more complicated, like the donut and two-holed donut in the figure below. The spacetime is the inside of the shape—the dough inside the donut. Its surface, like the rim of the bowl in the earlier section, is where the quantum theory is “written,” in one fewer dimension.

The modular bootstrap starts with a spacetime shaped like an ordinary, one-holed donut, or torus. There are two different ways to interpret this shape, depending on which direction we call “time” and which we call “space.” In the first, the long direction around the hole is time and the other, shorter loop, around the tube, is space. In the second, they’re swapped: the long loop is space and the shorter loop is time.

In calculations like these, the length of the time loop sets the temperature: the shorter the loop, the hotter the universe. So swapping the two loops trades a cold universe for a hot one. The two interpretations correspond to the two competing histories that Hawking and Page studied. In 3D gravity there are no gravitons, but there is a transition similar to the one that Claude analyzed in N=4 super Yang-Mills theory.

Now we come to the crucial step. In relativity, space and time are on an equal footing. So it can’t matter which loop we label space and which we label time. As far as relativity is concerned, the two interpretations are equivalent, and the physics must be the same for every possible experiment.

Quantum mechanics, however, treats time differently from space. To calculate anything, you pick a direction to call time and track how the system evolves along it. So, when we calculate observable quantities in quantum gravity, the two interpretations of the donut lead to calculations that look very different. But in relativity, it doesn’t matter which loop is called time, so the two calculations have to give exactly the same answer.

If we knew the theory of 3D quantum gravity, we could do both calculations and confirm that they match. We don’t, but with the bootstrap method, that isn’t a problem. Physicists scan over the possible values of c (the number of building blocks) and M₁ (the mass of the lightest black hole). For each pair of values, they try to prove that the theory would be inconsistent—that the two calculations on the donut couldn’t possibly give the same answer. This involves searching for a special mathematical object called a bootstrap functional. If physicists find one, then the theory is inconsistent, and those values are ruled out.

The hope, supported by the bootstrap’s successes elsewhere, is that combining this approach with other bootstrap methods will eventually rule out most possible theories of 3D quantum gravity. The theories left standing would be the only ones consistent with both gravity and quantum mechanics. Physicists could then use them to explore some of the biggest questions in quantum gravity: is there only one theory? Does it rely on string theory, or are there alternatives? What are black holes made of at the microscopic level, and how do we sum over spacetime histories?

This strategy can work in principle, but it’s very difficult in practice, because bootstrap functionals are hard to find. What matters for quantum gravity is what happens when c is large—but the larger c gets, the harder the search becomes. In the original paper on the modular bootstrap, Hellerman proved that in any consistent theory of 3D gravity, M₁ can be no larger than c/6, up to small corrections. Theories with a heavier lightest black hole can’t possibly be consistent with both gravity and quantum mechanics.

The goal of the modular bootstrap is to push this ceiling as low as possible, pinning down how heavy the lightest black hole can be. Physicists write the ceiling as M₁ ≤ c/Λ. A lower ceiling means a larger number Λ. The results below, including Claude’s, concern the simplest and most-studied version of the method, which ignores how the black holes spin.

Over the past 15 years, physicists have worked to lower the ceiling and pin down the theory as tightly as possible. There are two ways to do this. One is analytical—building a bootstrap functional by hand, with pen-and-paper mathematics. The other is numerical—using computers to search for functionals and optimize them. The results of these numerical calculations suggested that the modular bootstrap could push the ceiling down to about c/9.1. But these calculations get harder as c grows, so they couldn’t settle the question. On the analytical side, Tom Hartman, Dalimil Mazáč, and Leonardo Rastelli proved a ceiling of c/8.5, but because this was higher than the numerical results had suggested, this clearly wasn’t the best the modular bootstrap could do. Since then, just how tightly the modular bootstrap can constrain quantum gravity has remained an open question.

Claude set about solving the problem by running numerical experiments and reproducing results from the literature. In doing so, it noticed a pattern that physicists had so far missed, involving the way the numerical ceiling shifted slightly as c grew. Based on that pattern, Claude developed a hypothesis about the behavior of the bootstrap functional, using a mathematical tool called a spectral curve. Spectral curves are well known in other areas of mathematics and physics, but had never been applied to this problem. Claude found the spectral curve that governs the modular bootstrap at large values of c. It then used it to prove that the lightest black hole in 3D quantum gravity can be no heavier than c/Λ, where Λ is given by an exact formula and is approximately 9.1266. It also proved that this ceiling is optimal: it’s the lowest this version of the modular bootstrap can ever go. Claude delivered the solution across four research notes, which it later merged into a single paper.

This is one of the first times the bootstrap has been used to derive an exact result in the regime of quantum gravity where black holes matter. It also shows that this version of the method can’t, on its own, push the ceiling down to c/12, the mass at which black holes first appear; that will take other ingredients. Although it doesn’t pin down the theory of quantum gravity in three dimensions by itself, it provides a starting point and helps to narrow the search.

These two results show Claude doing two very different kinds of research. The first is a known calculation that physicists had set aside for two decades, because the work was too long and error-prone to complete by hand. The second required a new idea: spotting a pattern others had missed and bringing in a mathematical tool from another area of physics. Both were carried out largely autonomously. Neither result settles the question of quantum gravity, or even comes close to doing so, but each pushes physics forward in a longstanding area of research.

Unlocking Stable On-Policy RL on TPU with Bitwise-Zero Train–Inference Mismatch

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Reinforcement learning (RL) for large language models is notoriously sensitive to numerical instability: even tiny discrepancies between rollout generation and gradient updates can destabilize training or stall convergence. In LLM RL, the model serves as the policy, π(action | previous tokens), and true on-policy learning requires every action in an update to be generated and evaluated by that exact same policy. While a synchronous loop ensures each rollout batch is scored using the same weights that generated it, a critical numerical requirement remains: the sampler and trainer must evaluate that policy identically.

In practice, production RL stacks run sampling and training through separate, specialized execution paths. The sampler chooses each action token during incremental decoding and records its behavior logprob. The trainer evaluates the same token in batched forward-backward passes to compute its current logprob and gradients. Under the same weights, these differing execution paths can still assign different logprobs to the same token and context. The training schedule is on-policy, but the numerical execution has introduced a hidden off-policy gap that compounds over multi-turn trajectories.

Our zero train–inference mismatch (Zero-TIM) implementation, developed in Tunix and verified at multi-host scale across 64 TPU v5p chips (DP8×TP8), closes this numerical gap. At the same weights, the sampler and trainer assign bitwise-identical logprobs to the same action and token history—bringing state-of-the-art, true on-policy stability to TPU RL training.

A reduction is a tree of floating-point additions. Floating-point addition is not associative, so changing the tree changes where rounding occurs. The effect is easier to expose in bfloat16, which retains less precision than float32.

The same issue appears in model reductions: matrix products accumulate partial sums, normalization computes statistics, and tensor-parallel shards combine partial outputs. In our original stack, sampler decode and trainer forward used different token-array shapes and forward implementations. JAX specializes computations to their input shapes, and the training forward also runs inside a differentiated program.

Across these paths, reduction order and intermediate precision were not consistently aligned. Floating-point rounding turned those arithmetic differences into different logprob bits. The weights and model equations were identical; the arithmetic was not.

The contract is simple: across sampler decode, serving prefill, and trainer forward, all three paths must produce bitwise-identical logprobs for every sampled action—comparing raw floating-point bytes under the same rollout weights, token history, and sampling settings, with zero rounding or tolerance.

Why three paths instead of two? In a decoupled RL stack, a direct sampler–trainer disagreement shows that a numerical gap exists, but not where it enters. Introducing serving prefill as a middle checkpoint turns alignment into a systematic two-step diagnostic that any RL stack can adopt:

Sampler decode vs. prefill verifies that the serving engine agrees with itself between incremental token generation and full-sequence re-scoring, while prefill vs. trainer verifies that the serving and training engines agree across compilation and differentiation boundaries.

Achieving Zero-TIM does not require merging the sampler and trainer into a single monolithic engine. Instead, we use a generalizable four-part recipe that bridges separate serving and training pipelines across multi-host TPU meshes: locking within-shard kernel accumulation, ordering cross-shard reductions, standardizing the logprob scoring tail, and wrapping serving kernels with custom differentiation rules.

Inside each tensor-parallel rank, batch-invariant Pallas kernels control accumulation order and intermediate precision. Projection kernels traverse fixed contraction blocks in order, keeping the reduction blocking independent of token-row count. Partial products accumulate in float32, with explicit output casts.

For row-parallel projections, each tensor-parallel rank produces a partial output. We separate communication from addition: ppermute transports the partials, then every rank adds them in the same source-rank order.

The reduction order is explicit in the program rather than selected by a collective implementation.

Alignment must extend beyond model logits to the probability of the sampled action. Decode, prefill, and trainer apply matching sampling transforms, use the same fixed Pallas log-softmax construction, and select the recorded action token.

Prefill and trainer score the action generated during rollout rather than choosing another action. We also preserve sampled token IDs across environment turns so that every path evaluates the same token history.

Agents are sessions, not requests: size GKE node pools for AI agent sessions

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Your company wants to move its coding agents from developer laptops to a shared GKE cluster, for persistence, scale, and governance. Each of those agent sessions stays open all day but works only part of it, and how you run them decides how much of your node bill pays for waiting.

By the end of this article, you will know why an agent session needs a different runtime than a web request, how Agent Substrate and AX run sessions on GKE, and how to identify cost optimization opportunities in your GKE node pools. This article assumes you know Kubernetes Pods and node pools. If not, check out the GKE overview.

A web request lasts milliseconds. The server answers it, keeps nothing, and serves the next one, so Kubernetes scales a web app by its traffic: more requests, more Pods.

An agent session works another way. A coding agent's workspace can stay open for the developer's whole workday: it keeps its memory and its files between prompts, and it works only after a prompt arrives.

How much of that day does the agent really work? Much less than it waits. In a study of GitHub Copilot agent traces, Banruo Liu and colleagues found that in a typical session with several prompts, the agent waits for the user about 80% of the time. A turn, one prompt and the agent's whole response to it, takes 396.3 seconds of agent work on average.

Put those numbers on one developer's day. With 2 prompts an hour over an 8 hour workday, which is our assumption, the agent works 22% of the day and waits for the other 78%. If each session holds a machine, you pay for that 78%.

Teams usually pick one of two answers, and both work well for web apps.

The first is to give each session its own Pod. The session keeps its state, but its Pod holds 1 vCPU and 1 GiB all day. For example, a c3-standard-4 node fits 3 such Pods after GKE's reservations, so in our cost model 100 coding agents need 34 nodes and about USD 1,288 a month, for agents that work 22% of the time.

The second is to scale the Pod to zero between prompts. The node is free, and files on a persistent volume survive, but the process and its memory do not, so the next prompt starts a new agent process.

Based on that, you want a third option: keep the session, and free the machine while the session waits.

Agent Substrate and AX are built for that: the developer's session stays open, and the sandbox that serves it gives its machine back while the session waits. Together they cover the parts of an agentic platform that are the same for every team: the control plane, the runtime, and the sandbox. Agent Substrate answers where and how an agent runs. AX answers what the agent is.

Following the CNCF donation of gVisor, Agent Substrate, and OpenRL in November 2026, you have the flexibility to run this open source, agentic platform across any environment. Agent Substrate 1.0 is generally available on GKE.

One distinction helps from here on. The session is the multi turn interaction between the developer and the agent, with its conversation history. The compute that serves it is an isolated sandbox: an actor in Agent Substrate, or a Task in AX. Parking acts on the sandbox, and the session stays open.

Agent Substrate answers where and how an agent runs. It separates the stateful agent from the compute it runs on. An actor is the sandbox for one agent, and it holds the agent's running processes, files, and identity. A worker is a reusable sandbox drawn from a warm pool of Kubernetes Pods, and one worker hosts several actors.

An agent runs code that a model wrote, so anything in its environment can leak through a prompt injection or a bad command, API keys included. Gateways control the actor's inbound and outbound traffic and add credentials on the way out, based on the actor's identity, so the agent never holds the keys.

SuspendActor snapshots an idle actor to object storage and frees its worker, and the next request restores the actor on any warm worker. Using this approach, we can achieve higher density by multiplexing more agents to run on the same amount of compute.

AX answers what the agent is and what it knows. You declare a Task, which says what runs (image, command, and limits), and a Workspace, which says what the agent knows (repositories and files). AX runs each Task as an actor. ax suspend parks the task and keeps its /workspace. Today AX asks for a volume snapshot, so the files come back on resume and the agent's processes start fresh (runner guide).

AX passes suspend, resume, and delete to Agent Substrate. The AX roadmap adds budgets, policies, and gateways, which limit what an agent may do, and a record of what each task did.

Two details decide the bill before you size anything.

First, you decide when a sandbox parks. Agent Substrate does not suspend an idle actor by itself (#424), so your agent harness, or the service in front of it, calls the suspend API after an idle timeout. Until then, an idle actor keeps its worker. When idle actors hold every worker, a request for another actor waits up to 5 seconds by default, then fails with HTTP 503 (Agent Substrate docs).

Second, you decide how much CPU each sandbox is promised. An actor is sized by its CPU and memory limits, and each worker books those limits against the capacity you declare for its pool (API guide). Our cost model sets that capacity to what the node really has, so every actor keeps its full limit. You can declare more capacity than the node has, and then you overcommit CPU at the pool instead of in each Pod.

Our cost model prices parking for the same 100 coding agents, at Google Cloud list prices in us-central1.

With a 2 minute idle timeout, each session holds a worker 29% of its open time. A warm pool sized for bursts (the 99th percentile of sessions holding a worker at once, if sessions act independently, plus 15% headroom) needs 15 nodes. Add one node that runs around the clock for the Agent Substrate control plane and its PostgreSQL database, and a job that scales the pool to zero after hours:

Parking saves 40% of the node bill. The pool is full about once in 3,500 turns, so a prompt rarely meets that 503. In an hour at 2.5 prompts instead of 2, the same 15 nodes are full for 3% of turns, so size the pool for your busiest hour.

Keep in mind that parking pays most when your agents wait the most. At 2 prompts an hour, the saving is 40%. At 4 prompts an hour, the agent works 44% of the hour, the pool grows to 27 nodes, and the saving is 4%. Around 4.5 prompts an hour, one Pod per session becomes the cheaper option, so measure your prompt rate first.

Also, the pool size is yours to set. The autoscaling option in the GKE install guide follows worker Pods, not suspended actors, so scale the pool to zero after hours with a scheduled job: a pool that runs all 730 hours of the month costs about twice the one Pod per session bill.

Finally, the fleet size matters. The Agent Substrate control plane costs the same for 10 sessions or 1,000, so parking pays from about 37 coding sessions, or 13 for an assumed chat assistant that works about a minute an hour.

Overcommit changes the comparison. If your Pods request less CPU than their limit, Kubernetes places them by their requests. A Pod that requests 0.25 vCPU with a 1 vCPU limit packs 12 to a node, so the same 100 coding agents fit on 9 nodes, fewer than our parked pool. Agent Substrate can overcommit too, through the capacity you declare on the worker pool, and our cost model does not price that case.

Overcommit has the same cost on both sides: agents share CPU and slow down when their neighbors are busy, during builds and tests. Compare like with like before you decide to park: Pods and actors with the same CPU guarantee.

Back to the shared cluster. Agents are sessions, not requests: size the warm pool for the sessions that hold a worker, not the sessions that are open.

We would park long lived sessions that wait most of the hour, and keep one Pod per session when developers prompt more than about 4 times an hour. Size the warm pool for working time plus a short idle timeout at your busiest hour, and compare with your current Pods at the same CPU guarantee before you park.

The New Year is just around the corner, and it is a common time for people to revisit self-improvement-related goals. If you want to reduce or stop drinking alcohol, you may be wondering if switching to non-alcoholic drinks could be helpful. As it turns out, for some people, non-alcoholic drinks could be a helpful substitute for alcoholic beverages, but there are also reasons to be cautious about this overall approach.

Before reading this post, if you are not already familiar with non-alcoholic drinks, take a look at my recent post, “Is there alcohol in non-alcoholic drinks?” The short answer is that the amount of alcohol depends on where you live. Generally, non-alcoholic drinks contain much lower alcohol by volume (ABV) than traditional alcoholic drinks, but they may still contain some alcohol. If you are trying to stop drinking alcohol completely, it would be important to check the rules where you live to make sure your chosen non-alcoholic drink has 0% ABV.

There are many reasons why someone may want to stop or reduce drinking alcoholic drinks, which I will not go over here. More relevant to this topic are the reasons why someone would want to consume non-alcoholic drinks, which are made to look and taste like alcoholic beverages, rather than traditional beverages that do not resemble alcoholic beverages (e.g., soft drinks, juices, water).

One reason for the popularity of non-alcoholic drinks is that someone may enjoy the taste of alcoholic drinks, like beer and wine, and wish to continue to drink similarly flavoured beverages (without the risks associated with consuming alcohol). Another reason people may choose non-alcoholic drinks is that they find it helps them continue to participate in social activities and routines that typically involve alcohol. For instance, when interviewing adults who drink alcohol heavily and have used non-alcoholic drinks to reduce drinking, some interviewees described that ordering a non-alcoholic beverage at a bar, club, or other social gathering allowed them to participate in the outing and blend in with their peers who were drinking alcohol.1 Further, unlike drinking alternatives like soft drinks, they describe that the experience of drinking a non-alcoholic drink remains symbolic as an "adult treat" given its look and presentation.

People may take different approaches to substitute with non-alcoholic drinks depending on their specific needs. Some people may completely substitute their alcoholic drinks for non-alcoholic drinks. Alternatively, people may replace alcoholic drinks in specific contexts, such as primarily substituting their home-based alcoholic drinks versus outing-based alcoholic drinks with non-alcoholic drinks.1 When replacing alcoholic drinks one typically consumes, some may replace them completely with non-alcoholic drinks while others may alternate between drink types (sometimes referred to as “zebra striping”).

While people describe using non-alcoholic drinks to reduce their alcohol use, an important question remains about whether these drinks can actually help you stop or reduce drinking. As it turns out, the qualities that make non-alcoholic drinks an attractive substitute for alcoholic drinks may also get in the way of successfully reducing one’s drinking. Aspects of drinking (beyond alcohol itself) can serve as powerful reminders—referred to as cues—of alcohol. Specifically, qualities of the drink like its taste, container, and where it is consumed can, in fact, lead to a strong urge to drink alcohol itself. By definition, non-alcoholic beverages will not lead to intoxication. Once consumed, these drinks may strengthen the desire to become intoxicated (rather than quench that desire), and lead someone to return to drinking alcohol.

Initial studies have suggested that non-alcoholic drinks may help people reduce their drinking. When adults who drink were provided non-alcoholic drinks free of charge over a 12-week period, they reported greater reductions in their alcoholic drink consumption compared to adults who were not provided these drinks.2 This reduction in alcoholic drinks continued a month after these non-alcoholic drinks were provided. A follow-up study suggested, however, that the reduced alcohol drinking was not observed amongst people with higher symptoms of alcohol use disorder.3 Additional research is needed to understand if non-alcoholic drinks may be a helpful way to reduce or stop drinking among people with an alcohol use disorder.

Ultimately, there would be a net health and mental health benefit if someone reduces the overall number of alcoholic drinks by substituting non-alcoholic drinks. For instance, if someone switches from drinking four alcoholic drinks per week to two alcoholic drinks plus two non-alcoholic drinks, they would be reducing their exposure to harmful aspects of alcohol. However, depending on an individual’s circumstances, the chosen strategy for which drinks to substitute can affect how much they benefit overall. For instance, if a person finds they have more difficulty moderating their drinking when out with friends (versus at home), having a strategy to rely on non-alcoholic drinks when out with friends may be more beneficial than substituting their alcohol drinks consumed at home.

While research is still in its early stages, non-alcoholic drinks show promise in helping people reduce their drinking. For some people, substituting non-alcoholic drinks may provide the benefits of drinking alcoholic drinks without the negatives; however, it is important to make sure the substitute approach does not become an add-on approach. In cases where non-alcoholic drinks are too similar to alcoholic drinks that it leads you to drink alcohol anyway, then the substitution approach is not successful. Therefore, if you are thinking about giving this strategy a try, it is important to keep track of your drinking carefully to determine if it is actually helping you to reduce the number of alcoholic drinks you consume.

Guy Ritchie & Jason Statham's Best Movie Together Is Officially Streaming For Free

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Guy Ritchie is a beloved director with an expansive filmography. He's arguably best known for his crime stories and stylish approach to storytelling. Those crime tales feature larger-than-life characters, with slick writing that's often endlessly quotable.

Lock, Stock & Two Smoking Barrels is a great example of the formula, and feels like the blueprint for several other Guy Ritchie projects. The movie is about four friends who lose a rigged poker game. They end up owing a massive gambling debt to Hatchet Harry (P.H. Moriarty), with just a week to pay it back.

While the movie does focus on how the friends intend to pay off their debt, Ritchie takes the time to flesh out so many characters. Rory Breaker (Vas Blackwood), for example, plays more of a supporting role, but is also responsible for one of the funniest speeches in the entire movie.

Lock, Stock & Two Smoking Barrels is also a remarkable project for Jason Statham, as it was his feature film debut. Since then, Statham has appeared in numerous other Guy Ritchie movies, along with dozens of other projects with other directors. He's widely regarded as a modern action star, and Snatch, arguably his best movie directed by Ritchie, is now free to stream on Pluto TV.

Jason Statham played Bacon in Lock, Stock & Two Smoking Barrels. He is a main character and one of the four friends who lost in Hatchet Harry's poker game. Statham also stars in Ritchie's 2000 crime caper, Snatch. He plays Turkish, an illegal boxing promoter working alongside his close friend, Tommy (Stephen Graham).

The movie has the same storytelling style as Lock, Stock & Two Smoking Barrels, with several tales intertwining into something far grander. Turkish and Tommy run into trouble when their prizefighter is injured. Unfortunately for the duo, they had a fight arranged with another promoter and feared crime boss, Brick Top (Alan Ford). He's furious the fight can no longer take place, and fully capitalizes on the situation.

Turkish and Tommy are at the center of the story, but they are just one part of what makes Snatch a classic. Vinnie Jones was also in Lock, Stock & Two Smoking Barrels, and returns as Bullet Tooth Tony. His story is only loosely connected to the illegal boxing plot, but he remains an unforgettable character, responsible for some ridiculously over-the-top speeches.

Lock, Stock & Two Smoking Barrels is an adored crime caper. It has such a well-crafted story that even non-crime fans will find something to enjoy, but Snatch is arguably even better. The smaller stories in Snatch fit together masterfully, and while Turkish and Tommy are the main characters, they're only part of what makes the movie so charming.

Another major element of Snatch is a diamond heist that occurs at the start of the movie. The diamond ends up in London, but its arrival also draws the attention of mobsters like Boris the Blade (Rade Sherbedgia). The dialogue throughout the entire movie is arguably wittier and funnier than its predecessor, and no character in the extensive cast feels underutilized.

There are plenty of serious moments, but some of the sharp-tongued exchanges are exceptional. Brick Top, in particular, is a treasure trove of quotes, and some of his speeches are arguably among the best quotes in Guy Ritchie's entire filmography.

Lock, Stock, and Two Smoking Barrels will always deserve a special mention for its originality. Snatch feels like it builds on the proven formula. The story is more complex, yet it's not convoluted. Many of the characters are wonderfully grandiose, and somehow still convincing. The soundtrack will always be a personal preference, but several music choices really are perfect in Snatch. This is particularly true for the climactic boxing match, as "Fin' in the Bushes" by Oasis leads us nicely to one of the greatest characters in the entire movie.

Without delving too deep into spoilers, Brad Pitt plays Mickey, a wild traveler in Snatch. Turkish sends Tommy to meet Mickey and complete a transaction for a new caravan. The deal goes south, but the duo discovers that Mickey is actually a bare-knuckle boxing champion. They want him to fight for them, but he's unpredictable and will only play along for a price.

Pitt's Mickey is unforgettable in Snatch. He certainly looks tough with his shirt off, but he steals every scene he's in with some of the funniest dialogue in the entire movie. His accent is over the top, and he's purposefully difficult to understand.

Best of all, Pitt seems to be enjoying playing the character. His mannerisms are believable for such an over-the-top character, and he's so likable because audiences never know what he'll do next. Mickey also becomes embroiled in Turkish and Tommy's situation with Brick Top, and his chemistry with the rest of the cast is exceptional. He's arguably the biggest star in the movie, with an incredible screen presence, and a perfect actor for the role.

Since Lock, Stock & Two Smoking Barrels, Jason Statham has become a staple of sorts in Guy Ritchie movies. Statham starred in 2005's Revolver after Snatch. He also starred in Wrath of Man in 2021, a notably more serious, gritty revenge movie, wherein Statham plays H, a mysterious man of few words who starts working for a security truck company. It's a high-risk job, but H proves deadly efficient. He also has a unique motivation for joining that we learn about as the story unfolds.

Most recently, Statham teamed up with Ritchie for 2023's Operation Fortune: Ruse de Guerre. The movie is a spy thriller, starring Statham as Orson, an elite agent. The story is notably less complex than Snatch, for example, but still boasts a brilliant cast, including Josh Hartnett and Aubrey Plaza. Hugh Grant is also unforgettable as Greg, a powerful arms dealer.

Audiences who enjoy Ritchie and Statham's collaborations will have to wait until January 2028 for their next fix. Details on their upcoming movie are scarce, but there are already some details about Viva La Madness to get excited about. Statham is reportedly the lead, and Vinnie Jones is also part of the cast alongside Camila Mendes and Jason Isaacs.

Interestingly, Viva La Madness is a crime novel written by J.J. Connolly. That novel is also a sequel to Layer Cake, which had a movie adaptation in 2004, starring Daniel Craig. Both novels are about a London criminal called X, though it's unclear whether the upcoming Viva La Madness movie will be connected to Layer Cake. Regardless, the plot and setting alone should be enough to get fans of Snatch and Lock, Stock & Two Smoking Barrels excited, especially given Jason Stathan and Guy Ritchie's involvement.

Crystal Palace are Thomas Frank's third London club after previous spells with Brentford and Tottenham

"When the time is right I will look forward to my return as a manager, ready to embrace the job with great energy and dedication," said Thomas Frank in June.

Former boss Pierre Sage was sacked last week after just 16 league and cup games.

The role offers Frank an opportunity to rebuild his reputation after he was sacked by Tottenham in February after eight months.

Frank described it as "an honour" to be "in charge of this fantastic football club" as Palace announced his arrival.

"Crystal Palace is an iconic club in England and in the Premier League," Frank told Palace's website.

"I will do everything I possibly can to help and support the players and everyone around the place, and I'm really looking forward to meeting them."

Palace chairman Steve Parish said Frank was the "perfect fit for our club".

But how big is the challenge at Selhurst Park that awaits the man who made his name at Brentford?

"It's going to be a bit of a shock for Thomas Frank," former West Ham and England goalkeeper Rob Green tells BBC Sport.

"He had his success at Brentford - a very well-run club where players are brought in with a lot of thinking and planning to take over the players they sell for profit.
