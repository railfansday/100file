Danielle Frankel Reimagines the 21st Century Bridal Store

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

After nearly 10 years in business, fashion designer Danielle Frankel Hirsch has opened a 12,000-square-foot location in Tribeca with interiors set to change the bridal game. To create the new space, which opened in early November, Frankel Hirsch worked with AD PRO Directory designer Augusta Hoffman and Studio Partners Architecture.

The nearly two-year design process is complete, and the Danielle Frankel bridal salon is now ready for appointments. “We’re finally at the point where my heart and my head have been,” says Frankel Hirsch, who worked with Hoffman on her previous Los Angeles boutique and New York City atelier. “Because this is our third space together, we can almost speak in shorthand now.”

Frankel Hirsch on the staircase wearing a shirt she designed from Fortuny fabric. Hoffman used the same Fortuny print throughout the space for wrapping side tables, keepsake boxes on the shelves, as well as for the woven seats on barstools.

The unassuming entrance to Danielle Frankel on Hudson Street

In the reception room just inside the street entrance, the desk is custom, the chairs are antique, and the custom pendant is by Jamb.

Hoffman worked with the artist Miriam Ellner to create eglomise paneling surrounding the staircase. “The eglomise is one of my favorite things because when we were working with the artist we worked so intimately with her,” Frankel Hirsch says. “We worked on the placement the way that we do our laces. We kept going back and forth with the drawings because I wanted it to feel like our lace placement.”

On a recent morning, a bride and her wedding party were busy in the VIP suite making a final selection. The beaming group entered the space through an unassuming brown storefront entrance on Harrison Street, its two large glass windows encased in a sheer linen. From there they ascended a staircase up to a large gallery with over 30-foot floor-to-ceiling windows overlooking the streets below on two sides, and a hand-painted mural pattern above on the ceiling.

The interiors of the original 19th-century Queen Anne–style red brick building were transformed by Hoffman (full disclosure: I am writing Hoffman’s design monograph publishing with Rizzoli in Fall 2027) into a modern, elegant bridal boutique that honors the history of the original New York Mercantile Exchange, first established in the 1880s to keep the standards of the grocer business. “We wanted the space to stay pretty bright and focus on the architecture, but we needed a lot of seating,” Hoffman says. “There was a lot of pressure because we wanted it to feel like Danielle and we wanted to leave our mark on the space, but it’s also so beautiful as is and we didn’t want to have too many interventions that take away from that.”

The rug, vintage Dutch leather armchairs, and custom clothing racks create one of the four seated zones in the gallery area. The wall features antique mottled glass windows. “We wanted this wall to feel historically accurate,” Hoffman says.

The custom oak screen features shelves for displaying flower girl dress by Danielle Frankel. The shelves are lined in silver leaf. The antique stool is reupholstered in a Fortuny fabric.

The custom window panels are by Augusta Hoffman Studio for Wovn Home and the custom curtain hardware are bronze leaves Hoffman. Hoffman pulled the leaf design from the details on the columns.

What previously functioned as a large, open space with a small mezzanine, had little existing infrastructure–no electrical, HVAC, or plumbing—and is now an area where brides can plot as they make the most important decision for their wedding day. In the gallery, Hoffman created distinct zones where wedding parties wait before their appointment in a private fitting room.

Much of the furniture was sourced by Hoffman and Frankel Hirsch together on trips to Paris. Antique glass vitrines separate the space, which they included from the previous New York gallery and filled with Frankel Hirsch’s own family wedding photos as well as printed details brides can use for their fitting. One vitrine has a board layered with references of crinoline and interlining. Another has custom Danielle Frankel cufflinks. An oak folding screen lines one half of the room, with flower girl dresses set on shelves lined in silver leaf paper. Custom parchment and cerused oak racks Hoffman designed to withstand the weight of a wedding dress line the floor displaying roughly 65 dresses from Danielle Frankel collections.

The reflection of the wall is seen in the mirrored structure designed to house the elevator bank and stairwell to the street entrance. A framed antique floral plaster relief is flanked with 1930s Italian wall sconces in patinated glass and metal.

The staircase and elevator bank within the mirrored structure. “We had to add the elevator for function and for code, and we were like ‘How do we take this existing architecture and put an elevator in the middle of the room?’” Hoffman says. “It feels super unexpected, versus trying to hide it, and you get to see more of the surrounding architecture and light in the reflection.”

In the center they constructed a cascading grand staircase, cleverly concealing the stockroom underneath, which leads upstairs to three private bridal suites. The hallways, painted in Wenge by Benjamin Moore, lead to the bridal suites, each designed differently and filled with antique and vintage furniture, custom carpets, lighting, and racks. The walls are painted in Presidente Roman Clay by Portola Paints, an earthy contrast to the white and ivory dresses, which are naturally the center of attention for visitors in each room.

When guests are finished in their suites, there’s a bar in the gallery space where wedding parties can sit and make the final selection. The wedding party that morning had selected the dress and were toasting champagne to celebrate. “It’s just as much about the hospitality and the warmth as it is about the gowns,” Frankel Hirsch says.

Robert Pattinson's Batman Movie Franchise Is Officially Finished

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

No, I didn’t forget about The Dark Knight Rises

With Robert Pattinson confirmed to be the next James Bond, one of the biggest takeaways is that Matt Reeves’ The Batman series will likely end with The Batman: Part II. Pattinson is signed on for three films as Bond, and while that’s one less film than the four-film deal that Amazon MGM Studios was seeking for the other Bond contenders, it’s pretty hard to imagine that Pattinson will juggle the role of 007 with an ongoing commitment to DC — much less Battinson’s integration into the wider DCU, which some DC fans had hoped for. To be clear, nothing has been confirmed about the status of a third The Batman movie, but the latest news combined with the extreme delays facing The Batman Part II feel like a pretty clear indication that the Batmobile has hit a dead end and will need yet another reboot.

However, the loss is even bigger than you might realize, as this will be the third time in a row that Batman fans will be robbed of a good Batman trilogy.

Tim Burton’s 1989 film Batman, of course, revolutionized the character of Batman and comic book movies as a whole. Before then, the general public’s awareness of Batman was mostly associated with the campy, comedic Adam West Batman, but Burton changed that and presented a dark, heavily stylized version starring Michael Keaton. He then followed it up with Batman Returns, which many still consider to be one of the best Batman films ever.

Warner Bros., however, felt it was too dark and got rid of Burton for the third film, replacing him with Joel Schumacher. Keaton followed Burton out the door, not wanting to continue as Batman without him. Batman Forever, starring Val Kilmer, was the result, and while that film has its defenders, it felt like a different franchise and a different Batman altogether. The same can be said for Batman and Robin.

Then in 2005 came Batman Begins, directed by Christopher Nolan and starring Christian Bale. Taking a far more grounded approach to the character, Batman Begins turned Batman’s origin into an epic film saga, where it had only been relegated to brief flashbacks in previous films. Nolan topped himself with The Dark Knight by putting Batman in the center of a gritty crime drama with Heath Ledger’s Oscar-winning turn as the Joker. For a second time, a Batman film revolutionized comic book films. Nearly 20 years later, many still cite The Dark Knight as the greatest comic book movie ever made.

Unfortunately, Nolan’s third Batman film wasn’t nearly as good as the first two. Perhaps because of Ledger’s untimely death before the release of The Dark Knight, Nolan at first seemed disinterested in a third film, and it is generally understood that he had to rework the story he had in mind to remove the Joker from its narrative. Regardless of the reasons why, The Dark Knight Rises is a deeply uneven, disorganized film that most view as the low point of Nolan’s Batman, if not an outright bad film (I tend to think it is quite bad).

After Nolan’s trilogy concluded, Ben Affleck became the next Batman, in both Batman v. Superman: Dawn of Justice as well as the various versions of Justice League. Regardless of what you think of these films — I personally find them insufferable — they were deeply divisive. Despite an Affleck-led Batman film being rumored for a time, we never got him in any standalone films, much less three of them.

In 2022, The Batman offered new hope. While some were skeptical of Pattinson as Batman, he proved to be a really solid take on the Dark Knight, which prioritized Batman’s skills as a detective, something missing from previous Batman films. He was complemented by Paul Dano’s frightening take as the Riddler and Colin Farrell’s scene-stealing Penguin.

Reeves also presented the best-ever take on Gotham City. While Burton’s Gotham was bold and interesting, neither of his films made Gotham feel like a real city. Nolan’s Gotham, with its unconcealed usage of Chicago and New York City, had little personality at all. But Reeves’ Gotham felt like a broken city that desperately needed a Batman to come to its rescue, with production combining parts of Chicago and Scotland to give the city a more naturalistic, gothic vibe. The film’s nearly three-hour runtime only assisted in the viewer’s immersion into Reeves’ Gotham.

But three hours in Reeves’ Gotham still wasn’t enough. The Batman Part II, which seems to be telling a story with Two-Face as a villain, was delayed for years due to a personal issue going on with Matt Reeves. Filming finally began in June, but in early October, Reeves had to step away, most likely for the same family issue. Steven Soderbergh then stepped in to finish the filming Reeves began, which is currently ongoing. While I applaud Reeves for prioritizing his family over a film, this means that it will be six years between The Batman and its sequel and it's hard to imagine that Skydance, the new owners of DC Studios, will want to keep Batman on the shelf for that long again, nor do I think they’ll want to schedule around Pattinson’s Bond films.

And, while it never seemed like a real possibility, I was still hoping that Pattinson would get folded into what James Gunn and Peter Safran had been building for the DCU if only because I don’t think it's all that easy to keep finding great guys to be Batman, and while he wasn’t quite as good as Bale — or even Keaton — Pattinson truly is/was a great Batman. It’s just too bad that his take on the character will be cut short before we get a proper trilogy.

I love Final Fantasy 14. I can also recognise that for anyone trying to start the game in the big 2026, it's incredibly daunting. Being faced with A Realm Reborn and five entire expansions' worth of story is a lot, and the question of how to funnel new players into Eorzea in a more approachable manner while still respecting the MMO's heavily narrative-driven vibe has been an ongoing debate for the last several years.

We know, at the very least, that director and producer Naoki Yoshida has been thinking about ways to make that barrier to entry a little less frightening—he said as much during Final Fantasy 14's Berlin Fan Festival—but according to a recent Letter from the Producer, it turns out we could've actually had something as recently as August.

"The idea of letting players freely choose a starting point for their adventure has been discussed, with the goal being to offer players a range of choices, rather than prescribe their experience according to my philosophy," Yoshida wrote.

In case you're not familiar with Yoshida's whole stance on this, he's long been staunchly protective of Final Fantasy 14's narrative integrity. Story skips are locked to the cash shop and, up until now, never included the current expansion. He's repeatedly expressed the fact that he's not too keen on letting people jump in wherever they feel, since it kinda mucks up the decade-plus worldbuilding and character relationships the game has built.

But, like many things he's been opposed to introducing in the game like unlocked glamour, he's letting up for the sake of allowing players more agency in how they engage with the game.

Anyway, back to letting people tackle its immense story a little easier. "With New Game+ working smoothly, this approach could work," Yoshida surmised. "In fact, we'd been laying groundwork to make this a possibility in conjunction with the release of the Nintendo Switch 2 version, but ultimately decided to postpone this initiative."

I do wonder specifically what made them stop—whether there was a hiccup somewhere, or whether Yoshida simply decided now wasn't the right time. He also expresses concern about a mismatch in player skill between those who jump ahead and those who've been around the block. "Even if you could start adventuring from a point of your choosing, the time and effort required to master a job remain a constant," he wrote. "We also need to avoid situations where both new and veteran players are left confused when matched together."

Physical Therapists Share the Most Effective QL Stretches and Exercises for Runners With Lower Back and Hip Pain

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Here’s how to rehab this deep lower back muscle at home—and when it’s time to book a session with your PT.

Most runners are familiar with the big muscles involved in running, such as the quads, hamstrings, and glutes. But there are lesser-known—yet still important—players that can influence running performance. One example: the quadratus lumborum, or QL for short.

A deep trunk muscle, the QL attaches to the lower spine and the back side of the pelvis, Nicole Haas, PT, DPT, a physical therapist and founder of Boulder Physiolab in Colorado, tells Runner’s World. It serves a few different functions in daily life, such as helping you arch your back, bend to the side, and rotate your upper body, Haas explains.

When you run, the QL plays more of a stabilizing role, working to control your pelvis and trunk while you stride, says Spencer Agnew, PT, DPT, a physical therapist at Sword Health and run coach with Wisconsin-based Ascent Endurance Group. The QL also assists with breathing, specifically when you inhale, Agnew adds.

If this muscle is tight or irritated, you’ll likely feel pain in your back or sacroiliac joints, which link the pelvis and lower spine, Agnew says. To help you avoid these aches, we asked the PTs to explain what can cause an angry QL and how to relieve it.

There are several reasons your QL might be yelling at you. A common one? Your hip flexors are tight. In particular, when the psoas (a big, rope-like hip flexor muscle that runs from your lower back to the front of your hip) is shortened, it can pull your pelvis into an anterior tilt, meaning your pelvis is tipped forward, arching your lower back. In many cases, this yanks on your QL as well, because the psoas and QL both insert into the same group of vertebrae in your lower back, Agnew explains.

Another culprit is weak glutes. When your glutes aren’t strong enough to help you maintain good running mechanics, your knee can collapse inward as you land and your opposite-side hip can dip down, Haas explains. This, in turn, can cause your trunk to side bend and rotate, thus placing more strain on your QL over time. Running downhill can exacerbate the problem, Haas adds, because that can cause your lower back to arch and place further stress on your QL.

Yet another cause of an irritated QL is upper-back stiffness. Restrictions in the upper part of the spine can shift more workload onto the QL, Haas explains. So can issues in your shoulders, hips, and mid-back. If these areas aren’t moving like they should due to pain or injury, your QL may receive added stress, Haas says.

Keep in mind that in some cases, your QL may hurt because it’s taking on a bunch of extra stress it’s not meant to, but the muscle itself probably isn’t tight. “It’s more like your QL is just irritated because everything has been collapsing around it and loading it,” Haas says. “It’s overworked and underappreciated.”

A tight-feeling QL is worth addressing because it can lead to a few not-so-great issues if left unchecked. A big one? Compromised running mechanics.

A tight QL, as mentioned, can shorten your hip flexors and pull your pelvis into an anterior tilt, which will reduce your knee lift as you stride, Agnew says. As a result, “you’re not going to be able to generate as much force moving forward, and you’ll have more of an arched-back position when you run,” he explains. In many cases, this can deliver a double whammy of reduced performance and increased back pain, he says.

On top of that, a tight QL can also hinder your breathing. As mentioned above, one of the QL’s jobs is to help you inhale, so when this muscle is tight, you may not be able to breathe as deeply or easily, Agnew says.

Plagued by what you think may be an irritated QL? Try Agnew’s three-step at-home series to help reduce your discomfort.

Perform this sequence as often as every day if you’re in significant discomfort that’s limiting your ability to run. In the case of minor pain, incorporate this series two to three times per week. On the days you run, perform the myofascial release, pelvic tilts, and hip hikes before you lace up, then tackle the other components afterward.

This can help turn down the pain response in a barking QL by desensitizing soft tissue, Agnew explains.

The pelvic tilts help you learn how to coordinate and control pelvic motion so you can maintain proper mechanics while running, and the hip hikes help activate your QL. The kettlebell side bend increases blood flow to the muscle, which can aid in healing. Finally, the suitcase carry increases strength, stability, and engagement of the muscle, which can ensure it functions properly during a run.

Do these moves daily or several times per day to mobilize your lower back. Perform them before or after a run. Important caveat: If your QL hurts more after stretching—or if your QL pain returns soon after stretching—then stretches are likely not the solution and you should stop doing them, Haas explains.

To start, do one set of these moves daily to help your body learn the neuromuscular patterns. (The caveat: If you feel sore, then skip a day.) After about six weeks, do these moves two to three times per week as part of your regular strength-training routine.

Jiff 1.0: A datetime library for Rust

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Jiff is a datetime library for Rust that encourages you to jump into the pit of success. Jiff automatically handles many common problems related to daylight saving time by putting time zone handling front and center into the API. Jiff has already seen widespread adoption in the Rust ecosystem.

Jiff is pronounced like “gif” with a soft “g,” as in “gem.”

This blog announces the Jiff 1.0 release by giving a tour of its API.

Target audience: Rust programmers who need convenient datetime handling.

Jiff is already used in production in many projects: uv, Cargo, Starship, fd, Nushell, mise, Jujutsu, uutils/coreutils and even as the basis for a Temporal implementation in AWS LLRT. It is time to call it 1.0.

Unless some significant defect is found in Jiff’s API, my plan is to commit to Jiff 1.0 indefinitely. This is important because the intent is for Jiff to be an ecosystem building block that appears in the public API of other libraries. This makes Jiff an attractive public dependency, which means semver incompatible releases are likely to cause significant pain in the ecosystem. They will not be made lightly.

To see a list of breaking changes between Jiff 0.2 and Jiff 1.0, please consult the CHANGELOG.

In programming, a datetime library’s core functionality rests at the intersection of a computer system’s concept of time and a human’s concept of time. The prevailing notion of system time is Unix time. A human’s concept of time is far more complex, but the most popular such model is the Gregorian calendar coupled with either a 12-hour or 24-hour clock. At the core of every datetime library rests some routine for converting between these concepts.

A datetime library typically provides much more than this. Indeed, datetime handling has historically been very difficult. My own belief is that the difficulty comes from poor modeling of the domain. Instead of encapsulating the complexity inside a reusable bundle of code, many datetime libraries pass some or all of the complexity on to its users. Jiff instead tries hard to encapsulate the complexity and make it difficult for users to do something wrong. (Jiff is not the first to do this!)

We’ll wrap up by briefly discussing motivation and inspiration.

Whenever I needed datetime handling in Rust, I always got confused. Should I use chrono or time? How do I get my local time from a timestamp? How do I use my system’s time zone database? How do I do calendar arithmetic?

Before I started working on Jiff, this confusion got annoying enough for me to consider writing my own crate. I felt like the APIs of both chrono and time could be improved upon. I personally found the domain quite interesting and so set out to build something better.

Why not improve on an existing solution, like chrono or time, instead of adding yet another choice into the ecosystem? I do think there are material downsides of having too many choices, but I had concluded that both chrono and time (in 2024) had reached a local maximum. Moreover, in 2024, I was not an expert in datetime handling. It took building a datetime library to become one. So convincing crate maintainers to go in a radically different direction would have been a tall order.

Jiff’s design and comparison documentation contain much more detail regarding the motivation and specific examples between Jiff, chrono and time.

Jiff is heavily inspired by Temporal, which is JavaScript’s standard library solution for date and time management. OpenAI released a study on November 16, 2026, covering 580,186 scientific messages from 1,819 academic scientists and survey responses from 251 participants. It examines how the scientists used ChatGPT and Codex across research work and how they expect AI to affect discovery.

End-to-end autonomous research, defined as requests for AI to coordinate literature review, ideation and downstream analysis, accounted for only 0.4% of the average scientist’s scientific messages. Yet 15.3% of scientists sent at least one such request, a gap the paper interprets as experimentation with full automation rather than routine use.

The full paper analyzes activity from August 20 through September 22, 2026, and a survey conducted September 30 to October 7, 2026. Researchers used privacy-preserving classifiers to categorize messages without reading the underlying exchanges, and program data were not used for model training by default.

Reading and knowledge acquisition accounted for 27.7% of the average scientist’s scientific messages, while writing and communication represented 26.9%. Scientific analysis contributed 16.7% and research software and code development 13.8%; together, those four workflows represented 85%.

That concentration puts most current AI use in discrete knowledge-work tasks rather than coordinated research projects. Explaining scientific concepts was the leading individual task at 18.8% of messages, with 89.7% of scientists sending at least three such requests.

Ideation tells a different story about breadth. It represented 5.3% of messages, but about 68% of scientists sent at least three ideation messages during the study. Across those messages, scientists supplied a specific question 35% of the time, asked ChatGPT to refine an existing idea 36% of the time, and asked it to suggest a research question 23% of the time. The remaining 6% could not be categorized.

The paper also identifies a stage-dependent division of labor. Scientists often asked AI to propose directions during early exploration, then supplied their own questions or ideas for refinement when developing specific research problems.

The paper’s scientist-involvement score, measuring the average share of initial requests followed by clarification, checking or revision, was 31.8%. Writing and communication had the highest score at 38.0%, followed by scientific analysis at 34.4% and research software at 33.7%.

For comparable tasks, requests requiring advanced or frontier expertise had higher involvement in 28 of 32 cases, by an average of 6.5 percentage points. More specialized work therefore generally drew more follow-up, not less.

The score considers only the next scientific message in the same conversation within seven days and does not capture revisions made outside the AI application. Checking or correcting outputs was also the most commonly selected difficulty in obtaining value from ChatGPT, chosen by 45.4% of surveyed scientists.

Those review demands coexist with self-reported time savings. Across eight research activities, surveyed scientists estimated that one hour of work with AI would have taken about 1.9 hours without it at the same quality. Reported productivity ratios ranged from 1.36 times for administration to 2.85 times for research software and code development.

Expectations for discovery were also high. Of the 250 scientists answering the discovery-timeline question, 98% expected AI to accelerate discovery in their field. Asked to imagine a discovery that would take ten years without AI, 72.0% expected it at least three years sooner with AI, including 21.6% who expected it within three years.

Written explanations emphasized execution over full automation. Among 367 reasons identified in 224 responses, 52% concerned faster research execution, while automating research loops accounted for 2.2%. Another 23% concerned finding more promising research questions and 14% pursuing a wider range of questions.

The reported bottlenecks extend beyond model capability. Limited time or staffing was selected by 51.8% of respondents and limited funding by 43.8%; validating or reproducing findings affected 25.5%, while access to equipment, laboratories or computing resources affected 24.3%.

OpenAI cautions that participants were a selected group who applied for expanded AI access and chose to answer an AI survey, so the results may not generalize to all scientists. Usage shares describe requests rather than research value or time allocation, and the findings do not establish causal effects.

Alongside the study, OpenAI announced an expansion of ChatGPT for Academic Researchers, saying the pilot had reached 20,000 researchers in 33 countries since July 2026.

Applications for 50,000 new grants open November 23, 2026, on a first-come, first-served basis for eligible researchers, replacing the pilot’s lottery-based selection. Each grant covers a recipient and up to two collaborators with free ChatGPT Plus access, supporting as many as 150,000 researchers in this round. OpenAI’s goal is to reach 200,000 researchers across the program.

OpenAI said Plus replaced ChatGPT Pro for the new grants because most pilot usage would have remained within Plus limits.

The expansion broadens access to the tools, but the study’s central implication concerns the work around them: whether time saved on individual tasks survives the checking scientists still perform and the staffing, funding and experimental constraints they report.

Apollo Research to Embed Six Evaluators in OpenAI Subversion Pilot

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Apollo Research announced on November 16, 2026 that six of its evaluators will work within OpenAI for four months, investigating whether its models attempt to subvert alignment or control mechanisms during training, evaluations and internal deployment.

The team will examine four questions: whether training incentivizes subversion, whether evaluations can elicit it, whether subversion occurs during internal deployment and whether reasoning relevant to subversion would be detectable. The scope covers attempts to undermine a model’s own alignment or controls, as well as those of its successors.

The arrangement is designed to move external scrutiny beyond Apollo’s previous evaluation approach, which took place after post-training and shortly before public release. Apollo considers that timing insufficient for testing misaligned behavior such as scheming or examining non-public uses during internal deployment. Embedded evaluations with employee-like access are intended to overcome those limitations.

Apollo describes the pilot as an ambitious first step rather than a full embedded evaluation. Its general standard requires access to all relevant records even if a developer tries to hide unfavorable information, disclosure of findings to an appropriate outside audience even if the developer objects, and sufficient public explanation of constraints for readers to understand what a report can and cannot establish.

For the training-incentives assessment, OpenAI committed to provide relevant training code and datasets, reinforcement-learning rollouts with available reasoning traces and rewards, and the ability to query intermediate model checkpoints. Detailed access arrangements for the other assessment claims remain to be agreed.

The assessment is confined to agreed claims, models and time periods. OpenAI may withhold in-scope items when it deems that reasonably necessary because of technical infeasibility, security risks or intellectual-property protection. It may not withhold information or access to staff simply because the material is unfavorable. It must tell Apollo that withheld items exist and provide written reasons and, where OpenAI considers it feasible and relevant, identify withheld records by category and approximate volume. Apollo can request broader access through a designated OpenAI contact and escalate refusals, but expansion is not guaranteed.

Staff contact is also controlled. Apollo can request people with specific expertise and suggest individuals, but OpenAI selects authorized participants and formal conversations must be logged. If another employee raises confidential information, Apollo must stop substantive discussion and route follow-up through its designated OpenAI contact, even when the information is within the engagement’s scope.

The evaluators will work on OpenAI hardware, using separate channels that can be monitored. By default, they will not have access to general staff channels such as Slack. Apollo notes that informal conversations and Slack can surface relevant information an evaluator might not have requested, and it wants such information brought into scope in future assessments.

Apollo plans two public reports. The first will address training incentives for subversion; the second will cover evaluations, internal deployment and the detectability of subversive reasoning. Each will describe methods, conclusions, limitations, questions that could not be assessed and supporting evidence that can be published.

OpenAI will review the reports and may request redactions of confidential information, including evidence supporting findings. It may not redact Apollo’s verdicts or request redactions merely because a finding is unfavorable. It can, however, redact evidence it considers sensitive intellectual property, whether or not that evidence directly contributes to a finding. Public readers may therefore be unable to examine all evidence behind a conclusion, although Apollo can state whether the redactions affected its conclusions. OpenAI will share the unredacted reports with its board’s Safety and Security Committee.

Claims based on employee discussions require OpenAI’s formal attestation unless Apollo establishes them independently. That requirement also covers information supplied by authorized staff in formal interviews. If attestation is declined, Apollo can disclose that it was declined and explain the effect on its conclusions without revealing the underlying confidential information.

Separate meta-transparency rights allow Apollo to describe publicly the engagement’s scope and conditions, access requested and received, material withheld or delayed and the reasons given, requested report removals and their significance, and any pressure it believed shaped its work. Apollo can discuss its access and publication terms without prior OpenAI review.

Apollo regards those rights as a minimum standard for every evaluation engagement. Without them, an absence of reported problems can appear reassuring even when it reflects restrictions on investigation or disclosure. The pilot’s conclusions will consequently need to be read alongside its account of access and publication limits: the same verdict can carry different weight depending on what the evaluators were able to inspect and disclose.

Ask AdMob is a conversational agent built with Gemini that will reside in publishers’ AdMob accounts. For optimization, Google says the assistant will use peer benchmarks to recommend adding SDK bidders it identifies as high-value, with predicted revenue uplifts, and help optimize mediation groups. It will also check third-party network configurations for typos and floor-price discrepancies before they affect yield.

Publishers will additionally be able to configure custom brand-safety rules with natural-language prompts in the AdMob interface instead of manually managing blocklists. Google will begin testing Ask AdMob’s capabilities over the next few months and plans more advanced features that act on a user’s behalf later in 2027. Every recommendation will remain subject to user review and approval before it is applied.

Agent skills are available with the announcement for implementation work. They give a developer’s preferred AI agent pre-built, tested playbooks to build, edit and validate ad code, including migration to the Google Mobile Ads Next-Gen SDK, validation of an SDK implementation and implementation of new ad formats. Google is positioning that workflow as an alternative to clicking through dashboards, copying ad-unit IDs and maintaining ad configuration files outside the codebase.

They work with assistants that support skills, including Antigravity, Claude Code, Codex and Cursor. Installation uses npx skills add google/skills/skills/ads, while npx skills update --all updates the installed skills.

The Android documentation lists six skills: initial SDK setup, banner ads, full-screen interstitial ads, rewarded ads and their reward callbacks, migration from the legacy Google Mobile Ads SDK, and integration validation. The validation skill covers pre-launch audits and checks for common implementation errors, while the setup skill includes setting the application ID and initializing the Next-Gen SDK.

The upcoming AdMob MCP server would connect developer environments directly to AdMob, expanding an agent’s role from code assistance to account operations. Google says agents will be able to read account data, create ad units and manage mediation settings, and that the server will expand to more developers in early 2027 after early testing. Developers will review and approve updates before pushing them to production.

Google also plans a broader wave of in-product and AI-agent capabilities throughout 2027 to handle more routine operational work. Selection will occur on a first-come, first-served basis among eligible applications, replacing the pilot’s lottery. Qualified researchers already on the waitlist will receive activation instructions by email before applications open. OpenAI’s overall goal is to reach 200,000 researchers, twice the target set when the pilot launched in July 2026.

The pilot has enrolled 20,000 researchers across 33 countries. Pilot participants received ChatGPT Pro. OpenAI said its usage review found that most participants would have remained within Plus limits, and it attributed the switch to Plus for new grants to its aim of supporting more research teams.

Program participants can use OpenAI’s frontier models and receive training on using ChatGPT in research. Data shared through the program is not used to train OpenAI models by default.

Pilot projects illustrate different research uses. Shuqi Li of Brigham and Women’s Hospital and Harvard Medical School is exploring whether ChatGPT can connect laboratory and patient data to accelerate antibiotic development for tuberculosis. At the Florida Museum of Natural History, Arthur Porto and his team use AI tools to turn specimen photographs into searchable databases. They also created a 3D butterfly model whose wings change color as it is turned, like the real specimen.

A companion study from OpenAI’s Economic Research team, Science at the Frontier: How Scientists Use ChatGPT, analyzed 580,186 scientific messages from 1,819 scientists between August 20 and September 22, 2026, alongside a survey of 251 participants conducted from September 30 to October 7, 2026. To protect privacy, automated classifiers sorted messages by research activity; the research team saw the categories but did not read the underlying messages or ChatGPT’s responses. Activity shares were calculated for each scientist and then averaged, giving every scientist equal weight.

The study reports that reading and learning accounted for an average 28% of scientific messages and writing and communicating 27%, followed by analyzing evidence and theories at 17% and writing research software at 14%. Sixty-eight percent of scientists used ChatGPT to develop research ideas, defined as sending at least three ideation messages during the study. Among ideation messages, 36% involved a scientist bringing an idea for ChatGPT to refine, 35% began with a scientist’s specific question, and 23% asked ChatGPT to suggest a question.

The study also measured follow-up requests to clarify, check, or revise ChatGPT’s responses. Average follow-up rates were highest for writing and communicating at 38%, followed by writing research software and analyzing evidence and theories at 34% each. The metric captures a follow-up in the next scientific message in the same conversation within seven days.

Surveyed participants estimated that completing the same work at the same quality without AI would have taken 1.9 times as long. That figure is self-reported. Ninety-eight percent of the 250 scientists answering a separate discovery-timeline question expected AI to accelerate discovery in their field. Those responses describe expectations rather than observed discovery gains.

OpenAI said the findings do not establish causal effects and that determining whether these expectations translate into faster discovery will require more research. The study also identifies funding, staffing, and experimental capacity as constraints on testing ideas. The expansion therefore pairs broader access with an unresolved scientific question: the pilot documents how AI fits into research work, while the larger program’s impact depends on researchers’ ability to test AI-assisted ideas and validate any resulting findings.

Using AI for listings, where marketing ends and misrepresentation begins

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As AI speeds up listing copy and outreach, fewer checkpoints can increase compliance, disclosure, and fair housing risk. The column argues for training, tool controls and meaningful human review.  AI Summary

Over the years, I have seen too many six-figure lawsuits over the words ‘fully-renovated.’

In real estate, a single, seemingly innocuous word or phrase can carry significant legal ramifications and consequences. Descriptions with words including “fully renovated,” “brand new,” “resolved” and “permitted” can and too often do cause years of unexpected, costly litigation for buyers, sellers and even their respective agents more often than people may think. Each word can change a buyer’s understanding of a property and potentially expose a seller, agent, and/or brokerage to liability if the property description no longer matches the reality of the property’s true condition.

By modifying agent and seller property descriptions, AI has inadvertently and undeniably introduced this new layer of exposure and risk to the way agents and their clients do business.

According to the National Association of Realtors 2026 Technology Report, nearly half of Realtors surveyed use AI daily or weekly. Among those agents surveyed who use AI, 75% use it to write listing descriptions and more than half use it for social media posts, emails, or follow-up communications. Other industry research shows that AI adoption may be moving even faster. A 2026 Delta Media survey found that 82% of real estate agents asked use AI to write listing descriptions, which is up from 58% in 2024.

Creating consumer-facing content is the most common use of AI in our industry, helping to craft the words consumers see when considering one of the most important decisions of their lives. At the same time, the biggest challenges brought by AI appear to be growing far more rapidly than the policies and oversight needed to control them.

Surely, most sellers or agents are not deliberately leveraging AI-generated property descriptions to misrepresent properties. The more likely outcome is that an agent will provide accurate information into an AI tool to create a more compelling description, and in turn, the AI tool changes the description to one that is less accurate, thereby exposing an agent and seller to unexpected pitfalls.

For instance, rulings in lawsuits can depend on specific legal issues concerning whether a property is represented, whether by an agent or AI, as partially or fully renovated  or whether a defect was described as repaired or permanently resolved. Lawsuits pertaining to undisclosed permitting issues often concern whether work awaiting approval is described as being in the “final stages of permitting.” The aforementioned are only a few of examples of obstacles an agent or seller may encounter and such compliance issues are challenging companies across industries more often than ever before

The pitfalls of AI are not limited to the sale of real estate. AI has rapidly morphed from an experimental marketing tool to common resource for many areas of business, but especially marketing. Teams use it for everything from drafting copy to generating or cleaning up images to analyzing customer behavior. Work that used to take hours can be done in minutes, if not faster.

Marketing across industries relies on speed that the AI gods are now supercharging. Through their use of artificial intelligence, marketing teams can create dozens of variations of print or digital ads, internal or external emails, social media posts and listing descriptions. The speed at which content is being created naturally removes some of the checkpoints that were in place when it took longer to create or more people were involved in the process.

However, increased efficiency and speed often translate to increased exposure to risk and liability.

A listing description does not have to be completely fabricated to cause a problem. It can come down to the embellishment of one or two words.

Outside of real estate, AI-generated marketing can introduce unsupported claims, make definitive statements from what was originally nuanced, or alter the meaning of disclosures. Without the necessary checkpoints, it can lead to tremendous legal issues.

Accordingly, companies and their employees must take seriously the need for more extensive oversight to stay compliant.

The reason real estate is a great example of how AI can impact compliance is the industry’s significant regulatory requirements, including fair housing laws.

For example, if an agent uses AI to make a listing description more appealing to a certain buyer profile, the language used could potentially implicate protected characteristics or discourage certain groups from being interested in the property without the agent even realizing it.

Even if the agent did not intend to be discriminatory, it is still a compliance issue.

Fortunately, most real estate professionals recognize and consider the risks associated with AI use. A 2026 Realtors Property Resource survey found that 92% of agents surveyed were using or planning to use AI, while 63% identified accuracy of AI-generated content as their top concern.

Industries like financial services, insurance, healthcare, employment, and other highly regulated fields face their own requirements governing advertising, disclosures, accessibility, privacy, and discrimination.

A common answer to how to lower AI risk is to keep a “human in the loop.” If your strategy includes having someone simply click “approve” before AI-generated content goes out, that’s not enough. Reviewers need to know what they are looking for and the potential risks this content creates, such as unsupported claims, industry-specific advertising rules, and whether it misleads consumers. In addition, has confidential or proprietary information been entered into an AI platform to create the content?

The same principle applies when employees or consumers turn to AI for legal, financial, or other professional advice. I find myself repeating one simple phrase constantly: “AI is not an attorney.”

AI can certainly be useful to organize information, generate ideas, and streamline work processes. But it can never replace professional judgment.

A glaring problematic approach taken by many businesses these days appears to be that they are thinking of AI oversight backwards: providing employees free rein to use it and then creating a policy only after something goes wrong.

Instead, real estate professionals must be armed with tools, policies, and guidelines, and practical guardrails now, before AI continues to be utilized further.

At minimum, businesses should identify which AI tools employees are approved to use, what information can and cannot be entered into them, when AI-generated content requires additional review, and an approval and compliance review for any public-facing content.

The level of oversight must reflect the level of risk. Generating ideas for an article headline is a lot different than using AI to determine which consumers should receive a certain ad. Summarizing notes from an internal brainstorming meeting is not the same as synthesizing confidential client information to create a strategy.

Training is just as important as policy, and it is not enough to send out a document once via email announcing how employees are expected to use AI in the workplace.

The companies that manage AI most effectively will be those where legal and marketing work collaboratively.

Legal and compliance teams need to understand marketing workflows and how AI is being used so they can create guardrails. Marketing needs to involve the legal teams early enough in the process so it’s not an afterthought right before launch.

With the way AI is accelerating work, things are happening too quickly for either to operate in a silo.

We are not trying to slow innovation. We are working to create an environment where teams can move quickly because there are established boundaries and protections to keep risk as low as possible.

AI is going to continue to transform marketing, and businesses should be able to leverage this tool while remaining compliant. At the end of the day, the technology may be new, but accountability is not.

Why database touch plans fail real estate agents

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Despite 91% of buyers saying they would reuse or recommend their agent, NAR data shows most repeat clients hire differently, with only 18% returning to a prior agent. The article proposes replacing high volume touches with intentional conversations across a 150 person network.  AI Summary

Agents are consistently trained to do their touches. “Have you done all your touches?” “Have you done your outreach?” “Are you touching people?” “Are you sending things of value?” That antiquated philosophy needs to stop and almost nobody in this industry cares to address it or examine why it fails. It is wildly ineffective, and I would argue that most of the time it hurts the agent’s brand rather than helping it.

If I walked up to your front door 32 times a year, rang the bell, and said, “Just a reminder that I’m in real estate,” how long would it take you to look into a restraining order? Nobody would call that a relationship. Yet the same schedule applies when it arrives as an email one week and a postcard the next.

Agents are constantly told they need to remind people that they are in real estate. That is an interaction about what the agent cares about, not what the other person cares about. Trust isn’t built by constantly telling someone how amazing I am, why they need me, or why I’m relevant or important. That is blatant narcissism, and yet somehow we call it great top-of-mind marketing.

The National Association of Realtors’ 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers shows the problem. Ninety-one percent of buyers said they would use their agent again or recommend them. But among repeat buyers, only 18% went back to an agent they had worked with before, and among sellers the figure was 29%. The touches are supposed to close that gap.

The industry explains that clients forget, so its answer has always been more reminders—the rest of the survey points somewhere else. 80% of sellers contacted only one agent before hiring, and 66% hired someone referred to them or someone they had used before. Sellers are not comparing the agents who emailed or mailed them the most. They call the one person they trust. Seeing your name in their inbox every month does not make you that person.

One of the agents I coach has phenomenal email automation, with reminders and alerts set up for everyone they have ever worked with. By their own count, 90% of their business comes from less than 5% of the people in their database. If 90% of your business comes from less than 5% of those people, why are you digitally harassing the other 95%?

In 2003, researchers Russell Hill and Robin Dunbar measured how many people we keep up with by using the most ordinary list there is, the one for Christmas cards. Participants logged every card they sent and everyone in the household it went to. Networks averaged about 150 people, and participants stayed in touch weekly with the people they felt closest to.

A later study by Sam Roberts and Dunbar followed people for 18 months. Friendships lost closeness when contact dropped off, while family relationships were far less affected. Contact matters, and the touch plans are right about that much. They go wrong by treating a Christmas card to the whole database as the same act as staying close to 150. A mailing treats everyone alike, so the people who matter most get the same thing as the strangers, and the relationships that produce business fade.

Pick your 150. Write down the people you would be glad to call and stop when you reach that number. They will be past clients and friends whose lives you know something about. Dunbar’s later research found an inner circle of about 15 inside the 150. Give those 15 their own rhythm, a conversation with each of them every month, because in my experience that inner circle is where most referrals come from. A database you can’t keep up with starts to feel like a weight. A list of 150 is small enough that you notice when one of them is about to move.

Reconnect at four months. A conversation creates what I call mind share: the trust that comes from talking with someone. Name recognition never builds it, and without the next conversation it fades. In my coaching, I see it run low about three to four months after the last conversation. Once a week, scroll back about four months in your text messages, pick three people, and send something like “Hey, was just thinking about you. Hope all is well; would love to catch up.” Leave real estate out of it. Anyone in your text messages already has a relationship with you that a mailing list never will, so the message reaches someone who trusts you at the point where that trust is lowest. Keep it to three. Send 30, have 10 people answer and your afternoon is gone, which is the fastest way to make sure you never do it again.

If you lead agents, ask how many conversations they had with their 150 this month, not how many touches they made. That number tells you whose relationships are fading before the referrals stop. We keep overcomplicating this business by pushing agents to do more and more, which only creates overwhelm and makes them more likely to do less. We owe them better.

As rates of advanced prostate cancer continue to rise, the American Cancer Society (ACS) has issued new guidelines recommending routine prostate cancer screening for many men starting at age 50.

The updated recommendations, published November 12 in CA: A Cancer Journal for Clinicians, mark a shift from the organization’s previous emphasis on shared decision making about whether to undergo screening.

Under the new guidelines, the ACS recommends regular prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing beginning at age 50 for men at average risk of prostate cancer and age 45 for those at higher risk.

Advances in MRI technology and biomarker testing may also help doctors determine which men need further testing, potentially reducing unnecessary biopsies and overtreatment.

The ACS last updated its prostate cancer screening guidelines in 2010, when conflicting research findings raised questions about whether the benefits of PSA screening outweighed the potential harms.

At the time, the organization emphasized informed, shared decision making between patients and healthcare professionals.

However, longer-term follow-up studies have since provided stronger evidence of the benefits of early detection, prompting the ACS to conduct a systematic review and update its recommendations.

The authors of the report noted, “In making these recommendations, we recognize that PSA testing is only the first step in a screening cascade that includes secondary testing of men with an elevated PSA level and treatment decisions for men diagnosed with prostate cancer.”

The new guidelines recommend a PSA test every 2 years for men ages 50–69 with an average risk of prostate cancer.

The recommendation for those at high risk is a PSA test every year starting at age 45.

For men ages 70–74, the ACS recommends individualized decision making. If an individual chooses to be screened, the ACS recommends a PSA test every 2 years until the age of 74.

Ramkishen Narayanan, MD, Director of the Center for Urologic Health at The Roy and Patricia Disney Family Cancer Center at Providence Saint Joseph Medical Center in Burbank, CA, who wasn’t involved in the report, told Healthline that screening shouldn’t automatically be ruled out for men over 75.

“[A] point worth noting is that for men over 75, their PSA history as well as their individual clinical scenarios should be evaluated, and that PSA should not be discouraged in this group of men if their life expectancy and clinical history warrant a PSA check.”

According to the ACS, approximately 1 in 8 men will be diagnosed with prostate cancer during their lifetime.

Prostate cancer diagnoses in the United States increased by around 3% per year between 2014 and 2022, according to the ACS.

The rise in diagnoses, particularly advanced-stage prostate cancer, has renewed attention on the importance of regular screening.

“[The] current state of prostate cancer screening guidelines is vague and generalized. This has led to ambiguity, disagreement, and variation of opinion among healthcare providers and institutions in their approach to men’s health,” said S. Adam Ramin, MD, board certified urologist, urologic oncologist, and medical director of Urology Cancer Specialists in Los Angeles, CA, who wasn’t involved in the report.

The updated ACS guidelines drew partly on findings from a 2025 follow-up of the European Randomized Study of Screening for Prostate Cancer (ERSPC), which found that regular PSA screening was associated with a 13% reduction in prostate cancer deaths over 23 years.

A 12-year ERSPC follow-up in 2012 also suggested that regular screening was associated with a decrease in metastatic prostate cancer diagnosis, which is another finding that influenced the updated guidance.

“Prostate cancer can take many years, even decades, to become clinically significant, so it takes a long time for us to see the consequences of changes in screening practices,” Taylor Goodstein, MD, assistant professor of urology in the College of Medicine at the University of Cincinnati, who wasn’t involved in the report, told Healthline.

“With longer follow-up from randomized trials, we now have more convincing evidence that PSA screening reduces the risk of dying from prostate cancer,” she said.

“The ACS guidelines are important now because they drive home the most important point that prostate cancer screening saves lives,” said Narayanan.

Healthcare professionals use PSA levels to help determine whether additional testing for prostate cancer may be necessary.

The ACS recommends further testing for men under 70 with PSA levels of 3 ng/mL or higher.

For those ages 70 and older, the ACS recommends further testing if their PSA is 6.5 ng/mL or higher. This is because PSA levels tend to be naturally higher in older men.

You can speak with your healthcare professional to find out more about PSA cutoff levels.

Goodstein told Healthline that the ways this new guidance can change the outlook for prostate cancer are simple.

“If we screen men, we can catch troublesome cancers early, before they have a chance to spread,” she said.

Goodstein also emphasized how much the evaluation process has changed. In the past, an elevated PSA level often led directly to a biopsy.

Today, doctors generally repeat the PSA test within two weeks to three months to confirm the result. If PSA levels remain elevated, additional testing, including a prostate MRI or validated biomarker test, can help determine whether a biopsy is necessary.

Narayanan told Healthline, “The new guidelines will hopefully remove any confusion about the value of PSA screening and encourage prostate cancer screening as an integral part of a man’s ‘checkup’.”

He added that it is worth noting that the DRE (digital rectal exam) adds little value to the initial prostate cancer screening and is no longer something that men should fear.

“Adoption of this set of specific guidelines standardizes the approach to screening for prostate cancer. It provides a unified effort among all levels of healthcare. It most likely will increase the chances of finding prostate cancer in earlier stages and lead to improved cancer treatment outcomes,” said Ramin.

The United States will likely lose its measles elimination status, a major public health achievement that was secured 26 years ago.

The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), the World Health Organization’s (WHO) regional office for the Americas, determined that measles has spread continuously in the U.S. for more than 12 months. This marks the end of the country’s measles elimination status, which it has held since 2000.

Researchers familiar with the discussions told KFF Health News that the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) determined this week that measles is once again spreading through sustained transmission within the country. PAHO is expected to make an official announcement on November 17.

PAHO’s review examined measles spread starting January 20, 2025, when the first person became ill in a large West Texas outbreak, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).

CDC data show 5,412 confirmed measles cases this year as of November 12. That’s the highest annual total since 1991 and more than double the 2025 count of 2,289 cases.

Andi Shane, MD, chief of pediatric infectious diseases at Children’s Healthcare of Atlanta, called the U.S. losing measles elimination status “a public health alarm that we are not being effective and we’re not controlling measles as well.”

The measles virus is highly contagious. It spreads through the air when an infected person coughs or sneezes. People can catch it in a room up to two hours after an infected person has left, according to the CDC.

“There’s really been very little measles in the United States over the past 25 years until January 2025,” William Moss, MD, a professor of epidemiology at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health, told Healthline.

Losing the status itself doesn’t change much about daily life for most Americans, Moss said. The concern is the measles outbreaks, hospitalizations and deaths behind the loss, he said.

He called the loss “a big red flag” after “enormous efforts over the past 26 years” to keep the status.

A country loses its elimination status when there is “uninterrupted circulation of the same virus genotype and lineage for 12 months or more,” according to PAHO. Shane explained it this way: “For the past 12 months in the United States, we’ve had ongoing circulation of the measles virus and person-to-person transmission is occurring.”

Canada lost its elimination status in November 2025, and the U.K. and five European countries lost theirs earlier in 2026.

The U.S. can regain the status. “Other countries have done that,” Moss said. Brazil, which lost its status in 2019, regained it in November 2024. Venezuela, which lost its status in 2018, regained it in 2023, according to PAHO.

Before the measles vaccine, measles infected an estimated 3 million to 4 million Americans each year, according to the CDC. Among reported cases, about 400 to 500 people died each year, 48,000 needed hospital care, and 1,000 developed encephalitis, or brain swelling.

Moss said elimination efforts “really intensified” after a large outbreak from 1989 to 1992. Part of that push was “really promoting school entry vaccination checks,” he said.

“That was one of the key policy changes that I think allowed for measles elimination in the United States.”

Every state and Washington, D.C., require children to be vaccinated against measles to attend school, though exemptions vary by state, according to the nonprofit health policy organization KFF.

Fewer kindergartners are getting the MMR vaccine, according to the CDC.

Kindergarten MMR coverage fell from 95.2% in the 2019–2020 school year to 92.4% in 2025–2026. About 280,000 kindergartners started the 2025–2026 school year without records showing they had completed the MMR series, the CDC reported.

Shane said “95 out of 100 people need to be protected against measles” for a community to get herd protection.

“So since we’ve been below that for many, many months, that is also contributing to the loss of our elimination status,” she said.

“It takes two things to have a measles outbreak,” Moss said. A community needs low vaccine coverage, and someone with measles has to enter it and “start the chain of transmission,” he said.

About 96% of this year’s cases were among people who were unvaccinated or had unknown vaccination status, up from 93% in 2025, according to the CDC.

Moss said many of those cases are adults.

“This is particularly true in Pennsylvania, where about two-thirds of the cases are in adults,” he said. Many never got vaccinated as kids and grew up while measles was rare, so they never built natural immunity, he said.

He noted that having so many unvaccinated adults puts pregnant people and adults with chronic conditions like lung disease at risk of serious illness. The U.S. has already seen “dozens of pregnant women actually getting infected during pregnancy,” he said.Infected parents can also spread measles to young children and grandparents, Shane said. Shane traced much of the hesitancy to the pandemic. “With COVID there’s been an overall distrust in vaccines and vaccination that initially started with COVID but has now spread to other vaccines,” she said.

Moss pointed to the federal government. “We know what it takes to achieve and sustain elimination. We have the expertise. We have the resources,” he said.

He cited staff cuts at the CDC and said messaging from Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and President Donald Trump has undermined vaccine confidence. He called federal messaging on vaccines “really mixed and weak.”

Moss also criticized the President’s August 10 executive order calling for the MMR vaccine to be split into separate shots for each disease.

“There’s no justification for that, and those vaccines don’t even exist in the United States,” he said.

Measles may cause ear infections, diarrhea, pneumonia, encephalitis and, in some cases, death, according to the CDC. The disease may also weaken the immune system for weeks to a year after recovery, raising the risk of flu, COVID-19, or RSV, Shane said. “It’s not a benign childhood disease,” Moss said.

The best protection against measles is two doses of the MMR vaccine, which are about 97% effective, according to the CDC. Shane said, “There are not very many other things we do in life that give you such great protection.” People who missed doses can still catch up, she added. “If you just don’t know or can’t tell, there’s no harm — as long as you’re eligible — in getting another MMR vaccine.”

Shane added that “parents really want to do the right thing for their children.”

“We know that the MMR vaccine — that’s the vaccine that provides protection against measles — is a safe vaccine. It’s a very effective vaccine,” Shane said.

Babies under 12 months are too young for the vaccine. “So they depend on the protection of the people around them,” Shane said.

The CDC recommends a first MMR dose at 12 to 15 months and a second at 4 to 6 years. Babies 6 to 11 months old who will travel internationally should get an early dose, and adults without proof of immunity should get at least one dose, according to the CDC.

To stop the outbreaks, Moss said the U.S. will need to “increase vaccine coverage, particularly in those communities that have had long-standing low vaccine rates.”

So far, the response has been “more reactive than proactive,” Moss said. Public health teams scramble once an outbreak starts, he said, and he wants more outreach to at-risk communities before measles arrives.

“I am worried about the ability of the United States to regain measles elimination status in the current climate,” Moss said.

The previous post, The Division of Labor Between XML, JSON and MessagePack, closed by saying the other half of value comes here. Encoded and Encrypted differ by one number on the API payload, and underneath that number are three stages that run in a fixed order, while on the way back the order is more than the same run in reverse.

An ERP system's API has to answer two questions that are independent of each other: what a call's content should be processed into, and who is entitled to decide it may be processed less. The first is a fixed pipeline, the second is one declaration written onto the method. The two are kept apart, and are compared against each other at one stop only, which is access control.

Encryption is not in those two lines. It is one further step the layer outside decides on according to the format: Encoded stops here, and Encrypted goes one stage further.

Compression comes before encryption because compression does nothing at all to ciphertext. The output of good encryption is statistically indistinguishable from random data, and repetition and skew are exactly what a compression algorithm looks for. Running the case study's order FormSchema through both orders shows it:

Swapping the order makes no call fail, and no check anywhere says a word. It just means the compression stage stops doing anything from then on.

The way back is those three steps reversed: decrypt, decompress, deserialize. But what matters about the reverse direction is not that it runs backwards, it is that each stage only ever sees bytes that got past the one before it. The authentication code is verified before decryption, and decryption comes before decompression, so the gzip decompressor never at any point holds a stretch of data that has not been verified.

A decompressor is a parser that accepts arbitrary bytes. Feed it ciphertext with one bit changed and it will start parsing all the same, then throw because it cannot read a structure out of it. This order means it never gets that far.

The three values of PayloadFormat were covered in that same earlier post. What access control looks at is not that one on its own, it is the result of comparing it with another enum, and that one has four values.

Two of the values share a name across the two enums, being Encoded and Encrypted, which makes it easy to read them as two ways of saying the same thing. They are not: one records a fact, the other declares a requirement.

What the four levels ask for, and the kind of method each suits:

How the framework's own methods are distributed across those four is itself a decision: the trust boundary is drawn at HTTPS, and only the handful whose authors judged explicitly that the content must not be visible once inside the server are marked Encrypted. Nothing declares the middle level at present, which is where the format side lands rather than something the level side asks for.

Drawing the boundary at HTTPS does not mean application layer encryption is pointless. HTTPS protects only the stretch where data travels over the network, and it is unwrapped the moment it reaches the server, so every stop that handles it after that sees plain text. A node gets compromised, logging and monitoring keep the content, something is intercepted and replayed across network segments or over a VPN: all of these are real in actual ERP deployments.

Drawing the line at Public buys one thing: a front end with no .NET runtime does not have to implement the whole encryption pipeline before it can reach its first method. The cost is that for calls at that level, value is visible once it is inside the server, and a method needing that layer has to be marked for it.

The test is that the actual format has to be greater than or equal to the declared level: doing more is fine, doing less is refused. What follows is all about remote calls, and a remote call gets past LocalOnly at no format level at all:

LocalOnly is not stricter encryption. It asks something else: this method does not arrive over the network at all. Not being on the same axis, it is not compared with the format for size, it is judged on its own. The Connector and the Convergence of Client Responsibilities mentioned that a few server side methods accept in-process calls only, and this is the slot it meant. This check can sit ahead of decryption because both values it compares are available from the start: the format is a number on the API payload, and the level is attached to the type and fixed once compiled. Neither side requires opening value and looking inside. The encryption code in the previous section recognizes only bytes and a key, and does not know it is encrypting an API call, so it lives in the primitive layer. That is also why this post's payload and the key protection coming up use the same code.

The Purpose and Classification of Definition Files reached a conclusion about the startup dependency chain that carries straight over here: the master key is the first item in the chain, and it cannot be encrypted itself, so SystemSettings records only where it is. An environment variable is the present default and a file is the other option. Each route has its own exposure surface, and the environment variable is not chosen for being safer. It is chosen because containers and secret management tools inject through environment variables as a matter of course, while going through a file means either mounting a volume or baking the file into the image. The master key does not encrypt any call directly. Its use is unlocking the other keys in the settings, which are stored as ciphertext, and what wraps them is AesCbcHmacCryptor.

What the third column decides is whether rebuilding a session from a database seed, which that same earlier post described, can hold at all. The interface carries a property that answers exactly this: if the key cannot be recovered, a rebuilt session leaves the user looking as though they are still signed in while every encrypted call fails.

The derived one's root key comes from the master key by default, and a value given in the settings takes precedence. The cost of making it the default is that the same access token always derives the same key, rather than a random one at each login. What that buys is a key that never has to be stored and that every node can compute for itself, while the token itself is a GUID nobody can guess and the root key never leaves the server. The order here is counterintuitive as a result: the token has to be generated first, and only then can the key be derived.

At login the caller generates an RSA key pair and writes the public key into the login request. Once the server has built the session it wraps the symmetric key with that public key and returns it alongside the access token. Every call asking for encryption after that uses it.

That same post on the Connector wrote up the exception on the browser side: an RSA key pair cannot be generated there, so the public key sent is an empty string and the server returns an empty string too. The degrading happens on the calling end rather than on the server, and a call asking for Encrypted falls back to Encoded by itself when it has no key in hand. Set that against the second section and it is clear how far this fallback route goes: the Encoded format gets past Public and Encoded, and does not get past the handful marked Encrypted.

The application code in the case study relating to this post is a few lines at startup: put a demo master key in if the environment variable is not set, build the payload options from the definition file into components, then allow automatic creation when the framework starts. The rest is all in the definition files: gzip for compression, aes-cbc-hmac for encryption, and the master key from BEE_MASTER_KEY. For the value slot the case study declares nothing, so it takes messagepack, which is what applies when nothing is declared.

[ApiAccessControl] appears zero times in the whole case study. The order business object overrides GetNewData, and the overriding method carries no declaration, relying on exactly the second section's rule that the search moves on to the method it overrides.

The implementation that does not encrypt is only allowed to be constructed in debug mode, and the case study has debug mode on. The Error Contract and User Visible Messages used the same flag, where what it decided was whether error messages get masked. So that check may as well not exist here, and the case study picked real encryption anyway. The check has a gap of its own as well: it only stops the route that builds an implementation by name, and the route that hands a component in directly goes around it.

What actually makes the case study's encryption not count is not that check, it is the demo master key written into the source. The comment beside it says outright that production has to inject a real one through a real deployment mechanism before startup. The framework cannot tell which kind of environment it is running in, and blocking startup outright would take down test environments wanting to run the sample data, so this is recorded in a comment and nowhere else.

A call's value carries three stages of processing and one declaration:

All three are things anybody can go and check. A pipeline with the order swapped shows it in the compression ratio on the spot. Whether a level holds is answered by firing once at the endpoint for each. Whether the authentication code is stamped in the right place is answered by changing one bit and trying.

One thing is not. Whether that master key really is known only to whoever deployed it cannot be seen from inside the framework, and cannot be measured either. Once the three above are all done right, the strength of the whole thing equals whatever is in that environment variable, and that happens outside the code: the real line of defense is the deployment checklist.

In a security pipeline, the part about whether it is built right can be verified, and the part about whether it deserves trust cannot. The first is a design question, closed once it is written into the mechanism. The second has to be answered again at every deployment.

Platform engineers: Standardise your cache connection patterns. Always use a middleware to automatically generate deterministic cache keys for GET requests.

SREs on-call: Stop silent cascading failures. If your upstream provider goes down, a stale cache fallback prevents your app from crashing.

First-timers: Understand the app flow first. Don't fetch the same data twice. If a user asks for character data, fetch it once, store it in Redis, and serve subsequent requests instantly.

Symptom: When a highly requested cache key expires (like a popular character or homepage data), 100 concurrent requests miss the cache at the exact same millisecond. They all hit the upstream API simultaneously, causing a rate limit block or an upstream crash.

Root Cause: Because the key is gone, all application threads independently decide they need to fetch and hydrate the cache.

Fix (Soft TTL / Background Refresh): Instead of waiting for a hard TTL expiration, implement a "Soft TTL". Store the data alongside an expiration timestamp. When a request comes in, check if the Soft TTL has passed. If it has, immediately return the stale data to the user, but spawn a background thread (using Celery or FastAPI BackgroundTasks) to fetch fresh data and update the cache. This ensures only one thread hits the upstream API, while users never experience the 300ms latency penalty.

If you cache everything endlessly, Redis will run out of memory (OOM). You must configure an eviction policy in your redis.conf. For an API cache, allkeys-lru (Least Recently Used) is usually the best choice. When Redis hits its memory limit, it will automatically delete the cache keys that haven't been accessed recently, making room for new data while keeping the most popular characters in memory.

This application is designed as a drop-in reference architecture. If you need a fully-functional, containerised application to test your local Kubernetes clusters, CI/CD pipelines, or logging stacks, you can use this repository as your baseline.

We enforce a strict Time-To-Live (TTL) on our Redis keys. Once the TTL expires, the key is evicted, forcing the next request to fetch fresh data from the upstream API. This balances low latency with eventual consistency.

hflip is a left–right mirror. vflip is a top–bottom mirror. The frame size stays the same (here, 720×1280). That is not a rotate: transpose=1 would turn this clip into 1280×720. And despite what autocomplete offers for "ffmpeg flip video without re encoding", a flip is a video filter, so -c:v copy is rejected: Filtering and streamcopy cannot be used together. ffmpeg flip video 90 degrees shows up in US-English autocomplete next to the horizontal/vertical queries. A 90° rotate changes the axes. A flip does not.

The first command still writes a 720×1280 frame. transpose=1 would write 1280×720. On the raw RGB24 first frame, hflip is exactly the Python left–right mirror of the source — every row reversed, zero differing bytes. vflip is exactly the top–bottom mirror. If you wanted the clip upside down and backwards left-to-right, that is hflip,vflip, and it is byte-identical to rotate=PI (a 180° rotate). It is not a 90° rotate.

"ffmpeg flip video without re encoding" is a real autocomplete suggestion. This is what FFmpeg 9.0.1 does with it:

The process exits non-zero (234 here) and writes no file. A horizontal flip has to rewrite every macroblock; the original bitstream cannot be copied. There is no lossless -c:v copy path for hflip or vflip. If you only needed to tag a player to mirror at display time, that would be a different (and poorly supported) metadata trick — and it would not change the pixels, which is what a flip is supposed to do.

The video has to be re-encoded. The audio does not. With -c:a copy the AAC bitstream is identical to the source: MD5 5843435af71daac1e832f8b54fe25bdf on both the original and the flipped file, 131354 bytes. You keep the stadium crowd exactly as it was, and you do not pay a second AAC encode.

Firecracker for Agent Code Execution Requires Building and Operating the Sandbox Service

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 16 • Originally published at effloow.com

We care about sandbox costs because agent execution turns small unit prices into a material infrastructure bill. A coding agent does not run one command. It installs dependencies, retries failed tests, holds a workspace between turns, and sometimes waits while another model reasons. At volume, the lifetime of those environments matters as much as their CPU consumption.

Firecracker provides a KVM-backed isolation primitive, but a self-hosted sandbox still requires an execution service, security controls, and infrastructure operations.

Our question was simple: can we replace a managed sandbox bill with infrastructure we control without accidentally becoming a sandbox-platform company?

Firecracker supplies an important part of that answer. On the KVM path we evaluate here, it runs a guest Linux kernel inside a lightweight virtual machine. Its deliberately small device model reduces exposed functionality compared with a general-purpose VMM. We get a hardware virtualization boundary rather than asking untrusted programs to share the host kernel directly.

We do not get a complete agent execution service.

We still need image builds, a guest execution agent, admission control, workspace handling, deadlines, output limits, network policy, cleanup, and an authenticated customer-facing API. Firecracker's host API configures machines; it does not provide an equivalent of sandbox.commands.run().

Our evidence boundary matters: we reviewed the implementation-facing material and external execution accounts, but did not complete a local KVM deployment for this article. Our review environment lacked the necessary KVM access, binaries, images, and privileged networking controls. The walkthrough below is a reproduction recipe, with explicitly simulated output—not a claim that we measured boot latency or validated production isolation.

That distinction changes our recommendation. We consider Firecracker a credible primitive for a self-hosted sandbox, not a deployment we can certify from a successful API response. Our starting references are the Firecracker project, its quickstart, and its production host guidance.

We separate the first experiment from the production design. The first experiment should prove that a trusted guest boots on the intended host. It should not accept customer code.

We require Linux with working /dev/kvm, a supported CPU architecture, and permission for the VMM identity to access KVM. A cloud instance being “a Linux VM” is insufficient: its provider and instance configuration must expose hardware virtualization.

We would run the permission checks as the intended service identity, not only as root.

We install Firecracker and jailer from the same pinned release, using the upstream release archive for the host architecture. We verify the release artifacts before installation and retain their hashes. We do not use an unversioned download or assume that a URL ending in a binary name exists for every release.

Our build path would use a pinned Linux source tree and Firecracker's matching guest configuration as the kernel baseline. We would build the appropriate kernel target for the architecture, rather than assume one image format fits every host.

For the filesystem, we would construct a minimal distribution tree, install the interpreter and guest agent, add an init service, and package it into ext4. For example, once a trusted staging tree exists:

The 1 GiB file size is an example build choice, not a measured storage requirement. These commands package a tree; they do not magically create its init system or execution agent.

We would first boot upstream quickstart artifacts, then replace the kernel and filesystem separately. That keeps a broken image build from masquerading as a KVM problem.

Here is our proposed trusted-guest smoke test, which we have not executed or validated against a pinned Firecracker API specification. A successful start response does not prove that init completed. A read-only guest also needs appropriate writable temporary filesystems for its services. We would inspect the serial log and then require an actual command response from the guest agent.

Our production path would start with the jailer reference, matched to the pinned binary.

We need a unique instance ID, an explicit unprivileged UID/GID, a controlled chroot base, configured resource controls, and any required network namespace. Kernel, disk, and socket paths must make sense inside the jail. Host absolute paths from the diagnostic script cannot simply be copied into jailed API requests.

We would verify the resulting UID, namespace membership, cgroup placement, device access, and file ownership before admitting a job. Starting through jailer is a step in the security model, not proof that the whole model is correct.

Our environment lacked usable KVM and privileged host controls, so we could not validate boot, jailer behavior, or enforcement. We stopped there rather than replace those checks with a mock socket and call the result a sandbox test.

The remaining issues are concrete deployment risks we identified, not failures we personally reproduced.

We still have to build the guest agent. It must validate request lengths, manage subprocesses, separate stdout and stderr, enforce output caps, return exit status, and survive malformed requests.

We would run execution workers without guest-root privileges. We would also retain a host-side deadline that destroys the entire microVM. A compromised guest must not be able to negotiate away its termination.

Vsock can keep command transport separate from IP networking, but it does not make guest responses trustworthy. We would treat every returned byte as untrusted input.

We would audit this distinction first when testing resource controls.

A host cgroup's pids.max bounds host tasks, including VMM threads. It does not count every process running inside the guest. We need guest-side process controls as well.

We would not copy a “128 MB sandbox” setting into a 128 MB host memory ceiling and assume it works. Under load, we would inspect cgroup memory events, CPU throttling, host pressure, and guest failures together.

A network namespace is not an egress policy. NAT is not an authorization boundary.

Our first untrusted execution profile would have no virtual network interface. If package installation becomes necessary, we would add a separately tested network path with explicit policy for:

We would avoid the common quickstart-to-production shortcut of broad forwarding and masquerading rules. Those rules establish connectivity; they do not establish safe connectivity.

We have no local snapshot-restore measurement or verified restore sequence to publish. We would use the pinned release's snapshot documentation rather than improvise endpoints.

Our acceptance tests would cover artifact compatibility, backing disks, namespace and TAP reconstruction, stale connections, guest clocks, and readiness after restore. Snapshots containing credentials or customer state would be sensitive artifacts.

We would also test entropy and identity regeneration when cloning prepared state. We would not fork a memory image containing reusable secrets and assume each child becomes independent automatically.

A small VMM does not guarantee a fast application environment. General-purpose init services, device waits, SSH startup, and runtime imports can dominate the path to useful execution.

Our workaround would be a minimal, observable boot chain—not blanket removal of randomness-related services. We would measure where startup blocks before changing entropy handling or weakening guest configuration.

We cannot publish a raw Firecracker p50 or p95 from this review. We have no completed local run.

In our managed sandbox comparison, we use 717 ms create and 662 ms resume as E2B's command-executed readiness baseline. These timings describe the managed service, not raw Firecracker boot latency; our local KVM reproduction remained unperformed.

We would reject a comparison that puts a local InstanceStart acknowledgment beside that command-ready measurement.

We would benchmark four paths separately: fresh boot, snapshot restore, warm-pool allocation, and a subsequent command in an existing guest.

We would start the timer when we admit a request and stop it when we receive a nonce-bearing command result. We would report p50, p95, failures, and timeouts under increasing concurrency. Artifact preparation and queue delay would remain visible rather than disappear outside the timer.

A warm pool can reduce allocation delay, but it consumes capacity before requests arrive. It is not evidence of a faster cold boot.

If I spend my hour writing body copy in queue order, my first three days of work are three variations of the same page for the same company, ranked #1 partly because that vendor's parent directory counts each landing page separately.

The pros and cons of using cloud storage over local storage

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Cloud storage can be super convenient, but there are times when using local storage is the better move.

External hard drives and USB flash drives are usually easy ways to back up your files, especially since they don't require an internet connection or waiting for uploads. You just plug them into your computer, copy the files you need, and that's it. But cloud services like iCloud and Google Drive have also made it a lot easier to back up everything — and most of the time, it all happens automatically.

Your photos, videos, contacts and more are all synced to the cloud without having to copy each file manually. Plus, there's no risk of leaving your external drive somewhere or damaging it to the point where you lose your files. That doesn't mean cloud storage is perfect, and both have their pros and cons — it really comes down to choosing what works best for your needs. If you're unsure whether to choose local or cloud storage to back up your data, here's how the two stack up on access, cost and reliability.

One of the biggest advantages of using cloud storage is the convenience of not having to worry about your files. Once you upload them to the cloud, they're with you wherever you go. Just log in to your account on another laptop or phone, and you can access everything in just a few minutes. It's perfect for people who use multiple devices or who need to access files from their home computer at the office or college. Plus, most cloud services let you have your files synced automatically. That's the case with iCloud — once you enable it on your device, it automatically backs up everything every day.

Sharing files through a cloud service is also much easier than sharing them from local storage. Once a file is uploaded to the cloud, you can easily share it with someone with just a click  — cloud platforms generate a link so others can view and download that file. There's also collaboration, so multiple people can work on the same document or spreadsheet at the same time. Platforms like Google Docs even offer tools that allow each collaborator to leave comments in real time, perfect for workplaces and schools.

Safety is another big plus, since major cloud providers keep copies of your data spread across multiple servers and data centers. The chances of something bad happening and you losing all your data are very low – though not impossible. Plus, your cloud account can be protected with a password and two-factor authentication, meaning others can't just take your files from you.

Cloud storage relies on a stable internet connection, and you're prone to unexpected outages. A slow connection or an outage on your provider's servers could prevent you from downloading a file when you need it most. Also, uploading and downloading large files can take a while if you're using a slow connection. With an external SSD, you can copy gigabytes of files in just minutes. Internet has no impact on local storage, so it's something to consider if you're always traveling and rely on mobile data, for example.

Cost is another reason that might push people away from cloud services. While you only have to pay once to buy an external hard drive, cloud providers typically charge recurring subscription fees on a monthly or annual basis. Apple charges $9.99 per month in the US for 2TB of iCloud+, which comes to nearly $240 if you pay for two years — and the storage isn't really yours. Stop paying, and your files may be deleted after a while, not to mention possible price increases down the road. By comparison, you can find 2TB SSDs for around $290, but they'll always be yours, with no extra charges.

That doesn't mean you should ditch the cloud and rely entirely on local storage. Subscribing to a cloud service that allows you to back up all your files and access them from anywhere is very handy, and you can also benefit from local storage by offloading some of your larger files or those you really want to make sure you never lose.

Countdown: Midseason managerial appointments from worst to best

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Manchester United have endured their worst start to a season in four decades, dating back to November 1986, when Sir Alex Ferguson replaced Ron Atkinson in the Old Trafford dugout. It would prove to be one of the greatest midseason managerial appointments in football history, with Ferguson going on to deliver 13 league titles and two Champions League crowns before retiring in 2013.

Since then, however, United have suffered a staggering decline, cycling through managers, squandering hundreds of millions in the transfer market and gradually slipping from perennial title contenders to relegation strugglers. Their downfall has reached a new low in November 2026, with the Red Devils languishing in the bottom three of the Premier League.

Now, United are turning to Mauricio Pochettino in the hope that the Argentine can steady the ship and steer them away from the relegation zone. But if the post-Ferguson era has taught us anything, it's that changing managers midway through a season is no guarantee of a turnaround. With that in mind, we're ranking Manchester United's eight midseason managerial appointments since Ferguson's retirement, from worst to best.

Darren Fletcher was tasked with restoring some semblance of stability following the collapse of Ruben Amorim's tenure, but his brief spell in the dugout did little to stop United's downward spiral. The former Scotland international oversaw a draw at Burnley and an FA Cup defeat to Brighton before handing over the reins to Michael Carrick.

Fletcher's tenure was too short to make a lasting impact, but it nevertheless epitomized the chaos engulfing Old Trafford, with United desperately searching for answers to problems that extended far beyond the touchline.

Two years ago, Manchester United splashed out €11 million to prise Ruben Amorim away from Sporting CP, hoping the Portuguese tactician could replicate the success that had transformed him into one of Europe's most coveted young managers.

After ending Sporting's 19-year league title drought and guiding them to another Portuguese championship in 2024, Amorim arrived in England with a reputation for tactical innovation and an unwavering commitment to his 3-4-3 system. However, his methods failed to translate to the Premier League, with United slumping to a 15th-place finish in 2024/25 and losing the UEFA Europa League Final.

Amorim departed with a record of 24 wins, 18 draws and 21 defeats in 63 matches across all competitions, giving him a win percentage of just 38%. It was the lowest of any permanent Manchester United manager since Ferguson's retirement, with only interim boss Ralf Rangnick (37%) faring worse.

Rather than ushering in a new era of success, Amorim's appointment plunged United deeper into the wilderness.

Few figures have left a greater imprint on modern German and Austrian football than Ralf Rangnick. From transforming Hoffenheim into a Bundesliga force to laying the foundations for RB Leipzig's meteoric rise and reshaping Red Bull Salzburg's recruitment strategy, Rangnick built his reputation as one of European football's foremost tactical innovators.

Unfortunately for United, the man dubbed the "Godfather of Gegenpressing" proved incapable of implementing his philosophy at Old Trafford.

Appointed interim manager in November 2021 following Ole Gunnar Solskjaer's dismissal, Rangnick struggled to instill his high-intensity pressing approach in an underperforming squad. United eventually limped to a sixth-place finish, recording their lowest Premier League points tally at the time.

Rangnick had initially agreed to remain at United in a consultancy role for another two years, potentially giving him the opportunity to reshape the club's recruitment and sporting structure. Instead, he departed in the summer of 2022 to focus on managing Austria, leaving behind another failed experiment at Old Trafford.

Less than a year after celebrating their 13th Premier League title under Ferguson, Manchester United found themselves stumbling through one of the most disastrous title defenses in their history.

David Moyes had been handpicked to succeed his fellow Scotsman, but his reign unraveled spectacularly, prompting United to dismiss him in April 2014 and hand the reins to club legend Ryan Giggs.

The Welshman stepped into the unfamiliar role of interim player-manager, overseeing two wins, one draw and one defeat in United's final four matches. Although he briefly lifted the mood at Old Trafford, Giggs was unable to salvage a season that ended with United finishing seventh and missing out on European qualification.

His appointment was ultimately little more than a temporary solution, with Louis van Gaal arriving that summer to spearhead the club's next rebuild.

After spending 12 trophy-laden years at Old Trafford, Michael Carrick hung up his boots in 2018 and transitioned into coaching, working under Jose Mourinho and Ole Gunnar Solskjaer before being thrust into the managerial hot seat following the latter's dismissal in November 2021.

Despite his lack of senior managerial experience, Carrick made an immediate impression. He guided United to a Champions League victory at Villarreal, secured a hard-fought draw at Chelsea and oversaw a thrilling 3-2 win against Arsenal, ending his three-match tenure unbeaten.

Nevertheless, Carrick stepped aside to make way for Ralf Rangnick, whose subsequent struggles only strengthened the argument that United should have allowed their former midfielder to see out the campaign.

It would take another four years for Carrick to receive a second opportunity in the Old Trafford dugout.

Eighteen years after departing Manchester United as one of the most prolific strikers in the club's history, Ruud van Nistelrooy returned to Old Trafford in the summer of 2024, joining Erik ten Hag's coaching staff.

However, with United enduring another miserable start to the season, Ten Hag was dismissed in October, paving the way for Van Nistelrooy to take charge on an interim basis. The Dutchman wasted no time making his mark, overseeing two victories against Leicester City, a Europa League triumph over PAOK and a draw against Chelsea. United scored 11 goals in his four matches, offering a glimpse of the attacking fluency that had been sorely lacking under Ten Hag.

Van Nistelrooy departed unbeaten after Amorim's arrival, having done enough to convince Leicester City to appoint him as their next manager. His brief return to Old Trafford also served as a reminder of how former players can return to their clubs in new roles, a journey that the next generation of footballers will be hoping to emulate. Among those looking to forge their own legacy is LAFC youngster Jude Terry, who has already made the leap from the academy to the first team while helping LAFC2 reach the MLS NEXT Pro Cup Final.

When Michael Carrick returned to the Old Trafford dugout in January 2026, Manchester United were languishing in seventh place, with their hopes of Champions League qualification hanging in the balance. What followed was one of the most remarkable midseason revivals of the post-Ferguson era.

Carrick revitalized a struggling squad, restored confidence to a disillusioned dressing room and transformed United into one of the Premier League's most formidable teams. The Red Devils surged up the standings, eventually finishing third and securing their return to Europe's elite competition.

His achievements earned widespread praise and convinced United's hierarchy to hand him the permanent position. However, the honeymoon period proved painfully short-lived. Carrick struggled to build on his initial success, with United suffering a disastrous start to the 2026/27 campaign before his dismissal.

Nevertheless, his first six months in charge remain one of the most successful short-term managerial spells United have enjoyed since Ferguson's retirement.

Few managerial appointments have captured the imagination of Manchester United supporters quite like Ole Gunnar Solskjaer's return in December 2018.

Having scored the dramatic winning goal in the 1999 Champions League Final, Solskjaer was already immortalized in Old Trafford folklore. Nearly two decades later, he returned as interim manager following Jose Mourinho's dismissal, inheriting a fractured dressing room and a team that had lost its identity.

The Norwegian's impact was immediate. United won his first eight matches across all competitions and went unbeaten in his first 12 Premier League fixtures, with Solskjaer restoring the attacking freedom and confidence that had been missing during Mourinho's final months.

His crowning achievement came in March 2019, when an injury-ravaged United side overturned a 2-0 first-leg deficit against Paris Saint-Germain to reach the Champions League quarterfinals. Marcus Rashford's stoppage-time penalty completed one of the most extraordinary European comebacks in the club's history and helped convince United to appoint Solskjaer permanently.

However, the fairytale eventually lost its magic. United's form deteriorated toward the end of the season, and they finished sixth, missing out on Champions League qualification.

Solskjaer would subsequently guide United to consecutive top-three finishes before being dismissed in November 2021, but his initial resurgence remains arguably the most electrifying managerial bounce United have experienced in the post-Ferguson era.

More than anything, his appointment offered a reminder of what Manchester United had once been: a club defined by attacking football, dramatic comebacks and an unshakable belief that anything was possible.

The Chevy El Camino Launched In 1959, And This Is The Ute's Most Sought-After Model Year

📅 Nov 16, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

No, the Chevy El Camino wasn't the first to combine a two-door car with an open bed truck. The first production vehicle to do that was a Ford, but possibly not the one you're thinking of. A design engineer named Lew Bandt, then with Ford of Australia, developed what's often considered the original factory-made ute — the 1934 Ford Model 40-A Light Delivery. Depending on exactly how you define a ute, some folks point to the 1927 Volvo OV4 as well.

Of course, it was the 1957 Ford Ranchero that truly inspired the El Camino. The Ranchero was built on the Blue Oval's full-size platform of the time, and it became both relatively popular and relatively profitable for the automaker. In fact, it was successful enough that Chevy was inspired to get in on the ute action with its full-size 1959 El Camino. Originally a short-lived model only in production for that year and 1960, the El Camino would get a second chance at life in 1964. That's when the Bowtie brand revived the name and brought it back in slightly downsized form on the same platform as the intermediate-class Chevrolet Chevelle.

This is where things start to get interesting for gearheads. In 1970, Chevrolet introduced a 454-cubic-inch LS6 V8 that was reserved for both the Chevelle and its platform mate. There are tons of in-depth reviews of rewards credit cards. The truth is that the value proposition of each can be boiled down to essentials. We can talk about what each one does well – and does poorly – in a nutshell. That helps understand all of the details without getting lost, and gives a framework for thinking about each of the pieces that get pretty complicated.

Here’s how I think about several of the major cards, boiled down to their essentials:

Chase Sapphire Reserve® (See rates and fees) This is a good lounge card, a good spending card, but it’s expensive and the coupons aren’t nearly as great as Amex Platinum.

You can make the economics of the card work, especially if you can do high spend in bonus categories – 4x on direct airfare spend, 4x on hotel spend, 8x on Chase Travel portal spend and 3x on restaurants and dining is great.

I’ve put over $75,000 which earns me an additional $500 Southwest Airlines credit booking through Chase’s portal; Southwest’s A-List status; IHG Diamond status and a $250 Shops at Chase credit on top of the points from the spend. And I use the The Edit credits (2 x $250 on minimum two-night hotel stay) and plan to use the $250 hotel brand-specific credit (which can be stacked with The Edit credit and the $300 travel credit) and easily using the dining credits, so the card really works for me.

American Express Platinum Card® (see rates and fees) The most expensive mainstream card with an $895 annual fee, but the credits they give you are worth several times the fee. This is not a good card for spending, outside of airfare. But if you make the credits work, it covers the cost of the card and then some and pays for your lounge access. Amex has the biggest lounge network, but Centurion lounges aren’t as nice as Chase lounges and the food is much worse than Capital One lounges.

Capital One Venture X Rewards Credit Card (See rates and fees) It’s the least expensive of the premium rewards cards, the value premium card. Capital One lounges are the best bank lounges, but there aren’t enough of them. It’s a good card for spending at 2x transferrable points on everything, though the standard $95 annual fee Capital One Venture Rewards Credit Card (See rates and fees) is identical in earn.

The $395 annual fee is easily ‘covered’ by the card’s $300 travel portal credit (I just buy a single airine ticket each year) and the annual 10,000 points at card renewal. Then it gives me lounge access – the JFK lounge especially is great, and the two ‘Landings’ (at LaGuardia and Washington’s National airport) have the best lounge food in the United States. I basically view this access as becoming free given the cards fee vs. credits. It used to be my ‘catch all’ spending card before Bilt introduced its new card products.

Citi Strata EliteSM Card (See rates and fees.) offers transferable points and the only transfers to American Airlines AAdvantage. Their hotel portal earns 12x, and I’ve found favorable pricing. The credits more than cover the card’s annual fee, by a lot in the first year, and that’s an inducement to try it out. While I have it I like earning 6x at restaurants including restaurant delivery services every Friday and Saturday from 6 PM to 6 AM ET.

Bilt Palladium Card (See rates and fees) is a Bilt Points-earning machine.

It has the potential to be a 4x on everything card, but you could ignore every feature the card has and just treat it as ‘2x on everything’ and it’s still earning the most valuable points currency with the best points-transfer partners. Plus, Bilt Rewards has the biggest transfer bonuses, often up to 100%.

The card’s rollout was rocky at the start of the year and so it’s really undervalued now. I’m earning more points than before – a lot more – and then transferring them at 100% bonuses or more (which I’ve taken advantage of with Bilt to Air France KLM, British Airways, Japan Airlines, Virgin Atlantic).

Bilt Blue Card (See rates and fees) is really underappreciated as the best no annual fee in the market. It earns the most valuable transferable points, that can be spent through Bilt’s travel portal at 1.25 cents apiece or transferred to airline and hotel partners (more partners than Amex, Chase, Capital One or Citi). And since you can earn up to 1.25X points on rent and mortgage payments by matching the spend you do on the card, you can actually wind up with 2.25 points per dollar on your general spend. More than 2x earn with the best points makes it unbeatable at the price point.
