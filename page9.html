SC Ventures and SBI Join Soter Insure's Series B Alongside Follow-On from Token Bay Capital

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The strategic investment from two global institutional-grade players, alongside follow-on support from Token Bay Capital, joins a Galaxy-led round that also includes Coinbase Ventures, Franklin Templeton and Brevan Howard Digital.

ABU DHABI, UAE, Nov. 5, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Soter Insure, a specialist insurer for the digital asset economy, today announced that SC Ventures, the innovation and ventures arm of Standard Chartered, and Japanese financial group SBI Group have joined its Series B as strategic investors, adding to the round led by Galaxy.

This second close also includes follow-on investment from Token Bay Capital, a Soter backer since its Series A. The new investors join Coinbase Ventures, Franklin Templeton and Brevan Howard Digital, which participated in the first close announced in July 2026.

SC Ventures and SBI join Soter Insure's Galaxy-led Series B, alongside follow-on from Token Bay Capital

Insurance capacity remains one of the main missing pieces for banks and asset managers entering digital assets. Without it, institutions cannot apply the same risk standards they use for every other asset class. The backing of two global financial groups that are active in digital assets reflects a growing recognition that specialized coverage is a prerequisite for institutional participation.

Soter Insure will use the proceeds to increase its insurance capacity, deepen its underwriting and technology capabilities, and expand into mature and emerging digital asset markets, including Asia-Pacific through its Hong Kong presence, Europe and the US.

"Banks can't scale in digital assets without the risk protection they rely on everywhere else, and today that protection is still scarce," said Henson Orser, Founder and CEO of Soter Insure. "With SC Ventures and SBI Group joining Galaxy, Coinbase Ventures and Franklin Templeton, we now have traditional finance and digital asset natives behind the same goal: insurance capacity at institutional scale. Our strategic investors now span most of the jurisdictions and networks at the forefront of digital asset adoption. We're grateful to Token Bay Capital for backing us again, and to Galaxy for leading the round."

Herve Francois, Principal, Digital Assets Fund, SC Ventures, said: "Insurance is a critical but often overlooked part of the digital assets ecosystem. Our investment reflects our belief that Soter is uniquely positioned to build a regulated insurance platform that can scale to meet institutional needs and support the industry's continued development globally."

Yangte Kim, Representative in the UAE of SBI Group, added: "As digital assets continue to gain broader adoption, we believe the development of robust insurance solutions will be an increasingly important part of the ecosystem. Soter brings together deep expertise across regulation, digital assets and traditional insurance to address this important market need. Through our investment, SBI Group looks forward to supporting Soter's growth and contributing to the continued development of a more resilient global digital asset ecosystem."

For more information about Soter Insure, visit soter.insure.

Headquartered in Abu Dhabi and operating out of Bermuda, with offices in London, New York and Dubai, Soter Insure was incubated by Further Ventures and WebN Group. The company offers specialized digital asset insurance products for financial institutions, including Directors & Officers (D&O), Professional Indemnity (PI), Crime, Specie, Slashing and Smart Contract Failure coverage, uniquely denominated in both fiat and native digital assets.

SC Ventures is Standard Chartered's innovation, fintech investment and venture-building platform. Since 2018, it has built, operated and scaled ventures, and made minority investments, deploying more than $1 billion across 30+ portfolio companies globally. It builds, invests in and partners with ventures shaping the future of financial services across digital banking, digital assets, SME growth, financial inclusion, trade and cross-border infrastructure. In the Middle East, SC Ventures has grown from 2 ventures to 10 in two years across the UAE and Saudi Arabia, with Egypt next on the roadmap, supporting regional growth through disciplined venture building, strategic partnerships and regulated innovation aligned with national economic visions.

Founded in 1999, the SBI Group is a comprehensive internet financial group and a pioneer of internet-based financial services in Japan. The Group operates a Financial Services Business centered on securities, banking, and insurance, providing highly convenient products and services at competitive prices through the internet. In addition, the SBI Group operates Asset Management Business, PE Investment Business, Crypto-asset Business, and Next Gen Business on a global scale.

Token Bay Capital is a venture capital firm investing in digital asset and blockchain infrastructure. Founded in Hong Kong in 2021 and now headquartered in Abu Dhabi Global Market (ADGM), Token Bay Capital Limited is authorized and regulated by the Financial Services Regulatory Authority (FSRA) as a Venture Capital Fund Manager. Keith Wyness hits out at Aston Villa and Jamie Carragher over bust-up antics

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Aston Villa took the wrong option by threatening to take legal action against pundit Jamie Carragher for his recent comments.

That is according to former Villa chief executive Keith Wyness, speaking exclusively to Football Insider, who believes that both parties will have seen their reputations damaged by the saga.

This comes after the club threatened to take legal action against Carragher for suggesting that Villa will "never be a Manchester United" during a recent episode of the Overlap.

Wyness believes that while Carragher arguably overstepped the mark with his comments, his former club should have merely ignored the statements.

Instead, chairman Nassef Sawiris threatened to take legal action against the former Liverpool defender, only for him to go back on that plan after Carragher issued an apology.

The saga has garnered plenty of attention in recent days, and Wyness believes that Villa blew the whole thing out of proportion.

Villa's former chief Wyness - who served as CEO at Villa Park between 2016 and 2018 and now runs a consultancy business advising elite clubs - believes that legal action is "never" the right option for clubs to take in these sorts of situations.

The Scottish businessman is firmly against the notion of footballing matters being decided in courtrooms, unless it is absolutely necessary.

Speaking on the latest edition of Football Insider's Inside Track podcast, Wyness explained how neither Carragher nor Villa have emerged well from the situation.

"I think they've both come out of it diminished; in fact, I don't think it was handled particularly well, but I understand it," he said.

"I've been in that situation where you see stuff talked about your club in the media or individuals in your club in the media, and you want to react badly and strongly and sue people and do all the rest of it. It's football.

"And I'm afraid we've got to learn that, you know, it is yesterday's fish and chip paper, the old theory. And also today's podcasts and today's Overlaps are also yesterday's electronic fish and chip papers.

"So I think you know, I think Carragher did overstep it. I don't think he's done himself any favours; I think he looked very poor.

"So I think Villa could have ignored him quite well, but I understand Nassef sticking up for the club as well, but I think out of it all they both come a little bit diminished. Threatening legal action is never a great way to go, and this one would have been a difficult legal action to handle."

Former Villa scout Mick Brown told Football Insider earlier this week that his former club are likely to tie manager Unai Emery down to a new contract to end speculation over his future.

The Spaniard has been heavily linked with the Manchester United job of late, but Villa have no intention of losing him any time soon.

Brown feels that a new deal for Emery would be the perfect "public show" of "commitment" to both him and the project.

The former Arsenal boss is already under contract until 2029, but Brown believes that it would make sense for Villa to extend that further.

What the Democratic midterms sweep changes for U.S.-China relations

📅 2026-11-05  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

BEIJING — Democrats taking power from Republicans in Congress raises risks for U.S.-China tensions around Taiwan, while hardening tech competition, analysts said.

The Democrats swept this week's midterm elections, taking majorities in the House and Senate and flipping governors' offices in places like Texas, Nevada and Iowa. Most of the new election winners, however, won't begin their terms until January.

Before then, U.S. President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping will likely maintain their fragile truce by meeting twice: in Shenzhen this month, and in Miami, Florida, next month.

"As a lame duck who is increasingly focused on his personal legacy rather than the Republican Party's success, Trump may resist increased pressure for confrontation with China and continue his pursuit of 'strategic stability,'" Gabriel Wildau, managing director at Teneo, said in an email.

High on the Democrats' agenda is removing Trump's tariffs, as part of a broader focus on addressing domestic inflation, CNBC has learned.

"On balance as a result of the midterms the Democrats may nudge the U.S.'s China policy in a more hawkish direction through hearings and legislation, particularly on economic security issues and Taiwan," said Scott Kennedy, senior advisor and trustee chair in Chinese business and economics at the Center for Strategic and International Studies.

But any efforts will "be constrained by the executive branch's wide range of latitude and China's growing power and leverage, which can't be easily undone," he said.

Beijing has hosted a flurry of global leaders this year, while major economies have struggled to slow a surge of imports from China. Chinese dominance in rare earths has given Beijing leverage in trade talks with the U.S. and the EU.

"Taiwan remains the most dangerous issue in U.S.-China relations and, in my view, the area where a Democratic Congress is most likely to challenge Beijing," wrote George Chen, partner and co-chair of digital practice at consultancy The Asia Group.

"Congress has repeatedly shown a willingness to pursue Taiwan initiatives even when administrations are more cautious," he said, noting Trump's balancing act will likely become more difficult.

Nancy Pelosi's visit to Taiwan while House Speaker in 2022 angered Beijing, which considers the democratically self-governed island part of its territory. The U.S. maintains unofficial relations with Taiwan under a "One China" policy.

Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries (D-N.Y.), who is poised to become the first Black Speaker of the House in U.S. history, met in 2023 in New York with Tsai Ing-wen, who was then president of Taiwan.

During Trump's trip to Beijing in May, Xi warned that mishandling Taiwan would put U.S.-China relations in "great jeopardy."

Maintaining stability over Taiwan has grown more critical in the artificial intelligence race as the island is home to semiconductor giant TSMC, which manufactures many of Nvidia's chips.

Over the last several years, the U.S. government has tightened restrictions on the ability of Chinese companies to buy advanced chips from Nvidia, spurring Beijing's efforts to develop its own version of the technology.

U.S. stocks surged Wednesday after Trump accepted the Democrats' election wins, and oil prices fell following indications Washington would do more to end the Iran war. Apple and most "Magnificent Seven" tech stocks surged, despite declines in Nvidia.

But there's an "emergence of a broader consensus in Washington that America's technological lead should not be used to strengthen China's military or strategic capabilities," Chen said, noting a Democratic Congress may be motivated to be more aggressive.

"As a result, Beijing may discover that technology becomes a growing source of friction even if Trump and Xi continue to maintain relatively cordial summit diplomacy," he said.

The tech outlook ultimately depends on bigger questions about AI.

Chinese tech stocks climbed Thursday, extending a recent rebound from a sharp sell-off last month.

Beijing's latest regulatory intervention has helped support the market, said WisdomTree's Liqian Ren. But she said Chinese tech stock performance is ultimately tied more to global investor sentiment on AI.

"There's a lot of uncertainty on how much AI can deliver for U.S., or for China," she said.

—CNBC's Justin Papp and Kevin Breuninger contributed to this report.

CMS finalizes steeper Medicare physician pay cuts for 2027, delays restriction on third-party RPM vendors" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services finalized sweeping Medicare payment reforms it says will shift the program from volume-based "sick care" toward prevention, primary care and accountable care.

The 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule rule includes a roughly 2% reduction in baseline physician payment rates with targeted increases and new payment opportunities for accountable care, primary and chronic care and behavioral health. CMS finalized steeper physician payment cuts than it had proposed in July.

An unpublished PDF version of the 2,468-page final rule can be found here.

In a press release, CMS said it is "prioritizing prevention, strengthening primary care, cracking down on duplicate billing and reinforcing fiscal stewardship across Medicare."

The final rule modernizes physician payment structures to "reward health maintenance and coordinated primary care rather than disjointed, volume-based intervention," CMS said.

"Medicare should reward clinicians for keeping people healthy," HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, Jr. said in a statement. "Under President Trump’s leadership, we are putting prevention and primary care at the center of Medicare, closing payment loopholes, and cracking down on waste. This rule directs resources toward coordinated care that puts patients first. We are changing what Medicare pays for to change what our health system delivers."

Under the final rule, CMS will officially sunset the traditional Merit-based Incentive Payment System (MIPS) reporting option beginning with the CY 2031 performance period. Clinicians will have through CY 2030 to transition to the MIPS Value Pathways (MVPs), which already cover approximately 98% of clinicians, or Advanced Alternative Payment Models. This reflects two additional years of transition time compared to the proposed rule. The rule also finalizes three new MVPs focused on diabetes, hypertension, and hospitalists, and requires clinicians to report at least one specialty-specific "Core Measure" starting in CY 2027.

For physicians, while the proposed rule includes statutory payment increases, Medicare payment rates face an overall baseline decrease for 2027 due to the expiration of a temporary 2.5% legislative increase from the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) in 2026.

Under the Medicare Access and CHIP Reauthorization Act (MACRA), the CY2027 conversion factors reflect statutory updates of +0.75% for qualifying Alternative Payment Model (APM) participants and +0.25% for non-qualifying participants. The changes to the PFS conversion factors for CY 2027 include these updates as required by statute and an estimated -0.12% adjustment necessary to account for proposed changes in work RVUs for some services, according to CMS in a fact sheet.

For clinicians who don't qualify through an advanced alternative payment model, the conversion factor drops from $33.40 to $32.63, a 2.31% reduction. For qualifying APM participants, the conversion factor drops 1.83% from $33.57 to $32.95. In the draft rule issued in July, CMS projected cuts of 1.68% and 1.19%, respectively.

CMS is also addressing what it sees as overpayment and duplicate reimbursement when multiple services are delivered during the same patient encounter. Under the final rule, when an office visit and minor procedure occur on the same day, CMS will reduce payment by 20% for the lower-cost service, reducing duplication in payment by patients and the Medicare program for clinical work already covered in the procedure’s valuation.

CMS had initially proposed paying the higher-cost service in full and reducing payment for the other service by 50%.

Physician groups were quick to criticize CMS' payment changes in the final rule.

"Notwithstanding modest policy improvements to enhance value-based care through MSSP, MGMA is disappointed by CMS’s decision to finalize several destabilizing policies in the 2027 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule," Anders Gilberg, senior vice president of government affairs at the Medical Group Management Association, said in a statement.

"Conversion factor cuts of 1.83% for qualifying APM participants and 2.31% for all other clinicians further degrade an already fragile payment system and amplify severe financial challenges caused by years of inadequate Medicare reimbursement," Gilberg said.

Compounding this ongoing reimbursement decline, CMS will cut payments by 20% when office visits billed with modifier 25 are performed on the same day as certain procedures, "interfering with physician practices’ ability to offer same-day care," he noted.

MGMA is urging Congress to intervene by immediately fixing cuts to the conversion factor, along with changes to modifier 25. "To prevent annual disruption, Congress should prioritize comprehensive MACRA reform by passing the Patients First Act," Gilberg said.

The agency is overhauling the office/outpatient evaluation and management visit complexity add-on code, HCPCS code G2211. CMS is transitioning HCPCS code G2211 from a flat add-on payment to a modifier that would increase the payment of the associated E/M code by 16%. That would maintain an equal percentage increase across all levels of E/M codes, CMS said.

The agency also finalized a new modifier that would offset the costs incurred by practitioners in accountable care organizations (ACOs) when providing longitudinal care and care coordination. The modifier would only be available for practitioners participating in a Shared Savings Program ACO or participant providers in a Long-term Enhanced ACO Design (LEAD) Model ACO and would increase payment of the associated E/M visit by 32%, CMS said.

The final 2027 PFS rule also establishes new 340B reporting requirements. Covered entities will have to report certain Medicare Part D claims involving drugs purchased through the 340B Drug Pricing Program to the Medicare Part D Claims Data 340B Repository beginning with claims with a date of service on or after Jan.1, 2027. The deadline for the first quarterly submission is delayed until Sept. 30, 2027.

In the final rule, CMS tightened remote patient monitoring and remote therapeutic monitoring requirements but backed off an immediate restriction on contractors.

Beginning in 2027, as CMS outlined in the proposed rule, RTM services can only be furnished to established patients, and providers must provide a separately reportable initiating visit before billing for RPM or RTM services. CMS initially proposed only allowing payment for RPM or RTM services when performed by clinical staff employed by the practice and not when those services are delivered by third-party vendors. The agency provided a two-year reprieve on that requirement as it goes into effect Jan. 1, 2029, "giving practices that rely on contractors two additional years to adapt," CMS said.

Health tech companies, providers and lawmakers pushed back on the proposal to restrict third-party vendors, urging CMS to reconsider the proposed changes to preserve patients' access to these tech-enabled services. Many rural, small, and independent practices, as well as both urban and rural health systems, rely on specialized clinical and technology partners to make remote monitoring services available to their patients.

CMS finalized changes to the Medicare Shared Savings Program, the nation’s largest value-based payment program, to "accelerate accountable care service delivery, further CMS’ goal of aligning spending and value in Original Medicare, and support achieving other related strategic objectives," the agency said in a fact sheet.

The overhaul to the MSSP program aims to make ACO participation more financially attractive, encourage organizations to take on risk, expand beneficiary participation and ease some quality-reporting requirements.

The agency says the changes are projected to grow the program by adding up to 1 million assigned beneficiaries in an average year and produce $5.5 billion in net federal savings over 10 years. CMS also projects the changes will generate an overall increase in shared savings net of losses to ACOs by $1.1 billion over a decade.

The final rule changes the Shared Savings Program’s benchmarking and financial methodology to balance incentives between Level E of the BASIC track and the ENHANCED track. The shared savings rate for Level E of the BASIC track increases 50% to 60% and reduce the maximum weight used in calculating the positive regional adjustment for lower-spending ACOs participating under the ENHANCED track from 50% to 35%.

The agency is increasing the prior savings adjustment's scaling factor from 50% to 75%, allowing more of an ACO's previous savings to be reflected in future benchmarks.

Under the final rule, CMS will risk-adjust the 5% cap on benchmark increases, potentially allowing ACOs that care for sicker, higher-cost patients to receive larger upward adjustments.

Data shows that patients aligned with an ACO are less likely to experience uncontrolled diabetes or hypertension and are more likely to receive recommended preventive care, according to CMS. In performance year 2024, 75% of the 476 participating ACOs earned shared savings totaling $4.1 billion, while generating net savings for the Medicare Trust Funds of approximately $2.5 billion compared to projected spending benchmarks.

Under the final 2027 PFS rule, approved ACOs may reduce or eliminate Part B cost-sharing for many items and services for assigned beneficiaries, directly lowering out-of-pocket costs for essential care. That change goes into effect April 1, 2027.

The rule also increases shared savings rates for eligible ACOs, provides new incentives for first-time participants and sets more predictable spending targets. And, the rule simplifies electronic health record reporting requirements, streamlines patient notice mandates and removes outdated administrative hurdles.

New research suggests that caffeinated energy drinks and stimulant supplements are associated with significantly higher risks of serious heart problems, including cardiac arrest and heart attack in young adults.

The findings, to be presented at the American Heart Association (AHA) Scientific Sessions 2026 November 6–9 in Chicago, are preliminary and can’t prove that energy drinks cause heart problems.

Still, the results highlight concerning cardiovascular effects of these popular products, particularly among adults under 35 who consume them regularly.

“These products are marketed as everyday pick-me-ups and sold next to soda and bottled water, so most people assume they carry no real risk,” study author Sai Samayamanthula, a fourth-year medical student at the University of Virginia School of Medicine in Charlottesville, VA, said in a news release.

“For many healthy people, if these products are consumed occasionally, the risk is probably small. However, individuals who consume energy drinks or other stimulant-containing supplements regularly can quickly exceed the recommended daily limit for caffeine, so it’s important for people to know that there may be a risk of cardiovascular harm with these products,” Samayamanthula continued.

Researchers from the University of Virginia School of Medicine analyzed 28,345 reports submitted to the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) between 2017 and 2025.

Of these, 854 involved caffeinated energy drinks or stimulant supplements, while the remaining reports involved non-stimulant products.

Compared with non-stimulant products, cardiac arrest was reported approximately 11 times more often with energy drinks and stimulant supplements. Heart attacks were reported 4.5 times more often, while ventricular arrhythmias and palpitations were reported about four times more frequently.

Nearly 45% of heart-related reports involved adults ages 18 to 34.

“Our findings support the case for more systematic safety monitoring of caffeine- and stimulant-containing products. In addition, healthcare professionals should remember to ask their younger patients who have palpitations, chest pain or fainting spells if they recently or regularly consume these products,” Samayamanthula said.

Gregory M. Marcus, MD, professor of medicine at the University of California, San Francisco School of Medicine, and chair of the 2026 AHA scientific statement on caffeine and cardiovascular disease, wasn’t involved in the study, but said the research “raises concerns that products with very high amounts of caffeine may be harmful to the cardiovascular system.”

“Although there are biological reasons why that connection could be possible, the study does not prove that caffeine products cause these events, because other factors may have influenced the results found in this analysis,” Marcus said in a news release.

“More research is needed to understand whether these stimulant products are truly responsible for the reported heart-related events.”

Cheng-Han Chen, MD, board certified interventional cardiologist and medical director of the Structural Heart Program at MemorialCare Saddleback Medical Center in Laguna Hills, CA, said the findings, although concerning, are not surprising. Chen wasn’t involved in the study.

“We know that energy drinks can have significant effects on the cardiovascular system, primarily because of their stimulant properties,” he told Healthline.

“Caffeine can increase blood pressure and heart rate, while other ingredients, such as guarana and taurine, may further contribute to cardiovascular stimulation and increased stress hormone levels. Chen agreed that occasional energy drink consumption is unlikely to have long-term consequences for heart health in most healthy individuals. However, he said that “frequent or excessive consumption can potentially increase the risk of hypertension and cardiac arrhythmias.”

Not Every Slow Query Needs a New Index

📅 Nov 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

What a SQL review taught me about assumptions, AI-written queries, and common-sense tuning

A development team asked me to review the SQL behind a change-data publishing job before they moved it from once a day to once an hour. In QA, one full run took 10 to 12 minutes. After the review, the same run took under 30 seconds. In production, most of its queries now finish in under a millisecond.

Almost none of that came from adding indexes. It came from reading the queries, asking what the job actually needed, and checking each assumption against real data. This post walks through what we found, in the order we found it, and what I would tell any team (or any AI assistant) writing SQL against a busy production database.

The job polls a PostgreSQL read replica for rows that changed since its last run and publishes them as events. It is a classic checkpoint pattern:

I'll use a generic schema for the examples:

Every table has created_at and updated_at. The source system also maintains updated_at_num, a numeric YYYYMMDDHHMISS copy of updated_at, and that column is indexed on every table. Keep that last detail in mind.

Every change-window filter in the job looked like this:

The intent is reasonable: if a row was never updated, fall back to its creation time. But PostgreSQL can only use a B-tree index when the predicate matches the indexed expression. Wrap the column in COALESCE, and the index on updated_at (or updated_at_num) is no longer a candidate. Every window query became a sequential scan of the whole table.

The COALESCE also encoded an assumption: that updated_at can be null. So before changing anything, we checked:

Zero on every table. The source system sets both timestamps when a row is created, so the fallback could never fire. The fix was to drop the function and filter on the indexed column directly:

That one change turned every window query into an index range scan.

The COALESCE wasn't the only defensive habit. One query filtered on TRIM(a.account_type) = 'STAFF'. In QA that is harmless: small tables, fast scans, correct results. In production it does two quiet things:

No padded values, so the TRIM came out, and the queries became consistent with each other.

I want to be clear about where this comes from. The developers I work with write good SQL. But we are now in an era where a lot of SQL is drafted by AI assistants, and they are very good at producing queries that return the right answer. They are much less good at knowing your data. A model will happily add COALESCE, TRIM, LOWER or DISTINCT "just in case", because in the abstract each one is safer. On a 10,000-row QA table you'll never notice. On a 10-million-row production table, each one can turn an index lookup into a full scan.

The result being correct does not mean the assumptions behind it are. Before a query ships, check it against the data's real shape: null counts, value distributions, duplicates, and how skewed the busiest keys are. Or ask a database engineer to review it. It never hurts.

The partner-count query looked like this:

The outer query was narrowed to one organization and a one-hour window: 59 candidate rows. The subquery wasn't narrowed at all. PostgreSQL read 1.77 million membership rows, built a distinct list of 831 IDs, then matched that list against the 59 candidates. That was 31,395 buffers and 83 ms to return a count of zero.

A note on IN versus EXISTS: PostgreSQL already turns IN (subquery) into a semi-join, so the two often plan the same way. The DISTINCT adds nothing, since a semi-join never returns a row twice, and it can push the planner toward building the full list first. Switching to EXISTS helps. The bigger win is adding the filter. If the outer query is limited to something you also know about the subquery's rows, say so in the subquery too. Don't rely on the planner to infer it.

The slowest query in the job didn't return any data. It computed the run's ceiling:

It took 853 ms and about 1 million buffers to return one number. The plan showed three different behaviours:

PostgreSQL has a neat shortcut here. As the CREATE AGGREGATE documentation explains, a MIN- or MAX-like aggregate can be answered from an index, because SELECT max(col) FROM tab is equivalent to SELECT col FROM tab ORDER BY col DESC LIMIT 1. The planner walks the index backward and takes the first non-null value. It also works on partitioned tables and UNION ALL views, by merging each child's index. It does not work through a join. The planner source (planagg.c) limits the shortcut to queries that reference exactly one table, so the join branch had to build the whole join just to find its largest value.

That is where understanding the use case matters more than tuning. This value is an upper bound for the next change window. It doesn't have to be the exact newest staff row; it only has to be at least as high. Dropping the join and taking max(updated_at_num) over all of staff_links gives a bound that is never lower, and costs a single index probe. The join filter still applies in the query that reads the window.

The same review turned up a second easy win. The ceiling was global, but the job ran it once per scope, inside a loop, so it returned the same value several times per run. Computing it once per run cost nothing to change.

The password_changed_at branch I left alone. More on that in Lesson 6.

My first round of EXPLAIN ANALYZE used a one-hour window, the job's steady state. Most queries looked great: under a millisecond. Then I noticed how often the plans said (never executed). In that hour, no organization had changed, so every join after the first step was skipped. The plan was cheap because there was no work, not because the work was cheap.

Widening the window to 30 days told a different story. One query that ran in 0.7 ms on the hourly window chose a completely different plan:

The cause was a row estimate. The planner expected 188 primary memberships and got 15,368, about 80 times more. With 188, starting from memberships looks cheap. A likely reason is how PostgreSQL combines filters: by default it multiplies each condition's selectivity as if they were independent, so correlated filters (type, deleted flag, end date) can be badly underestimated. Check that statistics are fresh with ANALYZE first. If the columns really are correlated, CREATE STATISTICS can tell the planner how they relate.

So I now test every change-data query with three windows:

The first run is easy to forget. Here it was fine: page one of the full-history query took about 20 ms, because the planner could stop as soon as it had 500 rows. But you only know that by running it.

After the rewrites, I had a list of index candidates that would have made every remaining query near-instant: one on password_changed_at, a partial index for a rare account type, and a few more. Before building any of them, I added up what an hourly run actually cost without them:

The slowest single statement was under a second, far from any timeout. Every index you add costs writes, storage and maintenance forever, and here the database was a replication target that would pay for each one on every write. So most of the candidates were dropped. That is a fine outcome. Not every slow-looking query needs an index; sometimes it needs nothing.

One query was different, because its cost multiplied. It loaded full account details in chunks of 100 IDs, and each chunk joined org_members on account_id, a column with no index. Every chunk scanned a 9-million-row table: about 0.7 s.

That is the test I use now: don't ask whether a query is slow, ask whether its cost grows with the data or the backlog. Fixed costs can usually be tolerated. Costs that multiply need an index. We added exactly one: org_members (account_id).

The new index worked exactly as planned: the org_members step went from scanning 5.4 million rows to reading 2,217 buffers. Then I looked at the total for the chunk query. It had gone from 723 ms to 26.9 seconds.

With org_members suddenly cheap, the planner switched to a nested loop and probed memberships once per organization row through an index on org_id. That index holds every membership in the organization, one per member account. The accounts in this chunk belonged to very large organizations, so each probe walked about 14,000 entries to find the one primary membership it needed.

The planner expected roughly one row per probe. When it knows a value in advance, PostgreSQL can look it up in its most-common-values statistics. Here each probe's value came from the outer row at run time, so the planner estimated an average number of rows per organization. The handful of very large organizations didn't fit that average, and re-running ANALYZE doesn't change how the estimate is made.

The fix was another index, but a very small one: a partial index containing only the rows the query asks for.

It holds about 15,000 rows instead of 650,000, so it is small and cheap to maintain, and each probe now lands directly on the row it needs. PostgreSQL uses a partial index only when it can prove, at planning time, that the query's WHERE clause implies the index predicate. The docs point out that a bind parameter such as membership_type = $1 can't prove that. Because this query uses membership_type = 'PRIMARY' AND is_deleted = false as literal values, the planner can match the partial index without any code change.

The lesson isn't "avoid indexes". It's that an index changes the plan around it, not just the step it targets. Re-run the full EXPLAIN ANALYZE after every index, and read the total, not just the line you were fixing.

Both indexes went in with CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY, so writes kept flowing. A few things I check every time:

1. Look for old transactions first. Per the CREATE INDEX docs, a concurrent build waits before each table scan for transactions that have modified the table, and after the second scan for any transaction holding a snapshot older than that scan. My pre-check found 65 sessions from a replication tool sitting idle in transaction for 28 days. Before cancelling anything, check whether they actually matter:

Ours held no snapshot, no transaction ID and no locks on the target tables, so they couldn't block the build or hold back vacuum. That's a cleanup item, not a blocker.

2. Run with autocommit on. CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY cannot run inside a transaction block, and some SQL clients wrap scripts in one.

3. Decide on lock_timeout deliberately. It lets a build fail fast instead of queueing behind a long transaction, but a build that fails partway leaves an invalid index behind. I left it unset and watched the progress view instead.

4. Check for invalid indexes afterwards. CREATE INDEX ... IF NOT EXISTS sees an invalid leftover's name and skips it with only a NOTICE. Drop it with DROP INDEX CONCURRENTLY and build again, or rebuild it with REINDEX INDEX CONCURRENTLY.

5. Build smallest to largest, and watch replication lag during the big ones.

This is what I now run through for any SQL headed to a large production table, whether a person or an AI wrote it.

Looking back, the biggest gains came from questions, not configuration:

None of that needed database expertise the developers couldn't learn. It needed someone to explain how the planner reads a predicate, why a function hides an index, and why QA data isn't evidence for production. Once that was clear, the developer rewrote the queries himself, and the run time in QA went from 30–50 minutes to under 30 seconds.

Some tuning really is just common sense and basic troubleshooting: read the plan, check the data, and ask what the query is for. If your team writes SQL, especially with an AI assistant drafting it, get a database engineer to look at it before it meets production data. It never hurts, and sometimes it saves you an hour-long first run, or a 27-second query you didn't know you had.

$72,000 ÷ 0.60 = $120,000 revenue needed (example figure)

Example: 35 working hours a week, 10 of them non-billable, so 25 billable hours × 48 working weeks = 1,200 billable hours a year.

$120,000 ÷ 1,200 = $100 an hour (example figure)

Some clients may prefer a fixed price for a whole project. Break the work into tasks, estimate each in hours, then add a contingency for the unknowns: the third-party API with thin docs, the legacy code nobody has touched in years.

1. Are contestants paid to be on Love Is Blind? Former star Matthew Duliba said they are paid $5,000 to appear on the pod period of the show.

The real money is made in sponsorships afterward, as season two alums Deepti Vempati and Natalie Lee said they each made about $500,000 as influencers in under two years after their episodes aired in 2022.

2. Where do the cast members sleep while in the pods? According to Netflix, the contestants slept on cot beds in trailers next to the lounges during season one, but for season two they upgraded to hotel rooms to be more comfortable. They're escorted to and from their rooms by staff so they can't secretly see anyone.

"You're actually at a hotel," season four's Micah Lussier shared on TikTok in 2025. "That's where you're staying, and then the pods that you see is a set, so think of a big studio, and then the bathrooms are outside in trailers."

Shayne Jansen shared of their vacations, "You don't have your phone or anything on you."

4. The pods portion of the experiment lasts about 10 days, with 40-50 hopeful singles serving as the initial cast. But over the course of the process, some singles are let go.

"There was a certain point where we did a whittling down of people just to focus internally on the people who were really connecting, to allow them more time," Love Is Blind creator Chris Coelen told E! News. "We ended up having maybe 20-25 people in the pods at that point."

5. The first dates between all of the singles were set up speed-dating style rotation, with everyone spending eight to 10 minutes with each person of the opposite sex. From then on, there were no limitations put on who you could talk to, when you could talk to them and for how long, as long as a production intermediary set it up as they were unable to communicate with each other outside of the pods.

6. According to Sal Perez from season two, the singles are given "talking points" and "questions" before heading into their dates, but they don't have to use them.

"There were kind of topics for the day," Kyle Abrams added in an interview with E! News. "It was good if there was a lull in conversation, you just refer to the little notebook and you can drum up conversation."

"During the first 10 days of filming," she said, "we could choose from catered food that included options like grilled fish, chicken, steak, vegetables, pasta, rice, and fruit."

“Everyone gets engaged on that one day. And then the next day is the big reveal day," Kelly said, per Cosmo. "And then the couples separate and go back through their own door. "You have scheduled filming days, like every other day," she explained. "So of course, you get a ton of time off camera to get to know each other without them in your face."
