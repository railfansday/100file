‘Root-to-Tip’ Eating: Are You Missing Out on Fiber by Discarding Scraps?

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Eating fruit and veggie scraps may boost fiber intake and cut food waste.

This study from King’s College London found that peels, skins, seeds, and stalks of fruits and vegetables—parts often discarded—contained significantly more dietary fiber than the commonly eaten portions in 10 of 12 produce types analyzed.

In some cases, the fiber content was up to five times higher in these “root-to-tip” components.

These findings, recently published in the International Journal of Food Sciences and Nutrition, are noteworthy, since around 95% of U.S. adults aren’t getting enough fiber. What’s more, around one-fifth of all food produced globally is wasted every year.

The researchers identified 12 fresh produce items that were deemed suitable for “root-to-tip” inclusion:

In the laboratory analysis, they measured the dietary fiber content of both the “root-to-tip” components (skins, peels, seeds, stalks) and the parts that people typically eat.

The key findings of the study showed that in 10 of the 12 produce types, the “root-to-tip” components contained more fiber than the parts typically consumed.

However, broccoli stalks contained 28.4% less fiber than the florets that people usually eat, meaning individuals are already consuming the most fiber-rich part.

Most participants in focus groups saw the nutritional value of the “root-to-tip” approach as more of a motivator than its sustainability.

However, these findings show that a “root-to-tip” approach to many fruits and vegetables could not only support higher dietary fiber intake but also reduce food waste.

Kristin Kirkpatrick, president of KAK, Consulting, LLC, and a dietitian at the Cleveland Clinic Department of Wellness & Preventive Medicine, said that the takeaway isn’t to eat every peel or seed, but to look for safe, simple ways to get more fiber from the foods you’re already buying. Kirkpatrick wasn’t involved in the study.

This applies to some of the plants analyzed in this study, but not all produce. For instance, you can only eat a kiwi that’s peeled. This is still a better choice than no kiwi (or a processed-food snack in its place) at all.

Fiber is important for promoting health and preventing chronic conditions such as cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, colorectal cancer, and obesity.

“Fiber does much more than support regular bowel movements. It helps regulate blood sugar, supports beneficial gut bacteria, and certain types of soluble fiber can help lower LDL cholesterol by increasing bile acid elimination,” noted Michelle Routhenstein, a preventive cardiology dietitian at Entirely Nourished, who wasn’t involved in the study.

On average, adults are recommended to get 25–30 grams (or more, depending on age) of fiber daily.

Both nutrition experts told Healthline that people generally aren’t getting enough fiber because of their food options or choices.

“Most likely because individuals are not consuming enough plants, and overconsuming processed and ultra-processed foods which are typically very low in fiber,” said Kirkpatrick. “Simply not eating enough fruits and vegetables or choosing white refined grains over whole grains can be why a person’s diet is lacking fiber.”

Routhenstein added, “I think it is important that when we talk about fiber we point out that it doesn’t have to mean completely changing how you eat. I encourage people to incorporate fiber-rich ingredients into familiar, culturally meaningful foods they already enjoy.”

“Start with small increases with foods you enjoy and build gradually to allow your digestive system to adjust.”

It is important to always research any new plant parts before eating them, wash thoroughly, and consult a healthcare professional if you have digestive conditions or concerns.

And remember that not all produce scraps are edible or recommended.

“Any dietary change will be dependent on personal preference. Some individuals may not enjoy or may not know how to add components of a plant they are not used to,” said Kirkpatrick.

She noted that, based on the study’s examples, an easier option might be to add more kiwi peels and seeds. “In fact, kiwi skin is probably the most practical fruit peel to highlight for consumers,” she said.

Routhenstein added, “I think there is a knowledge gap that the skins, peels, from the root to the tip are edible and nutritious, so we need to start there.”

She suggested that one way to start is by eating the skins of apples, pears, and well-scrubbed carrots. Trim and thinly slice broccoli stalks for stir-fries or soups. Sautée beet greens and stems just as you would spinach. Freeze appropriate vegetable trimmings, such as carrot ends and celery leaves, for homemade broth.

“Root-to-tip eating helps reduce food waste by encouraging us to use more of the edible portions of fruits and vegetables rather than discarding them. It also maximizes the nutritional and economic value of the food we purchase while making better use of the water, land, and energy required to produce it,” said Routhenstein.

“The key is finding simple, enjoyable ways to incorporate these parts into meals we already eat.”

AI Agents Are Becoming a Commodity. Your Company DNA Can Be Your Competitive Advantage

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Imagine two banks using AI agents to handle customer inquiries. One has built its reputation on flexibility, waiving fees and showing empathy when customers face financial hardship. The other prides itself on transparency and consistency, applying the same clear rules to everyone. When a customer disputes an overdraft fee, both agents might provide the same generic response unless they’ve been trained to reflect their bank’s approach to customer service.

Most organizations assume that the solution to this problem is a better AI model. But the real issue is often a lack of organizational context.

According to McKinsey, nearly nine in ten respondents say their organizations regularly use AI in at least one business function. While widespread adoption promises efficiency and productivity gains, it also makes technology less of a differentiator. As AI agents take on more work, make decisions and interact with customers on a company’s behalf, an important question emerges: Do those agents truly understand the company they’re working for?

Microsoft’s 2026 Work Trend Index highlights the growing integration of AI agents into organizational workflows and the importance of human judgment in how that work is designed. Yet, many businesses are building on the same foundation models and AI platforms. Without the right organizational context, their agents risk looking and sounding alike, eroding the qualities that make each customer experience different. The difference often lies in the decisions employees make every day, drawing on experience, customer knowledge and an understanding of what their organization values. These are the nuances that generic AI systems may struggle to replicate without additional guidance.

What if a company could encode its corporate identity into its AI systems? The challenge for organizations is ensuring these agents reflect what makes the company unique: its secret sauce and competitive edge.

AI outputs can be technically correct but still fall short of how a company expects work to be done. For example, an AI agent at a luxury hotel responds to a guest’s request for a restaurant recommendation. It books a table at a highly rated restaurant without asking about the guest’s dining preferences or dietary restrictions. While the reservation might be made quickly, the guest could end up somewhere with few suitable options, an experience that reflects poorly on the hotel.

Agents need more than basic training. Like employees, they require ongoing guidance, performance monitoring and coaching to ensure their actions reflect the company’s approach to customer service.

Beyond workflows and policies, AI agents need to understand the company’s DNA: the institutional knowledge, expertise, decision-making principles, culture and values that shape how work gets done.

With AI capabilities becoming increasingly commoditized, company DNA becomes more valuable. Used right, AI should amplify what differentiates a company, not dilute it. That means teaching agents how work actually gets done, including the informal practices and judgment calls that contribute to success.

AI agents can also help preserve this knowledge as organizations grow, ensuring it remains accessible even as teams change.

Embedding company DNA into AI doesn’t necessarily require overhauling existing systems. Leaders can start with several practical steps.

Define success metrics a competitor couldn’t copy: If agents are measured only on resolution rates and handling time, organizations risk creating the same experience as everyone else. Leaders should define what success looks like for their company, whether that’s helping customers feel supported during financial hardship or delivering the consistency they expect. Those differences should shape how agent performance is measured.

Codify the judgment calls, not just the rules: Mission statements and standard operating procedures provide a starting point, but company DNA often shows up in how employees handle exceptions. When do they make an exception to a policy? How do they verify information or draw on past experiences? Coaching notes, quality reviews and conversations handled by top performers can help capture this knowledge and make it accessible to AI agents. Organizations can then use these examples to show agents how experienced employees navigate situations that don’t have a straightforward answer.

Map how work actually gets done: Leaders can create a “work graph” that captures how work moves across a team, including how customer support teams prioritize issues or managers make decisions. This should include the informal workarounds employees use when established processes fall short. These workarounds can reveal gaps between formal procedures and the realities of day-to-day work. By documenting these practices, organizations can help agents understand not just what gets done, but how and why.

Write down how to break a tie: Rules can’t anticipate every situation. Agents need guidance on how to navigate trade-offs, such as balancing policy with customer goodwill or speed with empathy. These principles should also define when an agent needs to involve a human, so escalation becomes a designed decision, never a failure.

Onboard agents like new hires and let your best people grade them: Before wider deployment, organizations should put agents through the same kinds of scenarios used to train new employees, including role-play exercises, difficult customer conversations and situations requiring. Experienced employees can review these interactions and identify where an agent’s response falls short of how the company would approach the situation.

Make performance reviews a two-way coaching process: Review AI agent performance regularly, and have your people update its guidance as products, policies and customer expectations change. In return, AI interactions reveal where your people need help. For example, if the AI keeps escalating billing disputes and those calls still go unresolved, that’s a clear coaching priority. The result is a continuous feedback loop: people make the AI better, and the AI makes people better.

In the coming years, powerful AI agents will be available to nearly every company. Those that codify and embed their identity into AI will be better positioned to scale without sacrificing what sets them apart.

Embedding company DNA is only the first step. Organizations must continuously guide and improve how agents perform, so they reinforce what makes the company different. Just as organizations evolve, so must their AI agents.

EpochGeo develops spatiotemporal AI/ML, which examines information across location and time, alongside data-management tools and mission-focused multi-source analytics. Its technology fuses sources including radio-frequency and signals intelligence for mission-critical defense applications. Those capabilities broaden BlackSky’s existing imagery and analytics business by supporting analysis across space-based and terrestrial inputs, with the stated aim of helping users rapidly detect and respond to relevant changes.

BlackSky described the acquisition as a response to defense and national-security organizations investing in space-based intelligence and seeking to use AI to fuse diverse data sources, understand patterns and anticipate developments. The company’s broader strategy is to deliver real-time intelligence on locations and events affecting national and economic security.

Founded in 2018, EpochGeo counts major U.S.-based defense prime contractors and the U.S. government among its customers. BlackSky will continue supporting those customers through existing contracts. The acquisition therefore includes established defense relationships alongside the analytics technology.

BlackSky CEO Brian O’Toole said EpochGeo has developed and deployed mission-critical analytics with end users in secure operational environments, including support for U.S. combatant commands and international ministries of defense. “These are exactly the capabilities our largest customers are asking us to provide as an integrated extension to real-time imaging services,” he said.

Shortly after the acquisition’s closing, BlackSky intends to grant restricted stock unit awards to as many as 43 EpochGeo employees as an incentive to join the company in new, non-executive roles. The awards would cover up to 565,000 shares of BlackSky common stock if all of the prospective employees agree to join.

The grants will use an inducement equity incentive plan approved by BlackSky’s board on October 29, 2026 without stockholder approval under the New York Stock Exchange’s employment-inducement exception. One-quarter of each award is scheduled to vest on December 10, 2027, followed by quarterly vesting of one-sixteenth of the underlying shares. Vesting remains subject to each recipient’s continued service through the applicable date.

The acquisition links EpochGeo’s deployed defense analytics and customer relationships to those distribution channels. Sony’s iconic Walkman headphones are back in the USB-C MDR-3R and, after quickly selling out on launch day, they’ve now been restocked. And, after using them, I can see why they sold out so quickly.

The MDR-3R are Sony’s revival of the wired over-ear headphones that many used with a classic Walkman player. The retro style still looks great in 2026, but using an original pair is a bit of a headache in today’s age of headphone jack-less smartphones. So it was nice to see Sony give this a revival with USB-C.

Update 11/24: After we first published our hands-on with MDR-3R last week, Sony’s USB-C headphones sold out pretty quickly again. As of today, though, they’re back in stock and still shipping in early December – don’t miss out!

While the style won’t be for everyone, I think these work brilliantly. The sound quality is excellent and having a pair of headphones that doesn’t fully enclose my ears is pretty refreshing. There’s no noise cancellation, but with open-ear earbuds becoming more popular, I actually really like these as a similar solution (though high volumes will definitely be heard by those around you). I’m not fully cut off from the world around me, but I’m also able to get good sound quality. Lows are the weakness here, with unimpressive bass drops, but the sound quality is crisp and clear and pretty neutral, and the open-ish style makes it feel like the room around me is filled with music.

The physical hardware is great. While a plastic and metal build is objectively not as comfy as a premium pair of ANC headphones, the extremely light weight helps MDR-3R become an afterthought. You simply forget you’re wearing them after a few minutes.

The earpads are swappable, with orange and blue options included in the box.

As for the USB-C cable, it’s fully plug-and-play. No app needed and it worked across every device I tested (including Pixel, which doesn’t work with all USB-C audio output). I do wish the cable was perhaps a few inches longer, but it’s long enough to wear the headphones with my phone in my front pocket. There’s a remote along the cable for play/pause and volume adjustments, though I think it’s a little too high on the cable.

For $59.99, these are a fun pair of USB-C headphones to have on hand. They sound good, work great, and don’t cost an arm and a leg. I can see why they sold out so quickly on launch day – in just 10 hours, Sony told me.

Sony MDR-3R are back in stock now and shipping in early December. If you want a pair, I say don’t wait. They’re well worth at least trying if you can get them for retail price – please don’t give in to the scalpers selling these at double, they’re not worth that.

High-Speed Nanoscale Brain Imaging Uncovers Hidden Synapses

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Researchers dramatically accelerated electron tomography to map large volumes of brain tissue at 15-nanometer 3D resolution. The breakthrough captures fine neural structures missed by conventional connectomes, including rapid-fire electrical synapses and uncharacterized protrusions where neurons appear to “nibble” neighboring glial cells.

To understand how billions of neurons orchestrate thoughts, memories, and movement, neuroscientists construct connectomes, detailed wiring diagrams mapping how brain cells connect and communicate. While macro-scale MRI scans chart major white-matter highways and standard electron microscopy traces individual chemical synapses, scaling up to large tissue volumes has historically forced a difficult compromise: sacrificing nanoscale structural detail for imaging speed.

As a result, even the most ambitious brain wiring maps have routinely missed ultra-fine cellular architectures, including electrical synapses (also called gap junctions), microscopic channels that enable direct, near-instantaneous electrical communication between adjacent neurons.

Now, in a study published November 19 in Nature Methods, researchers at Harvard Medical School (HMS) and the University of California San Diego have overcome that bottleneck by supercharging electron tomography—a 3D imaging technique that reconstructs nanoscale architecture by tilting tissue samples and capturing views from multiple angles.

Led by co-senior authors Dr. Wei-Chung Allen Lee (associate professor of neurobiology at HMS and associate professor of neurology at Boston Children’s Hospital) and Dr. Mark Ellisman (UC San Diego), alongside co-first authors Dr. Aaron T. Kuan and Dr. Sébastien Phan, the team demonstrated that large blocks of brain tissue can be mapped in exquisite 3D nanoscale detail without prohibitive imaging times.

Conventional electron tomography is painstakingly slow because it typically requires capturing hundreds of tilted images across razor-thin tissue slices. By refining image acquisition and computational reconstruction, Lee, Ellisman, and colleagues discovered they could cut the required number of images by roughly 50-fold while imaging tissue sections 20 times thicker than standard electron microscopy protocols, preserving an extraordinary 3D resolution of 15 nanometers or finer.

“We can now see brain structures that we haven’t been able to see before in connectomics data,” explained Lee. “We hope this work will help us understand how nervous systems are built; what changes with age; and what goes awry in neurodevelopmental, cognitive, and neurodegenerative disorders.”

When the researchers applied their high-speed tomography pipeline to tissue from the mouse cerebellum, the 3D reconstructions revealed both long-sought neural connections and surprising cellular behaviors:

“We’re seeing uncharacterized structures, suggesting that neurons are ‘nibbling’ their neighboring cells,” Lee said.

In addition, the researchers traced delicate nerve fibers continuously through the thick 3D tissue block right down to their exact synaptic contact points with target neurons.

While scaling this accelerated tomography workflow to map an entire mammalian brain will require automated tissue-handling robotics, the breakthrough bridges a critical resolution gap in modern connectomics—equipping neuroscientists to examine how fine electrical and glial-neuronal connections form in early life and break down in brain disease.

Why do traditional brain wiring maps (connectomes) miss certain structures?

Mapping large volumes of brain tissue at high speed usually forces scientists to sacrifice ultra-fine resolution. As a result, critical microscopic features, such as tiny electrical synapses (gap junctions) that connect neurons directly, often blur out or go undetected in large-scale datasets.

How did researchers speed up nanoscale 3D brain imaging?

The team optimized electron tomography so that it requires about 50 times fewer multi-angle images on tissue slices 20 times thicker than usual, while still maintaining a 3D resolution of 15 nanometers or finer.

3 defaults that quietly take your system down (and the one-line fixes)

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Most outages I've read about weren't caused by too little hardware. They were caused by a default setting nobody questioned, which worked fine until the traffic changed shape.

I build Bugpit, a browser game where your system design runs against simulated traffic and breaks. This week I added three levels where you can't buy your way out: a bigger machine is over budget, so the fix has to be a setting. Each one comes from something real systems actually do.

The default: LRU, least recently used. When the cache is full, throw out whatever nobody asked for in the longest time.

When it breaks: a news site gets 24,000 requests a second, and the cache answers 85% of them. Then a search engine crawler reads every old article once.

To LRU, every one of those crawled articles is the most recently used thing in the cache. So it keeps articles nobody will read again, and throws out the front page to make room.

The fix: LFU, least frequently used. It counts how often each key is read, so one read from a crawler can't outrank a page read thousands of times a minute.

Where you've seen this: MySQL's InnoDB buffer pool doesn't put newly read pages at the front of its LRU list. It inserts them in the middle, so a full table scan can't flush out the hot pages. Caffeine, the Java cache library, uses W-TinyLFU partly because it holds up under scans.

The catch: LFU trusts history. When something new gets popular, old favourites with huge counts hang around, and the new page keeps getting evicted. There's no best policy, only one that fits your traffic.

The default: round robin. Server 1, 2, 3, 4, then 1 again. It never looks at how busy anyone is.

When it breaks: a team dashboard gets 7,000 requests a second on 4 servers that can handle 8,000 together. On paper there's room. But 1 request in 10 is a CSV export that takes 10 times as long as the rest.

A server that picks up a few exports in a row gets stuck on them, and round robin keeps handing it new requests anyway. They pile up behind the exports while another server sits idle. Round robin spreads requests evenly, not work. On average the servers are fine, but the busiest one overloads.

The fix: least connections. Send each request to the server with the fewest requests in flight. A server stuck on exports has many open, so new requests go elsewhere until it catches up.

Where you've seen this: HAProxy has balance leastconn and recommends it for long sessions. AWS Application Load Balancer offers "least outstanding requests" as an alternative to round robin.

When round robin is fine: when every request takes about the same time. Then taking turns spreads the work evenly too, and it costs nothing to track.

The default: a fixed window. Count requests per minute, and reset to zero on the minute. It's the easiest thing to build:

When it breaks, part 1: scrapers learn the reset. They send a full minute's worth at 0:59, the counter resets, and they send another full minute's worth at 1:00. For a moment your servers get twice the limit.

In the sim, a limit of 7,000 that 4 servers can carry lets 14,000 through around each reset, and the servers fall over right on the minute. Halving the limit stops the crash, but then you turn away half the people you could serve all day long.

The fix: a sliding window. Count the last 60 seconds, whenever now is. There's no reset to aim for. Cloudflare has written about using a sliding window approximation for their rate limiting for this reason.

When it breaks, part 2: real users aren't smooth either. Say each user gets 10 requests a second and averages 2. Then they open a dashboard that fires 30 calls at once. Any window, fixed or sliding, counts 30 in one second and refuses 20 of them. Half the widgets show "Too many requests".

The fix: a token bucket. Each user gets a bucket that refills at 10 tokens a second and holds up to 30. Each request spends one token. A user who was quiet has a full bucket, so the burst goes through, and the average still can't go over 10.

Where you've seen this: AWS API Gateway throttling is set as a rate plus a burst, which is a token bucket. Stripe's engineering post on rate limiters describes token buckets in Redis.

All three defaults have something in common:

None of them are wrong. Each one assumes your traffic is smooth and even, and real traffic usually isn't. A bigger machine hides the problem for a while. Knowing what your traffic looks like fixes it.

All three are free levels in the Core concepts section of Bugpit. Each starts already broken. Press Run, watch what fails, and change one setting. The numbers in this post come straight from those levels.

Kalshi daily volume of ‘perps’ trading begins to top its prediction markets

📅 2026-11-24  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Kalshi is known for its prediction markets across sports, politics and culture.

But now, for the first time, perpetual futures, Kalshi's second financial product, has surpassed the notional trading volume of its event contracts on consecutive days.

On Thursday, perpetual futures — commonly known as "perps" — on Kalshi saw $3.4 billion in notional volume as artificial intelligence laboratory Anthropic stock debuted on public markets while the rest of the market sold off. That exceeded about $3 billion in notional volume for Kalshi's prediction markets on the same day.

Perps are futures-style contracts where there is no expiration and traders do not need to own the underlying asset. Contracts instead continuously track the price of an asset, with funding payments to keep the contract price aligned with that of the market. Funding payments are made periodically between holders of short or long positions.

Perps trading surpassed event contracts one day in September, but on Friday perps topped prediction markets volume for a second day. Now Kalshi expects that performance to continue as it introduces perps tied to more individual stocks. On Monday, for example, Kalshi launched contracts linked to Anthropic.

Kalshi was the first to debut perps in the U.S. after winning federal regulatory approval in May to list perps tied to cryptocurrencies.

Perps trading volume includes any leverage that speculators acquire along with their trade, while event contracts are fully collateralized — for now.

Since the original launch in May, Kalshi has received approval to introduce perps tied to metals, crude oil, stock indexes and individual stocks.

Offshore perps — those available on exchanges not regulated in the U.S. — generated more than $90 trillion in notional trading volume in 2025, so it's not surprising that the product has grown so rapidly on Kalshi, according to Udesh Jha, chief risk officer at Kalshi Klear, the company's clearing house.

"We're just in very early innings. We're launching products, we're growing the participant base."

Perps were Kalshi's first product to mark its transition from a prediction market-focused platform to a full-blown financial exchange where various assets are traded. Last week, reports said that Kalshi is planning to offer trading on equities on its platform in the coming months.

At the same time, the company's perps product has come under fire. Some incumbent exchanges where futures are traded — which saw their stock prices decline following the approval of U.S. perps — believe the product was improperly approved. CME Group is suing the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, the federal derivatives regulator, for approving perps.

Others have simply expressed concerns about the efficiency of the markets. CNBC previously reported that trading volume on Kalshi's most popular perps markets — tied to bitcoin and ether cryptocurrencies — was dramatically greater than open interest, or cash actively in the markets. It's a phenomenon that isn't common with U.S.-regulated traditional futures, nor other offshore perps markets.

Kalshi said at the time it has "zero concerns" about the distinction, and that it appears because less leverage is offered compared to offshore exchanges and there are fewer incentives for traders to park capital on its perps markets.

While Kalshi started with cryptocurrencies, Owen Lau, an analyst at Clear Street, said perps tied to individual stocks likely represent the most promising opportunity for growth in the U.S.

"That's how I foresee the way perps will penetrate further into traditional asset classes," Lau said. "Perps on single stocks and [key performance indicators on individual companies] could change how investors hedge their portfolio or even make a directional bet."

Kalshi's growth has been powered by the fact that right now it's the only exchange that can actively list perps in the U.S., though crypto exchange Coinbase received approval in May to route U.S. users to offshore platforms for trading. That monopoly status, however, will probably prove short lived.

Coinbase has filed to list single-stock perps in the U.S., and President Donald Trump in August said the CFTC is working to bring Hyperliquid — a dominant player in offshore perps activity — to domestic traders.

But Jha at Kalshi isn't worried about the competition.

"What will carry us is ... making sure that we have the right products for the marketplace, making sure we have the right surveillance of the products that we are trading."

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

World of Warcraft: Forever players will now be able to get even more benefit from the game's strongest buffs, as developer Blizzard's latest hotfix has made World Buffs persist across loading screens as intended.

World Buffs are powerful stat-boosting effects granted to players in a particular area for certain events. The most common World Buff players see is Warchief's Blessing on the Horde and the new Might of Stormwind for the Alliance, which are granted to all players in Orgrimmar, Stormwind, and their surrounding low-level zones when players on their respective faction turn in a specific high-level quest.

Now that players are reaching endgame and turning in the required quest, these buffs are popping off constantly. They grant a whopping 300 hit points, 15% melee haste, and 10 mana regen per five seconds for one hour. At lower levels, those are some serious stats, particularly the 300 HP.

Unfortunately, players haven't been able to take full advantage of the buffs, as a bug was causing them to be lost when hitting a loading screen. That meant any time a group stepped into a dungeon or took a boat or zeppelin to a new location, their World Buffs would be removed.

That has finally been fixed in the latest update, so players can now enter dungeons with World Buffs to help maximize their damage and health. This should be particularly useful for doing dungeons at a slightly lower level than is intended, as the extra HP and melee haste is definitely noticable.

Blizzard clarified in the patch notes that World Buffs will still be removed when entering raids, so players won't be able to stack as many World Buffs as possible before raid night, as was the case in previous versions of classic WoW. 

Other changes found in the patch notes include a fix for an Uldaman boss that players were exploiting to make fast gold. Another is a fix for "a few spells and abilities" that were causing more weapon-proc effects than intended, likely nerfing the DPS of whatever classes were taking advantage of the bug. You can read the full patch notes below.

When Francis Halzen first proposed burying a $271 million telescope deep beneath the South Pole, he expected to be laughed off stage. Four decades later, it won him a Nobel Prize.

Consisting of thousands of light sensors strung like rosary beads inside a cubic kilometer (0.2 cubic miles) of pristine ice, his detector, aptly named IceCube, is not an obvious way to study the universe. But Halzen, a theoretical physicist at the University of Madison-Wisconsin, isn't looking for something obvious.

His quarries are neutrinos, near-massless, chargeless, shapeshifters that are the most abundant material particles in existence. They form from nuclear processes and high-energy collisions, swarming out of our sun and whizzing from the outskirts of black holes. Yet though neutrinos stream through stars, planets, and us every second in their trillions, you still have a 75% chance of living your entire life without a single one striking an atom in your body.

On the rare occasions neutrinos do interact with atoms, they create charged particles that zip through water or ice faster than light does, casting a faint blue cone — the optical equivalent of a sonic boom.

Since 2011, IceCube has been retracing this blue light to study neutrinos produced by the most violent astrophysical events, probing energies far beyond those of Earth-based laboratories to find cracks in the laws of physics.

In the wake of his Nobel win, Live Science sat down with Halzen to discuss neutrinos, IceCube's journey from fanciful proposal to historic success, and the future of mapping the cosmos using the trails of ghosts. Here's what he had to say.

Ben Turner: What first got you into physics?

Francis Halzen: I got into physics kind of by accident. When I came out of high school, I didn't know what to do. I was interested in languages, I was interested in science. I'm a bad example of an inspirational career.

Francis Halzen is a Belgian-American particle physicist at the University of Wisconsin-Madinson who won the 2026 Nobel Prize in Physics for his contributions to the IceCube Neutrino Observatory. Halzen first proposed the telescope's prototype, called AMANDA, in 1988.

I did mathematics because in the Belgian system it was very much in the old French style — in fact, it was created by Napoleon — and hadn't changed when I was in high school. And so, if you were a good student, mathematics was the thing to do, right? So I did mathematics for two years, and then I did physics in parallel, and then switched to physics. When I switched, I got a professor who became my supervisor and he gave me the paper of George Zweig [who co-proposed quarks alongside Murray Gell-Mann] on quarks, which was a fundamental discovery.

George Zweig was actually an experimentalist, and he made these calculations using little figures. I recognized, as I had done mathematics, that this was group theory; and I said "I can do this better." I actually wrote several papers on the subject; and so suddenly I was a theoretical particle physicist. And that's what I still am. My heart never left particle physics.

BT: And then quarks eventually became neutrinos for you. Why neutrinos?

FH: I actually didn't study neutrinos until I really got involved in starting this experiment. Half of my career, which was passed by the time I started AMANDA [IceCube's prototype] and IceCube, I really had very little overlap with neutrino physics.

I became interested in neutrinos again [while] solving a theoretical problem. In fact, the idea of detecting neutrinos with ice originated with [Gurgen] Askaryan, an Armenian physicist, in 1963.

You cannot see neutrinos. They have no charge. They have basically no mass. So the way to detect neutrinos is when they interact with the nucleus. We are moving now in a sea of neutrinos, but neutrinos don't interact with atoms, so we don't know it. But if they accidentally hit the nucleus of an atom, then they make a nuclear reaction, and that emits charged particles. It's like a spark, and you can detect a spark with antennas.

So Askaryan's idea was to put radio antennas in ice. We are doing exactly that. But instead of looking at the radio emission, we look at the light that's emitted, which is just blue light. We use that blue light to reconstruct the neutrino and all its properties, including its direction — [that's] why it is a telescope, right?

Building on Askaryan's idea, The Askaryan Radio Array (ARA) complements IceCube by searching for high-energy neutrinos with radio antennas buried 200 meters (670 feet) below Antarctica's ice.

I was worrying about that experiment because they had not been able to calculate the power of the spark, so they didn't know if this worked or not. Then someone told me at some point that the Russians — I guess it was still the Soviet Union — are actually doing this at a station in Antarctica by pointing [radio] dishes into the ice. I said: "This is crazy."

But I had ice all over my brain, right? When I saw in the late 80s how difficult it was to do these experiments in deep ocean water it was normal to have the idea to do it in ice. And, to our credit, we understood that it actually may be simpler.

BT: Why is being able to calculate the power of the spark so important?

FH: [It's about] how much light is made, and how far it travels through your detector. If you don't make enough light, and it's immediately absorbed, then you have no experiment.

The reason we are talking to each other [today] is because we had this incredible luck that blue light travels 100 meters [328 feet] and more through this ancient ice without being absorbed. So it's relatively easy to instrument an ice block of a kilometer cubed with light sensors.

BT: When you first proposed melting 61 two-kilometre [1.2-mile] holes in the coldest place on Earth, you said you expected to be laughed off stage. Then when you weren't, you said you continued to worry about it. What were you worrying about?

FH: Well, the worry was that I was totally out of my league. I didn't understand glaciology, I am not an experimentalist, so I didn't really grasp the technological challenges of doing this. The experiments in water were not doing very well: There was the leading experiment off the coast of Hawaii [the Deep Underwater Muon And Neutrino Detector, or DUMAND, project] and it happened that the man leading this experiment, John Learned, was a good friend of mine, and I eventually contacted him.

And the glaciology, of course. Nobody knew what the optical properties were of ice at a depth of one kilometer [0.6 miles] or more under the South Pole. There were [ice] cores that had been taken, but cores are small — the size of a teacup — so you cannot tell how far the light travels [through the ice]. So people thought it was a cute idea that would never work.

If I had known more about glaciology, about hot water drilling, and about the technical challenges, I would never have started this experiment. It's the advantage of being ignorant. It's why young people make great discoveries, and when you're 82 years old you don't make this progress — you know too much.

An artist's illustration showing what the interior of IceCube would look like without the ice.

BT: You speak about AMANDA and IceCube with a lot of ambivalence. You said you "got dragged into it by accident" and "tried to get out of it many times."

FH: Oh yes, absolutely. You know, theorists write two types of papers: [the first are] papers where they think they contribute to the progress of science — they usually don't. But the other type of paper is just to show you're smart. And this was definitely in the second category. And then two people from Berkeley actually started the experiment.

BT: Well, you won a Nobel Prize for it. If you wanted to look smart, you succeeded.

FH: Yeah, without a lot of luck, I wouldn't be sitting here.

They're this almost non-existent piece of reality.

BT: But surely it wasn't just showing off? Despite all of the uncertainties and worries, there must have been something about neutrinos — these barely detectable, misbehaving particles — that's so important it made you stick around. What's the draw to learn about them?

FH: Neutrinos are something really special, right? When you look at the Standard Model of matter [which describes all the known particles and forces], it has quarks, leptons, and neutrinos.

But neutrinos are special in the sense that they're supposed to have no mass. They're just like photons: they travel at the speed of light, so you can do astronomy with them. But then they have the advantage that they go through planets; they go through the Earth; they reach you from the beginning of time; they reach you from the edge of the universe. They're this almost non-existent piece of reality.

So they always had a special fascination. We knew they existed since 1930; because without neutrinos you cannot change a proton into a neutron. They were ghosts — that's where the name "ghost particle" comes from. They were part of physics, and particle physics, and nuclear physics, but they had never been seen.

It's only 1956 that [Frederick] Reines and [Clyde] Cowen finally discovered the neutrino by putting a detector close to a nuclear reactor, where there are lots of them. If they don't interact, then you compensate by going to a place where there are lots of them, and a nuclear reactor was an opportunity.

It's a fascinating particle. Of course now it fascinates us because they do have mass, which is not allowed by the structure of the Standard Model, and so they carry some information on new physics — the new physics we are looking for with the Large Hadron Collider in Geneva. They are the particles that have carried the message of that new physics most directly, and that's our domain.

We should have all the advantages. Sadly, they just behave like the Standard Model in everything we have done over the first 15 years of this experiment.

Construction workers inside one of the two colossal caverns created for the particle detector modules of Fermilab's Deep Underground Neutrino Experiment (DUNE). By probing the properties of neutrinos, physicists are hoping to assess key theories, including neutrinos' hypothesized role in tipping the universe into containing more matter than antimatter.

BT: Famously, in 1993 to '94, microscopic air bubbles trapped in Antarctica's ice scattered neutrinos' blue light like frosted glass, nearly killing the project. You saved it by drilling to where the ice sheet's weight crushed those bubbles out of existence, up to 2.5 kilometers [1.6 miles] beneath the surface and one kilometer deeper than you originally planned. How hard was that?

FH: Well, the drilling almost killed the project twice.

It's actually an interesting story, because we now detect a neutrino every minute as we are speaking, but they are produced in the atmosphere. They [atmospheric neutrinos] are our background — interesting background, but a background.

As energetic particles called cosmic rays strike Earth's upper atmosphere, they create showers of smaller particles, including neutrinos, that can be detected on the surface. IceCube's scientists filter out lower-energy atmospheric neutrinos (alongside those produced by the sun) to find only those made in astrophysical cataclysms.

After we had solved all these problems and could detect neutrinos, the only threshold to cross was to produce a drill that could build IceCube. That involves melting ice over two and a half kilometers over an area the size of a basketball. The technique existed in glaciers, but [produced] a shallow hole about the size of a bottle of wine.

So we went to companies specialized in building hoses, and we needed a hose that was 2.5 kilometers long. The first company we went to told us: "Look, you hang a hose the size of a tea cup over two and a half kilometers of hot water and what do you think will happen?" It will collapse. Even the theorists could understand that it would collapse.

And that was the end of the project. For weeks I thought it was finished. I told one of our engineers, a brilliant guy who contributed much more than what I'm going to tell you, and he didn't give up. He found a company in Padua, Italy, near Venice and they said: "We can build anything." And they did.

Then, when we started drilling, it was still a problem. We drilled one hole over a whole [South Pole] season. We had brilliant engineers working on this totally new piece of equipment — you have no idea how complex this is — and they learned from the mistakes of the first year and the second year it worked. They deployed 86 strings without ever failing, which is amazing actually. It's like when you launch 86 satellites, you're probably going to fail some. That's what we thought. But they never did.

A view down along one of IceCube's 86 detector strings, dangling in holes drilled up to 2.5 kilometers into the ice.

BT: The detector went online in 2011, then that year and the next came "Bert" and "Ernie" — the first ever detections of astrophysical neutrinos from beyond our solar system. After all that effort, all those near misses, and every doubt you ever had, what did it feel like when they arrived?

FH: We were not sure we believed it. It was clear then that this was a major discovery — if it was true. It took us six months or more to have the courage to write the paper.

I remember seeing the first events on my screen in October of 2012, and they looked like they couldn't have come from the atmosphere — such high energy! We discovered the first two serendipitously in the first two years of data. Then, after we went to look for more knowing what these events look like, we had 28. So we had the courage to publish.

But then, three weeks later, we were declared the physics breakthrough of the year. I must say I was not happy about that. I had to give a press conference, and I was all the time thinking: "If this is not true, how am I going to walk this back?"

It took me quite a while to convince myself that this wasn't just something that looked like cosmic neutrinos. But this is such an unusual corner of science, you don't know whether you are fooled by some other phenomena you haven't thought of.

FH: Well now we are seeing sources of neutrinos. You see 100 neutrinos come from an active galaxy, and you point [a different telescope] back at it with 0.04 degree resolution. There's no doubt now. But being convinced was a continuous process; at least in my case. My colleagues always refer to me as a total optimist, but it took me some time. I was not my usual self on this.

But now there's no doubt. The statistics of observing cosmic neutrinos is totally impossible [to deny] with the precision we have now. But we're talking about 14 years of data. It's really incredible how quickly we've progressed.

A crane deploys the Baikal-GVD neutrino telescope from the ice cover of Lake Baikal, Siberia.

And you realize that, officially, none of the things we see have been confirmed by someone else. There are two experiments, [Baikal-G in Lake Baikal and [KM3NeT] in the Mediterranean Sea, but they're under construction. And they both have produced some kind of evidence, but a real confirmation of seeing the same thing we see has still not happened. So the Nobel Prize [Committee] had more courage than me.

I had forgotten that it was Nobel season.

BT: You were in a hotel room in Italy when the call came from Stockholm. What went through your mind in the first quiet moments after hanging up?

FH: Well, after hanging up, I was sent a Zoom link, and they asked me questions, which I answered. But I didn't know that I was actually giving a press conference. I still haven't looked back at that press conference.

But that's how crazy it was, because I had forgotten that it was Nobel season. The call came by total surprise. Well, it wasn't a total surprise because there's a game of predicting the Nobel Prizes. And I had been there [in the running to win] for several years.

So I cannot say that I was unaware of this. But this was a difficult season for me, right? People literally would send me condolence messages every year for not winning. And they meant it — they're my friends — but it makes you feel strange. You feel you've disappointed your collaborators.

So that's the first thing I thought: "Okay, the problem is solved now. Everybody can get credit for this experiment." And deservedly. Because IceCube was becoming mostly me, and data and science. The fact of its history and the contributions people made was being forgotten very fast. The great thing about this Nobel Prize is that it's for a difficult problem that was solved by so many brilliant people over almost four decades.

BT: Aside from winning the Nobel, what's been the highlight of working on IceCube?

FH: The highlight for me was to show that the technique works. Seeing these neutrinos go through the Earth, and then through the detector, was incredibly exciting because there was no doubt you were looking at a stream of particles going through your detector initiated by neutrinos that came through the Earth. There was no doubt. What you see is what you get.

We had this attitude that if you build something this big you're going to do interesting science. This is so different from anything that has been built before. I don't want to insult astronomers, but our joke was: "We are all particle physicists, guys. God forbid we have to do astronomy." But that's what we are doing.

I must say, it's very, very exciting to learn astronomy when you're 82 years old.

BT: IceCube–Gen2 is targeted to come online by 2033 and will be eight times larger than the original detector. What do you want for its future?

FH: Whenever I give a talk, I have a list of conclusions, and my first is always that neutrino astronomy exists, and that's our contribution. Doing neutrino astronomy will require more instruments, bigger instruments. In astronomy there's not just one telescope, right? To do real astronomy we'll need more instruments covering the sky completely, and we'll need more neutrinos with better angular resolution.

The nice thing is that will happen: There are ambitious projects in China now, and [the upgrade of] our own project too. How can you pass it up? Because now the technology is much better: we understand the ice perfectly; we can design this detector, optimize it, and gain an order of magnitude [improvement] for the same cost as the original.

China's Jiangmen Underground Neutrino Observatory (JUNO) detector while it was under construction in 2024. The detector, which is designed to probe neutrinos from space and from Earth, came online in August 2026.

BT: You famously don't like to make predictions, even one year ahead, about what we might learn about neutrinos. So I'm going to try to be sly with this final question: What do you hope the future has in store for neutrino astronomy?

FH: I hope for a galactic supernova [within the Milky Way]. I don't know if you know the history, but in 1987 there was a supernova in a [nearby] satellite galaxy, but it [meant physicists] detected 20 neutrinos. And what we learned from these neutrinos is incredible — literally thousands of papers have been written about those 20 neutrinos, and they're all interesting.

Neutrinos are supposed to have no mass and be just like photons. The best limit we could set on the [tiny and still unexplained] mass of the neutrino came from that supernova. So we made very fundamental measurements that we hadn't been able to make with [laboratory] neutrino beams. And then the whole story that a star really collapses into a neutron star. All that suddenly became reality. I was not, and am still not, an astronomer, but from the point of view of stellar physics this was a major event.

Now, instead of three experiments, we have multiple neutrino detectors of all kinds. Some will detect thousands of neutrinos; we'll detect a million neutrinos. We can't actually reconstruct them, but we can make a very detailed movie telling you everything that went on in a supernova while the star collapsed and then exploded. The science we will do from a few seconds of data will be incredible.

I can tell you it hasn't happened since the mid-1990s because we have been looking for it continuously with AMANDA and Ice-Cube. There are supposed to be like two per century. We are due.

Honda’s bestseller goes hybrid only as it enters its seventh generation and will arrive in 2027 with a fresh design inside and out.

Honda’s bestselling model—and the third-bestselling vehicle in the United States through the third quarter of 2026—is getting a complete makeover. The CR-V will follow the path set forth by the sixth-generation Toyota RAV4, going hybrid only as the CR-V enters its seventh generation for the 2028 model year.

We expect the 2028 CR-V Hybrid to wear a sharp new design, with spy photos showing slim daytime running lights above a series of thin horizontal grilles, while the rear end features a light bar that stretches the full width of the SUV. It will likely also be the first vehicle in the U.S. to wear Honda’s new “H” logo, which was originally introduced for its future EVs but will now be applied to other vehicles in its lineup after Honda hurriedly canceled said EVs earlier this year.

The cabin is also expected to get a significant makeover, with the Avancier—a three-row SUV sold in Asia—providing hints about what to expect. The Avancier skips out on most real buttons and knobs, instead putting nearly every function into a large screen mounted to the dashboard, a dramatic shift from Honda’s current setup that prioritizes physical controls. The Avancier’s interior is wrapped in white upholstery, accented by glowing ambient lighting, and features a squared-off steering wheel. Honda also appears to be switching to a column-mounted gear selector.

While Honda was once led by the Accord and Civic sedans, in today’s SUV-obsessed market, the CR-V has become the Japanese automaker’s most important model. Hybrid sales of the current-generation CR-V have been strong, accounting for over half of the SUV’s total sales so far this year, making it a perfect choice to launch Honda’s new platform and hybrid powertrain.

The CR-V will arrive on a new platform that Honda promises will be stiffer and lighter than before, taking a modular approach that will lead to greater parts sharing and reducing costs. This platform will presumably be adapted to underpin future versions of the Accord and Civic, along with Acura models such as the upcoming next-generation RDX Hybrid.

Honda will ditch the standard 190-hp 1.5-liter four-cylinder engine for the 2028 CR-V, which will arrive exclusively with the company’s new hybrid powertrain. The fifth generation of Honda’s two-motor hybrid setup will continue to use an Atkinson-cycle four-cylinder, along with a pair of electric motors, but the 2.0-liter engine will be a fresh design, featuring a new cooling system. As before, the electric motors will power the wheels most of the time, but the gas engine can also be engaged to directly drive the wheels.

The electric motors are more compact than before, and Honda has developed a new battery too. Honda will also need to make sure it can match or exceed the efficiency of rivals like the RAV4 while still providing adequate performance and a spacious, usable cabin, given how closely matched the compact SUV space is these days. Moving away from physical interior controls could also upset the Honda faithful who are used to the company’s simple-to-use buttons.

Production is set to kick off at Honda’s factories in East Liberty, Ohio, and Greensburg, Indiana, in April 2027. Sales should begin shortly after, and with the current CR-V Hybrid starting at $37,580, we expect the next-generation model to come in at around $38,000.
