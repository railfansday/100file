This month's force wipe patch notes unveiled some additional details about the changes coming to Rust Premium "soon." Rust Premium is now available on the staging branch, the build that allows Rust players to test upcoming features before they go live. Within the Rust Premium menu, we can see which perks will be available to Premium users; among them is character customization — a feature that fans have been eagerly awaiting since the player model update.

This feature was scheduled for 2026 Q4, meaning it's likely coming in the next force wipe. Rust fans can therefore expect Rust Premium and character customization to become available on Thursday, Dec. 3, when the monthly update drops.

After digging through the staging branch files, user Protox believes that once players customize their character, they won't be able to change it for the rest of the wipe. So, they'd be stuck with it for a whole month. This makes sense as a way to prevent players from changing their appearances mid-wipe to unfairly deceive others in-game.

The first month of Premium features the Cobalt Premium pack, which includes launch reward skins for Sheet Metal Doors, Cobalt skins for the Metal Chest Plate and Code Lock, a Cobalt charm for weapons, and the Cobalt tattoo pack, a customization feature which will allow players to give their characters tattoos via a tattoo artist chair.

Similar to character customization, it's likely that players will be able to add tattoos onto their character throughout the wipe but will only be able to remove them the following month. While it's possible to play around with the upcoming tattoo feature, character customization can only be applied using code. Either it's not completely finished yet or deliberately inaccessible.

Now, after 19 games in charge, the Imps have opted for change. Here, we put forward three contenders the club should consider for their vacant position…

Given that Lincoln City are likely to be in a Championship relegation battle, they could do with someone who has experience in this league. Among the available bosses, few have the level of second-tier pedigree that Paul Heckingbottom has.

The Yorkshireman was let go by Preston North End after a poor start to the season but he has been there, done that and got the T-shirt in the Championship. Not only has he led successful survival fights, but he took Sheffield United to promotion as well.

Heckingbottom’s pragmatic management can make the most of the squad on offer at Sincil Bank. He can lead them to safety before looking to push up the division in the long term.

A similar but perhaps slightly more ambitious option than Heckingbottom is Steve Cooper. The Welshman is on the market after his spell in charge of Danish side Brondby came to an end in May.

Like Heckingbottom, Cooper has led Championship struggles before and led them up the table. His stint at Nottingham Forest was perhaps the best of his career to date, taking them from the lower reaches of the second tier all the way to the Premier League.

He might not have a move to the Championship or Lincoln City in mind, but he is an active and successful manager despite his age. He is a no-nonsense boss known for turning around struggling teams with defensive discipline and mental resilience – two key attributes in a relegation fight.

Kombouare may be an unfeasible option, but perhaps he could be tempted to take on a new challenge in England having spent almost his entire managerial career in France.

Congress Just Voted to Redraw Yosemite National Park Boundaries. The Bill Is Heading to Trump’s Desk.

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The famous Tunnel View at Yosemite (Photo: Kenny McCartney/Getty Images)

A new law is heading to President Trump’s desk that would shift Yosemite’s boundaries by consolidating land between the National Park Service (NPS) and the National Forest System.

On November 18, Congress passed the Ackerson Meadow Land Interchange Act, a bill co-signed by Democratic U.S. Senator Alex Padilla and Republican U.S. Representative Tom McClintock, both from California.

If the president signs it into law, the Ackerson Meadow Land Interchange Act will transfer 160 acres of Stanislaus National Forest land to the NPS for management as part of Yosemite. Another 170 acres of Yosemite National Park land will be transferred to the U.S. Forest Service (USFS) for management as part of Stanislaus National Forest.

The decision comes after a flurry of debate following announcements for public land swaps and transfers. An August report alleged that the NPS was attempting to execute a land exchange within Yosemite for a private road, which reportedly would have given property within park boundaries to a Nevada-based real estate company. Then, in September, Major League Baseball approached the Trump administration about hosting a game at Grand Teton National Park in northwest Wyoming.

Lawmakers say the Ackerson Meadow decision will address a problem in federal land management in the Sierra Nevada: the checkerboard of boundary lines. In 2016, the Trust for Public Land bought the 400-acre Ackerson Meadow parcel and gifted it to Yosemite. However, the chunk was almost completely surrounded by the Stanislaus National Forest with very little contiguous border to the park itself, lawmakers said.

“Ackerson Meadow is one of the most critical wildlife corridors in our state, yet the current configuration of the region puts habitat conservation at risk,” Padilla said in a statement. “This land transfer is a simple and beneficial exchange that will help protect endangered wildlife, mitigate wildfire threats, and sustain our rural economy.”

Jackie McGuinness, Padilla’s communications director, told Outside that the agreement will change Yosemite’s boundaries. A map McGuinness sent shows the park boundaries shifting to the east, filling in a checkerboard pattern left blocked out by the previous perimeter lines.

USFS manages land ranchers use for livestock grazing on national forests and grasslands, while the NPS restricts most of these operations within park boundaries. The land exchange between the two agencies eliminates confusion caused by the jurisdictional split, McClintock said in a U.S. House floor statement on September 14.

“The proposed land exchange would make that cooperative approach easier by eliminating the confusion caused by split jurisdiction,” he said. “Ranchers need to know where their permits apply, where infrastructure can be maintained, and who is responsible for decisions affecting their operations.”

It’s unclear whether or when Trump intends to sign the bill into law.

Watford appoint Iordanescu as head coach

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Edward Iordanescu took Romania to the last 16 at Euro 2024

Watford have appointed former Romania boss Edward Iordanescu as the Championship club's new head coach.

The 48-year-old replaces Alessio Dionisi, who was sacked on Sunday after only five months in charge following a run of seven defeats in their past 10 league games, culminating in Saturday's 4-0 loss to Sheffield United.

"I am very proud and happy to be at this football club," Iordanescu told the club website., external

"I can see the team is in a difficult moment but I have already watched many of the games over the last few days and I'm very confident about the future."

He becomes Watford's sixth new boss in less than 18 months and is the 25th manager since the Pozzo family took over the club in 2012.

Pioneer or coach-eater? The complex world of Pozzo's Watford

The former CFR Cluj and Legia Warsaw manager will be joined at Watford by backroom staff Alexandru Radu, Grzegorz Mokry and Michele Iannucci, but will watch Wednesday's Championship match at home to Blackburn Rovers from the stands before taking charge for Saturday's game at Derby.

Adrian Mariappa will oversee Wednesday's match at Vicarage Road (19:45 GMT), alongside Paul Furlong and Lloyd Doyley.

The Watford job is Iordanescu's first since leaving Legia Warsaw in October 2025, where he won the Super Cup last season.

It is the 10th managerial post of his 15-year coaching career, which has taken in Bulgarian club CSKA Sofia as well as Steaua Bucharest and Cluj - who he guided to the Romanian title and two domestic cups after joining midway through the 2020-21 season.

He also spent two-and-a-half years in charge of Romania, guiding them to the last 16 of Euro 2024, where they were beaten by the Netherlands.

Iordanescu takes over a Watford side currently three points above the relegation zone, having won only four of their 16 games so far.

Watford will be Iordanescu's first job in English football

Although untried in English football, Watford are getting a trophy-winning head coach with a wide-ranging CV spanning spells in his homeland, Bulgaria and Poland, as well as elite-level international experience with Romania.

His connections with his national side run deep as the son of his country's legendary former head coach Anghel Iordanescu, who famously took them to the quarter-finals of the 1994 World Cup during a reign of more than 100 games.

It is likely he will need every bit of that nous and inspiration as he becomes the latest boss to try to revive a club which continues to plough through managers at an alarming rate.

Dionisi was Watford's 12th head coach, not including interim bosses, since the end of the 2020-21 season, with Iordanescu becoming the sixth permanent boss since Tom Cleverley was sacked in May 2025.

The Hornets' slow start to the season - with only four home wins and one point out of 24 away - has played out against the backdrop of uncertainty and unrest off the pitch.

Watford's owner, Gino Pozzo, is currently standing trial in Spain as one of a number of defendants accused of tax offences relating to Spanish team Granada, a club he used to be associated with.

He denies the allegations, which are related to player trading at Granada.

The case continues a tumultuous period for the club, with supporter and former owner Elton John voicing his frustration after a 4-0 home defeat by Burnley last month.

Fans have also questioned the running of the club, with one supporters' group declaring a loss of confidence in the ownership.

Iordanescu's immediate concern will be improving results, particularly Watford's form on the road, where they have yet to win and have conceded 18 goals in eight matches - with only Burnley letting in more.

He arrives with a reputation for defensive organisation and stability, which the Hornets are in deseprate need of.

How AI Has Changed The Build-Versus-Buy Math For Mid-Market Companies

Mario Žderić is CTO at DECODE, a software engineering company building custom software for mid-market companies and global enterprises.

​If you run a mid-market company, odds are your business depends on off-the-shelf tools that barely fit your processes, with manual work covering the rest. Custom software was always the obvious fix, but building it came with an enterprise price tag.​

The economics of custom software development have changed. For a mid-market budget, you can now get a more complete system, built around how your business actually works and delivered much faster.​

AI-native engineering made it possible. Based on my experience as the CTO of a firm that develops custom software for companies of all sizes, the harder question now is what’s worth building and what isn’t.

Finding people with the skills and expertise to solve complex engineering problems without breaking the bank was the bottleneck for mid-market companies for decades.

AI changed the math: With AI-native workflows, senior engineers can now deliver quality software on a much shorter timeline.​ According to the 2026 Stack Overflow Developer Survey, 83% of developers use at least one AI tool in their work. This is an industry-wide shift.

Integrations, data migrations, tests, documentation and boilerplate used to take up months of work on any project. Now, these tasks get done much faster. On the first fully AI-native project I supervised, an AI concierge app for a European hotel group, the team delivered a scope we initially estimated at 10 months in four. That means we were 2.5x faster than originally estimated.

Our setup made this possible: AI worked from our coding standards, each change went through automated review and QA, and a senior engineer owned every feature from start to finish.​

AI doesn’t change what matters most in development: deciding what to build and understanding the business. That’s still on you.​ However, with a strong in-house engineering team or an experienced external partner, projects that once required an enterprise-level budget are now much more realistic.

The opportunity for mid-market companies isn’t rebuilding every system from scratch. Replacing business-critical core systems like ERPs, accounting and payroll software is rarely worth the risk. The real gain is improving the systems you already rely on without disrupting your team’s daily work.​

For instance, you can build custom software to remove friction slowing down your business: automating workflows between different systems, retiring the 10-year-old spreadsheet holding the business together, removing manual re-entry and replacing brittle no-code automations. That’s where bad data, numbers that don’t match between systems and reports you can’t fully trust come from.​

A U.S. wealth management firm we work with had its work spread across more than a dozen tools and calculated P&L by hand in spreadsheets. No off-the-shelf product fit how it worked, and for a long time, custom solutions were too expensive. Its teams filled the gaps with manual work. Those tools are now one custom platform, built on a mid-market budget.

There’s a second payoff: better AI readiness. According to the RSM Middle Market AI Survey 2026, data quality issues (53%) and integration challenges (47%) are the top barriers among organizations reporting moderate or limited AI pilot success. Custom software can solve both. The same project that makes your operations run more smoothly also provides AI with high-quality, connected data.

The risk with AI is that code is now easier to produce and harder to maintain. Anything you build without engineering discipline will more than likely fail, and your team won’t have the context to fix it.

So, you still need engineers who understand what they’re building.

In the same Stack Overflow survey I mentioned before, only 16% of developers said AI handles complex tasks “very well.” With how fast AI can generate code, there’s more output to review and less time to build context. Engineers split across several projects can’t keep up. ​

That’s why you need a team that only works on your project. They need to fully own and understand the project and your business, whether it’s your own team or an external partner. This lets them challenge bad decisions and catch when an AI agent goes wrong.

Common sleep and anxiety drugs could be harming aging brains

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Adults age 65 and older who have dementia or other cognitive disorders are particularly vulnerable to the harmful effects of medications used to treat anxiety, sleep disturbances, psychosis, and other conditions affecting the central nervous system.

Potential consequences include more emergency room visits, longer and more frequent hospital stays, declining mental health, and an increased risk of premature death.

New research from the University at Buffalo (UB) offers some encouraging news, however. Between 2011 and 2022, the proportion of older adults with cognitive disorders taking medications that could impair their cognitive function decreased significantly.

After analyzing data from 2,796 older adults with cognitive disorders who lived independently, researchers found that exposure to potentially inappropriate medications fell from 39.3% in 2011 to 29.3% in 2022.

"This is a population with some mental issues that relate to the central nervous system. These drugs also act on the central nervous system, so they can be too much for older adults if inappropriately used," says Haowen Hsu, PharmD, a postdoctoral fellow in the Department of Pharmacy Practice at the School of Pharmacy and Pharmaceutical Sciences and first author of the study, published in the Journal of the American Geriatrics Society.

Reducing the Risks of Common Prescription Drugs

Older adults who have spent years taking benzodiazepines such as Xanax for anxiety or sleep medications such as Ambien may benefit from a medically supervised reduction in dosage or gradual discontinuation. These medications can negatively affect cognitive function, balance, and coordination.

"A central word for patients in this age group is 'deprescribing,' which means, for example, removing inappropriate medications and decreasing the frequency or the dosage. In some cases, it means replacing the current medication with a milder alternative," Hsu says. "This is why it's so important for health care providers to work closely with community pharmacists and homecare nurses to optimize medication safety."

To identify potentially problematic drugs, the research team consulted the American Geriatrics Society's Beers Criteria, which identifies approximately 100 medications that may pose risks to older adults. The researchers then concentrated specifically on medications that could present additional dangers for people with diagnosed cognitive disorders.

Using data from the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey, the team examined a sample representing approximately 29.7 million older Americans with cognitive disorders over the period from 2011 to 2022. They compared individuals prescribed at least one potentially inappropriate medication with those who had not received any of these drugs.

Medication Safety Has Improved, but Risks Remain

Although the study did not examine what caused the decline in exposure to potentially harmful medications, Hsu points to growing awareness among researchers and clinicians over the past two decades. The gradual but sustained improvement may reflect broader advances in geriatric medicine and efforts to make prescription drug use safer.

"We were happy to see the percentages of harm decline over that 11-year period," he says. "However, one-third of our target population is still exposed to these harmful medications, which isn't good."

Older adults, even those without cognitive disorders, are particularly susceptible to harmful drug interactions and inappropriate prescriptions. In some cases, these complications can increase the risk of death. These dangers are a central focus of Team Alice, an ongoing interdisciplinary research initiative at the University at Buffalo dedicated to medication safety.

Team Alice takes its name from Alice Brennan, who died in 2009 following a prescription for a muscle relaxant identified as potentially inappropriate under the Beers Criteria. The prescribing error went unrecognized within the health care system, and Brennan died following six weeks of preventable complications.

Her daughter, Mary Brennan-Taylor, shared her mother's experience with University at Buffalo researchers in the hope of bringing about systemic changes that would spare other patients and families from similar harm.

Hsu began the new study shortly after joining the university's pharmacy school and Team Alice, where he works in the UB Patient Safety Learning Laboratory.

"This work extends a series of national analyses our group has published on potentially inappropriate medications, quality of life, and health care use in older adults," says David Jacobs, PharmD, PhD, associate professor of pharmacy practice and a principal investigator on Team Alice.

"It is especially important as older adults with cognitive disorders are the least able to tolerate medications that worsen memory or thinking, yet nearly one in three are still receiving them. Most of these patients have a regular source of care so these medications can be identified and addressed with their care team."

Improving Medication Safety for Older Adults

Other contributors to the study included Team Alice's fellow principal investigators Ranjit Singh, MD, associate professor and interim chair of the Department of Family Medicine at UB's Jacobs School of Medicine and Biomedical Sciences, and Robert Wahler, PharmD, clinical associate professor of pharmacy practice.

Collin Clark, PharmD, clinical associate professor of pharmacy practice, and Steven Feuerstein, an information systems analyst at the Jacobs School, also contributed to the research.

The findings underscore the value of thorough medication reviews during routine outpatient appointments. The researchers also suggest incorporating clinical assessment tools such as the Drug Burden Index into acute and outpatient care. Such tools can help health care professionals assess the combined effects of medications that act on the central nervous system and potentially prevent avoidable complications.

Further research is needed to establish whether improving medication use can enhance quality of life and reduce hospital admissions and emergency room visits among older adults experiencing cognitive difficulties.

A Rapidly Aging Population Raises New Concerns

The findings arrive at a significant moment in global demographic history. According to a recent report from the U.S. Census Bureau, adults age 65 and older now outnumber children from birth through age 5 worldwide for the first time in human history.

However, greater longevity does not necessarily translate into more years of good health. The same report indicates that nearly 75% of adults age 65 and older have at least two chronic health conditions. Managing multiple illnesses often requires taking more medications, some of which may have detrimental effects on the aging body and brain.

"This is an issue that has been building for decades, but the growth of the older adult population has arrived sooner than we expected," Hsu says. "This means that health care professionals need to pay closer attention than ever to medication safety, especially for older adults experiencing cognitive decline or other complex health concerns."

Studies have shown that data centers do indeed contribute to rising energy costs, not to mention the noise pollution they produce. In fact, data centers are already so prevalent that there’s a decent chance one of your Thanksgiving guests lives near one. (You may recognize them by the way they keep saying, “Huh? What? Huh?”) Sore subject.
  • Too technical. The last major tech wave, mobile computing, was pretty easy to conceptualize. They turned cellphones into internet devices with big screens and made computer programs called apps to run on them. AI is different. Very few laypeople can explain how a neural network is trained and how it operates. Even PhDs don’t know exactly how AI models develop new skills, some of which have come as a complete surprise. That creates a problem for Thanksgiving conversation. Somebody at the table who listens to a lot of podcasts may seize the moment to nerd-splain AI to everybody. Or worse, they may go on and on about how LLMs are just stochastic parrots—word-guessing machines that will never be capable of anything more. Wizards of the Coast, a Hasbro subsidiary and the publisher of popular tabletop games Magic: The Gathering and Dungeons & Dragons, has filed a massive lawsuit against a provider of proxy Magic cards, PrintMTG Enterprise, seeking more than $28.6 million in damages.

Proxies are replicas of Magic cards printed to resemble real items. Although they are not allowed in official tournaments, proxies are still somewhat popular in casual play, especially within the Commander format. Notably, Premodern, a format that includes cards from 1995 to 2003 and is steadily growing in popularity, allows proxy cards in some tournaments.

However, while some Magic players are already concerned about a possible visit from the Pinkertons, things aren't as bad as they seem. Wizards is not suing PrintMTG Enterprise for producing proxy Magic cards, but for marketing their products as proxies "to lend them a veneer of legitimacy while actually plying a business built around the sale of counterfeit cards," the lawsuit reads. The distinction is important: "An honest proxy is a stand-in for a real card that makes no claims to authenticity, by appearance or otherwise." On the other hand, "A counterfeit is made to be mistaken for the real card and to take its place as something owned, traded, or sold."

According to the lawsuit, PrintMTG Enterprise operates as "a complex and sophisticated network of individuals and shell corporations," whose purpose is to profit from counterfeiting and trademark and copyright infringement. One of the websites operated by the group, ProxyKing.biz, states: "At Proxy King we try to make our proxies as realistic as possible. This lets you join games and play without people noticing you are running proxy cards in your Magic deck."

Plaintiffs withdrew the lawsuit without a ruling, so it can be revised and refiled at a large date

Magic has expanded quickly in the last few years to become a massive business built on the value of its collectibles. In a global trading card industry estimated at around $15 billion, Magic ranks second, behind​​​​​​​ the Pokémon Trading Card Game. While Wizards does not sell single Magic cards, the secondary market still drives the company's product sales. Counterfeits thus hurt the Magic business as a whole, the lawsuit claims. It's not surprising, then, that Wizards isn't going to look the other way when it comes to a counterfeiting network.

However, the lawsuit also makes it clear that the company doesn't have an issue with proxies: "Wizards recognizes that private and noncommercial proxying is a vehicle players use to further engage with the game, not to undermine it." The tech industry group CCIA Europe has called on EU leaders to throw out a proposed 3% tax on digital services as leaders meet in Brussels this week to negotiate the bloc’s 2028-2034 budget.

The tax resurfaced on 21 November in a new negotiating proposal from European Council President António Costa. Leaders will use it as the basis for talks at a special summit starting on Thursday, and it revives a plan the European Commission first put forward in 2018, which member states never agreed on.

This time, the money would flow straight into the EU budget as a so-called own resource, rather than to national treasuries. Moreover, it would also replace CORE, a broader levy on company revenues that the Commission had proposed and that European businesses opposed.

Under Costa’s plan, the tax would apply to companies with more than €750M in global turnover and over €50M in EU digital revenues. They would pay 3% on income from online advertising, digital marketplaces, and the sale of user data. The Commission expects the tax to raise around €5.9bn a year, according to an internal document reported by Rapporteur.

CCIA Europe, whose members include Amazon, Google, and Meta, says the levy would fall hardest on US companies even though it treats all nationalities the same on paper. The group adds that many European companies would also be caught, including retailers and media groups that depend on advertising.

“The EU wants more tech investment. But imposing a discriminatory tax on successful digital companies sends exactly the opposite message,” said Daniel Friedlaender, head of CCIA Europe.

The EU-wide tax would also sit on top of the national digital taxes that France, Italy, and Spain already charge. Nothing would stop other governments from adding their own, either. Those national taxes, the group says, end up passing costs on to online sellers, advertisers, and app developers.

“National experiments already show who ends up footing the bill: European sellers, app developers, and advertisers,” Friedlaender said.

The proposal also arrives at a tense moment with Washington, as in June, US President Donald Trump threatened 100% tariffs on any country that taxes American tech firms. A Commission spokesperson replied at the time that such taxes apply to all large companies, whatever their origin.

Any deal on the tax will need the backing of all 27 member states, but Costa is aiming to secure unanimous agreement on the budget by the end of the year.

A Journey Through the Absurd Hell of Trump’s Third-Country Deportations

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The warden kept telling Pheap Rom not to think of himself as a prisoner. Instead, the jailer insisted, Rom and his fellow deportees from the United States were “the king’s guests.”

Here was the warden’s idea of royal hospitality: Each morning at 7:30, Rom—a chiseled, compact, 43-year-old Cambodian man with a pronounced Philly accent—was roused from a bare cell he shared with three other men in Eswatini, a tiny kingdom in Southern Africa. Then he was made to stand at attention as the country’s national anthem, “Nkulunkulu Mnikati Wetibusiso temaSwati,” reverberated through the halls of the Matsapha Correctional Centre. Most meals consisted of a portion of rice splashed with thin broth. It was late 2025, and the weather was warm and wet. For 15 minutes a day, Rom and the other men were allowed to go outside. Before they were locked up for lights out, the anthem played again; thick clouds of mosquitoes kept Rom thrashing all night.

Sometimes Rom would banter with the towering, bespectacled warden, who went by the name Kuenene. If I’m a guest, then why am I being held in a prison with criminals? Rom would ask. Why aren’t I allowed to leave? But Rom knew these exchanges with the warden were absurd, like everything else about his situation.

Before the second Trump administration, the practice of deporting immigrants to countries where they have no ties was a rare and tightly restricted move in US immigration enforcement—reserved mainly for cases where a person simply could not be returned to their nation of origin due to risk of persecution or torture. It has since expanded into a signature White House policy of mass removal. Over the past two years, the administration has inked deals with some 35 nations to accept third-country deportees from the US. The kingdom of Eswatini, which agreed to take up to 160 migrants for a fee of $5.1 million, is one of them. Some of the other destination countries—the Central African Republic, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, South Sudan—are so violent and unstable that they rank on the State Department’s “Do Not Travel” list.

Often making a spectacle of its own transgressiveness, the administration has steamrolled past public outcry, an investigation by Democratic lawmakers, and a warning from the United Nations about a “cascade of human rights violations” resulting from these removals. At least twice in the past two years, US federal courts have ordered the Department of Homeland Security to stop shipping migrants to third countries with insufficient due process. Both times, the Supreme Court has quickly stepped in to let the administration resume.

In September, during one of those brief, court-ordered pauses in removal flights, DHS general counsel James Percival went on X to publish the most comprehensive figures yet on how many migrants have been affected by the practice. He posted a GIF of Taylor Swift mouthing the words “That’s my husband” and pointing excitedly at her wedding ring. Percival’s caption: “How my wife looks at me when I tell her we have deported 25,000 illegal aliens to third nations.”

Rather than slowing down in the face of resistance, in fact, President Trump’s third-country push appears to have inspired imitators. Five European Union countries announced in November that they are working toward a plan to send rejected asylum seekers to a deportation hub in ​Rwanda. In the past two years, Italy has begun sending asylum seekers to Albania. The United Kingdom quietly sent an asylum seeker to Eswatini earlier this year; the man, originally from Sri Lanka, died last month, the government of Eswatini reported. According to local media, the apparent cause was suicide.

Rom’s lawyer, Tin Thanh Nguyen—who is himself the child of refugees from Vietnam—predicts that, if the administration keeps getting away with these expulsions, additional wealthy countries will be emboldened to offload their undesirables to poorer nations for a price. “This is going to be the new model for the West, not only for the United States,” says Nguyen, who also represents three other men currently held in Eswatini and Rwanda. “It’s just kind of out of sight, out of mind for these Western countries.”

But as Rom’s experience reveals, some of these removals may rest on little more than bureaucratic fiction. The US claims it has followed due process with every one of the 25,000 migrants deported to unfamiliar lands. “Their own countries say, ‘We do not want them back,’” US attorney general Todd Blanche told Fox News in early October. Yet in Rom’s case—among countless other Kafkaesque details—his own country never even appears to have been asked.

An American in virtually everything but citizenship status, Rom came to the United States as a 3-year-old refugee from Cambodia. Around 1979, his parents fled their home to escape the horrors of the Khmer Rouge, a genocidal regime that seized power in the wake of Richard Nixon’s carpet-bombing of the country. From there, the family went to a Thai refugee camp—where Rom was born in 1982—and then to a resettlement center in the Philippines. As Rom’s father was selling off possessions to raise money for their ultimate move to America, he was killed by roadside thieves. Rom sat by his side after the attack, his mother later told him, trying to wake his father up.

Rom’s first memories of America are of a cramped, dimly lit bedroom in a small apartment in the Bronx, where he arrived with his mother and six siblings in 1985. “There was just one mattress, and we all slept on that,” he says. “It was a new world to us; everything was new.”

Rom and his family had arrived in the middle of the largest resettlement effort in US history. As America’s chimerical war efforts in Southeast Asia faltered in the 1970s, hundreds of thousands of people began arriving in the US from Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia. Of the nearly 158,000 Cambodians who gained entry between 1975 and 1994, the majority, like Rom and his family, were refugees deeply marked by the atrocities of the Khmer Rouge.

Around 10,000 of them came to be resettled in areas of the Bronx that were blighted by high crime and poverty. Many were placed in dilapidated housing, with limited job opportunities. Rom’s mother, who didn’t speak English, relied on government support checks and whatever extra income she could make sewing and scavenging. As a child, Rom would help her collect cans and carry the overstuffed bags to the grocery store to return for cash.

With the help of an uncle, the family eventually relocated to Philadelphia, where an adolescent Rom was enticed by a newly formed gang—a Cambodian clique within the Crips called the Blue Dragons. “If you don’t want to get bullied,” older members of the gang told him, “you need to link up with us.” Rom was barely a teenager. An older gang member dubbed him Menace, after Dennis the Menace, because he was “funny and bad at the same time.”

By the time he was 18, Rom had done three stints in juvenile detention and was caught in ever-escalating street beefs. At a New Year’s Eve party in 2008, rival gang members jumped him. Rom waited for a day off from his job—caring for abandoned dogs at a local rescue—to seek revenge. He parked outside the gang’s hangout, and when his targets exited, he opened fire from his car. Rom contemplated running when he heard that the police were looking for him but decided against it largely because he had a young daughter. In jail he would be able to see her, even if it was from behind glass. On the lam, he’d have to disappear. He pleaded guilty in April 2010 to multiple charges, including attempted murder.

Rom was a legal permanent resident of the US, a status he had been granted as a refugee. Though refugees are entitled to obtain full American citizenship, Rom had been told he didn’t stand a chance due to his juvenile record, an obstacle that would greatly alter the trajectory of his life.

Under US immigration law since the 1990s, legal permanent residents who commit crimes that qualify as “aggravated felonies”—an expansive and, critics say, somewhat arbitrary legal category—can lose their immigration status and become subject to deportation after they’ve served out their sentences. In a case of what immigrant advocates call “double punishment,” Rom’s conviction for attempted murder meant he would lose his green card. And he knew that in the post-9/11 era, his home country had bowed to US pressure to accept the repatriation of deported Cambodian refugees.

Rom likes to say that prison saved his life. He spent much of his time in lockup reading, exercising, and making cents an hour working in the kitchen to save up for small gifts for his daughter. As he served out his sentence, a generation of shifts in America’s immigration policy flew by—from President Obama’s support of the Dreamers and record-high deportation numbers to Trump’s policy of family separation to Biden’s border crisis to Trump’s return to the White House. But in Rom’s mind, none of that made a difference for him: His fate was already sealed. He was fully prepared to be deported to Cambodia, a country he had never visited, once his sentence was finished.

Rom’s reserved mood as his prison term neared its end confused fellow inmates. “Yo, why are you not happy?” they asked. “You’re about to be released, you made parole.” On November 13, 2024, Rom walked out after serving 15 years. He was immediately transferred to Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) holding, where what he believed would be a straightforward process turned into a bewildering journey through the American deportation machine.

From detention, Rom did what he could to hasten the process of his own removal. He frequently called the Cambodian embassy in Washington, DC, seeking information on his departure process. He was told that his questions should be directed to the Department of Homeland Security. Officers in Pennsylvania, he says, had no updates. They told Rom they were swamped by the number of cases they were trying to handle. At one point, they told him he would be deported to Thailand, his country of birth, which he told them was incorrect. After six months of detention, Rom filed a writ of habeas corpus arguing that he was being unlawfully detained.

He continued to wait for another three months. On September 24, 2025, his daughter’s birthday, Rom saw on a tablet device that his commissary account had been zeroed out. Among ICE detainees, this is a telltale sign that deportation is imminent. The following day, Rom was transferred to Pine Prairie ICE Processing Center in Louisiana. He was moved again a few days later to Alexandria, Louisiana, where the airport hums with obscure charter airlines that ferry deportees abroad. He waited there for another 72 hours before he started, again, asking officers what was happening.

At first, they said they didn’t know, a claim Rom found hard to believe. When an officer checked his case on the computer system and then quickly clammed up, Rom’s suspicions grew. Finally they told him to pack up, and Rom caught a look at the immigration paperwork. It listed Eswatini as the country of deportation. Confused, Rom turned to another detainee: “Hey, bro, is Eswatini another ICE facility in Louisiana?” he asked. The man responded that it was in southern Africa.

Rom ran to the phone and made a panicked call to his sister in Las Vegas. She searched online and confirmed that Eswatini was indeed in Africa. Rom asked her to call the Cambodian Embassy. Another detainee’s lawyer suggested the group request “reasonable fear” interviews: US Citizenship and Immigration Services conducts these interviews through their asylum officers to ascertain if a person has a reasonable fear of persecution or torture. Immigration agents seemed amused by the suggestion, Rom says. What could the men be afraid of? they asked. “Y’all a bunch of murderers,” Rom recalls them taunting.

Rom pleaded with the guards. Eventually, one officer told him and the others that if they agreed to be shackled, he would take down their request in writing. They stepped into the restraints, and the officer set about filling out some paperwork. As they sat waiting, their feet and hands in chains, Rom gave the request for a “reasonable fear” interview one last try. “Hey,” he asked, “you think you are going to give us that interview?” He realized it was “all fake” and that the officers never intended to file anything.

One by one, under heavy guard, the men were loaded onto a small airplane. When the door closed it was “like my coffin is closing,” says KV, another deportee on the flight who asked to be identified by his initials because he remains detained. The plane flew from Alexandria to Puerto Rico. The officials accompanying the men gave them no information. They tried, and failed, to disable the small TVs that showed the plane’s flight path.

The plane carried on to Senegal, where they were delayed as officials sought permission to travel onward. “They were basically smuggling us through Africa,” Rom says. When the flight touched down at King Mswati III International Airport early on the morning of October 6, the runway was lined with dozens of armed military personnel. Rom was loaded into a paddy wagon. As the van turned onto a dirt road, Rom was overcome with fear. Oh yeah, they are going to kill us, Rom thought. They’re definitely going to kill us.

Eswatini, a country about the size of New Jersey surrounded almost entirely by South Africa, is the continent’s last remaining absolute monarchy. In April 1973, King Sobhuza II suspended the constitution and banned all political parties. The ban remains in place. His son, King Mswati III, took power in 1986, at just 18 years old. Challenges to the monarchy have been met with violence. In June 2021, security forces cracked down on pro-democracy protests, killing 46 people, including two children, according to a government inquiry. Civil society organizations say this is an undercount. A leading human rights lawyer and pro-democracy activist was assassinated at his home two years later. No one has been held accountable for his death.

Eswatini has the highest HIV prevalence rates in the world. Poverty in the country is pervasive. A 2024 report from the US State Department describes Eswatini as having “significant human rights issues,” including torture, media restrictions, and the worst forms of child labor. The king is reportedly married to a cohort of wives and lives a life divorced from his country’s brutal reality. At an April event to mark his 40 years on the throne, he held an extravagant party at a luxury convention center that included a performance by Grammy-nominated singer Davido.

The men, none of whom had committed any crime in Eswatini, were deposited in a high-security area of the Matsapha Correctional Centre. Rom’s cell sat behind a large metal door with a barred window painted forest green. When the detainees arrived, the cells were dirty. They asked for cleaning products but were told that none were available. Basic hygiene supplies took four months to arrive. In a dirt courtyard outside, the men had access to a row of concrete sinks to hand-wash their clothes during their 15 daily minutes of outdoor time. One detainee was allowed to retrieve the clothes in the evening.

Rom’s 15-year sentence in the US proved to be a useful experience. He fell back into his workout routine from prison, knocking out 500 pushups a day along with burpees and sit-ups. He read books donated to the prison, churning through the Hunger Games and Twilight series. His lawyer sent him a copy of First They Killed My Father, a memoir about surviving the Khmer Rouge. Rom had expected the weather to cool down at night, but there was little reprieve from the heat. He was given heavy blankets, which meant choosing between sweltering or being swarmed by mosquitoes. He asked jailers the same question he had been asking the guards in the US. “How long am I going to be here?” No one had an answer.

Gradually, the men were granted a few freedoms. They were brought to a mobile store so those without phones could buy them using money from their families. On the way back, they were allowed to eat at KFC and buy two beers apiece. These were the first excursions Rom had taken outside of detention facilities in more than 15 years.

In a post on X, a DHS official had said that the men being deported to Eswatini were “depraved monsters,” and those statements had spread in local media. So Rom smiled and waved, saying hello to the people who stopped to gawk.

The men were permitted to make one phone call a week using WhatsApp. The calls were broadcast in a common room; a prison official would sit nearby, scribbling notes. When Rom wanted to discuss something sensitive with his sister, the two would switch to speaking in Khmer. Local lawyers were barred from visiting. Shortly after Rom was deported, his sister saw a post on Facebook that mentioned Tin Thanh Nguyen, the lawyer in North Carolina. She got in touch, and Nguyen agreed to take Rom’s case.

Thanks to a TV in the common room that was set to the news, Rom was well aware of the controversy surrounding his fellow deportees. He watched a report about one fellow Matsapha inmate from Cuba, Roberto Mosquera del Peral, who had arrived earlier and was on a hunger strike. Each morning, when officers checked Mosquera del Peral’s cell, Rom says, they would shout, mockingly asking if he was still alive.

How Rom and the others came to be trapped in Eswatini is still unclear. According to a report from Reuters, the deal was brokered in February 2025 between Caitlin Piper, a diplomat who was leading the US embassy in the capital of Mbabane, and Russell Dlamini, the country’s prime minister, who sought and received approval from the king. Neither Dlamini nor the acting government spokesperson, Thabile Mdluli, responded to requests for comment. In May, a spokesperson for King Mswati told Reuters that “the king embraced the deal as Eswatini’s contribution to world order.”

Piper has since departed Eswatini. She was most recently based at the US Consulate in Erbil, Iraq, according to social media posts from the consulate. The consulate did not respond to requests for comment on her current position.

In November 2025, more than a month after Rom arrived and four months after the first deportees were deposited at the prison, Eswatini’s finance minister confirmed that he had received a $5.1 million payment from the US. He said the money would be funneled toward Eswatini’s National Disaster Management Agency. The country said it would accept 160 deportees as part of the agreement. The State Department eventually posted a copy of the memorandum of understanding signed between the two countries on May 14, 2025, which confirmed these details. The signature of the US official includes a looping C, but no name is printed underneath. Asked for comment, the Eswatini Press Inquiries Team at the embassy directed questions to the Department of State in Washington. The Department of State did not respond to questions regarding who signed the agreement.

This payment disclosure led the men in detention to charge that they had been trafficked. “You are moving people around, and money is exchanged,” says KV. “So what is that?” If Eswatini got all that money, Rom wondered, why was he having to spend his own on basics like a fan for his cell?

The Southern Africa Litigation Centre (SALC), a human rights NGO, joined several other groups in filing a legal challenge against Eswatini in 2025, aiming to declare the deportation agreement unconstitutional, obtain the terms of the agreement, and secure an order stopping further arrivals until the matter was legally resolved. The initial challenge was dismissed. SALC and the other groups filed an appeal to the Supreme Court of Eswatini in March. (Just weeks before her reported meeting with Eswatini’s prime minister to facilitate the deportations, Piper had addressed an SALC workshop, urging participants “to bring injustice to light, to demand the respect of those in power, to advocate for a world in which our most cherished freedoms are secured.” )

Rom’s personal path to Eswatini appears to have come about through a combination of cruelty and bureaucratic ineptitude. Nguyen points out that Cambodia has been accepting deportees for more than two decades and that the country is generally responsive and professional in issuing the requisite travel documents. He says that the Cambodian government moved to begin rectifying the situation within a week of his contacting them in October 2025.

Nguyen believes that the US erroneously thought Rom should be sent to Thailand, the country of his birth, not citizenship, but never bothered to contact government officials from that country or from Cambodia. Chann Rotana, a spokesman for Cambodia’s foreign ministry, says they also do not understand why Rom was sent to Africa. He adds that the US did not contact the country’s embassy in Washington, DC, regarding Rom’s case before he left the country. Rotana described the deportation to Africa as “strange” and said the embassy only became aware of the situation when Rom’s sister got in touch. Asked about Rotana’s comments, the Department of Homeland Security directed questions to the Department of State, which declined to respond.

“There’s no rhyme or reason as to why any of my clients were picked to go to Eswatini versus the clients that I have who are actually in Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia,” Nguyen says. “There’s nothing that makes them distinguishable.”

After an interview with Cambodian officials, Rom’s case for removal progressed while the other men sat hoping for similar developments. When he was cleared to be sent to Cambodia in early February, he requested that he be escorted by a representative from the UN’s International Organization for Migration as a form of insurance. Rom finally arrived in Cambodia on March 26, becoming the second US deportee freed from Eswatini. (The first, a Jamaican man named Orville Isaac Etoria, was deported to Eswatini in July 2025 and repatriated to Jamaica in September of that year.)

When I met Rom in June, at a restaurant in the Cambodian capital of Phnom Penh, he was dressed in all black. The sleeves of his polo shirt were taut around his thick, tattooed arms. We met within walking distance of S-21, a Khmer Rouge torture and interrogation center that is now a museum dedicated to the genocide that set his family on its path to America. He is contemplative about his journey and speaks softly.

After arriving in Cambodia, Rom enrolled in a creative writing class, hoping to detail his experience. He was taking teacher training courses, hoping to land a job at an international school, and picked up work fixing air conditioners when he could.

The jarring transition to life in Cambodia was made harder when his older sister—who had been his lifeline in detention—died in July. “Im trying to hold it together,” he texted me. In August, Rom sent a string of messages saying he’d gotten a job teaching English to a rambunctious group of third graders.

Once a week, Rom calls the deportees who remain in Africa. There are more now. A few weeks after Rom and I met, Eswatini announced that it had received a fourth batch of 11 deportees. Another group followed. Thirty-two have been forced to the country in total. KV remains imprisoned. “With the holidays around the corner,” he texted in late September, “it feels I’m wasting my life sitting here for no reason.” It is unclear when he will be released.

The fate of all third-country deportees stands to be clarified in the coming months. After at least twice granting the Trump administration temporary stays to keep deporting migrants to third countries, the US Supreme Court will start hearing oral arguments on the ultimate legality of the practice in December.

Shortly after he arrived in Cambodia, Rom noticed that Kuenene, his warden at Matsapha, had added him as a contact on Telegram and WhatsApp. Another Eswatini official requested to connect on Facebook and follow Rom on Instagram. Rom accepted some of the invitations and declined others; he couldn’t tell why they were trying to befriend him. Maybe Kuenene wanted to keep tabs on what Rom might say publicly. “He was one of those guys that tell you what you want to hear, smile at your face, make you believe that he is the most supportive guy in the world, and then he will stab you in the back,” Rom said. “I didn’t trust him.” Before he was released, Rom told the guards and other deportees that he might return to Eswatini to visit as a tourist. In reality, he has no intention of ever returning. One visit as a royal guest was enough.

The ‘True Crime Community’ Is Inspiring Violent Attacks Through TikTok

📅 Nov 25, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The fetishization of mass murderers has been around since long before TikTok.

Bonnie and Clyde were folk heroes. Serial killer Ted Bundy was adored by a legion of female fans. And the mother of Columbine shooter Dylan Klebold reported receiving love letters to her son in the mail written by young girls.

More recently, this phenomenon has manifested in a network known as the True Crime Community, or TCC, a group of mainly young people who lionize mass shooters like Klebold. (This group is distinct from true crime enthusiasts who like to read books or listen to podcasts about unsolved crimes.)

While this community has been around for years, in 2026, it moved en masse to TikTok. There, supercharged by the video-sharing platform's powerful black-box algorithm, the community’s violent, nihilistic worldview expanded its reach dramatically, serving up graphic content to users who had never been exposed before.

Tracking the scale and real-world impact of the network has been difficult, because the coded language, symbols, and memes used within the group are constantly changing to avoid detection by platforms and law enforcement. On TikTok some of the group members most interested in violence began referring to the group as the True Cringe Community. When TikTok blocked that term, they began referring to themselves as the True Larp Community.

But now, for the first time, the National Contagion Research Institute (NCRI), a Princeton, New Jersey–based nonprofit that tracks online extremism, has collated data that shows that attacks and attempted attacks linked to the TCC have exploded by 200 percent over the last two years. The data, shared exclusively with WIRED ahead of publication, shows that the number of annual attacks or attempted attacks have gone from 13 reported in all of 2024 to 30 by mid-October this year.

The footprint of these attacks also expanded globally. Attacks and attempted attacks linked to the TCC were registered in 13 different countries in 2026, with two of every three incidents happening outside the US. And NCRI’s researchers predict that without intervention, the situation is about to get much worse.

“This is heading towards an explosion,” Joel Finkelstein, chief science officer with NCRI, tells WIRED. “That's what the data is suggesting, and the data is unambiguous. It's heading next year to a point where this is going to be happening more than once a week. That's really alarming.”

The TCC is part of a broader online nihilistic violent extremism movement and often overlaps with the extremist crime network known as “The Com,” which incorporates groups like 764 and No Lives Matter,

The TCC’s members, a large proportion of which are teenagers, share content online that references, glorifies, mimics, and simulates school shooters, mass shooters, and serial killers from all over the world. This is done through flattering videos posted on social media, taking pictures replicating the hand signals attackers used during their killing sprees, or wearing the T-shirts worn by mass shooters. The members also use AI to turn videos of mass shootings into cartoons or anime in order to avoid detection by online platforms’ trust and safety teams.

Beyond posting fan fiction about their favorite school shooters, this community also encourages others to carry out their own attacks. If successful, those attackers in turn become part of TCC’s ever-expanding lore and mythology, becoming part of a self-reinforcing cycle.

While writing its report, NCRI researchers documented numerous other attacks linked to the group, including the September 18 shooting at a high school in the Philippines and an October 1 attack at a secondary school in Torreón, Mexico. There was also a shooting on October 12 at a middle school in France and a knife attack on October 13 in South Africa that have also been linked to the TCC community.

While TCC was found on a lot of platforms online, for the first 10 years of its existence, much of its activity was centered on Tumblr. But in January, the site implemented a ban wave that drove much of the community to TikTok, where the platform’s unique algorithm put TCC-related content in front of a much broader audience, according to a report from the Institute of Strategic Dialogue published earlier this month.

“Algorithmically driven engagement has supercharged its spread,” the report’s authors wrote. “Unlike Tumblr, where the fandom is largely isolated, TikTok actively works to expose users to new content through its algorithm, resulting in TCC videos being served to a much larger audience.”

Finkelstein says that while TikTok does remove some of the accounts his team flags to the platform, in general the company is not doing enough to combat the problem.

“[TikTok’s] capacity to manage this is very spotty,” Finkelstein said. “This is an uncontained open wound. Whatever measures they think they're making to manage this are obviously inadequate to deal with the damage that the community is unleashing. If you took TikTok offline for a few weeks, my guess is these attacks would slow down because there's not a permission structure in place that's encouraging vulnerable youth to go out and murder people in order to be famous with their peers because they're so depressed and living in their mom's basement and spending 10 hours a day on social media.”

TikTok, whose US operations are now owned primarily by a group of investors including Oracle, controlled by GOP megadonor and Donald Trump ally Larry Ellison, said it had rules in place to proactively remove TCC-linked content.

“We enforce strict rules against content that promotes violence or glorifies perpetrators of mass violence, and we had already banned a large number of the accounts in this report,” a TikTok spokesperson tells WIRED.

WIRED shared the report with several other researchers who closely track TCC activity online, and all broadly concluded that NCRI’s assessment was accurate. One independent researcher, Ry Terran, pointed out that accurately tracking this movement is difficult given the dense nature of the language they use around attacks.

“When examining perpetrators' social media, I see that they frequently frame threats as a joke, a LARP, or AI-generated,” Terran tells WIRED. “Social media companies should not take these claims at face value, but rather report any credible threats. Without appropriate interventions, TCC-inspired attacks will likely continue to increase.”

Another European-based researcher, who requested anonymity due to fears of retaliation, tells WIRED, “We're panicking here in Europe.” The researcher collaborates with European law enforcement agencies and says they are concerned about the increasing number of attacks taking place globally.

Finkelstein says that US lawmakers and law enforcement need to do more in order to combat this problem. “The FBI seems to want to deal with it by arresting kids. None of this makes any sense,” he says. “We've got to step back and see the scale of this. We should be turning to our law enforcement agencies and our national security apparatus that has the ear of the president and say something's got to be done about this.”

The FBI declined to comment, and the White House did not respond to a request for comment. The Department of Homeland Security referred WIRED to the FBI.

“The Justice Department remains vigilant against the threat of such violent extremist networks that operate within the United States and around the globe,” Wyn Hornbuckle, a spokesman for the Department of Justice, tells WIRED.

‘Root-to-Tip’ Eating: Are You Missing Out on Fiber by Discarding Scraps?

Eating fruit and veggie scraps may boost fiber intake and cut food waste.

This study from King’s College London found that peels, skins, seeds, and stalks of fruits and vegetables—parts often discarded—contained significantly more dietary fiber than the commonly eaten portions in 10 of 12 produce types analyzed.

In some cases, the fiber content was up to five times higher in these “root-to-tip” components.

These findings, recently published in the International Journal of Food Sciences and Nutrition, are noteworthy, since around 95% of U.S. adults aren’t getting enough fiber. What’s more, around one-fifth of all food produced globally is wasted every year.

The researchers identified 12 fresh produce items that were deemed suitable for “root-to-tip” inclusion:

In the laboratory analysis, they measured the dietary fiber content of both the “root-to-tip” components (skins, peels, seeds, stalks) and the parts that people typically eat.

The key findings of the study showed that in 10 of the 12 produce types, the “root-to-tip” components contained more fiber than the parts typically consumed.

However, broccoli stalks contained 28.4% less fiber than the florets that people usually eat, meaning individuals are already consuming the most fiber-rich part.

Most participants in focus groups saw the nutritional value of the “root-to-tip” approach as more of a motivator than its sustainability.

However, these findings show that a “root-to-tip” approach to many fruits and vegetables could not only support higher dietary fiber intake but also reduce food waste.

Kristin Kirkpatrick, president of KAK, Consulting, LLC, and a dietitian at the Cleveland Clinic Department of Wellness & Preventive Medicine, said that the takeaway isn’t to eat every peel or seed, but to look for safe, simple ways to get more fiber from the foods you’re already buying. Kirkpatrick wasn’t involved in the study.

This applies to some of the plants analyzed in this study, but not all produce. For instance, you can only eat a kiwi that’s peeled. This is still a better choice than no kiwi (or a processed-food snack in its place) at all.

Fiber is important for promoting health and preventing chronic conditions such as cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, colorectal cancer, and obesity.

“Fiber does much more than support regular bowel movements. It helps regulate blood sugar, supports beneficial gut bacteria, and certain types of soluble fiber can help lower LDL cholesterol by increasing bile acid elimination,” noted Michelle Routhenstein, a preventive cardiology dietitian at Entirely Nourished, who wasn’t involved in the study.

On average, adults are recommended to get 25–30 grams (or more, depending on age) of fiber daily.

Both nutrition experts told Healthline that people generally aren’t getting enough fiber because of their food options or choices.

“Most likely because individuals are not consuming enough plants, and overconsuming processed and ultra-processed foods which are typically very low in fiber,” said Kirkpatrick. “Simply not eating enough fruits and vegetables or choosing white refined grains over whole grains can be why a person’s diet is lacking fiber.”

Routhenstein added, “I think it is important that when we talk about fiber we point out that it doesn’t have to mean completely changing how you eat. I encourage people to incorporate fiber-rich ingredients into familiar, culturally meaningful foods they already enjoy.”

“Start with small increases with foods you enjoy and build gradually to allow your digestive system to adjust.”

It is important to always research any new plant parts before eating them, wash thoroughly, and consult a healthcare professional if you have digestive conditions or concerns.

And remember that not all produce scraps are edible or recommended.

“Any dietary change will be dependent on personal preference. Some individuals may not enjoy or may not know how to add components of a plant they are not used to,” said Kirkpatrick.

She noted that, based on the study’s examples, an easier option might be to add more kiwi peels and seeds. “In fact, kiwi skin is probably the most practical fruit peel to highlight for consumers,” she said.

Routhenstein added, “I think there is a knowledge gap that the skins, peels, from the root to the tip are edible and nutritious, so we need to start there.”

She suggested that one way to start is by eating the skins of apples, pears, and well-scrubbed carrots. Trim and thinly slice broccoli stalks for stir-fries or soups. Sautée beet greens and stems just as you would spinach. Freeze appropriate vegetable trimmings, such as carrot ends and celery leaves, for homemade broth.

“Root-to-tip eating helps reduce food waste by encouraging us to use more of the edible portions of fruits and vegetables rather than discarding them. It also maximizes the nutritional and economic value of the food we purchase while making better use of the water, land, and energy required to produce it,” said Routhenstein.

“The key is finding simple, enjoyable ways to incorporate these parts into meals we already eat.”

While widespread adoption promises efficiency and productivity gains, it also makes technology less of a differentiator. As AI agents take on more work, make decisions and interact with customers on a company’s behalf, an important question emerges: Do those agents truly understand the company they’re working for?

The difference often lies in the decisions employees make every day, drawing on experience, customer knowledge and an understanding of what their organization values. These are the nuances that generic AI systems may struggle to replicate without additional guidance.

What if a company could encode its corporate identity into its AI systems? The challenge for organizations is ensuring these agents reflect what makes the company unique: its secret sauce and competitive edge.

Agents need more than basic training. Like employees, they require ongoing guidance, performance monitoring and coaching to ensure their actions reflect the company’s approach to customer service.

Beyond workflows and policies, AI agents need to understand the company’s DNA: the institutional knowledge, expertise, decision-making principles, culture and values that shape how work gets done.

With AI capabilities becoming increasingly commoditized, company DNA becomes more valuable. Used right, AI should amplify what differentiates a company, not dilute it. That means teaching agents how work actually gets done, including the informal practices and judgment calls that contribute to success.

AI agents can also help preserve this knowledge as organizations grow, ensuring it remains accessible even as teams change.

Define success metrics a competitor couldn’t copy: If agents are measured only on resolution rates and handling time, organizations risk creating the same experience as everyone else. Those differences should shape how agent performance is measured.

Codify the judgment calls, not just the rules: Mission statements and standard operating procedures provide a starting point, but company DNA often shows up in how employees handle exceptions. Organizations can then use these examples to show agents how experienced employees navigate situations that don’t have a straightforward answer.

This should include the informal workarounds employees use when established processes fall short. These workarounds can reveal gaps between formal procedures and the realities of day-to-day work. By documenting these practices, organizations can help agents understand not just what gets done, but how and why.

Write down how to break a tie: Rules can’t anticipate every situation. Agents need guidance on how to navigate trade-offs, such as balancing policy with customer goodwill or speed with empathy. These principles should also define when an agent needs to involve a human, so escalation becomes a designed decision, never a failure.

High-Speed Nanoscale Brain Imaging Uncovers Hidden Synapses

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Researchers dramatically accelerated electron tomography to map large volumes of brain tissue at 15-nanometer 3D resolution. The breakthrough captures fine neural structures missed by conventional connectomes, including rapid-fire electrical synapses and uncharacterized protrusions where neurons appear to “nibble” neighboring glial cells.

To understand how billions of neurons orchestrate thoughts, memories, and movement, neuroscientists construct connectomes, detailed wiring diagrams mapping how brain cells connect and communicate. While macro-scale MRI scans chart major white-matter highways and standard electron microscopy traces individual chemical synapses, scaling up to large tissue volumes has historically forced a difficult compromise: sacrificing nanoscale structural detail for imaging speed.

As a result, even the most ambitious brain wiring maps have routinely missed ultra-fine cellular architectures, including electrical synapses (also called gap junctions), microscopic channels that enable direct, near-instantaneous electrical communication between adjacent neurons.

Now, in a study published November 19 in Nature Methods, researchers at Harvard Medical School (HMS) and the University of California San Diego have overcome that bottleneck by supercharging electron tomography—a 3D imaging technique that reconstructs nanoscale architecture by tilting tissue samples and capturing views from multiple angles.

Led by co-senior authors Dr. Wei-Chung Allen Lee (associate professor of neurobiology at HMS and associate professor of neurology at Boston Children’s Hospital) and Dr. Mark Ellisman (UC San Diego), alongside co-first authors Dr. Aaron T. Kuan and Dr. Sébastien Phan, the team demonstrated that large blocks of brain tissue can be mapped in exquisite 3D nanoscale detail without prohibitive imaging times.

Conventional electron tomography is painstakingly slow because it typically requires capturing hundreds of tilted images across razor-thin tissue slices. By refining image acquisition and computational reconstruction, Lee, Ellisman, and colleagues discovered they could cut the required number of images by roughly 50-fold while imaging tissue sections 20 times thicker than standard electron microscopy protocols, preserving an extraordinary 3D resolution of 15 nanometers or finer.

“We can now see brain structures that we haven’t been able to see before in connectomics data,” explained Lee. “We hope this work will help us understand how nervous systems are built; what changes with age; and what goes awry in neurodevelopmental, cognitive, and neurodegenerative disorders.”

When the researchers applied their high-speed tomography pipeline to tissue from the mouse cerebellum, the 3D reconstructions revealed both long-sought neural connections and surprising cellular behaviors:

“We’re seeing uncharacterized structures, suggesting that neurons are ‘nibbling’ their neighboring cells,” Lee said.

In addition, the researchers traced delicate nerve fibers continuously through the thick 3D tissue block right down to their exact synaptic contact points with target neurons.

While scaling this accelerated tomography workflow to map an entire mammalian brain will require automated tissue-handling robotics, the breakthrough bridges a critical resolution gap in modern connectomics—equipping neuroscientists to examine how fine electrical and glial-neuronal connections form in early life and break down in brain disease.

Why do traditional brain wiring maps (connectomes) miss certain structures?

Mapping large volumes of brain tissue at high speed usually forces scientists to sacrifice ultra-fine resolution. As a result, critical microscopic features, such as tiny electrical synapses (gap junctions) that connect neurons directly, often blur out or go undetected in large-scale datasets.

How did researchers speed up nanoscale 3D brain imaging?

The team optimized electron tomography so that it requires about 50 times fewer multi-angle images on tissue slices 20 times thicker than usual, while still maintaining a 3D resolution of 15 nanometers or finer.

What surprising new structures did they see in the brain tissue?

Alongside hard-to-see electrical synapses in the mouse cerebellum, the researchers discovered tiny protrusions between neurons and neighboring support cells (glia), suggesting that neurons physically “nibble” or take up material from adjacent cells.

3 defaults that quietly take your system down (and the one-line fixes)

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Most outages I've read about weren't caused by too little hardware. They were caused by a default setting nobody questioned, which worked fine until the traffic changed shape.

I build Bugpit, a browser game where your system design runs against simulated traffic and breaks. This week I added three levels where you can't buy your way out: a bigger machine is over budget, so the fix has to be a setting. Each one comes from something real systems actually do.

The default: LRU, least recently used. When the cache is full, throw out whatever nobody asked for in the longest time.

When it breaks: a news site gets 24,000 requests a second, and the cache answers 85% of them. Then a search engine crawler reads every old article once.

To LRU, every one of those crawled articles is the most recently used thing in the cache. So it keeps articles nobody will read again, and throws out the front page to make room.

The fix: LFU, least frequently used. It counts how often each key is read, so one read from a crawler can't outrank a page read thousands of times a minute.

Where you've seen this: MySQL's InnoDB buffer pool doesn't put newly read pages at the front of its LRU list. It inserts them in the middle, so a full table scan can't flush out the hot pages. Caffeine, the Java cache library, uses W-TinyLFU partly because it holds up under scans.

The catch: LFU trusts history. When something new gets popular, old favourites with huge counts hang around, and the new page keeps getting evicted. There's no best policy, only one that fits your traffic.

The default: round robin. Server 1, 2, 3, 4, then 1 again. It never looks at how busy anyone is.

When it breaks: a team dashboard gets 7,000 requests a second on 4 servers that can handle 8,000 together. On paper there's room. But 1 request in 10 is a CSV export that takes 10 times as long as the rest.

A server that picks up a few exports in a row gets stuck on them, and round robin keeps handing it new requests anyway. They pile up behind the exports while another server sits idle. Round robin spreads requests evenly, not work. On average the servers are fine, but the busiest one overloads.

The fix: least connections. Send each request to the server with the fewest requests in flight. A server stuck on exports has many open, so new requests go elsewhere until it catches up.

Where you've seen this: HAProxy has balance leastconn and recommends it for long sessions. AWS Application Load Balancer offers "least outstanding requests" as an alternative to round robin.

When round robin is fine: when every request takes about the same time. Then taking turns spreads the work evenly too, and it costs nothing to track.

The default: a fixed window. Count requests per minute, and reset to zero on the minute. It's the easiest thing to build:

When it breaks, part 1: scrapers learn the reset. They send a full minute's worth at 0:59, the counter resets, and they send another full minute's worth at 1:00. For a moment your servers get twice the limit.

In the sim, a limit of 7,000 that 4 servers can carry lets 14,000 through around each reset, and the servers fall over right on the minute. Halving the limit stops the crash, but then you turn away half the people you could serve all day long.

The fix: a sliding window. Count the last 60 seconds, whenever now is. There's no reset to aim for. Cloudflare has written about using a sliding window approximation for their rate limiting for this reason.

When it breaks, part 2: real users aren't smooth either. Say each user gets 10 requests a second and averages 2. Then they open a dashboard that fires 30 calls at once. Any window, fixed or sliding, counts 30 in one second and refuses 20 of them. Half the widgets show "Too many requests".

The fix: a token bucket. Each user gets a bucket that refills at 10 tokens a second and holds up to 30. Each request spends one token. A user who was quiet has a full bucket, so the burst goes through, and the average still can't go over 10.

Where you've seen this: AWS API Gateway throttling is set as a rate plus a burst, which is a token bucket. Stripe's engineering post on rate limiters describes token buckets in Redis.

All three defaults have something in common:

None of them are wrong. Each one assumes your traffic is smooth and even, and real traffic usually isn't. A bigger machine hides the problem for a while. Knowing what your traffic looks like fixes it.

All three are free levels in the Core concepts section of Bugpit. Each starts already broken. Press Run, watch what fails, and change one setting. The numbers in this post come straight from those levels.

Kalshi daily volume of ‘perps’ trading begins to top its prediction markets

📅 2026-11-24  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Kalshi is known for its prediction markets across sports, politics and culture.

But now, for the first time, perpetual futures, Kalshi's second financial product, has surpassed the notional trading volume of its event contracts on consecutive days.

On Thursday, perpetual futures — commonly known as "perps" — on Kalshi saw $3.4 billion in notional volume as artificial intelligence laboratory Anthropic stock debuted on public markets while the rest of the market sold off. That exceeded about $3 billion in notional volume for Kalshi's prediction markets on the same day.

Perps are futures-style contracts where there is no expiration and traders do not need to own the underlying asset. Contracts instead continuously track the price of an asset, with funding payments to keep the contract price aligned with that of the market. Funding payments are made periodically between holders of short or long positions.

Perps trading surpassed event contracts one day in September, but on Friday perps topped prediction markets volume for a second day. Now Kalshi expects that performance to continue as it introduces perps tied to more individual stocks. On Monday, for example, Kalshi launched contracts linked to Anthropic.

Kalshi was the first to debut perps in the U.S. after winning federal regulatory approval in May to list perps tied to cryptocurrencies.

Perps trading volume includes any leverage that speculators acquire along with their trade, while event contracts are fully collateralized — for now.

Since the original launch in May, Kalshi has received approval to introduce perps tied to metals, crude oil, stock indexes and individual stocks.

Offshore perps — those available on exchanges not regulated in the U.S. — generated more than $90 trillion in notional trading volume in 2025, so it's not surprising that the product has grown so rapidly on Kalshi, according to Udesh Jha, chief risk officer at Kalshi Klear, the company's clearing house.

"We're just in very early innings. We're launching products, we're growing the participant base."

Perps were Kalshi's first product to mark its transition from a prediction market-focused platform to a full-blown financial exchange where various assets are traded. Last week, reports said that Kalshi is planning to offer trading on equities on its platform in the coming months.

At the same time, the company's perps product has come under fire. Some incumbent exchanges where futures are traded — which saw their stock prices decline following the approval of U.S. perps — believe the product was improperly approved. CME Group is suing the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, the federal derivatives regulator, for approving perps.

Others have simply expressed concerns about the efficiency of the markets. CNBC previously reported that trading volume on Kalshi's most popular perps markets — tied to bitcoin and ether cryptocurrencies — was dramatically greater than open interest, or cash actively in the markets. It's a phenomenon that isn't common with U.S.-regulated traditional futures, nor other offshore perps markets.

Kalshi said at the time it has "zero concerns" about the distinction, and that it appears because less leverage is offered compared to offshore exchanges and there are fewer incentives for traders to park capital on its perps markets.

While Kalshi started with cryptocurrencies, Owen Lau, an analyst at Clear Street, said perps tied to individual stocks likely represent the most promising opportunity for growth in the U.S.

"That's how I foresee the way perps will penetrate further into traditional asset classes," Lau said. "Perps on single stocks and [key performance indicators on individual companies] could change how investors hedge their portfolio or even make a directional bet."

Kalshi's growth has been powered by the fact that right now it's the only exchange that can actively list perps in the U.S., though crypto exchange Coinbase received approval in May to route U.S. users to offshore platforms for trading. That monopoly status, however, will probably prove short lived.

Coinbase has filed to list single-stock perps in the U.S., and President Donald Trump in August said the CFTC is working to bring Hyperliquid — a dominant player in offshore perps activity — to domestic traders.

But Jha at Kalshi isn't worried about the competition.

"What will carry us is ... making sure that we have the right products for the marketplace, making sure we have the right surveillance of the products that we are trading."

Báo cáo là khung tham chiếu cho Chính phủ Albanese hoàn thiện luật AI đầu tiên của Úc. Nguồn trích dẫn: InnovationAus
