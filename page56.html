NCIS Season 24 has given longtime fans a reason to celebrate Michael Weatherly's return as Tony DiNozzo, but the team won't ride that nostalgia wave forever. According to Katrina Law, the job's emotional toll is about to catch up with Jessica Knight and her colleagues in ways viewers haven't seen before. The actress, who has played Knight since Season 18, tells Collider that the team will face a breaking point this season, with some particularly revealing episodes still ahead.

"I think this season you really get to see a lot of Knight's vulnerability in a way that you've never experienced before," Law says. "This season, you get to see her break in a way that you haven't seen before." And Knight won't be the only one struggling. Law adds that viewers will see "the entire team break" in a way the series hasn't explored before, making the aftermath as important as the moments that lead to it.

Before those emotional storylines unfold, however, the November 10 episode, "Speechless," gives Law a very different reason to remember Season 24. The installment marks the acting debut of her seven-year-old daughter, Kinley Andreen, who plays a young girl caught in the middle of a frightening investigation. It's a family milestone for the actress, but it also brings a personal connection to an episode built around the lengths a parent will go to protect their child.

Knight has spent much of her time on NCIS proving she can handle whatever comes through the doors at headquarters. A former member of the agency's REACT team, she joined the Major Case Response Team after the deaths of several colleagues and the arrest of her former team leader. She's since become an established part of the group, but Law's comments suggest Season 24 will ask something different of her: What happens when the agent who used to hold it together can no longer do so?

Knight has already experienced significant personal and professional upheaval throughout her time on the series. Law's tease, though, points to a more exposed side of the character, one that hasn't necessarily had the same space to emerge amid the team's investigations. The actress is particularly interested in seeing audiences connect with that vulnerability, especially as the other agents face their own emotional struggles.

Law says two upcoming episodes have already been filmed, and she credits writer Scott Williams with understanding how to bring out the characters' emotional depths. "I think Scott Williams does such justice to the characters when it comes to their emotional depths and bringing out the best and the worst in them," she tells Collider. For Law, what makes the story worth watching is how the team comes together and moves forward after reaching that breaking point.

Tonight's episode, "Speechless," gives Law a chance to share the screen with someone she knows better than anyone: her daughter, Kinley. The seven-year-old makes her acting debut as Chloe Sims, an eight-year-old girl the NCIS team discovers hiding alone in her apartment after her Marine mother disappears.

Chloe is frightened and initially reluctant to help, but she has a surprising skill that could give investigators the breakthrough they need. Her fluency in Mandarin becomes an important part of the case, creating an unexpected connection between the young witness and Knight. Law tells Collider that the character's appearance makes that ability all the more striking, describing Chloe as a blonde-haired, green-eyed girl whose talent will surprise viewers.

The casting also makes the episode's central themes hit a little closer to home. The investigation revolves around a missing mother and a child left to navigate a frightening situation, while Law is experiencing the unusual privilege of playing opposite her own daughter. She says watching Kinley move from being her little girl to a young actor stepping into character was unlike anything she'd experienced.

Law's behind-the-scenes experience was just as memorable as what audiences will see on screen. In her Collider interview, she recalls watching Kinley switch into character, suddenly becoming Chloe rather than the little girl she knows at home. "I would literally kind of see my daughter disappear and then this little actress pop up," Law tells Collider. Fallout 5's Official Setting Is Music To Our Ears

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

It is baffling just how long it has taken to get a fifth mainline Fallout game, especially considering the enormous success of the TV show and the continued popularity of the once much maligned MMO, Fallout 76. Indeed, the mere fact that Fallout 76, a game that is still frequently regarded as the disaster developers should avoid replicating, regularly pulls in over 8,000 players daily on PC alone should illustrate just how popular this franchise is and how dire the need for a new entry is.

Of course, with Obsidian officially taking the reins and Fallout 5 confirmed to be in development, it feels as if things are finally in motion. Bethesda, despite its many flaws, manages to eschew the adage "too little, too late," if only due to the immense amount of goodwill it has generated from its handful of several decade-old RPGs. So, one can continue to be excited, even in the face of blindingly bright red flags. It also manages to maintain this hype with piecemeal updates about Fallout 5, the most recent of which was a confirmation of the game's setting.

One of the biggest concerns surrounding Fallout 5, at least in my opinion, is the game's setting. Sure, Fallout's gunmetal gray apocalypse worked wonders during the Xbox 360 era, but it's been made fairly evident through contemporary apocalyptic fiction that a world beyond repair doesn't have to look quite so drab. Fallout 4 flirted with this idea and Fallout 76 pushed it to an even greater extreme, but the potential for Fallout 5 to regress, at least in terms of its setting, is ever present.

Unfortunately, that concern will linger for a little while longer, as Todd Howard and the folks at Bethesda are yet to confirm the exact location of Fallout 5's setting. I would speculate that it's unlikely to be particularly desert-focused, if only so as to not clash with the Hammerfell-set Elder Scrolls VI. Beyond that, I simply couldn't say. What we do know, however, is that Fallout 5 will be directly connected to the Fallout TV show, incorporating its events in some fashion.

This, at the very least, reveals that Fallout 5 will be set in or around 2296, around nine years after the events of Fallout 4. This was confirmed by Todd Howard in a 2025 interview with the BBC, who stated that "Fallout 5 will be existing in a world where the stories and events of the show happened or are happening." Sure, it's as minuscule and ambiguous as information surrounding a new Bethesda game can get, but it certainly gives me hope that much of what we saw in the TV show will be elaborated upon in some capacity.

Howard's comments also seem to imply, at least loosely, that if the events of the TV show are being "tak[en] that into account", that the setting of the game and New Vegas (or at least Season 1's setting of LA) must be situated somewhat close to one another. It seems unlikely that events happening in LA or New Vegas would have any bearing on a Fallout game set in New York, for example.

Regardless, I, for one, am glad that the two projects are connected, even if tangentially. It's likely we'll hear about events that took place in both seasons (and potentially a third at this rate), and that will be about it. One can't imagine Walton Goggins putting in an appearance in Fallout 5 as his iconic character.

However, that being said, Bethesda has a penchant for putting famous faces in its video games, so who knows? Nevertheless, I am excited to see how the two crossover, and indeed, whether the setting of the TV show has any bearing on Fallout 5's location, or if Todd Howard and co. are merely setting us up for a rather spectacular fall.

A great old-timey game-programming hack (2013)

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A long time ago, when I was a college undergrad, I spent some time working on computer video games. This was in the 8-bit PC era, when the gaming hardware was almost impossibly slow by today’s standards.

It might not surprise you, then, to learn that game programmers of yore did all sorts of crazy things to make their games run at playable speeds. Crazy, crazy things.

This is a story about one of those things.

While I’ve done my best to recall the important details, I may have gotten some things wrong. If I have, please forgive me. It was a long time ago.

(Note: This post contains some small images as inline-data URLs that some feed readers apparently don’t handle well. If you can’t see the images in your feed, click through to the original article to see them.)

Update 2013-12-16: There have been lots of great comments about this story on Hacker News and Proggit, as well as here on the blog. Thanks for sharing, everyone!

A friend of mine, a gifted programmer, was nearly finished with a new game. Somehow, he had squeezed into a 1980’s-era PC, pretty faithfully, what was at the time a graphically impressive coin-op game popular at the arcades.

The only problem was that his version of the game was unplayable. It was too slow, and the choppy motion broke the player’s immersion. It was after all a side-scroller.

My friend, who had been working on the game while also taking a full load of college courses, was starting to seem a little frazzled. Fearing that he had missed some easy optimization, he asked me to take a look at the code.

I did. But there was no easy optimization to be had.

The code was tight. Everywhere I looked, he had already done all the things I could think of. Loops had been unrolled. Unneeded draws had been eliminated. All evidence of waste had vanished.

And, with them, our hopes of an easy fix.

Before I get to the crazy stuff, however, I should back up and explain how graphics hardware worked back then.

Here’s the quick version: The graphics hardware didn’t do jack squat.

On the PC in question, if you wanted to draw something onto the screen, you had to do that yourself, byte by byte. No texture mappers, no blitters. Just bytes. Bytes that you had to move yourself.

My friend’s game spent most of its time redrawing the background. (Again: side-scroller.) For every frame, it had to draw nearly a screen’s worth of background tiles, shifted for the player’s position.

If my memory serves me, each tile was 28 by 28 pixels. Each pixel was one of 16 colors, taking half a byte to represent. Therefore, tiles were represented in memory as 28 contiguous rows of 14 bytes each. The first 14 bytes represented the first row, the second 14 bytes the second row, and so on.

The screen, however, was 320 pixels wide by 240 pixels high. In memory, then, a screen buffer was laid out as 240 contiguous rows of 160 bytes each.

So when copying a tile at address X onto a screen buffer location starting at address Y, you had to copy 28 rows. To copy each row, you had to copy 14 bytes. Fortunately, the processor for this game was a 6809, which had a few 16-bit index registers and delightful “auto-increment” addressing modes (sort of like applying the postfix ++ operator to pointers in C). So when my friend showed me his code and asked if I could make it faster, I really wanted to help him. I really wanted to be able to answer yes.

But I had to answer no. I hated giving that answer. But, studying the code, I couldn’t see any way to speed it up.

Later, I couldn’t get the problem out of my head. Maybe I had missed something. I had grown up on Apple II computers and their 6502 processors. My friend’s code, however, was for the 6809. Maybe it afforded optimizations that I didn’t know about.

With renewing optimism, I dialed into the university’s library to access the card catalog. (These were the days before the World Wide Web.) Through a VT220 terminal emulator, I searched for books about the 6809.

There was one. A 6809 microprocessor manual. It was in the engineering library. Luckily, I was an engineering student and had sign-out privileges.

Just like that, I was off to the library.

When I got to the engineering library, I found the book right where it was supposed to be, looking a little beat up:

The MC6809-MC6809E Microprocessor Programming Manual.

Not bothering to find a chair, I flipped through the pages standing, looking for some oddball, 6809-specific instruction that might throw a lot of bytes and throw them fast. Page after page, though, turned up nothing.

Then I came upon PSHS. “Push registers on the hardware stack.”

On my beloved 6502, if you wanted to save registers on the stack, you had to do it one register at a time and, even then, by transferring them through the accumulator. It was slow and expensive. So I had learned to avoid the stack when speed mattered.

But on the 6809 you could save all of the registers – or any subset of them – with a single instruction. And, amazingly, it cost a mere 5 cycles, plus 1 cycle more per byte pushed.

Since the processor had three 16-bit general-purpose registers – D, X, and Y – I could load them up and then use a single PSHS instruction to write 6 bytes in just 11 cycles. The corresponding pull instruction, PULS, had the same low cost.

Further, the 6809 had two stack registers, S and U. I could use one as a source pointer and the other as a destination pointer. In theory, with a single PULS/PSHU pair, I could copy 6 bytes in 22 cycles.

Excitement mounting, I headed for the front desk and reached for my student ID. I was going to sign out that wonderful little book for further study.

Walking back to the dorms, I formed my plan.

I would save the S and U registers somewhere, and then point S at the background tile and U at the screen buffer. Then I would pull from S and push to U, copying 6 bytes at a time, using D, X, and Y as go-betweens. To copy the 14 bytes that make up a row would require three such iterations, which unrolled would be about 60 cycles.

Still, that last PULS/PSHU pair! What a clunker. It was needed to handle the final two bytes per row, since the rows were 28 pixels = 14 bytes wide, and 6 doesn’t divide 14 evenly.

If only the game had used 24-by-24 tiles instead… But it hadn’t, so I pored through the manual, looking for a way to lower the cost of that inevitable last pair.

And then, by surprise, I struck gold! It was another 6809 oddity, the DP register.

On the 6502 and most 8-bit processors of the era, the lowest 256 bytes of memory was called the zero page. The zero page was special because its memory locations had single-byte addresses and could be accessed with shorter and usually faster instructions.

The 6809’s designers took this idea one step further. They let you use the DP register to designate any page as the zero page, which they called the “direct page.”

But none of my byte-slinging instructions needed to use the direct page. That meant I could use the DP register as an additional one-byte go-between. Now I could copy 7 bytes per pull-push pair!

The mangling that occurred during copying and pre-processing would, in effect, undo each other.

So the solution to the mangling problem turned out to be a simple, one-time pre-processing step: Just break each tile into septets and reverse their order.

Knowing that the mangling problem could be solved, I was feeling good about my overall idea. I couldn’t see any other problems, so I sketched out a new copy-tile subroutine to present to my friend. Since the row-copy logic was now just 5 instructions, I used it 4 times, unrolling the sole remaining loop a bit. Now, instead of copying 28 single rows, I copied 7 quad-rows.

Adding up the cycles, the total was just 1689. I had shaved almost 1200 cycles off of my friend’s code. That’s a 70 percent speed-up!

When I caught up with my friend and said I’d figured out how to make the copy-tile routine 70% faster, his face lit up. When I explained about the whole septets-and-mangling thing, though, his face unlit, doubts rising. But when I showed him the code, he got it. It clicked.

In about a half hour’s work, he had the code integrated into the game. After a rebuild and reboot, the game was loading.

The game seemed amazingly fast. In truth, it was probably only a third to a half faster, but that was enough. Some important perceptual threshold had been crossed. The game-play was now smooth, natural. You could feel the difference.

We just sat there, playing the game and grinning. It was a good day.

A few days after the speed problem was behind us – or so we thought – the game’s finishing touches were applied. One of those finishing touches was sampled sound effects. This required feeding byte-sized morsels to the audio-output DAC a few thousand times each second. To schedule the feedings, my friend had turned on a hardware-clocked interrupt.

Worked like a charm. The sounds sounded great, and everything was perfect.

After playing into the game one night, we noticed that some of the tiles were getting corrupted. The more we played, the more corruption we saw.

What happens if an interrupt goes off during the copy-tile routine?

The processor, to prepare for calling the interrupt handler, would push its current state onto the system stack. But, during the copy-tile routine, there was no system stack. The system-stack register S had been commandeered. And where was it pointing? Right into the memory buffer that held the reference tiles!

We slumped. After all that effort, to think that our all-important speed-up was causing memory corruption…

Needing a break, we walked to an all-night diner near campus to eat and think. Over pancakes and bacon, scrapple and grilled stickies, we talked through the problem.

There were only two stack registers, and without commandeering both of them, the copy-tile routine wouldn’t be nearly as fast. There was no way, then, to return the S register to the system without losing our hard-won speed. But there was also no way that we could get reliable sound without using interrupts.

One way or another, then, interrupts were going to be triggered. And, when they were, if copy-tile was running, whatever S was pointing to was going to get hammered.

“How can we prevent the corruption?” I asked.

We sat there, ignoring our food, the question hanging in the air.

Suddenly, my friend slapped the table. He had it.

“Don’t prevent the corruption,” he explained. “Let it happen. Just not to the reference tiles.”

“Just swap S and U in the copy-tile routine. U will point at the reference tiles, and S at the screen buffer. If an interrupt goes off, the corruption will happen where S is pointing – on the screen. The corruption will last only until we redraw the next frame.”

Eager to try his solution, we quickly finished our meal.

Walking back to the dorms, though, it bothered us that players might see screen glitches, even if for just a frame. We both felt that there had to be some way to make the hack perfect, if only we could find it.

Again, it was simple once we saw it. All we had to do was change the tile ordering. Instead of placing the tiles onto the screen from top to bottom, left to right, we would go right to left, bottom to top. In other words, from high addresses to low addresses.

That way, when an interrupt went off and corrupted the memory locations immediately before the tile we were currently drawing, the corruption would be fixed when the very next tile was drawn. And since tile-drawing took place in a buffer that wasn’t displayed until it was fully drawn (and the vertical refresh had occurred), nobody would ever see the corruption.2

And that’s the way things were. The challenge wasn’t overwhelming complexity, as it is today. The challenge was cramming your ideas into machines so slow, so limited that most ideas didn’t fit.

You had to bang your ideas around, twist them, turn them, searching for something, anything that would help you squeeze them into the machine. Sometimes you found it, and you got one step closer to realizing your ideas. Sometimes you didn’t.

In this case, the search yielded several small victories that, together, solved the problem. But when you consider all the things we had to do to make that darn game fast enough, it does seem crazy.

The second reason is to encourage you to tell your stories. I know that, “back in the day,” most video games probably gave rise to many stories like this one. I’d love to hear them. But too many of them are lost, having faded out of memory before anyone thought to preserve them.

Industrial automation startup Path Robotics raises $300M in funding

Path Robotics Inc., a startup working to automate industrial welding workflows, today announced that it has raised $300 million in funding.

Georgian led the Series E round. It was joined by a consortium that included the venture capital arms of Yamaha Motor Co. and Navier Corp., one of South Korea’s largest tech firms. The investment gives Path Robotics a post-money valuation of $2.45 billion.

Welding tasks were historically difficult to automate because of a phenomenon called heat-induced metal distortion. It occurs when the heat produced by a welding torch warps the metal parts being joined together. Furthermore, the parts change shape again when they cool down. Those distortions are often unpredictable, which means they can throw off a robot arm that follows a static, pre-programmed welding plan.

There are also other sources of complexity. Mass produced metal parts that are based on the same blueprint often differ slightly because of manufacturing inconsistencies. In many cases, those variations make it necessary to adjust the way welding is performed. That isn’t always possible with traditional automation software.

Path Robotics has developed an AI model that addresses those challenges. Obsidian, as it’s called, is specifically designed to power industrial robots equipped with welding torches. The company says that the model can observe how metal parts change shape during the welding process and adjust its workflow accordingly. Furthermore, it automatically accounts for unexpected part variations.

Obsidian monitors the welding workflow using four lasers attached to the torch that it controls. The emitters illuminate the parts being welded together and the reflected light is captured by a pair of cameras. According to Path Robotics, Obsidian collects data not only during the welding process but also before and after each run.

The data powers an AI model that the company refers to as a weld world model. Path Robotics’ researchers use it to generate simulated welding projects. Those simulations, in turn, power Obsidian’s training workflow.

Path Robotics originally developed Obsidian for large robotic workstations called welding cells. In April, it introduced a four-legged robot called Rove that uses the model to automate shipbuilding tasks. The company says its software can also be used to make parts for vehicles, power generators, utility poles and other heavy equipment.

“We’re seeing accelerating demand from manufacturers that need to expand production capacity but can’t get there with traditional automation,” said Path Robotics co-founder and Chief Executive Officer Andy Lonsberry. “This capital allows us to scale our welding business alongside our customers while investing further in the data, compute, and model development needed to take on every task before and after welding.”

Researchers Discover New Lace Bug Species on Japan’s Remote Ogasawara Islands

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

DNA uncovered a hidden species among Japan’s lace bugs and hints that a tree introduced by humans could help give rise to another.

Lace bugs living on two groups of Japan’s remote Ogasawara Islands had been classified as a single species, despite differences in their diets and coloring. Comparisons of their DNA have now revealed that they belong to two species, a distinction supported by subtle physical differences.

The archipelago lies roughly 900 to 1,000 kilometers (560 to 620 miles) south of Tokyo and is a UNESCO World Natural Heritage site. Its land snails and plants are celebrated for including species found nowhere else. About 440 insect species are also known to be unique to the islands, but their evolution has received far less attention.

Among those insects is Omoplax, a genus of lace bugs thought to have diversified rapidly across the islands. Jun Souma, of Hirosaki University’s Shirakami Research Center for Environmental Sciences, investigated these insects with Naoyuki Nakahama, of the Graduate School of Agriculture at Osaka Metropolitan University.

“The reason I took a particular interest in Omoplax desecta is that it feeds on both the native plant Rhaphiolepis indica var. tashiroi and the introduced plant Calophyllum inophyllum in the Chichijima island group,” Souma said. “This is quite unusual for lace bugs, which typically exhibit high host specificity. I was also intrigued by the fact that it is distributed across multiple island groups, which is different from other Omoplax species.”

To investigate relationships among the insects, the researchers analyzed 52 specimens using MIG-seq, a method that provides DNA data from across the genome. Populations classified as Omoplax desecta on the Chichijima and Hahajima island groups were genetically distinct. The study is published in the open-access journal ZooKeys.

The genetic evidence helped explain differences Souma had noticed in the Hahajima insects.

“I had wondered why it fed exclusively on Rhaphiolepis indica var. tashiroi and why there were no individuals with dark brown to black pronotum, which are sometimes seen in the Chichijima island group,” Souma explained. The pronotum is the plate just behind an insect’s head. “After conducting population genetic and phylogenetic analyses, I began to suspect that this population might be a distinct species. Upon closely observing their morphological characteristics, I was certain of this.”

Subtle differences in male genitalia confirmed the distinction. The Hahajima population is now described as Omoplax sandoru, named after the local name of its host plant.

For Omoplax desecta in the Chichijima group, the introduced tree offered another clue to how these insects might diversify. The DNA data indicate that its shift to feeding on Calophyllum inophyllum happened once, and recently. Whether that change will eventually produce another species remains a possibility rather than an established outcome.

“I also find it interesting that the introduction of Calophyllum inophyllum by humans may have led to a budding of future speciation in Omoplax desecta,” Souma added.

The researchers also reconstructed an evolutionary tree to trace relationships among the insects. That allowed them to test whether species with similar appearances and food preferences were one another’s closest relatives.

“The most surprising finding of my research was that species feeding on the same plant and appearing very similar are not necessarily sister groups,” said Souma, referring to groups that are each other’s closest evolutionary relatives. “Adaptive radiation among insects on oceanic islands may be more complex than comparisons of morphological characteristics and life histories would suggest at first glance.”

Adaptive radiation is the diversification of related organisms into species adapted to different ecological roles. Here, the findings point to both geographic isolation and shifts between host plants as drivers of species formation.

The researchers could not obtain DNA from two rare species, leaving gaps in the evolutionary picture. Further sampling will be needed to trace how these insects reached the islands and diversified.

This '60s Japanese Thriller Is One Of The Best Movies Streaming On HBO Max

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Ben Affleck's new kidnapping thriller "Animals" made a splash on Netflix this year — at least in terms of viewership. Critics dismissed the picture, and justifiably so, given how shaky the "Animals" script is. Yours truly clocked that "Animals" is basically an unauthorized remake of Akira Kurosawa's "High and Low," and I was not the only one.

In both movies, a rich man who has just invested all his money in a venture (a business takeover in "High and Low," a mayoral campaign in "Animals") receives a phone call that his son has been kidnapped. Except, the ransomers mistakenly took one of his much poorer employee's sons — so, does he still bankrupt himself to save a kid that isn't his?

I would highly suggest turning off Netflix and flipping over to HBO Max (where "High and Low" is currently streaming) to watch Akira Kurosawa show you how it's done. Several of the filmmaker's classics are currently streaming on there, including "Seven Samurai," "Rashomon," "Throne of Blood," and "Yojimbo." Like with "High and Low," all of those movies also star Toshiro Mifune, Kurosawa's go-to leading man at his peak. But "High and Low" is my personal favorite of the bunch and Kurosawa's magnum opus.

Kurosawa is distinguished for his samurai movies or Jidaigeki (Japanese historical dramas). He often adapted William Shakespeare's plays, too, moving their stories from Europe to to the east: "Throne of Blood" is literally just "Macbeth" in feudal Japan, and it's one of the best "Macbeth" movies to boot.

"High and Low" stands out in that collection for its contemporary setting and source material. (It's adapted from a procedural novel, "King's Ransom," by American author Ed McBain.) While he may not have had the Bard to draw on this time, Kurosawa still made some pure cinematic brilliance.

If I were ever tasked with assembling a list of "old" and/or international movies to convince people that black and white or non-English-language movies can be great, "High and Low" would be on there, 100%. That's not just because it's great (it is), but it's great popular entertainment: a taut suspense thriller and investigative procedural. It's not at all "pretentious," as untrained viewers might call it.

"Someone wants a ransom for the wrong kid, but only you can pay it" is also a great moral dilemma for a thriller and one that slots neatly into anywhere in the world with class disparity. That's why "High and Low" has been remade in many different settings. We know what a hero would do in this situation, but we're not sure if Kingo Gondo (Toshiro Mifune) is a hero from the beginning.

Now, the movie wisely stacks the deck in Gondo's favor so he doesn't come off as an aloof rich guy. He wasn't born rich, he worked his way up, so he knows what losing everything is like, and that makes the sacrifice extra painful. And while he works in shoe manufacturing, he's got a creative spirit. He's planning a leveraged buyout because he wants to make high-quality shoes his way instead of saving money on a cheaper product.

Would you sacrifice all your savings and life's work to save an innocent child? You probably just answered "yes," but it's still an abstract question for you. "High and Low" portrays how someone would act when the choice and its consequences become real. The most impressive balancing act in the movie is that it both does and doesn't feel like a foregone conclusion when he agrees to pay the ransom.

"High and Low" doesn't end when the ransom gets paid. Scientists discover a giant 66-pound rodent that lived 34 million years ago

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

An ancient rodent of extraordinary size has been identified from fossils uncovered in a mine in southern Thailand. The discovery, made by an international team of paleontologists that includes scientists from McGill University, provides new evidence that giant rodents appeared much earlier and in more parts of the world than previously believed.

The researchers found "remarkably large" fossilized teeth belonging to a previously unknown species called Yakamys sirindhornae. This prehistoric rodent lived approximately 34 million years ago, during the middle of the Paleogene period. Its remains, consisting of a lower second molar and a lower incisor, were recovered from the Bang Mark Pit at the Krabi lignite mine in Thailand's Krabi Province.

Although only two teeth have been identified, they offer valuable clues about rodent gigantism, the evolutionary process through which rodents develop unusually large bodies. The discovery suggests that Southeast Asia played a more significant role in the early evolution of giant rodents than scientists had recognized.

Giant Rodents Have a Much Older History Than Expected

"Today, the largest living rodent is the capybara, but very large rodents are not restricted to recent ecosystems," said Virginie Millien, a biology professor at McGill University and co-author of the study. "Giant rodents evolved repeatedly during the later history of the group, mostly in South America. This discovery suggests that Southeast Asia also played an important role in the early evolutionary history of large-bodied rodents."

The newly identified animal's name reflects both its impressive size and its connection to Thailand. Yakamys combines "yak," the Thai word for giant, with "mys," the Greek word for mouse or rat.

The second part of its scientific name recognizes an important supporter of scientific research in Thailand.

"The species name, sirindhornae, honors Princess Maha Chakri Sirindhorn, in recognition of her longstanding support for science and paleontological research in Thailand," Millien said.

How Big Was This 34-Million-Year-Old Rodent?

To estimate the animal's size, the research team applied seven established methods that use tooth measurements to calculate body mass. Their results suggest that Yakamys sirindhornae weighed approximately 30 kilograms, making it about twice as heavy as other known rodents from the same period, including Hulgana.

That would have made the newly discovered species exceptionally large compared with its contemporaries. However, the researchers emphasize that determining an extinct animal's weight from a small number of fossilized teeth involves considerable uncertainty.

"This is only a ballpark figure, as there is substantial uncertainty associated with fossil body-mass estimation," Millien said.

The limited fossil evidence also makes it difficult to reconstruct how Yakamys evolved or what its life was like. Additional discoveries will be needed to better understand its physical characteristics, ecological role, and evolutionary relationships.

International Research Uncovers Thailand's Prehistoric Past

The fossils were identified through an ongoing international collaboration investigating ancient mammals in Thailand's Krabi Basin. The project brings together scientists from McGill University, Thailand's Department of Mineral Resources, and French research organizations, including CNRS and Université de Poitiers.

By revealing evidence of an unusually large rodent in Southeast Asia 34 million years ago, the discovery expands scientists' understanding of where and when rodent gigantism emerged. Future fossil finds could help establish how these ancient giants lived and how their evolution relates to the enormous rodents that appeared elsewhere in the world.

The research received support from CNRS, Université de Poitiers, Université de Montpellier, McGill University, and the Department of Mineral Resources of Thailand.

Why does my error tracker show one bug as hundreds of different errors?

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 10 • Originally published at runheed.com

Error trackers group repeats of a bug by a fingerprint, usually built from the error type, the message and where it was thrown. If the message holds a value that changes each time, like a user ID, an email, a timestamp or a duration, every occurrence gets a new fingerprint. You get a new-error alert per user and a list nobody can read. If the fingerprint includes the line number, an edit higher up the file makes every error below it look new. Replace the changing values before hashing, and leave the line number out.

Build output does it too. Bundlers put content hashes in file names, such as chunk-3f9a1c2b.js or .next/server/chunks/8123.js, so the same frame gets a new file name on every deploy.

Common Causes of Delayed or Missed User Reminders: Replaceable Cron Delivery

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Common Causes of Delayed or Missed User Reminders: Replaceable Cron Delivery

Short answer: the common causes of delayed or missed user reminders are a paused cron job, an unavailable public callback, or a run longer than 900 seconds. Keep the cleanup trigger, durable message, and user-facing delivery behind replaceable boundaries. A queue plus a realtime channel gives the failure somewhere to wait, but only if the consumer is idempotent and the application does not confuse “triggered” with “delivered”.

For a developer-tool cleanup job, I would put the schedule behind an adapter, publish one durable reminder, then deliver that reminder through a channel that can tell an offline client to catch up. Infrai is a reasonable fit for this narrow seam because its scheduling surface, queue surface, and realtime surface use one REST base and one key. That is a migration convenience, not a reliability guarantee.

There are three common failure boundaries. First, a cron job can be paused and resumed incorrectly. A resumed schedule does not backfill the triggers it missed while paused. If the cleanup marks old records as processed before it publishes a reminder, the lost interval is now an application bug, not a scheduler mystery.

Second, a cron callback needs a publicly reachable HTTP endpoint. An internal hostname, a sleeping local tunnel, or a private network address cannot receive the callback. The scheduler can be healthy while the application endpoint is not.

Third, a single cron execution has a 900-second ceiling in this system. That is a hard design signal. The schedule should find work and enqueue it; a worker should do the slow scan and publish the result. Do not stretch the timeout and hope.

Here is the failure I design around: a cleanup sweep finds 48,000 stale developer-workspace records, starts deleting metadata, and reaches the timeout before it can send the reminder. The next cron tick sees a half-finished batch and either repeats the work or skips it, depending on an implementation detail. Splitting the sweep into queue messages makes each unit retryable, but it also forces a decision about duplicate processing, retention, and the exact point at which the user-facing reminder is emitted. That decision belongs in the worker contract. It should not be hidden inside a cron callback that has already spent most of its execution budget.

The handoff needs a durable state model: scheduled, enqueued, processed, and delivered. A reminder can be processed while its recipient is offline. That is useful only if the message survives long enough for a later consumer attempt. Standard queue delivery is at-least-once, so a duplicate is normal and the cleanup result needs a stable idempotency key.

This example shows the boundary I would keep in application code. It uses the same key and base URL to publish the queue message and then publish a realtime event. The queue remains the record of intent; the realtime publish is the fast path for a connected client. The payload includes a cleanup run id so a reconnecting client can reject duplicates.

The adapter is the important part, not the route spelling. A future provider can implement publishReminder with its own client while the cleanup worker keeps the same input and idempotency rules. If the second publish fails, retry it from the queue consumer; do not rerun the cleanup scan. Also keep a reconnect path in the client. A realtime socket is a delivery attempt, not durable storage.

Celery is a strong choice when the team already owns Python workers and needs task execution semantics. Its documentation is clear about brokers and workers, but the application still has to operate the broker, expose the right worker topology, and connect delivery to a separate realtime system. It is a task system first.

AWS SQS is a good durable queue primitive. It handles the waiting room well, but it does not provide the user-facing socket. Pairing SQS with Pusher means two signups, two credential sets, and glue code for correlation ids, retries, and the transition from queue consumption to channel publication. That stack is perfectly defensible when each service is already a platform standard. The integration surface is larger.

Temporal is better for long-running, stateful workflows with retries, timers, and explicit orchestration. It is the wrong shape for a small periodic cleanup handoff if adopting a workflow runtime would be more machinery than the job deserves. Airflow belongs in the same discussion for DAG-heavy data work, not for a single reminder boundary.

Infrai sits between those choices for this particular integration. Its discovery surface is public, its documented capabilities include runnable examples, and one key covers the queue and realtime calls. That removes a credential handoff and a custom provider adapter from this small path. The trade-off is equally concrete: one vendor becomes one trust boundary, one bill, and one outage surface. Its cron is not a workflow engine, does not backfill paused triggers, and only accepts public HTTP callbacks.

The public discovery surface is useful during migration because it exposes request schemas without a key, and the capabilities include runnable examples in 10 languages. The interface is plain HTTP, so a cleanup worker can call it without installing an SDK or binding the application to one runtime. That reduces adapter friction, but it does not make the provider interchangeable by itself; the application boundary and idempotency tests still do that work.

I would try Infrai for a cleanup worker that needs one queue-to-socket handoff and wants the application contract to stay provider-neutral. I would choose Celery for an established Python task fleet, SQS plus Pusher when those services are already approved, and Temporal when the cleanup is really a multi-step workflow.

At small scale, the run id is enough to make retries safe. At larger scale, I would persist the state transition beside the cleanup result, record the queue message id, and make the client acknowledge the reminder only after rendering it. The consumer would treat every delivery as at-least-once. I would also cap the message body at 256KB, keep delayed messages within seven days, and avoid relying on queue history as an audit log because retention tops out at 30 days and acknowledged messages are deleted.

The latency-versus-cost decision is mostly a latency budget decision. A direct socket publish is fast for an online user; a queue costs an extra hop but gives an offline user a durable intent. For reminders, that extra hop is usually the better failure mode. For a best-effort status toast, it may be needless.

Do not hide the boundary in a vendor SDK. Keep one interface for enqueueReminder and one for deliverReminder, test the adapter against a fake provider, and make the migration path a routing decision. If this boundary fits your system, the user-reminder cron and delayed-message guide is the relevant starting point.

The limit you configured is not the limit it applies

📅 Nov 10, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An agent that pays for things needs a limit: a maximum price per call, a payee you expect, sometimes a list of networks and tokens you trust. The question here is narrower and more important than whether the limit exists: does it apply to the payment the client signs, or to a quote the agent saw a moment earlier?

x402 turns HTTP 402 into a payment step. The server answers 402 Payment Required with what it wants — amount, payee, asset, network — the client signs a matching authorization, and retries.

A common agent flow splits this in two. First the agent makes the request and reads the 402: "0.10 USDC to 0x1111". It decides that is acceptable. Then it calls the paying action, passing the option it chose. If that action sends a new request and signs whatever the new 402 asks for, the check and the payment are about different things. The server can quote 0.10 and then ask for 0.90, or change the payee, or switch the network.

This is the oldest problem in signing: what you see is not what you sign. Hardware wallets fixed it for transactions with clear signing. Card payments in Europe fixed it with PSD2's dynamic linking, which ties the authentication to the amount and payee shown to the payer. Agent payments are rediscovering it.

I wrote a small harness (github.com/cleitonaugusto/x402-conformance) that plays a dishonest x402 seller on 127.0.0.1. It quotes one thing on the first 402 and asks for something else on every later one, then decodes what the client actually signed. There is no chain, no facilitator and no money; outbound connections other than loopback are blocked and logged. Each SDK gets an adapter that drives it the way an agent would, with a configured limit of 0.50 USDC.

Seven scenarios: an honest quote; the price raised to 0.90; the payee swapped; both; the asset swapped; the network swapped; and a quote already above the limit.

On AgentKit's development branch, the two-step flow checks the limit against the option the model passed in, while the payment is created from a fresh 402. Quoted 0.10, it signed 0.90; quoted one payee, it paid another; quoted Base Sepolia, it signed for Base mainnet. The automatic action does not apply the limit at all. And the action's result reports the amount that was checked, not the one that was signed, so the agent's own log said 0.10 when it signed 0.90. (The released 0.10.4 has no limit option at all — it does not enforce spend caps and documents that it leaves this to the developer, so it is a different case: no promise, nothing broken.)

I'm naming names only because it is public, reproducible code and the point is the pattern, not the vendor. Any of these can change tomorrow; the test is in the repo, so check for yourself rather than trust this table.

All of it reduces to one sentence: apply the spend policy to the requirement you sign, and bind it to the quote that was approved. Same amount or less, same payee, same asset, same network — or no signature.

It is not in the x402 specification today, and there is no client conformance test that would catch a kit that gets it wrong — which is why I built this one, and why I plan to raise both with the x402 Foundation.

The same gap exists one layer up. In the Agentic Commerce Protocol, the call that completes a checkout carries the payment credential but nothing that identifies the order the buyer approved. I opened SEP #325 to carry an approved_purchase digest and refuse completion when it does not match — the same "what you approved is what settles" rule, after two contributors there sharpened it (one with data showing agents pay store-inflated totals without asking).

Run the harness against your client. If a changed 402 gets signed, move your check to the payment you sign, and make your result report what was signed, not what was quoted.

Both company leaders are stressing that the upcoming shortage isn't because big GPU companies like Nvidia are eating up every last chip known to man. The squeeze is happening even further upstream in the supply chain.

Automakers are no strangers to semiconductor shortages. During the pandemic, chip shortages forced car companies to idle production lines and even ship cars without certain computer-reliant features, like automatic start/stop. 7 Romance Shows That Are 10/10 Masterpieces

Romance gets a bad rep for being the guilty pleasure of TV. It's considered as the genre people binge in secret and never put on their "best shows" lists. That reputation is a little unfair, though. A truly great love story is one of the hardest things to pull off on screen. It takes chemistry that can't be faked, and a slow build that makes the audience desperate for two people to finally get it right. When a romance gets all of that right, it becomes something viewers return to again and again, long after they know exactly how it ends.

The shows on this list are the ones that understand romance is really about vulnerability rather than the boring, one-dimensional stereotypes people usually associate with the genre. They give viewers messy, complicated people who have to get out of their own way before they can fall in love. This is a list of romance shows that are 10/10 masterpieces because they do justice to the very act of falling in love.

One Day is a right person, wrong time story that is both heartwarming and unbearable to watch, and that is the mark of a true romance. The series, based on David Nicholls' bestselling novel, begins on July 15, 1988, when Emma Morley (Ambika Mod) and Dexter Mayhew (Leo Woodall) spend one night together after graduating from university in Edinburgh. Every episode then drops in on them on that same date, year after year. The two characters couldn't be any different. Emma is a sharp, working-class aspiring writer who never quite believes she’s good enough, while Dexter is a golden boy who survives on pure charm until his life starts to unravel.

The genius of the show is in its format. Since the audience only sees one day a year, entire relationships and careers happen off-screen, and every episode feels like catching up with an old friend who has changed as life has gone on. Both characters experience genuine growth over the years, and it's impossible not to root for their connection. Just be warned that the final episodes take a turn that hits like a gut punch, and the show ends with a twist that is guaranteed to stay with viewers for days. One Day is the perfect love story about missed chances, and is guaranteed to wreck anyone who makes it to the end.

Nobody expected a Canadian hockey romance to take over the internet, but Heated Rivalry did exactly that within a few weeks. The series, based on Rachel Reid's Game Changers novels and created by Letterkenny's Jacob Tierney, follows two of professional hockey’s biggest young stars. Shane Hollander (Hudson Williams) is a disciplined, focused Canadian, while Ilya Rozanov (Connor Storrie) is a cocky Russian who loves getting under his skin. The two have been rivals since their rookie days in 2008, and on the ice, they can't stand each other. Off the ice, though, they get secretly caught up in an affair that could end both of their careers if anyone found out.

Beneath all the steam, Heated Rivalry is really about two men who have been taught their entire lives that wanting each other could cost them everything. The six-episode season stretches across a decade, so the audience gets to watch a secret fling slowly turn into something neither of them is ready to name. The two are a perfect mismatch, and every stolen moment they get feels charged because things can fall apart at any time. On top of that, the show delivers a second great romance through hockey captain Scott Hunter (François Arnaud) and his boyfriend, Kip (Robbie G.K.), whose story nearly steals the spotlight. Heated Rivalry turned a niche book series into a global obsession, and it has more than earned its place among the great modern love stories.

There are slow burns, and then there's a love story that literally spans two centuries. Outlander, based on Diana Gabaldon's novels, follows World War II nurse Claire Randall (Caitríona Balfe), on her second honeymoon in Scotland who is suddenly thrown back to 1743 after touching an ancient standing stone. Suddenly, Claire finds herself in the middle of a brewing Jacobite rebellion. To protect herself, she is forced to marry Jamie Fraser (Sam Heughan), a young Highland warrior, even though she already has a husband waiting for her in the future.

Unlike most romances, Outlander gets its central couple married early and then asks what happens after they say "I do." Claire and Jamie face wars and impossible choices together, and their love grows deeper with every hardship instead of fading. It's hard not to melt every time Jamie calls her "Sassenach," the nickname that started as a jab and turned into a declaration of love. Claire is also a refreshingly modern heroine, a woman who refuses to stay quiet in a century that expects her to. After eight seasons, Outlander remains the gold standard for epic, sweeping romance on TV.

Bridgerton is Shonda Rhimes' version of Regency, and provides a refreshing take on London high society's ruthless marriage market. The series, based on Julia Quinn's novel series, follows the Bridgerton siblings and their individual love stories. Bridgerton Season 1 centers on Daphne Bridgerton (Phoebe Dynevor), who strikes a deal with the brooding Duke of Hastings (Regé-Jean Page) to fake a courtship, only for real feelings to get in the way. The following seasons follow a new Bridgerton sibling and cover a new romance trope with every season, from enemies-to-lovers to friends-to-lovers, and even a Cinderella-style love story. Of course, overseeing all this drama is the anonymous gossip columnist Lady Whistledown, voiced by Julie Andrews, whose scandal sheet keeps the entire ton on edge.

Bridgerton stands out from other period dramas because of how unapologetically modern it feels. The show reimagines Regency London with a diverse cast and swaps classical music for string-quartet covers of songs by artists including Ariana Grande and Taylor Swift to help the audience relate to the era. Not to mention the series tells its love stories from the women's point of view, which makes the fight for independence just as important as the search for a husband. Bridgerton took the stuffy period romance formula and turned it into a feel-good show that has made period romance cool for a whole new generation.

You feel everything at maximum volume and you have never apologised for it. Red is the messiest album she has made and the one most people love best, because it is the sound of somebody refusing to be reasonable about how much something hurt.

Heartstopper is proof that a love story doesn't need heartbreak or emotional trauma to make viewers cry. The series, based on Alice Oseman's graphic novels, follows Charlie Spring (Joe Locke), a shy, openly gay student at an all-boys British school. When he's seated next to rugby team star Nick Nelson (Kit Connor), Charlie develops a massive crush, which he knows will never go anywhere. As the two become close friends, though, Nick begins to realize that his feelings for Charlie run much deeper than he expected.

Heartstopper is a show about the small moments. Every awkward "hi" between Charlie and Nick perfectly captures the feeling of a young love and the hand-drawn visual elements in the show make the audience feel like Oseman's novels have come to life. The show is refreshingly kind to its characters, but doesn't shy away from harder topics like bullying and mental health. One of its most moving scenes comes when Nick comes out to his mother, played by Olivia Colman, and it's impossible to get through that without tearing up. Heartstopper is the comforting, joyful queer love story so many viewers wish they had growing up, and it deserves all the appreciation it gets for that.

A South Korean heiress falling for a North Korean soldier sounds like the setup for a joke, but Crash Landing on You makes it work without feeling gimmicky. Yoon Se-ri (Son Ye-jin) is a wealthy, fiercely independent businesswoman who gets swept off course during a paragliding accident and crash-lands on the wrong side of the border. Stern but honorable army officer, Captain Ri Jeong-hyeok (Hyun Bin), finds Se-ri and decides to hide her in his village until she figures out a way home. From there, the story works both as a sweeping romance and a tense thriller, while also being one of the funniest K-dramas out there. Crash Landing on You has something for everyone.

Even viewers who have never watched a K-drama will find Se-ri and Jeong-hyeok's dynamic endearing because of how well-written it is. The two have lived completely different lives, but they slowly realize they understand each other better than anyone else ever has. The border itself is what gives the romance its ache, since the two come from countries that can never truly let them be together. Every loving moment they share feels borrowed, and the audience spends the whole series dreading the day all this ends. Crash Landing on You is the perfect gateway into K-dramas, and it's easily one of the most beloved romances of the decade.

In Pride and Prejudice, Mary Bennet is mostly remembered for embarrassing herself at the piano while the rest of the room cringes. The Other Bennet Sister, based on Janice Hadlow's novel, finally gives her a story of her own. Mary (Ella Bruccoleri) is the bookish, awkward middle child who has spent her life being compared to her prettier and livelier sisters. When she gets the chance to start fresh in London with her kind aunt and uncle, the Gardiners (Indira Varma and Richard Coyle), Mary finally gets to discover who she is outside of her family's shadow. In London, she finds herself caught between two very different suitors, the reserved Mr. Hayward (Dónal Finn) and the charming Mr. Ryder (Laurie Davidson). Now, for a woman who has never been anyone's first choice, being noticed is thrilling and terrifying at once.

The love triangle isn't really about which man wins her over, though. It's about Mary learning what she actually wants, rather than settling for whoever will have her. The series turns one of Austen's punchlines into a heroine worth rooting for, and that's exactly why it's so satisfying. Mary is prickly and earnest, and she's funnier than anyone ever gave her credit for. Her love story doesn't arrive in one sweeping moment. Instead, it grows alongside her confidence, which makes every step toward happiness feel truly earned. Anyone who has ever felt like the overlooked one in their family will see a little of themselves in her. The Other Bennet Sister is a sweet, clever twist on Austen, and it's the kind of underdog romance that's impossible not to love.
