Fireclay Tile Turns Traditional Textiles Into Hand-Painted Tile

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Plaids and florals might seem more at home on upholstery or wallpaper than ceramic tile, but Fireclay Tile’s latest collection makes a compelling case for bringing those familiar patterns to harder surfaces. Called Idyllwild, the new hand-painted collection takes inspiration from traditional textiles, translating everything from delicate botanical illustrations to richly layered plaids into ceramic designs that feel both nostalgic and contemporary.

Interestingly, the collection didn’t begin with flowers at all. “We were seeing designers use a lot of intricate textiles, colors, and patterns together,” explains Jamie Chappell, VP of Brand and Product at Fireclay Tile, adding that early explorations focused on plaids and stripes. “As a true lover of ’90s alt-rock, I actually went down a rabbit hole early on, wondering if it should be a ‘grunge’ collection.”

Fireclay Tile’s Idyllwild collection in its Winter palette, featuring deep blues, earthy greens, and warm neutrals across floral, striped, and plaid patterns.

That direction eventually gave way to something softer. Recognizing the longstanding relationship between florals and plaids in interiors, the team introduced botanical patterns to balance the collection’s more structured designs. The name Idyllwild followed, referencing the California mountain town.

The resulting collection includes 16 patterns organized into four categories: Specimen Flowers, Idyllwild Florals, Idyllwild Stripes, and Idyllwild Plaids.

The Specimen Flowers series features four individual botanical motifs—Allium, Carnation, Lily, and Bluebell—hand-painted on ceramic tile. Photography courtesy of Fireclay Tile.

Specimen Flowers features four individual botanical designs—Allium, Carnation, Lily, and Bluebell—that can be arranged across backsplashes, niches, and accent walls. The Idyllwild Florals take a more expansive approach, with repeating patterns named Pomegranate, Tulip, Morning Glory, and Meadow. Their overlapping stems, leaves, and blossoms create the dense, layered effect of a garden growing beyond its boundaries.

The Stripes and Plaids explore a different kind of ornamentation. Four striped patterns—Stuga, Mokki, Blockhaus, and Thatch—introduce rhythm through repeated lines and geometric details. The Plaids, meanwhile, borrow the visual language of woven fabrics, with Chalet, Highlands, Minka, and Torp creating intersecting grids that can read as anything from understated texture to a bold, room-enveloping pattern.

The Idyllwild Plaids series features four geometric patterns inspired by traditional woven textiles, shown here in warm shades of burgundy, pink, and brown. Photography courtesy of Fireclay Tile.

While the designs reference traditional textiles, translating them into hand-painted ceramic required a different way of thinking about pattern. “We had to simplify and edit the artwork so our painting team could actually execute it by hand,” Jamie says.

The process uses resist lines to define individual areas of color, making the scale and spacing of each detail particularly important. “When we translate intricate woven textiles into screen art, we have to pay very close attention to the thickness of the lines, how small the fields are to fill in, and making sure the painters have enough natural ‘starts and stops’ so they can work efficiently,” Jamie says.

A closer look at Fireclay Tile’s hand-painting process, with glaze applied to the Allium motif from the Idyllwild Specimen Flowers series. Photography courtesy of Fireclay Tile.

Fireclay’s painting team was closely involved in prototyping, providing feedback on details that were too small or areas where the glaze couldn’t be applied smoothly. The company also developed a new glaze base specifically for the hand-application process.

The relationship between design and production extends to color. Each of the 16 patterns is available in four curated palettes inspired by the seasons, but developing those options required another significant undertaking.

The Meadow pattern from Fireclay Tile’s Idyllwild Florals series features an assortment of botanical motifs in the Spring color palette. Photography courtesy of Fireclay Tile.

Fireclay’s previous hand-painted palette had become disconnected from its broader ceramic offering, limiting customization opportunities. To address that, the production team spent months matching the company’s 50 core ceramic colors in both gloss and matte finishes. The resulting 100 finishes can be combined across the collection.

A selection of floral, plaid, and striped patterns from Fireclay Tile’s Idyllwild collection, shown in the Winter palette of deep blues, muted greens, and warm neutrals. Photography courtesy of Fireclay Tile.

To develop Idyllwild’s seasonal palettes, the team worked with physical tile samples alongside 4K renderings, testing combinations before translating them into paint sheets for production. The color stories also reflect shifts the company has been observing in interiors, including an interest in deeper reds and earthy blues.

“Organizing the collection into four seasonal motifs really shows how much color shifts the personality of a design: a plaid in soft pastels feels light and cottage-like, whereas that same pattern in deep, moody tones reads like a cozy wool blanket,” Jamie explains.

The Idyllwild Stripes series features four hand-painted patterns—Stuga, Mokki, Blockhaus, and Thatch—combining linear details and geometric motifs in shades of blue, green, and brown. Photography courtesy of Fireclay Tile.

The collection also introduces new sizes, including 2-by-8-inch and 3-by-6-inch tiles, designed to work alongside standard field tiles. This allows patterns to be introduced selectively or carried across larger surfaces, with stripes used as borders or accents and more intricate designs covering entire walls.

Although Idyllwild draws from traditional patterns, the finished designs are shaped as much by the practical demands of hand-painting as by their textile references.

Posted November 23, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

Fans of the X-Men film franchise, which premiered in the long-ago year of 2000, were treated to the original version of Hugh Jackman’s now-longstanding portrayal of the character Wolverine. Appearing frequently shirtless in the movie, at the time Jackman seemed muscular enough to be super-heroic. He has played Wolverine nine more times in the decades since then, but rather than growing older and more relaxed, Jackman’s physique has seemed to develop in the opposite direction. Bigger, leaner, and more defined, Jackman could stand for an increasingly familiar male ideal.

Like him, many men spend hours in the gym trying to bulk up. They may carefully monitor their diets, timing their protein intake for maximum effect. They may indeed grow larger and more muscular; family, friends, and partners will notice. They may share images of weight-lifting transformations on social media, or even consider anabolic steroids or testosterone injections. Despite it all, some of these men will continue to see themselves as too small, too ill-defined, and not muscular enough.

This condition is known as muscle dysmorphia, a form of body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) characterized by an unrelenting preoccupation with the idea that one is not muscular or lean enough. Even when a man is objectively well built, he may perceive himself as physically inadequate. It’s not common, but it’s real: a 2025 study of nearly fifteen hundred boys and men between the ages of 15 and 35 (Ganson et al., 2025a) concluded that the disorder likely existed in approximately 2.8% of those surveyed — across demographic groups, not only among weightlifters or athletes. (Indeed, a 2024 study in the Journal of Eating Disorders found higher muscle dysmorphia symptom scores among men who wanted to increase their body weight than among those who wanted to reduce it.)

As I’ve suggested, while physical fitness is usually worth aspiring to, there’s a significant difference between ordinary, healthy fitness and muscle dysmorphia. Even when a man is objectively strong, “cut,” and full of muscles, he may look at himself in the mirror and see only weakness. This preoccupation can then lead to repeated, muscle-seeking behavior, such as checking himself in the mirror, researching new exercise routines, measuring body parts, or seeking new nutritional information. It can also involve excessive weightlifting, spending too much time at the gym, or taking on rigid dietary pursuits that can even become unhealthy or dangerous.

But as all that exercise produces results, it never seems like enough: getting bigger just feeds back into the perception that they still aren’t big enough. And when men with the disorder can’t soothe their anxiety by working out, they can feel real emotional distress, as well. The central characteristic of muscle dysmorphia is being absorbed by the perception that one is not muscular enough; it describes men whose daily routine and sense of self are dependent on the pursuit of muscularity, not those who just don’t like missing a workout (Cooper et al., 2020).

These days, muscle dysmorphia is particularly associated with male body-image concerns, although women can experience it as well. As we’ve seen in Hugh Jackman’s transformation, men in popular culture are expected to be physically strong and imposing, with muscles sharply defined and standing out through their clothing. (So many superheroes!) Women face body image concerns as well, of course, but the pressure on women often focuses on losing weight and appearing thin rather than strong. For men, being muscular can stand in for physical attractiveness, competence, or even dominance.

It’s easy to find examples of these masculine norms on social media nowadays; just as in the past, we’ve been treated to male movie stars whose bodies are designed to show off a great deal of time in the gym; adherence to these norms has been associated with the symptoms of muscle dysmorphia (Grunewald et al., 2022). Research by Ganson and colleagues (2025b) supported this, finding that greater exposure to muscle-oriented social media content was associated with higher levels of probable muscle dysmorphia.

Filters, strategic lighting, and increasingly sophisticated photo-editing tools can offer further enhancement to these already extreme physiques, and sometimes the men in these social-media posts use performance-enhancing drugs, which pushes them still further out of the realm of the realistic. Overall, the fitness content on social media can complicate the distinction between healthy exercise and unhealthy physical enhancement. Psychological vulnerabilities may also play a role: in a 2024 study of men attending gyms, Kandemir et al. found that perfectionism helped explain the relationship between vulnerable narcissism and the risk of muscle dysmorphia.

Muscle dysmorphia can be harmful in a number of ways. Training too hard, even through pain, can lead to injuries — some of which can be lasting. Restricting one’s diet can also have serious consequences for one’s health, such as hormonal disruption (meaning reduced production of testosterone, among other things), reduced bone density, and an increase in the risk of fractures or hypotension (Mountjoy et al., 2023).

The obsession with being muscular can take its toll on one’s social life, too, when men seem to prioritize their nutrition or their workout schedules over their partners’ needs. Daily work obligations can be affected when muscle dysmorphia affects one’s ability to concentrate (Cooper et al., 2020).

And of course there are the serious emotional consequences of feeling ashamed of oneself — like depression, anxiety, or self-hatred — because of body-image-related beliefs. Even when a man’s friends seem to admire their discipline or dedication, or comment in positive ways about the change in his physical condition, these would-be compliments can have a paradoxically damaging effect if they spur a man to try even harder to achieve his unrealistic muscular aspirations, or if they make his beliefs seem more reasonable than they are.

As I’ve noted before, men may be comparatively less likely to seek help for emotion-related problems than women; when the topic is body-image concerns, they may be less likely still. Some may not even believe they have a problem, as the effort to become (and look) strong seems to be valued by their peers and by the culture overall (Brown et al., 2023).

In therapy, however, it is possible to draw a subtle but meaningful distinction between a realistic, healthy body image and a source of compulsive striving. Irrational or distorted cognitions may be examined; other sources of self-worth can be identified. In other words, the effort to become “big” may also have an idiosyncratic meaning to each individual man, and this meaning stands a chance of being explored and identified in the context of the therapeutic relationship. (Medical intervention may become necessary as well, in cases involving potentially harmful medications or nutritional restriction.)

In the end, muscle dysmorphia creates a slippery slope: trying to be big seems to promise better self-esteem, but the effort only results in further, more extreme beliefs about how big one needs to be to achieve those benefits. The only real way out is to grow in a different direction — to learn that feeling good means accepting yourself as you are, and trying to live in a healthy way without wondering what you still need to change.

This Is Why Your Mashed Potatoes Are Gluey (and How to Fix It)

Consider this your mashed potato primer, complete with tips on how to fix gluey mashed potatoes and how to prevent them from the start.

My mom’s potato masher is one of my favorite kitchen tools, from its retro design to the thick, grid-patterned bottom that hasn’t bent or warped over the years, despite heavy use. I attributed her consistently perfect fluffy mashed potatoes to this simple tool, even when she was experimenting with new mashed potato ideas. And it turns out there is some truth to it because it created smooth mashed potatoes quickly yet thoroughly, preventing her from pummeling the spuds into a sticky, gummy mess.

You see, overworked potatoes are the biggest reason for gluey mashed potatoes, so using the right tool is the best way to prevent them in the first place. And if you came here because your potatoes are already gummy, we have some helpful tips for how to fix gluey mashed potatoes after the fact.

The biggest culprit for gluey mashed potatoes is overworked potatoes. Potatoes are naturally starchy, and a little bit of starch is a good thing. Just as aquafaba (starchy cooked chickpea liquid) can be whipped into a rich vegan mayonnaise, potato starch thickens mashed potatoes and contributes to a creamy texture. But there is a limit.

As you mash chunks of potatoes into a mostly smooth mixture, the spud’s cell walls collapse, freeing the starch within those cells. The more you mash, the more cells are broken down, and the thicker, starchier and gummier the potatoes become. This can happen when you mash potatoes too long by hand, but it happens more quickly when using electric tools.

Food processors and immersion blenders have incredibly fast, sharp blades. As they whip around, they cut through the potatoes’ cells so rapidly that the once silken mash becomes gluey in a flash. I can’t urge you strongly enough to pass on these tools when mashing potatoes.

There are three components to avoiding gluey mashed potatoes: Choosing the right potato, using the right tool and considering temperature.

For starters, choose a starchy or all-purpose potato, like a russet or Yukon Gold. These potatoes are relatively low in moisture, helping the potato break down into a creamy mixture. They’ll give you more wiggle room as you mash than waxy potatoes, so the potatoes will turn out delicious even if you lose yourself in exuberant mashing.

Next, use the right kitchen tool for the job and reach for a potato masher, food mill or ricer. These handheld tools give you more control over speed and power as you mash. I prefer a potato masher with a crisscross grid pattern over those with a wavy design because there’s less open space, making each mash more productive.

Finally, warm your butter and milk before adding them to the potatoes. This step may sound finicky, but it prevents the warm potato starches from seizing against cold ingredients and becoming dense. Switch to a spatula and stir them in to keep a gentle touch and retain a fluffy texture.

You’ll never be able to totally remove the excess starch and get rid of the gluey texture without starting over, but you may be able to improve it. Adding more fat or liquid can improve the mouthfeel, and stirring in other ingredients can alter the overall texture.

Knowing what types of potatoes are best for which recipes can save you a lot of grief—and this knowledge will go a long way to preventing gluey potatoes. For the creamiest, smoothest, lightest-as-air mashed potatoes, go with a starchy russet potato. Some people like using all-purpose Yukon Gold potatoes for their naturally buttery flavor, but I prefer to use all russets (or a blend of russets and Yukon Golds for flavor).

It’s best to avoid waxy potatoes, like red or fingerling potatoes, for mashed potato recipes. They keep their shape when they’re cooked, which is why they’re better for potato salads and soups than creamy mashes. They do make an excellent smashed potato, though, if you’re looking to do something a little bit different with your side dish.

As with all cooking rules, this one is made to be broken. If you only have red potatoes in the pantry, you can still use them to make mashed red potatoes. The cheaper bag of pet food isn’t always the cheaper one to feed. A denser food holds more calories in each cup, so your pet eats less of it and the bag lasts longer. Before you buy or switch, divide each bag’s price by the days it lasts at your pet’s feeding amount.

The price per pound on a shelf tag misses how long each pound lasts. A pound of a calorie-dense food feeds your pet for more days than a pound of a less dense one.

The 10 Best War Books of All Time, Ranked

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

There’s a story below that’s older than books as they're currently understood, which speaks to how storytelling has always been a thing culturally, sure, but it also speaks to how war has always been a thing. Throughout the history of literature, stories about war have been significant, important, and dramatically compelling, and then there’s at least one great story about warfare technically from even earlier.

As best as possible, there are books about a range of wars below, and to keep things more interesting, there are a couple of fantasy novels that also count as war books included below, too. What you won’t see here are great non-fiction books about war, nor memoirs or strictly biographical novels. This ranking of the best war books of all time will include some works of historical fiction, though, and there are only a couple of inclusions that are about conflicts of an entirely fictional nature.

Like another book from the 1960s concerned with World War II that'll be mentioned shortly, Catch-22 is famously about how weird, despair-filled, and almost comically absurd war can be, but without the sci-fi elements of that other novel and also with a bit more of a focus on humor. It’s a very dark comedy, though, and the humor is of a surreal nature, too, all of it getting quite exhausting at a point.

Then again, that exhaustion does feel like the point of Catch-22, and at least it’s got a great premise, since it explores the notion that anyone who wants to exit the war must be sensible, and so they can’t request a check for insanity as a way to get out, since doing that demonstrates their sanity. There’s a lot more chaos on top of all that, and the novel is fairly episodic, at a point, albeit in a way that mostly works (and, again, feels deliberate).

All Quiet on the Western Front was written and published, as it just so happens, between the end of World War I and the beginning of World War II. So back then, it wasn’t called World War I, and you'd have to assume people were pretty hopeful there wouldn’t be any more world wars. Plenty of books post-World War II ended up being about that conflict, for fairly obvious reasons.

So, All Quiet on the Western Front, despite being nearly a century old, might well be the ultimate anti-war book about the First World War. The narrative here is what you get in many stories that take an anti-war approach to things, with young people being swept up in a conflict they don’t fully understand until it’s tragically too late, but when it’s done well, it has to be appreciated. And, to All Quiet on the Western Front’s credit, it did this kind of anti-war narrative before a good many other novels and/or movies with similar approaches and stories.

The Aubrey-Maturin series overall concerned the Napoleonic Wars, but the first of 20 (completed) books, Master and Commander, takes place shortly before the outbreak of the conflict proper. Still, like the rest of the series, Master and Commander does concern naval warfare, and then it’s set during the War of the Second Coalition, which was part of the French Revolutionary Wars, and those wars were the prelude to the Napoleonic Wars.

Sorry if that’s simplifying things. It’s not in the spirit of Aubrey-Maturin to simplify things, since Patrick O'Brian’s attention to detail is legendary, and you get a sense of authenticity almost right from the start of the first book, Master and Commander. It’s clearly the best place to start, and you're obviously in luck if you like what you read, seeing as the rest of the series is acclaimed and very readable as well.

The elephant in the room that’s always there when you're talking about A Song of Ice and Fire is that it’s a series that has infamously not finished, and it feels increasingly unlikely that it will. To paraphrase that now-dead meme, we really did get GTA 6 before A Song of Ice and Fire #6. Still, what does exist of the series is impressive, and war is a pretty big part of all the books.

Of those, A Game of Thrones (book #1) is where things happen that really set off the main war of the series, and so book #2, A Clash of Kings, feels the worthiest of being included here. Those potential kings really do clash, with there being a whole War of Five Kings that gets progressively messier and truly feels like the focus of the bulk of this book. It’s not where that conflict ends, by any means, but it feels most accurate to call A Clash of Kings a “war book,” out of all the entries in the series to date.

This is the one that pairs fairly well with Catch-22, since Slaughterhouse-Five is also bold, about World War II, and sometimes darkly funny. It’s also got some unusual sci-fi elements, in case the idea of a sometimes-funny, sometimes-sad, sometimes semi-autobiographical, and sometimes mind-bending book about World War II didn’t sound interesting enough on its own.

Here, a good deal of the book concerns the bombing of Dresden, which is something Kurt Vonnegut himself experienced, so he was a good authority to write on the subject, of course. The time travel and other parts are where it strays away from feeling autobiographical, yet the reliving of war and trauma is authentic, and made more literal with it being genuinely revisited and more than “just” flashed back to. Oh, and Slaughterhouse-Five does all this within a very short duration, all up. It’s wild stuff, and very little else out there is quite like it.

The Winds of War is a massive novel, and yet it’s hard to talk about it without also acknowledging that it’s just one half of an overall story that continued with War and Remembrance. If you want, consider them both worthy of taking up this spot, since they function as one grand family saga that spans World War II quite comprehensively, including the lead-up to it, at least from the U.S. point of view; like, the lead-up to the U.S. joining the conflict.

That’s what book #1 builds to, at least, with War and Remembrance perhaps being more about the war and being in the thick of it. Anyway, both stand out as great pieces of historical fiction, since the broad events here really happened, but many (albeit not all) of the characters are fictional. You do need to devote a lot of time to reading something like The Winds of War, and then even more time to (the even longer) second book, but it’s an ultimately worthwhile endeavor.

There was a lot of talk about the 2026 film adaptation of Homer's The Odyssey changing a good deal of the original text, but the changes there have nothing on all the changes made to Homer's The Iliad with 2004’s Troy. The name was changed there, so maybe that dulled the impact a bit. Also, The Iliad feels like a more difficult story to get right, if you're adapting something, since it’s got more moving parts and a higher number of truly important characters featured throughout.

It’s technically about part of the Trojan War, but not with all the events you might expect, and with fantastical elements (though not as many as you get with The Odyssey). Clearly, The Iliad is important as far as stories about warfare go, and that importance and the overall history/legacy here make it an essential read, even with it being quite complicated and overwhelming (to greater or lesser extents depending on which translation you tackle).

Oh, Gravity’s Rainbow. This is a fairly well-known book for something that’s also remarkably impenetrable and overwhelmingly dense, not to mention incredibly confronting and even disgusting in parts. Those factors could contribute to why it’s well-known, so maybe calling it notorious is more accurate, but maybe all that works in its favor as well. It’s a difficult one to summarize, beyond saying that it’s largely set during the final stages of World War II, and also, there’s a lot of talk about V-2 rockets.

Some people are searching for one particular V-2 rocket, seemingly, and then others aren’t really as tied to what seems to be going on, sort of just dropping in and out of what ends up being a very sprawling and complex novel. Parts of Gravity’s Rainbow are thrilling in an unusual sort of way, yet much of it’s also overwhelming, which is something that can be said about a great many Thomas Pynchon novels. But for capturing the madness of war, and for also being unforgettable (for better or worse), Gravity’s Rainbow does feel like a deservedly controversial classic of sorts.

There isn't much else that can be said about The Lord of the Rings, since if there is a reason for it to be ranked high on any kind of ranking, you do more or less have to put it there. And high. But here, it might be worth admitting that it’s more of a fantasy epic, and as far as war books go, it’s not conventional, seeing as the conflict here is a fantastical one, and the war’s entirely fictional and all (not like in Slaughterhouse-Five, where the war was real, but a sci-fi/fictional spin was put on things).

And then it also differs from A Clash of Kings, because the warfare in that book felt a bit more authentic, with less by way of magic and non-human creatures and all. You Know Animal-Print Rugs, But Do You Know Animal Pictorials? (2026)

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The oldest known pile rug—pulled from a frozen tomb in Siberia in 1949—features a procession of 24 fallow deer and a parade of men on horseback. Some 2,500 years after its creation, animal illustrations are making their way back onto the floor. A serpent coils through the Snake and Pomegranate rug by Christopher Farr and Studio Shamshiri in Lucy Hale’s Los Angeles home, and a Tibetan tiger stretches across Demi Lovato’s modern farmhouse and Ethan Cook’s SoHo loft. These aren’t spots or stripes standing in for a neutral—they’re meant to be noticed and discussed. “Animal print is a texture; an animal motif is a story,” says AD100 designer Pavitra Rajaram, who recently designed an animal-themed collection for Jaipur Living called Majnun. “I think people are tired of decoration that says nothing and are looking for objects that carry meaning.”

Rajaram is describing a literal animal rug, also known as an animal pictorial. These playful floor coverings often feature a bucolic scene of several animals or a single animal form woven into the textile, and they come in styles ranging from avant-garde and surrealist to Art Deco and folk-inspired.

“What we’re seeing now is a shift toward rugs where the whole animal is part of the design and has a role in the composition,” says Kerri Anne Besse, design director at rug brand Tumble. “The oversized, blown-up animal print feels dated.”

As for styling, Sophie Salata, head of brand at Vinterior, recommends that designers let the pictorials stand on their own. “I’d be inclined to treat the rug almost as a piece of art, allowing it to become a focal point rather than trying to make everything around it match,” she says. Pamela Marshall, creative director at artisanal rug brand Patterson Flynn, agrees with this approach: These designs are more than backdrops.

Reformation's nine-piece rug collection with Les Éditions de Tapis includes depictions of cats, swans, and tigers.

Recently, brands have been launching their own interpretations of animal pictorials. Last week, Reformation launched its first-ever home collection, a collaboration with Les Éditions de Tapis that features hand-drawn swans and cats. At Milan Design Week this year, Fornasetti launched a surrealist-inspired line of rugs with CC-Tapis depicting slithering serpents from the luxury designer’s archive. A few months earlier, Ruggable released its collaboration with Scalamandré to bring the upscale design house’s iconic zebras to more homes. And in October 2025, Nordic Knots launched its Swedish Grace collection, including the Grace 03 Fauna rug, which shows a wildlife scene. Looking further back, Beni’s serpent rugs have been a designer favorite since 2020.

One of the earlier examples of literal animal rugs is a collection between longstanding collaborators Patterson Flynn and AD100 designers Charlap Hyman & Herrero. It grew from a one-off rug into a full collection of graphic natural-and-black silhouettes that launched in 2018 and continues to expand. “Areas of inspiration for these come from Calder’s simplified animal forms, the surrealists’ play on scale, and 17th-century cabinets of curiosity, to name a few,” says Marshall, who credits Hyman’s “encyclopedic” design references for the originality. We would be remiss not to mention Diane von Furstenberg’s long-running collaboration with the Rug Company, which has been producing the iconic Climbing Leopard rug since 2005.

One of the world’s rarest fish lives in a single cave in Death Valley National Park, and earthquakes have left it on the verge of extinction

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Devils Hole pupfish live in just one cavern of Death Valley National Park. Earthquakes are rapidly pushing this fish towards extinction.

One of the rarest fish on Earth lives in just one place: a water-filled cavern in the Nevada desert called Devils Hole, part of Death Valley National Park. In the spring of 2025, after two earthquakes shook its tiny home, biologists counted only 38 of them.

The National Park Service announced the new figure in November. Divers and surface counters found the 137 Devils Hole pupfish during a survey on October 10, nearly double the 77 seen in the spring of 2026 and about double the 69 recorded in the fall of 2025.

Devils Hole is a cave about 12 feet wide and more than 500 feet deep. The bottom has never been mapped. The water stays at a constant 92 degrees Fahrenheit (33 degrees Celsius).

Pupfish have been found as deep as 66 feet, but they feed and spawn only on a shallow rock shelf near the surface, where algae grows.

The species has been isolated there for 10,000 to 20,000 years, longer than any other pupfish in the Death Valley system. The region’s caves keep other long records too, like a Nevada cave that reveals 580,000 years of climate swings.

It is the only naturally occurring population of the species, which was listed as endangered in 1967. Biologists from the National Park Service and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service have counted the fish almost every spring and fall since 1972, with scuba divers in the cavern and counters at the surface.

One of the fish’s main threats can come from thousands of miles away. Large earthquakes as far away as Japan, Indonesia and Chile have made the water slosh in Devils Hole like water in a bathtub, according to the park.

Waves can splash as high as two meters (about 6.5 feet) up the walls and sweep the shelf clean. The park calls it a tsunami in the desert, although it is tiny next to the ocean tsunamis that big earthquakes can unleash.

That is what happened in the winter of 2024 to 2025. Two earthquakes, one in December and one in February, set off waves that moved algae, organic material and fish eggs off the shelf.

The spring count in 2025 found 38 fish. One year earlier, the official spring count had recorded 191.

On July 29, 2025, just 21 minutes after a magnitude 8.8 earthquake struck Russia’s Kamchatka Peninsula, waves rippled through Devils Hole, nearly 4,000 miles away. Those waves were only about 10 inches high and removed just some material from the shelf.

Then, after an earthquake in Panama on October 9, 2026, nearly 3,000 miles away, biologists at first saw signs of stress in the fish. On a later visit, they found them in healthier condition.

The team had prepared for a crash like this for years. Pupfish eggs from Devils Hole had been collected regularly to build a backup population at the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service’s Ash Meadows Fish Conservation Facility.

In 2025, biologists added captive-raised fish to the wild population for the first time. Between March 2025 and February 2026, scientists released 72 of them into Devils Hole. They also gave the fish extra food while the algae grew back.

The fish live an average of only 10 to 14 months. Because of that, biologists believe most of the 137 fish counted this fall hatched and grew up in Devils Hole itself.

“It’s exciting to see Devils Hole pupfish numbers increase so significantly over the past year,” said Death Valley National Park Superintendent Mike Reynolds.

“These results reflect the dedication of our scientists and conservation partners working to protect this extraordinary species. While challenges remain, we’re encouraged by this progress and committed to its long-term survival.”

The pupfish has survived human threats before. In the late 1960s, a farming operation drilled large wells in the Ash Meadows area, and the water level in Devils Hole began to drop toward the shelf the fish need.

Biologists even hung a floating, artificially lit shelf in the cave as a substitute. The fish never used it.

A federal court halted further pumping in 1971, and the fight reached the U.S. Supreme Court. Its 1976 decision in Cappaert v. United States recognized the water right of Devils Hole as part of a national monument and limited pumping so the natural shelf would stay underwater.

Until the mid-1990s, spring surveys counted more than 200 fish. In the early 2000s, spring counts averaged about 90, and in 2013 the number fell to 35. The cause of that long decline has not been determined.

The new count is an encouraging step, but the park says the population naturally fluctuates and remains highly vulnerable.

It calls continued monitoring, research and habitat management essential if one of the world’s rarest fish is to keep its foothold in a single desert cave.

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Experts recommend understanding the risks of the glass cookware you’re using.

Here’s what you’ll learn when you read this story:

I clearly remember the very last time I used one of my reliable old pyrex baking dishes, and it makes me blush. I was making a chicken recipe but had forgotten to add enough liquid to the pan. In the interest of saving time, I took the hot pan out of the oven, opened a carton of chicken stock from the fridge, and tipped a little in. That’s when my pyrex baking dish shattered into three or four large glass shards, mingled with the now ruined chicken. It broke apart the moment the cold stock touched the hot glass.

Though I was shocked, I realized what my mistake was—the glass couldn’t bear the sudden temperature change. But it made me wonder: wasn’t pyrex designed specifically to hold its structural integrity under just these kinds of temperature fluctuations? When I started searching, I found that people have been complaining about their pyrex cookware exploding for years. Perhaps they just weren’t reading the instructions. Then, I stumbled across a different reason for my destroyed dinner: there’s pyrex, and then there’s PYREX.

The first experiment to use PYREX for cooking took place more than a century ago, in the kitchen of Corning Glass Works physicist Jesse T. Littleton, whose wife used a sawn-off glass battery jar to bake in the oven. It was made of borosilicate and that was the key. Used today in cookware and lab equipment such as beakers, Corning’s “Nonex” glass was invented for hot, burning-oil railway lanterns that needed to stay intact when exposed to cold weather. Calling it fire-glass, the company later named it PYREX®, a play on the word “pyro,” meaning “fire” in Greek, and “ex,” from the label that identified its earlier product.

Since then, PYREX has withstood the intense temperatures and harsh chemical onslaughts of laboratory work. It works well for temperatures of up to 425 degrees Fahrenheit in household kitchens.

The secret to the original PYREX’s heat resilience is its borosilicate composition, a glass made of silica and boron trioxide. Glass expands when it’s hot and contracts while cooling. Heat causes its atoms to vibrate and move apart, weakening the glass. However, boron atoms don’t vibrate or separate as much as other elements, making the overall glass more stable.

Around the 1950s, Corning also began making PYREX bakeware out of tempered soda lime glass, according to Smithsonian magazine. In 1998, Corning sold its consumer product operations to Borden, later renamed World Kitchen, then Corelle Brands. Corelle continued to produce the tempered soda lime bakeware in America—this is called “pyrex,” all lowercase. These days, Corning only makes PYREX borosilicate labware products, such as beakers, for scientific use. In Europe, the brand International Cookware still makes borosilicate cookware.

✅ To have a more accurate idea of what you're buying, here’s a good rule of thumb: “Pyrex” is a brand name with clear distinctions depending on how it’s written. Only cookware marked “PYREX”—all uppercase—is likely made of borosilicate glass. However, most of this is made in Europe; lowercase pyrex brand cookware made in the U.S. has been tempered soda lime for decades. It’s still possible to locate some Pyrex brand borosilicate cookware in the U.S., but it’s harder to find. If you see the label “pyrex,” it definitely means the glass is made of tempered soda lime.

Despite its deep roots in American kitchens, home cooks have been complaining about pyrex shattering in the oven or during rapid temperature changes since the early 2000s. In 2010, the nonprofit organization Consumer Reports released a warning following a yearlong investigation of pyrex shattering incidents. “When we examined 163 incidents in detail, we discovered at least 42 reports of injuries. While hundreds of millions of dishes are used safely each year, we believe the situation is serious enough that we have asked the Consumer Product Safety Commission to investigate,” according to the report.

During testing, soda lime glass shattered at lower temperatures than “more expensive” borosilicate Pyrex bakeware, the report continued. Consumer Reports found that the iconic glass bakeware produced in America today is of soda lime, while the European-made products investigators tested were borosilicate.

Consumers also complained that the manufacturer’s instructions were confusing. Consumer Reports found that the small print on the back of products containing the necessary precautions is “easy to miss.”

Borosilicate glass can withstand up to three times as much thermal shock as soda lime silicate. “When glass bakeware does shatter, consumers report, it can break into sharp shards that go flying, raising the risks of injuries. This contrasts with claims from one of the manufacturers that its glass bakeware breaks into ‘relatively small pieces generally lacking sharp edges,’” according to the report.

Soda lime glass is made of about 60 to 75 percent silica, 12 to 18 percent soda, and 5 to 12 percent lime. Heat-treated and then rapidly cooled on its exterior, the outside of tempered soda lime glass solidifies more quickly than the center. While cooling, the inside pulls at the stiffer exterior layer, creating tension that makes the whole piece more durable. If it does break, tempered glass forms rounded, cube-shaped chunks, instead of the sharp shards of untempered soda lime glass—think about the last time you dropped a drinking glass.

Untempered soda lime glass is used for items that aren’t supposed to be heated, such as drinking glasses and windows. It isn’t safe for either the oven or the microwave oven.

So look for a European provenance, or simply “borosilicate” in the description before you buy your next glass baking dish. I know I will, because I’m pretty sure I’ve only ever owned the soda lime version.

‘Superintelligence is arriving’: Mark Zuckerberg’s AI chief warns humanity may lose control as the AI bubble refuses to pop

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Alexandr Wang, Meta’s chief AI officer and the head of its Superintelligence Labs, posted on X on Sunday that “superintelligence is arriving.” He wrote that “the models are clearly going to be intelligent enough to automate all of AI research,” and that “it is hard to imagine a world where humanity can maintain control over fully unconstrained recursive self-improvement.” He said the remaining obstacles are “no longer intelligence; they are domain-specific know-how, plumbing, and experimentation.”

“How humanity acts over the next year will have great consequence on what future world we live in,” Wang wrote.

Social media erupted over the latest warning from inside the AI thunderdome during a weekend when the AI bubble seemed to finally have popped — the day before it controversially reinflated again. Wang wasn’t even posting about Anthropic’s IPO or Jensen Huang’s resignation from Nvidia and sudden reinstatement on Monday morning.

He was riffing on just the latest historic math breakthrough with the help of generative AI: the claim by Levent Alpöge, a mathematician on Anthropic’s technical staff, of a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis made with Anthropic’s Claude. The 167-year-old problem about the distribution of prime numbers is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute’s $1 million Millennium Prize problems.

“It is hard to overstate the magnitude of discovery that we have just witnessed,” Wang wrote about the milestone, describing it as completed “with the help of an alien mind that we don’t understand.” He added that building an atomic bomb is “a homework problem compared to the Riemann Hypothesis.”

Wang’s warning came as Meta expects to spend $135 billion to $145 billion on capital expenditures this year, driven largely by AI infrastructure costs.

Wang’s post spread fast. The Next Web counted more than 8 million views in its first half day, and the reaction crossed the usual political lines. Jon Favreau, President Obama’s former director of speechwriting, called it “f—ing madness” and said the people Americans elected should be doing everything they can to stop the frontier labs “from handing over control of humanity to superintelligent machines.”

From the right, Megyn Kelly posted: “This is the head of Meta’s superintelligence lab. We’ve had similar warnings from Anthropic, the former head of Alignment at Open AI, not to mention Elon himself at X. How many more do we need until we garner the all-hands-on-deck reaction this deserves?”

Wang argued against fatalism. He wrote that he noticed “a particular form of lazy thinking within the AI industry”: the view that “ASI is inevitable and the only problem to solve is how to do it safely.” He added, “We should not be okay with a ‘handover’ of society to AI.”

After the backlash, he wrote, “What I am saying is this path is not inevitable,” and “Leaders and leadership matter more than ever.” He pointed to Meta’s decision to delay the launch of its Muse model for months and to Zuckerberg’s call for most computing power to go toward serving customers rather than toward AI that improves itself.

Zuckerberg made Wang the center of Meta’s AI push in June 2025, when Meta invested $14.3 billion in Wang’s former company, Scale AI, for a 49% stake. In January, Zuckerberg told investors that “this is going to be a big year for delivering personal superintelligence.” Meta has raised the bottom of its 2026 spending guidance at every quarterly report since, from $115 billion in January to $135 billion in October.

As for the Riemann Hypothesis, Anthropic’s Alpöge has said he plans to explain the AI-generated proof in talks and a written account. Until mathematicians read it, the Clay Institute’s $1 million stays unclaimed.

For Some Artists, Residency-Hopping Is a Form of Survival

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Artists are increasingly turning to the programs as sites of refuge as they navigate precarity, commercial pressures, and burnout.

For Paul Maheke, a French-born performance artist in his 40s, a year of funding, a sunlit studio in a sixteenth-century palatial estate in Rome, and ample time alone represented a new phase in a career that has long been shaped by transience. Over the last decade, before landing at the Villa Medici, he’d completed more than 14 artist residencies.

By 2015, after graduate school, he began moving between residencies every few months, relocating to remote locations in France, Vienna, and Canada. He sought distance from London’s commercial gallery circuit, a networking scene he described as populated with “opening-rats” and an environment he felt was depleting his focus. “Residencies were a way around that,” he told Hyperallergic, four months into his stay at the Italian complex. “I think I’ve never been sedentary. I tend to think better when I’m in movement. As I’m traveling, it’s always shifting my way of working.”

As his ideas developed, Maheke said that the pressure to hold down a studio space better suited to a painter no longer made sense. His work had outgrown a conventional studio, encompassing choreographed performances with professional dancers and drawing on autobiography. He described enmeshing an “Afro-descendant European perspective” with ideas about queerness, memory, and the body as a vessel for social experience.

Across countries and career stages, artists like Maheke have found themselves increasingly turning to residencies to navigate precarity. In interviews with Hyperallergic, artists reported using residencies as alternatives for permanent studios and refuges from the commercial pressures of major cultural centers. What were once considered philanthropic sites for creative retreat or intellectual leisure have become for many a workaround for periods of economic volatility and increased pressure to sustain artistic output.

Yes, some everyday ingredients from the feasting table can make welcome treats for backyard birds, offering a little extra nourishment as temperatures drop and the nights draw in (and transform your outdoor space into a welcoming bird sanctuary in the process).

Whether you're hoping to see your birth month bird out there, or just happy to offer your kitchen scraps up to any local critters who might make use of them, here's how to use your Thanksgiving leftovers to keep bird feeders filled over the holidays...

Before we dive on in, a word of caution: not every Thanksgiving dish is suitable for wildlife, especially as salt, butter, rich sauces and seasonings can make seemingly harmless scraps unsuitable for birds.

The trick? Try to save a few simple ingredients before they make their way into the casserole dish or onto the dinner table. With that caveat in place, then, here are seven Thanksgiving kitchen scraps worth keeping...

Have a few apples left over from your Thanksgiving preparations? Birds such as robins and other fruit-eating visitors will definitely appreciate small pieces of fresh apple.

Cut the flesh into bite-sized chunks, removing the core and seeds first. Bruised apples are fine so long as they're fresh (they're not as fussy as we are!), but discard any mouldy or spoiled pieces. And avoid offering apple pie filling or cooked apples covered in sugar and spices.

Cranberries are a Thanksgiving staple, and you'd better believe that their fresh (and unseasoned, obviously) counterparts make a lovely treat for birds. Offer a few whole or chopped cranberries on a clean feeding tray like this one from MIXXIDEA.

Frozen cranberries are fine, too, once thawed. Just avoid cranberry sauce, as it typically contains added sugar and may include other ingredients birds don't need. Honestly, a little fresh fruit is all that's required.

Pecans (again, plain and unsalted) are another holiday favorite that can be shared with feathered visitors, along with walnuts and peanuts. All of these scrummy nuts are rife at Thanksgiving, and all provide fats and protein that can help birds maintain their energy during colder weather.

Take care to chop larger nuts into manageable pieces and check that they haven't been coated in salt, sugar, chocolate or seasoning. Just like the fruit, nuts should be fresh, too, rather than stale or musty.

Corn often features on the Thanksgiving menu in a big way, and a few plain kernels can be offered to garden birds; just set aside a small portion before adding butter, salt or other seasonings.

Cooked or fresh corn kernels can be placed on a feeding tray, but avoid creamed corn, buttery corn casserole and heavily seasoned dishes. Only offer a little at a time, as damp corn can spoil quickly.

If pumpkin or squash features in your holiday cooking (or your Thanksgiving decor, if you're Kristin Davis!), don't throw away all those seeds; instead, rinse away any stringy pulp, remove any seasoning and allow the seeds to dry thoroughly.

Offer them (have I gotten too repetitive yet?) plain and unsalted, either whole for larger birds or broken into smaller pieces for smaller visitors. They're a useful source of fats and nutrients, making them a worthwhile addition to your bird feeding station.

Leftover rice can also be shared with birds from your Thanksgiving feast, provided it hasn't been cooked with salt, butter, oil, garlic or onions. Both white and brown rice are suitable in small quantities when prepared plainly.

P.S. There's a persistent myth that uncooked or cooked rice swells inside birds and causes harm, but this isn't true. Nevertheless, rice shouldn't replace more nutritious bird food, so just offer just a small amount (you may find your birth month winter bird adores it) and remove anything uneaten so it doesn't have a chance to go bad.

A little leftover boiled or baked potato from your Thanksgiving prep can be another treat for your feathered friends. Set aside a portion before adding butter, milk, salt or gravy, then allow it to cool and chop it into small pieces.

Please never offer raw potatoes or green potato peelings, and avoid chips, roast potatoes cooked in fat and heavily seasoned mashed potatoes. Experts always advise that plain potato be treated as an occasional extra rather than a regular food source.

It's every bit as important to know what to avoid when it comes to feeding birds your Thanksgiving leftovers. Turkey fat and pan drippings might seem like an easy substitute for bird suet, but they're best kept off the feeding station. Audubon, in fact, warns that turkey fat melts more readily than traditional suet and can turn rancid quickly. It can also smear onto feathers, potentially affecting their waterproofing and insulation.

Gravy, stuffing and salty side dishes should also stay off the menu, as they often contain butter, salt, onion and garlic, none of which are suitable additions to a wild bird's diet. Avoid, too, anything that contains chocolate, along with avocado, sugary desserts and heavily processed foods, too. Bread and pastry aren't particularly nutritious (even if they won't do any harm), so think up some better ways to use your leftovers; might this author humbly submit the idea of a bread-and-butter pudding?

Once you've picked all of the bird-friendly scraps out of your Thanksgiving feast, be sure to present it properly! Always use a clean feeding tray rather than leaving piles of food on the ground, and try to put food out in the morning, as this is when it's most likely to be eaten.

Take care to remove anything uneaten before it spoils, and wash feeding surfaces regularly to reduce the risk of bacteria and disease spreading. It's also worth keeping fresh drinking water available, particularly as colder weather approaches, and remember that kitchen scraps are supposed to be occasional extras, not a replacement for a balanced wild bird food mix.

Still, all those warnings aside, there's no denying that sharing a little of your holiday feast with your local birds is a lovely way to give thanks to Mother Nature (and reduce food waste at the same time).

CrowdStrike Holdings Inc. today announced plans to acquire Zscaler Inc., a fellow provider of cybersecurity software, for about $43.9 billion.

The all-stock deal values the latter company at $260.4 per share. That’s a 34% premium to Zscaler’s closing price on Friday, the day CrowdStrike’s takeover interest became public. The transaction is the largest cybersecurity acquisition on record by a wide margin.

The companies reported their quarterly results in conjunction with the acquisition announcement. Both topped expectations across the board. CrowdStrike posted adjusted net income of $354.9 million on $1.58 billion in revenue, while Zscaler reported a $208.4 million adjusted profit and sales of $949.3 million. The former company also boosted its full-year guidance.

CrowdStrike’s flagship Falcon platform uses lightweight programs called sensors to protect employee devices, cloud instances and other technology assets. Zscaler’s Zero Trust Exchange, in turn, protects the network connections that link together those assets. The platform is positioned as an alternative to virtual private network tools.

The Zero Trust Exchange encrypts the traffic that flows between employee devices and business applications. When needed, administrators can decrypt the data to search for threats. Additionally, they can configure the platform to require more thorough authentication when users log into particularly sensitive systems.

The product portfolios of CrowdStrike’s and Zscaler overlap in some areas. Both companies offer tools that can protect artificial intelligence applications from threats such as prompt injections. They also compete in the secure browser category, although their respective offerings take different approaches to blocking malware.

Zscaler offers a tool that can automatically launch simulated cyberattacks against applications to find weak points. CrowdStrike joined the fray as well earlier this year with SafeMind, a set of custom AI models and related assets. It not only finds software vulnerabilities but also develops detections, automated workflows designed to spot hacking attempts.

CrowdStrike said that it will use the network telemetry collected by Zscaler to enhance SafeMind’s training dataset. In 2024, the latter company disclosed that its platform blocked more than 9 billion threats per day. That activity, which has no doubt grown in volume since, should give CrowdStrike’s AI models a more complete understanding of hacker behavior.

The company’s plans for Zscaler also extend beyond AI training. CrowdStrike will incorporate the network protection provider’s software into its Flex offering, which enables customers to buy multiple cybersecurity tools via a single contract. That could help expand the adoption of Zscaler’s products among CrowdStrike customers.

The companies have also identified other upselling opportunities. They disclosed today that about 8,000 Zscaler customers don’t yet use CrowdStrike products.

“With Zscaler, we’re building cybersecurity’s operating system: a single control plane to deploy AI, control AI, and enable any agentic application to run safely,” said CrowdStrike founder and Chief Executive Officer George Kurtz (pictured). “We see a path to become the largest AI safety and security company the world has ever seen.”

CrowdStrike expects to complete the acquisition in the second half of its 2028 fiscal year, which starts next February. The company stated that the deal will be “significantly and immediately accretive” to free cash flow per share upon closing.

CrowdStrike expects Zscaler’s financial impact to grow over time. It estimates that the deal will unlock $500 million worth of annual cost synergies within 18 to 24 months, mainly in the form of data center, supplier and operational improvements. The company also sees Zscaler boosting its annual revenue run-rate by $1 billion within 30 months.

With Carla, I think she has decided that she’s the most important person in that room, and she doesn’t feel the need to check in with others. I think she channeled whatever abandonment she experienced into that way of being as a defense mechanism, versus my equivalent — which is exhausting, but is also a defense mechanism.

[Playing] that was really fun. Once I made that decision to be like, “She’s not concerned with other people’s feelings or energies, and she’s very much orbiting herself” — it was so uncomfortable at first to be that way. And then anytime I stumbled upon something that my instinct would be to correct, I was like, “No, she wouldn’t bother her.” And then it was quite a liberating choice.

George has also talked about how you brought an unexpected softness to Carla that was not always part of his original conception of the character. Were there any particular moments in the season that you wanted to soften her a little bit or make her more likeable? Did you ever push back on some of her actions or decisions?

I didn’t feel like I needed to make her more likable. I sometimes needed reasons for the decisions about how she was [acting]. I felt like I really pushed back against the kind of hysterical breakdown on the talk show [in episode five], although people really enjoyed that, because I just didn’t want it to fall into any kind of “hysterical woman” trope situation. I didn’t want Morgan to appear reasonable and Carla to appear unhinged, but that’s often the way the world sees these relationships and sees the dynamics between a couple. If she’s ambitious or if she’s ruthless, she’s a bitch; if he is [those same things], he’s a great businessman. I think it does kick up some of those tropes, which I was really trying to push back on until I realized, “Well, maybe it’s a great mirror to see how women are treated and judged.” Obviously, it gets rectified, and she wins that particular battle.

But there were moments in this that I was trying to advocate for her to be more vulnerable, or more soft, or just more three-dimensional. But then if I didn’t find it, I would just lean into her being really unashamedly a bitch or whatever she is. The moment where she couldn’t remember her son’s age — I was like, “That’s a huge thing [to forget]!” And we were learning this as we were going on. I’m like, “Does she not know her son’s age?” And then I would talk to other cast [members] who were like, “Actually, it’s kind of funny. Just do it.” But I was trying to fight for her a bit in moments.

Even though you and Dominic play the leads of this season, your characters don’t actually spend a lot of time together because they’re always busy strategizing with their own lawyers. What did all of the brief glimpses of Carla and Morgan — in the back of a limousine, in an elevator, in the back alley of the courthouse waiting for their cars in the rain — reveal to you about the nature of their relationship?

I think the thing that Carla and Morgan were missing was time together, and time and energy spent on each other. When they’re forced into those situations, other than the car at the very beginning, I think they realized that there was so much there that was probably salvageable or that they cared about. And once you get into a war situation and it’s you versus them, it’s very easy to forget those moments, the good things. But I think what probably comes across in those poignant moments is a sense of love buried underneath all of it, or consideration at least, or some form of emotion, which I think the lawyers are very good at hiding from you.

Carla’s relationship with Morgan is also very much shaped by their own financial circumstances — he is now a billionaire tech founder, and she has been experiencing a lull in her acting career and a significant dip in her income.

I think through the process of divorce and how acrimonious that is and how savage it is, you can either really sink into the resentment and the loathing, or you can remember what it was that brought you together. In the cross-examination [during the last two episodes], we’re talking about moments when we first met. Parts of it are quite therapeutic for them.

I think she genuinely feels entitled to something for Zephyr. I think she really does feel like she contributed more than he is allowing to be let on to the formation of that company, but she’s just vengeful. I don’t even know that it was ever about the money. I think she was just unhappy and he was unhappy, and then it got ugly. She kind of made it ugly, but that’s because she was convinced he cheated. It’s a whole mess, but there are threads within it that I also struggle to follow. So I put it down to just them being erratic and love not being a linear thing.

Carla and Morgan clearly could have settled their divorce at any point during mediation or arbitration, but it’s not until the end of their two-day trial that they decide to reach a fair settlement on their own terms without any outside intervention. In their last scene together, the two of them share a lovely moment on the beach in Normandy, and they are last seen walking back to her cottage to have a hot chocolate with Gabe (Aran Murphy, son of Cilian), Carla’s son from a previous relationship. What did you make of the resolution of their relationship?

I think they’re both battle-weary by that point, and they’ve seen all of the duplicity amongst the legal teams, and that side of things is so ugly. They’ve humiliated each other publicly and been backed into corners, and I think they don’t recognize themselves in some of those moments. I found I was expecting more fireworks at the ending. (Laughs.) And then in reflection, I think the normalizing of divorce is something that should be more commonplace in the world. It doesn’t have to be hell. I thought that the ending was very poignant and sweet.

Basically, what happened was that Dom and I never really got to work together. So anytime we did, we were very happy, which I think serves the story and adds a little sort of something to the scenes. But I found that scene on the beach touching to shoot and surprisingly emotional. But again, there are so many different ways that this could have gone, and I have to defer to the writing team to come up with the best one. I think Carla should have won! I probably was drawn to it becoming more ugly. But actually, the lawyers are the ugly people, and that’s the moral of the story. Even though Carla and Morgan are also ugly and narcissistic. (Laughs.)

What do you think it says about us as a culture that we immediately think of the worst possible scenario for a high-profile divorce?

Well, I don’t know. I think it’s wealthy people. We want to see them being treated like normal people, being dragged through the mud. It’s the same with Succession. We loved watching those people get it wrong and fall apart. I think there must be some kind of schadenfreude. It’s wonderful to be in those aspirational worlds and then wonderful to be reminded that, irrespective of how aspirational this world is or how great it looks from the outside, they’re two people dealing with shit. It’s just with many more zeros on the end of it.

George has told me his theory about what happens to Carla and Morgan’s relationship, but what do you imagine Carla and Morgan’s lives — either together or apart — will look like going forward?

I think they’ll be really good friends, but I think he will regret [the divorce] more than she will. So that’s me defending my character, and Dom would say the same thing about his. (Laughs.)

Over the last couple of decades, you have played a lot of women — and particularly actresses — who are navigating intense public scrutiny. It’s hard not to think about how the early years of your career in the mid-2000s were overshadowed by British tabloid culture and paparazzi obsession. How do you reflect now on the harsh treatment that you endured in your 20s?

Yeah, it feels so alien to the world that we live in now that it’s almost comical. It’s not comical, but I’m baffled that that existed. The ’90s [had] a lad culture in England, and God forbid, you couldn’t take the piss out of yourself and laugh at yourself. If you couldn’t laugh at yourself or send yourself up, you weren’t worth talking to in England. So I think I kind of absorbed that lad culture and was quite gobby and probably quite fun for them fodder-wise. But the lad culture meant that the paparazzi were just ravenous and unstoppable and aggressive and violent, and it’s just so different to the world [now]. It just would not be allowed; it wouldn’t be tolerated. People on the street wouldn’t stand for it if they saw what was happening.

It was young women that were targeted and cameras up your skirts — just shocking. But you look back and you’re like, “God, that’s not long ago,” and it was absolutely accepted and paid for and devoured. So I’m more in a kind of anthropological way interested in it [now], because I feel maybe from some PTSD that I am separate from it. I can look back on it with curiosity and philosophy versus trauma and just be like, “God, that’s crazy that it wasn’t that long ago, and that’s how we lived.” But pre-social media, in 2008, the whole world shifted. In 2012, it really shifted, but it’s just interesting to look back on that time and to have been such a part of it and feel like it’s so foreign. I think it was too much for anyone to really live like 15 people outside your house and chasing you everywhere you went. I’ve always been the same — I’m quite a normal, grounded person that it just didn’t suit [my life]. I don’t want bodyguards and putting on makeup every day.

You’ve spoken about wanting to take yourself more seriously as an actress, and reaching a point in your life where you are decidedly less interested in hearing other people’s opinions. When did that shift start for you?

I think I always took my work really seriously. I just think it was a battle to be seen as a serious actor in my 20s, but we are way beyond that now. I think when I had my first daughter, life just suddenly felt more serious, and the choices that I was making, they had to be worthy of being away from home and taking my kid on the road, and there was just more levity to each decision. So I feel like the work is something I’ve always taken very seriously, but people didn’t see me as a serious actor until quite a while ago now.

Do you ever worry that playing women who are consistently judged by their image reinforces the narrative you’ve been trying to move past?

It’s interesting. I don’t ever think about that in relation to these actresses. I see them as they should be seen: as people. But I think what I was objecting to is my own personal [experience]. It was a different era of tabloid journalism and scrutiny, and we lived in a monoculture. There weren’t fringe people with opinions on the internet, and it was one media and pretty unanimous news that we were all digesting. So if there was a public persona of you, that was pretty much the [only] public persona of you, and it was very difficult to have a say in that. It’s such a different culture now that I don’t think it exists in the same way. I think that if I was treated the way that I was then, I would have a much louder voice or means to come back at it.

But in terms of [playing] the actresses, I don’t know. I think it’s less that I’m seeking out these roles and more that those are the roles that people see me as, probably because a famous actress is part of my identity in a way [but] I don’t feel is part of me. I feel like an actor who’s not very famous — and a mom. But it’s quite funny to perhaps send myself up by playing heightened versions of whatever I imagine people might assume that to be, maybe. Although I have played a lot of actresses — and I can’t even remember them all now — I’ve also played a lot of non-actresses, so it feels like there’s variety for me.

You and Carla are fundamentally different people, but I would imagine you also know what it feels like to not be able to leave your own house or to live in a gilded cage because of the paparazzi. Even though you have moved past that chapter of your life, do those feelings ever come up when you are playing these women? Or are you always able to separate your own experiences from the characters you play?

No, I feel like when I’m put actually in the circumstances of getting through a pack [of people] into a car, my heart starts racing. I do have some body memory of it, even though I know it’s pretend. That’s your job, I think, as an actor. It’s to summon things or have them just appear for you in this instance because your past experience is what you can draw on. But I think if I was to get another script of a famous actress, I’d probably not do it, unless she was really different.

Unless she was even more awful than Carla?

Megaspeed, the company that Alice Huang — the woman at the center of the alleged Chinese espionage scandal involving Nvidia — led for over two years, will be placed on a U.S. Commerce Department blacklist, Semafor reported Monday.

The company will be added to the Commerce Department’s Entity List, the outlet writes. This list “contains the names of foreign parties” subject to certain export restrictions designed to ensure that “sensitive technologies do not fall into the hands of those who would threaten U.S. national security or American citizens.”

Functionally, what this will mean is that U.S. companies will not be able to do business with or sell products to Megaspeed without first gaining a special license from the federal government. Semafor cites anonymous sources familiar with the matter to note the move is designed to stop the company “from obtaining US-made semiconductors.”

Megaspeed is a Singapore-based cloud and AI infrastructure provider that, during the period that Nvidia’s boss, Jensen Huang, self-admittedly had an affair with Huang, was one of Nvidia’s biggest chip customers. Last week, the Commerce Department also announced it had launched a criminal probe into the company. Authorities in both the U.S. and Singapore are now probing whether the company defied export restrictions and illegally smuggled Nvidia chips to China. The company has denied wrongdoing.

TechCrunch reached out to the Commerce Department for comment on the reporting and will update this story if it responds.
