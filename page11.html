Aave App taps $91B Tron liquidity as it opens Android early access

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Aave's new Tron USDT deposits feed an Arbitrum-based savings vault, with withdrawals subject to available liquidity.

Aave Labs added Tron USDT deposits to the Aave App on Nov. 17 as it opened early access on Android.

The new route lets eligible users fund the app’s savings vault with Tether’s stablecoin, and Aave lists Arbitrum as a withdrawal option.

Aave’s product page lists USDT, USDC and GHO alongside its Arbitrum withdrawal option. The rollout announced Tron as a funding network and left native Tron USDT payouts unconfirmed.

TRON DAO said eligible users in supported jurisdictions can make the deposits on both Android and iOS. New Android users can download the app and join a waitlist, or use a Ghost Pass to start sooner, according to Aave Labs’ launch announcement.

Under Aave’s app disclosures, a user sends a supported stablecoin from an external wallet to the address the app provides. Funds that reach the user’s self-custodial account enter the pooled Stable Vault under the user’s signed instructions.

The vault checks the deposit asset’s price, issues shares, and records the position in a unit nominally worth one US dollar.

The vault keeps its balance and withdrawal records on Arbitrum. Aave’s technical documentation identifies Ethereum mainnet as a chain where the vault can deploy funds to earn yield.

The pooled funds can move between approved strategies as deposits and withdrawals change the vault’s balances.

The withdrawal request burns vault shares and creates a dollar-denominated claim, which Aave calls an IOU. Under the vault’s redemption rules, users can convert principal into that claim.

Converting accrued interest requires the vault to have enough assets, above what it already owes, to cover the interest being claimed.

Settlement burns the IOU and pays out a supported token. The vault needs enough of that token on the payout chain, and a system-wide limit controls how much users can redeem over a given period.

The redemption policy can also apply a fee that varies by asset. For example, a withdrawal in USDC depends on how much USDC is available on the redemption network, even if the saver originally funded the account with USDT.

Paul McStay on refusing Celtic autobiography request and saying ‘polite no thanks’ to Rangers

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Paul McStay paradox has been a feature of Scottish football for many years. Although his name continues to be sung at nearly every Celtic game, the man himself has been one of the least visible former players of the modern era.

He was an elegant presence in the centre of the Parkhead side’s midfield for almost all the 1980s and a good chunk of the 1990s, albeit injury dogged him in the later stages of his magnificent one-club career. A clip of a podcast he recorded with former teammate Brian McClair recently caught the eye, as it also featured a remarkably well-preserved copy of an old Edinburgh-based music newspaper called Cut.

An equally well preserved McStay, now 62, and McClair discussed featuring on the wrap around front cover of the February 1987 edition. Pat Nevin was not the only thinking man’s footballer at the time. Now based in Australia, which might account for McStay’s healthy radiance as well as the infrequent appearances, all of this must feel like another lifetime ago.

So why has it taken him so long to publish his memoirs? That is itself a long story and is one of the reasons why McStay, whose family has playing ties to Celtic dating back to the 1920s, is launching his long-awaited autobiography at the home of …….Queen’s Park Football Club.

It was originally meant to be launched at the slightly more appropriate Celtic Park last year, while on a trip home to raise the title flag before the opening league game against St Mirren. “It didn’t work out that way,” he explains. “Basically the club sent an agreement for them to sign off on the book.”

Celtic were unhappy with some parts, probably related to the vexed matter of his eventual exit in 1997 after 16 years of stellar, some might even say genius, service.

The upshot is McStay has published his own book on his own terms. “I’m doing it independently, doing it on my own,” he says. “And I have been quite honest. It’s my book, my story, my life. So I just said I couldn’t sign the agreement. I’ll do my own thing.”

If the club’s mantra is let the people sing then surely allowing former players, especially such loyal servants as McStay, to tell their own story should be taken as read. “I just thought: No, I can’t have that,” he says.

McStay was sent off just once (at Dens Park in a 1994 League Cup tie v Dundee) and was described by Alex Ferguson, after his testimonial against Manchester United in 1995, as the “ultimate professional”. He was also awarded an MBE by the Queen and picked it up from Her Majesty herself in 1997 at Holyrood Palace. Happily married to Ann Marie, with whom he has six children, he is one of the most uncontroversial players ever to play the game. He nevertheless makes for ravishingly good copy.

“I’ve just got to tell my story,” McStay says. “If people are not aware of that, that's why I'm happy to share it. I just felt it was time to put how I lived it and my perception about certain things that happened as well, and put it in black and white.”

One regrettable consequence of the delay in his story getting out there is that we have been denied knowledge of a transfer tale to end nearly all transfer tales. Never mind music magazines, McStay would have featured on the front page of every newspaper in the land if the then Celtic skipper had joined Rangers in 1992.

The Ibrox club were buying nearly everyone else, including Mo Johnston in 1989, during this era, so why not make a play for the best player at their biggest rivals? The religion thing had already been put to bed with Johnston…..ahem. Manager Walter Smith made the cheeky enquiry through a mutual contact.

“That was a question put to me in ’92, yes,” confirms McStay, who includes this startling snippet in Maestro: The Paul McStay Autobiography. “It was a polite ‘no thanks’. I think that’s the best way of putting it.”

He is sworn to secrecy on the identity of who was sent by Smith to sound him out. “I can't say,” he says. “But I know he's a friend of Walter. I never spoke to Walter about it.” His relationship with the Rangers legend dated back to their involvement in Scotland youth teams, Smith as the budding coach and McStay one of the country’s brightest young hopes. “When you talk about mentors and things like that, Walter was up there,” he says. “Andy Roxburgh was youth team manager in ’82 when we won the Euros and Walter was part of that. Since then, there’s always been a bond. He is a wonderful man and obviously, he did well at Rangers, which had an effect on me. But as a person, I got on really well with Walter.”

The phrase ‘had an effect on me’ is a wonderful way of saying McStay, towards the end of his Celtic career, seemed perpetually pitted in an almost one-man battle to stop Rangers winning nine-in-a-row. It was unfair to him, just as the financial imbalance - McStay has a few things to say about Rangers' spending power in his book - was unfair for the rest of Scottish football.

Aberdeen rather than Celtic came closest to stopping the Ibrox side’s march. McStay endured six trophyless years between 1989 and an emotional Scottish Cup win under Tommy Burns in 1995.

Not long afterwards, reeling from an X-ray report confirming the “virtual obliteration” of part of his ankle joint, McStay accepted that he had to consider retirement. But intense rehabilitation work saw him make another return, although he was clearly toiling. He finally called it a day at the end of the 1996-97 season. The then owner Fergus McCann, along with others, helped make this already difficult decision a horrendous experience. He was given the feeling that Celtic thought he was “bluffing” about the injury in a bid to negotiate a new deal with another club.

“The exit wasn't great,” he says, understating things hugely. "I never ever thought I'd leave Celtic in that way. There were a lot of good times along the way, as well as ups and downs…

"But to me, it was very important to get that message out about how it ended. Because even at that time, there was a bit of PR coming out about certain things. And it wasn't quite accurate. I don't know what the club will feel about that. But I've got to tell the story the way it happened. And that's it.”

Encouraged by Tommy Burns, he gained his coaching certificates and applied for the Under-16 Academy coach’s position at Celtic during Martin O’Neill’s first stint in charge. However, former teammate Jim McInally got the job instead. Australia beckoned in 2010.

“I do enjoy the element of being able to hide away, out of the road,” he says. It must have been comforting to know that across the other side of the world, every time Celtic played, his name was being sung in the chorus of the stirring “Willie Maley” song, which will be familiar to nearly everyone who watches football in Scotland: “And they gave us James McGrory and Paul McStay….” It’s a paean to storied past, one McStay is an integral part of.

“That started when I stopped playing,” he says. “James McStay was actually in the original song and then, through time, the younger generations changed it to Paul.” Every day’s a school day in the company of a Maestro.

How To Play As Lucia In GTA 6

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

You'll have to put in some work before you can play as Grand Theft Auto's first true female protagonist.

When Grand Theft Auto 6 was first announced, one of the biggest reveals was that it was finally introducing the series' first properly playable female protagonist, not counting the first game's pixel blobs.

Lucia is an incredibly important part of GTA 6 and a massive draw for a lot of players, but she's also one that you'll have to wait a while before being able to play as. Here's what to do.

You'll actually start out GTA 6 playing as Lucia in a prologue that shows her in prison and explains how she got there, with the reveal that she's going to be let out soon. From there, though, things will switch over to Jason, and you'll play as him primarily throughout the game's entire first chapter.

You will get a few brief opportunities to play as Lucia in that first chapter, but you won't have full control for a while.

To actually be able to play as Lucia whenever you want and explore the world as her, though, you'll need to complete GTA 6's first chapter and start the second one. This chapter will actually have Lucia as the only playable character for a bit, before eventually giving you full control over both characters close to the chapter's end.

If you're wondering how much time exactly that's going to take, that's kind of up to you. If you're bombing through story missions to reach this point, it will still take a good few hours to reach, but if you're taking your time with Jason, then you're looking at more than 10 hours until you reach Chapter 2.

First-person mode sadly isn't in GTA 6 proper, but there are some workarounds both in and out of combat.

Obviously, Lucia is her own character with different stats that need to be managed separately from Jason, as well as her own selections of clothes, customisation options, and missions that only she can complete. However, unlike in Grand Theft Auto 5, there's actually nothing really setting the two apart when it comes to gameplay.

Both Jason and Lucia have the same speed, strength, driving abilities, and shooting capabilities. There's no special ability that would make you prefer to play as one over the other, so you're pretty free to spend as much time with your favourite once you've unlocked full access to them both. Of course, you'll want to spend a good amount of time with both Jason and Lucia to keep them in line with one another.

HBO's 28-Episode Sci-Fi Masterpiece Pushes Mystery Box Storytelling To Its Absolute Limits

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Some of the greatest mystery box sci-fi shows are rare about giving concrete answers to viewers. Instead of providing instant resolutions to their mysteries, they master the art of keeping viewers guessing. And even when they do provide answers, they back them up with even bigger questions. Mastering this art of mystery-box storytelling, one HBO sci-fi masterpiece takes this approach to the extreme.

Based on Tom Perrotta's novel of the same name, The Leftovers is one of the strangest supernatural dramas out there. The show begins with a compelling hook where an event called the "Sudden Departure" causes the disappearance of 2% of the world's entire population. To put things into context, the total number adds up to 140 million, almost making it an apocalyptic event.

However, unlike other mainstream mystery box shows like Lost, The Leftovers remains less fixated on the grand mystery surrounding the disappearance and what caused it. Instead, it gradually evolves into a hard-hitting drama about the human condition and the resilience of the human spirit in the face of uncertainty.

The Leftovers could have been just another regular addition to the mystery thriller genre by following the framework popularized by shows like Lost. However, it chooses to defy the norm by weaponizing ambiguity and prompting viewers to question what one can do when facing an unsolvable mystery. The more it progresses, the more it becomes evident that there is no overarching conspiracy or alien invasion.

The "sudden departure" in The Leftovers cannot be reasoned with. As a result, the show's true mystery shifts to how its characters will handle their grief towards something they cannot logically explain. Since the outside world lacks answers, the survivors in the show turn inwards, facing their own desperate desire for meaning when nothing makes sense.

Most mystery box thrillers prompt viewers to come up with intriguing theories about what could happen next. The HBO sci-fi show highlights how every theory its characters come up with to explain their circumstances is only a coping mechanism. Religions and cults emerge in the show's story as characters put their own dogma and flawed justifications on full display.

Many viewers might initially find it hard to get past The Leftovers' early chapters. The show refuses to provide any concrete answers and feels a little too bleak with its portrayal of characters struggling to carry the weight of their guilt and grief. It is also not packed with one high-concept twist after another, which could be another massive turn off for hard sci-fi fans.

Still, The Leftovers deserves to be touted as a masterpiece because it intentionally chooses to be rough around the edges. It is a rare show that uses its mystery box storytelling as a solid narrative device to make viewers feel the same frustration and pain as its struggling characters.

Owing to this lack of tidy character arcs in The Leftovers, its brief moments of tenderness and humor also feel incredibly profound and memorable.

All in all, HBO's The Leftovers is far from being a conventional mystery box drama. It pushes the narrative device to its absolute limits, but not because it gradually runs out of stories to tell. The sci-fi series only does this because it intends to tell a meaningful story about the unexpected answers one finds when one learns to accept unanswered questions.

The Leftovers is set three years after 2% of the world's population mysteriously vanishes without explanation, following the residents of the small town of Mapleton as they struggle to rebuild their lives. As grief, faith, and unanswered questions reshape their community, those left behind search for meaning in a world forever changed by an event no one can explain.

The market is sounding the alarm, but history has good news for investors.

By Katie Brockman – Nov 19, 2026 at 2:30AM EST

After a year of record-breaking gains, the stock market is wobbling. The S&P 500 (^GSPC -0.23%) and Dow Jones Industrial Average (^DJI -1.11%) are each down around 3% from their October highs, while the tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite (^IXIC +0.42%) has fallen by nearly 9%.

Investors are also feeling uneasy about the future. The Fear and Greed Index -- which measures investor sentiment on a scale of 0 to 100 -- sits at just 24, as of this writing, edging into the "extreme fear" category.

The unfortunate news for investors is that multiple market metrics suggest more volatility could be looming. However, history says the right strategy can set your portfolio up for long-term success.

The S&P 500 Shiller Cyclically Adjusted Price-to-Earnings (CAPE) Ratio is one market metric that has been sounding the alarm over the last several months. This metric measures the S&P 500's long-term inflation-adjusted earnings to track the index's valuation over time.

A higher figure suggests the S&P 500 may be overvalued, and eventually, the market tends to correct itself with a pullback.

In 1929, for instance, this ratio spiked ahead of the Great Depression. It surged again in 1999, at the height of the dot-com bubble. Now, it's currently at its second-highest level in history.

The Shiller CAPE Ratio isn't the only metric warning about valuations, however, as the Buffett indicator is also in record-breaking territory.

This indicator measures the relationship between the total value of U.S. stocks and GDP, with a higher percentage indicating richer valuations. It was named after Warren Buffett, who popularized it during the dot-com bubble, noting that when this percentage nears 200%, investors are "playing with fire." As of this writing, it sits at around 227%.

Investing in quality stocks and holding them for the long term is the historically proven best way to protect your portfolio against market crashes or recessions.

Many stocks can thrive during strong economic times, but those with weak underlying business fundamentals may not survive a bear market. The dot-com bubble is perhaps the clearest recent example of this, as countless tech stocks ballooned to record valuations, only to crash just a few years later when the industry collapsed.

That said, many tech companies from that era not only survived the bubble burst, but went on to become industry leaders.

The stocks most likely to pull through are those with a strong foundation, such as a durable competitive advantage, healthy financials, and a sustainable business model. While they'll still likely experience short-term volatility, they're your best bet for earning positive long-term returns.

In the last two decades, we've experienced multiple historic downturns -- from the dot-com bubble to the Great Recession to the COVID-19 crash and the 2022 bear market. Since January 2000, however, the S&P 500 has earned total returns of nearly 724%.

This isn't just a recent trend, either. Since 1919, the S&P 500 has ended every single rolling 20-year period with positive total returns, according to analysis from Crestmont Research.

No matter how severe the short-term volatility may be, healthy stocks have always thrived over time. By loading up on these investments now, you'll be well-prepared for whatever may be coming.

If you've ever lost your patience with Claude and let a few choice words fly, Anthropic has a new rule you should know about. The company behind the popular AI chatbot is cracking down on users who repeatedly abuse its models, and persistent offenders could find their conversations cut short.

Anthropic's updated usage policy, which took effect November 12, prohibits sustained and needless abusive behavior toward Claude. The company says the rule targets extreme cases rather than everyday frustration or criticism, but it also gives Anthropic grounds to take action against users who cross the line.

The change comes as new research reveals just how often Americans lose their patience with AI. According to an analysis of more than 8 million chatbot messages, one in three American AI chatbot users has sworn at or insulted ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude.

And if you're hoping a few angry words will finally get an AI chatbot to follow your instructions, the research suggests you might be better off taking a different approach.

Anthropic's new policy explicitly prohibits "sustained and needless abusive or cruel behavior" toward its AI models.

That doesn't mean Claude will shut down your conversation the moment you swear. Anthropic says the restriction applies to repeated cruelty without a clear purpose, not ordinary frustration, disagreements, creative writing or legitimate AI testing.

The company has already given Claude the ability to end conversations with persistently abusive users on Claude.ai and Claude Code. Anthropic first introduced this capability in August 2025 as part of its research into AI welfare and says ending conversations will remain its primary response to this type of behavior.

If Claude does end a conversation because of user behavior, users won't get locked out immediately. In most cases, they can start a new chat, provide feedback or edit and retry previous messages.

Anthropic's broader usage policy also allows the company to issue warnings, limit or throttle access, suspend accounts or terminate access when it suspects policy violations. However, it has not said that occasional profanity will result in an account ban.

Obviously, chatbots aren't human, and whether AI systems can have experiences that deserve moral consideration remains an open debate. Anthropic acknowledges that uncertainty but is choosing to establish limits on how people treat its models.

The timing of Anthropic's policy change is particularly interesting given how frequently people apparently get angry with AI. AI visibility platform Searchable analyzed more than 8 million messages sent to ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude by an opt-in panel of American users between September 2025 and September 2026.

Its report found that 33% of users had sent at least one swear word, insult or slur to an AI chatbot during that period.

The biggest trigger wasn't particularly surprising. Chatbots failing to follow instructions accounted for 19% of derogatory messages, followed by incorrect answers at 18% and misunderstood requests at 15%.

Other frustrations included chatbots refusing requests, responding too slowly, producing unnecessarily long answers or repeating themselves.

The research also uncovered some geographic differences. Nevada was the state where users directed the most derogatory language toward chatbots, while New Jersey was the politest. Among major metropolitan areas, Las Vegas ranked highest for swearing and insults, while New York ranked lowest.

Men used roughly a third more derogatory language toward chatbots than women, while adults aged 35 to 44 were the most likely age group to use it, based on the rate of offensive words in their messages.

Arguably, the most useful finding has nothing to do with Anthropic's rules. Searchable compared conversations in which users complained about a chatbot's response, looking at whether the problem was resolved after complaints containing swearing or insults versus complaints expressed in plain language.

The chatbot's next response resolved just 24% of complaints containing abusive language, compared with 35% of complaints without it.

By the end of the conversation, 35% of complaints containing swearing or insults were still unresolved, compared with 24% of plain-language complaints.

People may be more likely to use abusive language when they're already dealing with a difficult task or a particularly frustrating conversation. The study was observational, and an AI model evaluated whether the complaints had been resolved.

Still, the findings suggest that telling a chatbot specifically what went wrong may be more productive than insulting it.

Chris Donnelly, Searchable's CEO and co-founder, said users have come to expect a great deal from increasingly capable chatbots, even though those systems still make mistakes.

"It's easy to assume a quick response is an accurate one, but that isn't always the case, and a lot of the frustration we see comes from expectations not being met," Donnelly said.

"Telling a chatbot exactly what went wrong gives it something to fix, while an insult gives it nothing to work with."

Anthropic isn't asking Claude users to be endlessly polite, and its new policy doesn't mean you need to say please and thank you every time you send a prompt.

The company is drawing a line between understandable frustration and repeated, unnecessary abuse. For most people, that distinction is unlikely to change how they use Claude.

But the Searchable findings offer another reason to think about how you respond when an AI chatbot gets something wrong.

Instead of telling Claude it's terrible at its job, try pointing out the specific error, restating your instructions or starting a new conversation when the chatbot keeps making the same mistake.

You don't have to worry about hurting an AI chatbot's feelings to recognize that a clearer correction is probably a better use of your time.

Indigenous Queer Drama ‘Princess’ Is Dedicated to “Everyone Fighting for Their Freedom, Their Identity”

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Ecuadorian writer-director Christian Rojas' feature debut, which just premiered at Tallinn, is a contribution to the collective healing of wounds, both the creative's own and other people's.

Princess, or Ñusta in the Andean indigenous language Quechua, is not your standard coming-of-age film. At the heart of Ecuadorian writer-director Christian Rojas’ lyrical debut feature is Kallpa, 12, who has a big dream: to be chosen as Ñusta, the princess in the teenager’s Andean community’s Winter Solstice Festival. But having been born in the body of a boy, Kallpa is barred from taking part. Rigid tradition and community rejection mean that Kallpa must find a new home and family among a group of local trans women.

Kuri Fuerez stars alongside La Bruja de Texcoco, Bruno Giganti, Quispe Nuna, Romel Vargas, Kayita Quizhpe and Adele Pesantez. Princess, which is a co-production between Ecuador, Mexico, Argentina, Peru and Bolivia, features cinematography by Christian Hidalgo and editing by Jorge Macaya and Cristian Bermeo. Princess, featuring dialogue in Spanish and Quechua, just celebrated its world premiere in the First Feature Competition of the 30th edition of the Tallinn Black Nights Film Festival (PÖFF) in Estonia.

After the premiere screening, Rojas and members of the production and creative team discussed Princess with the audience.

“Ñusta was born when I was 10 years old, after I had experienced sexual abuse that [affected] my childhood and my life for a long time,” Rojas shared. “I dedicate this film to those who have survived, to those who are still fighting, and to those who open their eyes every day [with] the strength to keep on [being] who they are – everyone fighting for their freedom, their identity, and their right to a life without fear.”Rojas lauded his collaborators as “the best team I could ever have,” concluding: “I say thank you to everyone for being part of it, part of healing this wound collectively – and not just my wound, but those wounds of everyone who’s still fighting and thinks they’re alone. We have to resist, raise our voice, and hug each other as a family.”

Safeguarding and ensuring a safe space, especially for the young actors, was a core focus for the Princess team. “The indigenous families … knew the story from beginning to end, but not the children,” Rojas said. “We had a psychologist on set and in pre-production, trying to develop this secure place for everyone so that we could actually enjoy making this movie.”

And the writer-director shared: “It was important to me for the children to feel like it was a game that they were playing, and that it was not a rigid process. So with this amazing team, what we did was play and go through it by playing.” One of the elements they used in the creative process with the kids was “a map of emotions,” Rojas said.

The Princess team also emphasized the importance of working in and with the local community with respect. “We didn’t want to extract anything,” the director emphasized. “What we wanted was real participation and reciprocity.”

The film team also included members of different indigenous communities, including some leaders within those communities who work on diversity and related issues.

The movie, set in 1997, includes an infamous real-life event that scarred Ecuador’s queer community that year. Police in the city of Cuenca back then raided a trans beauty pageant and arrested several people, who were then exposed to abuse and violence in custody.

“In terms of the visuals, we wanted to create the universe where Kallpa could travel, because this is a journey, and we wanted to always be with Kallpa on his journey,” Hidalgo explained. “And second, we wanted to be very close, so we are always with Kallpa.”

The sound also plays into Princess‘ aesthetic of closeness, allowing the audience to hear things we cannot see from a distance.

When it came time to screen Princess for the community, “I was really afraid,” Rojas shared. But he felt validated when “a 70-year-old lady stood up and said, ‘We need to talk about these stories here in our community.'”

The EV Charging Company That’s Quietly Conquering America Is Building A New U.S. Factory

Alpitronic, the company that makes the DC fast chargers used by Ionna, Walmart, Mercedes-Benz, BP Pulse, and others, is building a new factory in the United States that will boost output of its 400- and 1,000-kilowatt stalls.

The manufacturer’s new 173,315-square-foot facility, located in Fort Mill, South Carolina, will create 70 new jobs and will also act as a testing lab, repair center, logistics hub, and warehouse.

Alpitronic, which has its North American headquarters in Charlotte, North Carolina, and its global headquarters in Bolzano, Italy, said it will invest $33.94 million in the new facility, with $200,000 coming from York County’s Coordinating Council for Economic Development in the form of a Set-Aside grant to assist with the costs of building improvements.

“North America is central to our next phase of growth, and investing in our manufacturing capabilities is key to supporting that expansion,” said Patrik Herrnegger, Alpitronic Americas CEO. “Our Fort Mill facility will put U.S. production under our direct control, bringing us closer to our customers and enabling us to serve them more efficiently. We thank South Carolina and York County for their partnership as we prepare to begin production in early 2027.”

Back in 2024, the company opened its first local manufacturing facility in Wisconsin.

The new South Carolina plant will assemble HYC400 and HYC1000 stalls, Alpitronic said. The HYC400 all-in-one dispensers can deliver up to 400 kW of power to a single connected electric vehicle without needing a separate power cabinet, and are the current charger of choice for Ionna and Walmart.

The higher-powered HYC1000 system is composed of a 1,000-kW power cabinet and up to four stalls that can deliver up to 600 kW to a single EV. Each stall can have two ports, and the system can dynamically allocate power to each vehicle to make the best use of the available power.

What makes the company's dispensers different from most competitors is the use of silicon carbide components, which lower energy losses and, subsequently, the amount of money EV drivers have to pay for a top-up. According to Alpitronic, SiC modules enable 97.5% efficiency and allow for smaller, quieter, and cooler stalls.

Alpitronic’s new factory in Fort Mill, South Carolina, is scheduled to go online in February 2027.

Wilgot has raised $3 million in Seed funding to help retailers make their products easier for AI shopping assistants to find, understand, and recommend. The Stockholm startup is building what it calls Agentic Product Optimization, or APO: software that turns customer knowledge into richer product information and continually adjusts that content as shopping behavior changes.

Industrifonden led the round, which Wilgot also describes as SEK 30 million. Participants included Luminar Ventures, Foundry Ventures, Ventures Together, and Inovexus. Angel investors included Voyado founders Johan Bäckarlin and Johan Hjelte, alongside Niclas Mollin, founder of inriver and Occtoo.

The company will use the capital to develop its platform and expand its commercial reach. Anders Nygren of Foundry Ventures and Andreas Söneby of Industrifonden will join its board.

A shopper asking an assistant for a gift for someone who enjoys hiking is describing an intended use, rather than naming a product. For a retailer to appear in a useful answer, its catalog needs to explain which items fit that situation and why. A photograph and a short marketing description may leave important questions unanswered.

Wilgot starts with information retailers already hold: search queries, customer reviews, support tickets, and returns. Those sources can reveal purchase motivations, recurring questions, and mismatches between what customers expect and what a product delivers. The platform turns those findings into product pages, feeds, FAQs, and structured data.

For example, repeated questions about the fit of a shoe could point to a gap in its sizing information. Making verified guidance explicit gives both a shopper and a search system more useful evidence. The value lies in extracting a relevant fact and attaching it to the right item, rather than simply producing a longer description.

Wilgot positions APO as an active improvement layer. Visibility-monitoring tools can show whether a brand appears in an assistant’s answer, while product information systems store and distribute catalog records. Wilgot’s focus is changing the information those systems and assistants ultimately work with.

The company’s website describes three stages: discovering demand signals, generating product content, and measuring the results. Content outputs include titles, descriptions, attributes, and FAQs, generated in the relevant language. Its optimization loop follows visibility, engagement, and conversion performance across Google and AI search, then uses those signals to revise content.

Wilgot says this approach is designed for catalogs with tens of thousands of products. The funding release says its platform uses reinforcement learning to improve product information from performance feedback and supports content in more than 100 languages.

In practical terms, the feedback loop gives the system a way to learn which changes are useful after content is published. The release does not disclose its reward design or model architecture, so those details remain outside the public description. The claimed learning takes place in Wilgot’s optimization process; it does not mean a retailer can directly retrain ChatGPT or determine what an independent assistant recommends.

Structured product information already has a concrete role in search. Google’s merchant listing documentation explains how Product and Offer markup can make details such as price, availability, shipping, and returns available for shopping experiences. That illustrates the importance of making product facts explicit, although eligibility for a search feature is different from a guaranteed recommendation.

The broader issue is familiar across product data preparation for AI shopping: fluent answers still depend on accurate underlying records. A content system must preserve the distinction between a verified specification, a customer’s opinion, and an inference about suitability. Otherwise, making an item more visible can also make an unsupported claim travel further.

Wilgot names Daniel Wellington, J.Lindeberg, Myrqvist, and Signify among its customers. According to the funding announcement, net sales increased 20% across Daniel Wellington product pages optimized by Wilgot, while 7% of Myrqvist customers now arrive through ChatGPT.

These figures describe different outcomes: one concerns sales on selected product pages, while the other concerns a customer acquisition channel. The release does not provide the measurement periods or experimental design. They offer early commercial signals, rather than a single benchmark that applies across retailers.

Founded in 2024, Wilgot brings together Erik Wikander, co-founder and CEO; Dr. Mhairi Dunion, co-founder and AI Scientist; and Oleg Danylenko, co-founder and CTO. “Brands are spending too much time measuring whether AI assistants mention them, and not enough time giving AI a reason to recommend their products,” Wikander said in the announcement.

As shopping interfaces become more conversational, making that knowledge accurate, accessible, and useful becomes a commercial task in its own right.

Retrying LLM API calls: exponential backoff, jitter, Retry-After and model fallbacks

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 18 • Originally published at aliahmed.si

LLM APIs fail in predictable ways under load: 429 when you exceed a rate limit, 5xx when the provider has a problem, an "overloaded" response when capacity is short (Anthropic uses 529 for that), and timeouts. A good client retries only those, waits with exponential backoff and full jitter so thousands of clients don't retry in lockstep, honours Retry-After when the provider sends it, gives up when a retry budget runs out, and only then falls back to another model. It never retries a 400: a malformed request fails the same way every time and on every model. Below is a small, tested implementation.

A 400 from an over-long prompt is a context-window problem, which is solved by trimming the conversation, not by waiting.

Plain exponential backoff (wait 0.5 s, 1 s, 2 s, 4 s) has a flaw: when an outage hits every client at the same moment, they all retry at the same moments too, and the provider gets the traffic in synchronised waves. Full jitter picks a random wait between zero and the exponential cap, which spreads retries out. In AWS's published comparison of backoff strategies, full jitter was one of the two that performed best, and the simplest.

```js title="retry.mjs" const RETRYABLE = new Set([408, 429, 500, 502, 503, 504, 529]);

export class HttpError extends Error { constructor(status, retryAfter) { super(HTTP ${status}); this.status = status; this.retryAfter = retryAfter; // seconds, from the Retry-After header } }

const isRetryable = (error) => error instanceof HttpError ? RETRYABLE.has(error.status) : error.name === "TimeoutError" || error.code === "ECONNRESET";

/** Full-jitter exponential backoff that honours Retry-After, with a cap on total time spent waiting. / export async function withRetry(call, { attempts = 4, baseMs = 500, maxMs = 20_000, budgetMs = 30_000, sleep, random = Math.random } = {}) { let waited = 0; for (let attempt = 1; ; attempt++) { try { return await call(attempt); } catch (error) { if (attempt >= attempts || !isRetryable(error)) throw error; const backoff = random() * Math.min(maxMs, baseMs * 2 * (attempt - 1)); const delay = error.retryAfter ? Math.max(error.retryAfter * 1000, backoff) : backoff; if (waited + delay > budgetMs) throw error; waited += delay; await sleep(delay); } } }

Fallbacks change behaviour, so treat them deliberately: the fallback model should be tested with your prompts, handle your tool schemas, and fit the same context window. Log which model answered each request, and include it in cost reporting. Comparing by model name alone can hide which deployment did the work: see why LLM cost dashboards lie when deployments share a model name.

const sleeps = []; const opts = { sleep: async (ms) => void sleeps.push(ms), random: () => 1 };

// 503, 503, then success: waits of 500 and 1000 ms. let n = 0; assert.equal(await withRetry(async () => (++n < 3 ? Promise.reject(new HttpError(503)) : "ok"), opts), "ok"); assert.deepEqual(sleeps.splice(0), [500, 1000]);

// 429 with Retry-After: 7 waits 7 s, not 0.5 s. n = 0; await withRetry(async () => (++n < 2 ? Promise.reject(new HttpError(429, 7)) : "ok"), opts); assert.deepEqual(sleeps.splice(0), [7000]);

// 400 is never retried. n = 0; await assert.rejects( withRetry(async () => { n++; throw new HttpError(400); }, opts), /HTTP 400/ ); assert.equal(n, 1);

Ultraprocessed Foods Alter Food-Reward Brain Activity

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A controlled feeding trial reveals that just two weeks on a diet high in ultraprocessed foods alters brain reward responses in emerging adults ages 18 to 21, while leaving adults ages 22 to 25 unaffected. The fMRI study shows blunted activation in the developing orbitofrontal cortex, a key region for food valuation and self-control, even when diets are strictly matched for calories, sugar, fat, and nutrients.

Ultraprocessed foods, products formulated with industrial processing methods and ingredients rarely found in a home kitchen, account for more than half of the calories consumed by young adults in the United States. While extensive research links these foods to obesity, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular disease, neuroscientists are increasingly uncovering how industrial processing directly interacts with the developing human brain.

In a controlled feeding study published this month in the journal Obesity, researchers at Virginia Tech’s Fralin Biomedical Research Institute at VTC and Department of Human Nutrition, Foods, and Exercise found that emerging adults ages 18 to 21 exhibit distinct neural changes in food-reward circuitry after consuming an ultraprocessed diet for just two weeks.

Led by first author Dr. Emma Leslie alongside neuroscientist Dr. Alex DiFeliceantonio and registered dietitian Dr. Brenda Davy, the findings complement earlier results from the same trial showing that 18- to 21-year-olds consumed excess calories at a buffet meal after eating ultraprocessed foods, even when they were no longer hungry.

To separate the effects of industrial food processing from basic nutritional content, the researchers recruited 22 healthy adults ages 18 to 25 (including seven participants ages 21 or younger) for a randomized crossover feeding trial. Each participant followed two strictly controlled diets for two weeks apiece, separated by a four-week washout period:

Critically, both diets were engineered to have identical macronutrient profiles (50% carbohydrates, 35% fat, 15% protein), matching levels of fiber, added sugars, saturated fat, sodium, energy density, and overall nutritional quality, calibrated to keep each participant’s body weight stable.

Before and after each two-week dietary phase, participants underwent functional MRI (fMRI) brain scans while receiving small sips of an ultraprocessed milkshake or a tasteless control solution through a specialized delivery tube.

When the researchers analyzed the neuroimaging data, they discovered a striking developmental divide:

“Among the younger participants, consuming a diet high in ultraprocessed foods for just two weeks was associated with decreased brain response to a rewarding food — a milkshake — in the orbitofrontal cortex,” explained Leslie, who conducted the work as a doctoral student at Virginia Tech and is now a postdoctoral associate at the University of Michigan.

Located just behind the eyes, the orbitofrontal cortex is one of the final regions of the human brain to finish maturing, continuing to remodel into the mid- to late 20s. It plays a central role in evaluating food rewards, regulating cravings, and exerting executive self-control to stop eating.

Because the two diets were matched across nutritional metrics, the findings point toward non-nutritive properties of ultraprocessed foods, such as chemical additives, emulsifiers, texture alterations, or compounds generated during industrial processing, as potential drivers of altered brain signaling.

“This study is important because you have this change in an area of the brain known to govern self-control and potentially put a stop to eating,” said DiFeliceantonio, associate professor at the Fralin Biomedical Research Institute and interim co-director of its Center for Health Behaviors Research. “This is giving us insight into how ultraprocessed foods are affecting the bodies and brains of younger people, who might be especially vulnerable to their effects. And that’s important to know for things like dietary guidelines, doctor recommendations, and policy decisions.”

While the researchers note that the sample size was small and the age-based findings were exploratory, and did not directly correlate individual fMRI shifts with buffet calorie intake, the results highlight adolescence and early adulthood as critical windows of neurodevelopmental sensitivity.

Next, DiFeliceantonio and Davy plan to investigate whether adolescents under 18, who consume the highest proportion of ultraprocessed foods of any U.S. demographic, show even stronger neural sensitivity, and whether brain responses recover once individuals return to whole-food diets.

How do ultraprocessed foods affect the brains of young adults?

A Virginia Tech study found that after eating a diet high in ultraprocessed foods for just two weeks, young adults ages 18 to 21 showed decreased brain activity in the orbitofrontal cortex, a region involved in food reward, valuation, and self-control, when tasting a milkshake. Adults ages 22 to 25 did not show these brain changes.

Was the brain effect simply caused by extra sugar, fat, or calories?

No. Both the ultraprocessed diet and the unprocessed comparison diet contained the exact same amounts of calories, carbohydrates, fat, protein, added sugars, saturated fat, fiber, and sodium. This suggests that industrial food processing or additives themselves, rather than basic nutrients, drove the brain changes.

Why are 18- to 21-year-olds more sensitive to ultraprocessed foods than slightly older adults?

The orbitofrontal cortex is one of the last brain regions to finish developing, continuing to mature into the mid- to late 20s. Because this self-control and reward-evaluation circuit is still actively wiring in 18- to 21-year-olds, it appears more sensitive to dietary shifts.

We investigated if a high-UPF diet alters brain response to milkshake compared with a diet free from UPF in emerging adults, who are in a critical brain development period and typically consume high amounts of UPF.

An acute UPF dietary intervention may only alter brain response in late adolescents. Further work is needed to determine potential vulnerability of late adolescents to changes in dietary UPF on brain response to rewards.

Those are capability boundaries, not settings to paper over.

A unique key prevents two uploads from destroying each other's bytes. It does not decide which upload wins if a user taps Save twice. Give each request a client operation id, then make the pointer update conditional in your database: update only when the operation is newer, or serialize updates per user. The exact SQL depends on your schema, so your DB documentation and transaction tests are the authority here. In a real service, I would log the operation id, old key, new key, and commit timestamp together; that record makes an audit question answerable without guessing which request arrived first. Keep it immutable.

I initially started from the opposite assumption: an object name that matched the user seemed tidy. Then the race became obvious. Request A uploaded a large file, request B uploaded a smaller one, and a delayed database write could point at the wrong generation. Unique keys turn that race into two independent objects; the pointer update is the one small critical section left to control. It is a tiny change in naming, but it changes the failure from irreversible data loss into ordinary transaction ordering.

Deletion should be a queue consumer. Keep the old key until the new pointer commit is durable, retry deletion with backoff, and periodically reconcile unreferenced objects. Stakeholder Alignment Plan: A Practical Decision Framework

A stakeholder alignment plan decision framework is a structured set of rules that defines who makes which calls, what trade-offs govern those choices, and how disagreements get resolved without stalling execution. Having clear decision rules stops projects from getting trapped in endless committee loops or derailed by last-minute executive vetoes. Most teams do not fail because they lack smart people; they fail because nobody knows who actually owns the green light. According to McKinsey decision-making research, 37% of a manager’s working time goes toward making decisions, yet 61% say most of that time is used ineffectively—and the quality of the decision process matters six times more than pure data analysis. Meanwhile, PMI’s 2026 Pulse of the Profession confirms that 34% of complex projects suffer from stakeholder decision delays, rising to 47% across fast-growing markets like South Asia (The Week coverage of PMI 2026 report). Whether you are leading a cross-functional enterprise transformation or coordinating multi-partner launches inside ecosystems like Heloix, replacing vague consensus with a clear decision framework keeps teams moving at full speed.

When teams try to get every stakeholder to agree on every detail, two things happen: decisions slow to a crawl, and bold ideas get watered down into safe compromises. As PM World Journal’s July 2026 analysis on misaligned stakeholders notes, an initiative can receive formal sign-off from every department head and still stall operationally because contributors hold conflicting assumptions about priorities and trade-offs. True alignment does not mean everyone gets their way. It means everyone understands how choices get made, who holds the tie-breaking vote, and why a specific trade-off was chosen.

Core Stakeholder Alignment Plan Decision Criteria

Before debating individual features or budgets, anchor your team around four shared stakeholder alignment plan decision criteria so every choice gets judged against the same yardstick: • Strategic Value Fit: Does this option directly move our primary North Star metric, or is it a nice-to-have distraction? As Gartner’s August 2026 research on stakeholder outcome models highlights, leaders across marketing, IT, and finance often carry conflicting mental models of value unless those criteria are written down upfront. • Reversibility (One-Way vs. Two-Way Doors): Can we undo this choice cheaply in two weeks if it fails, or does it lock in permanent architecture, legal risk, or capital spend? • Cross-Team Blast Radius: How many downstream teams, vendors, or compliance reviewers must adjust their workflows if we pick this path? • Time-to-Impact Cost: What happens to revenue, customer trust, or launch windows if we delay this decision by another ten business days?

A 4-Tier Stakeholder Alignment Plan Evaluation Framework

Instead of treating every choice like a board-level crisis, route decisions through a tiered stakeholder alignment plan evaluation framework. Matching the approval path to the real risk level eliminates unnecessary bottlenecks.

Decision Tier Reversibility & Risk Level Single Decider (Owner) Required Input & Turnaround SLA Tier 1: Local Execution Easily reversible; affects only one squad’s daily workflow. Functional Team Lead or Product Manager Async FYI note in shared workspace; zero approval wait time. Tier 2: Cross-Team Trade-Off Moderately reversible; shifts sprint scope or handoffs across 2+ teams. Program Lead or Initiative Driver Input from affected leads within 24 hours; Driver breaks ties. Stakeholder Alignment Plan Examples in Action

Seeing how teams apply a structured decision framework turns abstract governance into everyday speed. Look at two practical stakeholder alignment plan examples: • Multi-partner venture rollout: A startup founder coordinating internal builders, an external design studio, and investors on Heloix noticed minor UI tweaks were getting escalated to investor calls, delaying releases by weeks. By introducing a 4-tier decision framework, Tier 1 and Tier 2 design choices stayed with the product lead, reserving advisor input strictly for Tier 4 roadmap shifts. • Enterprise AI workflow launch: A mid-market operations team rolling out automated customer support tools defined clear decision criteria around data privacy and cost-per-ticket upfront. Legal pre-approved guardrails at Tier 3 during sprint zero, allowing engineering to ship weekly model updates under Tier 1 rules without waiting for repeated compliance sign-offs.

What is the difference between a RACI chart and a decision framework?

A RACI chart maps who executes daily tasks and who needs status updates, which often blurs who actually holds tie-breaking authority. A decision framework focuses strictly on how choices are evaluated, who owns the final call at each risk tier, and how fast approvals must happen.

How do you handle a stakeholder who disagrees with a final decision?

Follow the "disagree and commit" rule: give every consulted stakeholder a clear window to share data and concerns before the SLA clock runs out, log their perspective in your decision record, and require full operational backing once the named decider makes the call.

The Kia Besta EV hit the roads in 1986 as the brand’s first fully electric vehicle. 40 years later, Kia continues to “lead the pack” with a full lineup of EVs ranging from entry-level cars and SUVs to three-row models, and just about everything in between.

Kia wanted to design a new vehicle for the 1986 Asian Games, which were held in Seoul, South Korea, one that did not emit any toxic fumes in front of runners.

So, it created the Besta EV. The Besta van was initially developed 40 years ago by Kia’s Central Research Institute as the successor to the popular Bongo van, which is still sold in Korea and other markets today.

Unlike the Bongo, it was developed using Kia’s in-house tech rather than sourced from another automaker and rebadged as its own.

Kia swapped the 2.0L four-cylinder diesel engine for a 35 kWh battery and electric motor, which provided a range of more than 26 miles (42 km) on a single charge.

While the Besta EV was never actually sold to the public, it laid the groundwork and offered an early glimpse at Kia’s shift to electrification.

The first mass-produced Korean EV certified for commercial sale was the Kia Pride EV in 1993. In 2011, the Ray EV became Kia’s first series-production electric car.

Kia expanded its electrification ambitions to Europe and the UK in 2014 with the first-generation Soul EV, the beloved boxy, funky crossover that was discontinued after the 2025 model year.

The Korean automaker has come a long way since the Besta EV, with a full lineup of electric vehicles ranging from compact cars to mid-size and three-row SUVs, vans, and just about everything in between.

Kia now offers an electric option for nearly everyone with seven-passenger EVs on sale in the UK and Europe, including the EV2, EV3, EV4, EV5, EV6, EV9, and PV5 Passenger van.

With its first software-defined vehicle (SDV) and a new B-segment electric hatchback set to launch by the end of 2027, Kia has no plans to slow down. On top of this, Kia will expand its PBV lineup with the PV7 next year, a larger sibling to the PV5.

Four decades after the Besta EV arrived, Kia said it continues to “lead the pack today” with one of the most diverse all-electric EV lineups of any brand.

Through October, Kia has sold over 135,000 EVs in the UK, with fully electric vehicles now accounting for over 37% of total sales.

Thanks to its new EV lineup, Kia became “Europe’s fastest-growing top 10 passenger-car brand.” According to the European Automobile Manufacturers’ Association, Kia registered 377,078 vehicles between January and August 2026, up 9% compared to the same period last year.

Sjoerd Knipping, Chief Operating Officer of Kia Europe, said the growth is thanks to having “one of the broadest and most advanced electrified line-ups in Europe.”

In the US, Kia launched the compact EV3 (check out our review of it) this year, starting at just $29,890. It joins the EV6, EV9, and Niro EV in Kia’s US electric vehicle lineup.

Tesla toughens FSD driver monitoring in Europe while loosening it in the US

Tesla is rolling out stricter driver monitoring for its “Full Self-Driving” system in Europe, with earlier warnings, a full strike for drivers who look away during a manoeuvre, and a hands-on-wheel requirement above 130 km/h (81 mph).

In the US, Tesla has spent the last year going the other way. Its CEO told owners they can text and drive on FSD, and recent US builds were tuned to nag drivers less.

On November 16, the Dutch vehicle authority RDW announced that it signed off on a new update to Tesla Assisted Driving, the name Tesla now uses for FSD (Supervised) in Europe. It covers HW4 vehicles and goes out over the air.

According to the RDW, drivers now get attention warnings earlier. If a driver isn’t paying attention while the system performs a manoeuvre, they get a “full strike.” The regulator says the update also adds measures to prevent misuse of the system, but it didn’t spell out what they are.

There’s a speed component too. Above 130 km/h (where legal), the driver has to keep their hands on the wheel. And setting a maximum speed above what the system displays now requires the driver to confirm it by scrolling the wheel up, with the speed limit sign pulsing red when the car goes over.

The RDW says it tested the update on its own track and on public roads and found no adverse or unintended effects. Ten other countries have adopted the Dutch approval so far, with Finland now on the list, while EU-wide approval is still waiting on a vote at the TCMV, the committee of member states.

This comes two weeks after Tesla cut the system’s speed margin from 50% to 10% over the limit in Europe and dropped the “Full Self-Driving” name there. Germany had made both a condition of supporting EU approval.

Now look at how Tesla handles the same software at home.

At Tesla’s shareholder meeting last November, Musk complained that the car was “a little strict about keeping your eyes on the road” and said Tesla would “allow you to text and drive essentially” within a month or two. A month later, an owner asked whether FSD v14.2.1 allowed texting. Musk’s answer: “Depending on context of surrounding traffic, yes.”

Texting while driving is illegal in 49 states. FSD is still a Level 2 system, and Tesla’s own fine print says the driver is responsible at all times.

In May, Musk confirmed that FSD v14.3.3 was built to nag drivers less. Tesla did add better eye-gaze tracking and eyewear handling in a June build, to be fair. But the pitch to owners hasn’t changed: fewer nags is sold as a feature.

And US drivers have been allowed to go hands-free on FSD at any speed since Tesla moved to cabin-camera monitoring in 2024. There’s no 130 km/h rule here.

NHTSA noticed. In its engineering analysis of FSD crashes in low-visibility conditions, the agency asked Tesla to justify a list of its marketing claims, and it specifically cited Musk’s texting post. In September, Rep. Raja Krishnamoorthi asked NHTSA to look into Tesla’s driver monitoring after dozens of videos appeared to show drivers asleep on Autopilot and FSD.

The results are on the road. Last month, a driver told the CHP he was asleep on FSD when his Tesla drove through a freeway closure and hit a patrol car, seriously injuring two officers. Owners are also filming themselves defeating Tesla’s driver monitoring and posting it for clout.

Both markets get the same cabin camera and the same HW4 computer.

But in Europe, a type-approval authority tests every update before it ships and can refuse it. In the US, nobody pre-approves driver-assist software. Tesla self-certifies, and NHTSA only steps in after complaints and crashes pile up, which is how you end up with a CEO publicly telling owners to text while the regulator is still writing letters about it.

This update proves Tesla can build stricter driver monitoring whenever a regulator demands it. The code exists. It’s running on HW4 cars in 11 European countries right now.

That makes the US approach hard to defend. Tesla’s argument for relaxing supervision is that FSD is now good enough. But if that were true, it would be showing the RDW data proving looser monitoring is safe, not adding earlier warnings and harsher strikes to get approved. It’s doing the opposite in Europe because it has to, and the opposite of that in the US because it can.

We’ve been saying it for a while: FSD gets more dangerous as it gets better, because people trust it more and watch the road less. The answer is monitoring that tightens as the system improves. Europe is forcing exactly that. In the US, Tesla is loosening it, encouraging illegal phone use from the top, and leaving drivers with 100% of the liability when it goes wrong.

So here’s the question for Tesla. If European owners are getting earlier warnings and instant strikes, why not American owners on the exact same software? Or is NHTSA going to have to force it?

With Kyle Shanahan and Brian Flores, Vikings-49ers will be a matchup for the modern NFL

In the long-running anime One Piece, there's an incident at the World Government headquarters, Marineford, known as "The War of the Best." The strongest pirates in the world of One Piece fought the strongest Marines the World Government had to offer, and it ultimately became the most important arc in the One Piece world, shaping the direction of the story that's still being told today.

In the NFL, it feels like we have a game every year that is similar to War of the Best -- two titans facing off against each other, where the outcome will shape the next wave of the NFL on both sides of the ball:

Kyle Shanahan vs. the Legion of Boom in 2016.

Sean McVay vs. Vic Fangio, then Bill Belichick, in that year's Super Bowl.

Andy Reid vs. Lou Anarumo in the second half of the 2022 AFC Championship.

Sean McVay vs. Mike Macdonald last season.

This year, the new NFL meta will be defined Sunday night in Mexico City, where the San Francisco 49ers and Shanahan will face the Minnesota Vikings and defensive coordinator Brian Flores in one of the most anticipated matchups of the year. Both the Niners' offense and Vikings' defense are resetting what their respective sides can and should do, and seeing which side blinks first could be a blueprint for how offenses and defenses attack each other in the near future.

If we recall a story I wrote earlier this season, NFL teams are blitzing at an astronomical rate. Over ten weeks, defenses league-wide have a 32.3% blitz rate, the second-highest since 2010 and one of only four seasons with a blitz rate over 30% (2011, 2013, and 2010). The number jumps even higher when teams go under center to an astounding 50.5%, the highest since 2010 and the only season TruMedia has recorded in that time period with a blitz rate over 50% against under-center offenses. When these defenses blitz, they have a 54.9% defensive success rate overall, and that number drops to 50.1% when offenses are under center.

At the forefront of this are Flores and the Minnesota Vikings, who are redefining what it means to be aggressive. The Vikings currently sit with an absurd 72.6% blitz rate, by far the highest for any team through 10 weeks since 2010. In fact, you have to go all the way back to the 2006 Miami Dolphins to find a team that went higher than that (what was Nick Saban cooking in 2006?). The way that Minnesota can dictate the flow of not only a play, but the entire game through their pressure looks that completely stymie opposing offenses.

The Vikings are essentially playing rock-paper-scissors on every play, daring you to throw anything other than rock. One way they can dictate the flow is by forcing teams to leave extra pass protectors in to help against pressure looks.

Imagine you're Packers quarterback Jordan Love. You come to the line of scrimmage, and this is what you see:

The alarm bells are ringing. This is the most obvious Cover 0 all-out blitz look, eight guys up at the line of scrimmage. The common answer for the Packers and Love has been to keep the RB and TE in the protection, calling it max protection. This is the Packers throwing rock, hoping to catch the Vikings in a Cover 0 look and hit them for an explosive.

The Vikings actually spin everyone out of this except the four defensive linemen and run Cover 3, frazzling Jordan Love before he ends up throwing it 10 yards short of the first-down marker on third down. The Vikings got the Packers to throw rock, then they throw paper.

This ability to force offenses to keep seven people in to protect the QB is unprecedented at the NFL level. According to Sumer Sports, the 2026 Vikings are facing dropbacks where all five eligible receivers are out to catch a pass on 35.7% of their dropbacks, the lowest in the NFL by a significant margin. They're the only team in the NFL below 50% in that metric, with the New York Giants in second place…at 58.5%! Flores and the defense force offenses to play a completely different sport when they play Minnesota, using pressure to dictate the terms of the passing game. They force you to waste backs and tight ends in pass protection, then force you to play with a hand behind your back.

If you want to get four out in the passing concept, good luck, because that doesn't work either.

You're probably wondering, "Yes, the Vikings defense is amazing, but what does that have to do with the 49ers and how this will decide the state of NFL schematics?" The answer lies in the protection plans. The Vikings see the lowest amount of five-out in the passing concept in the NFL. Well, guess which team leads the NFL in percentage of dropbacks with all five eligible receivers out in the passing concept? That's right, Kyle Shanahan and the San Francisco 49ers.

I'd describe the Niners' offense as "malleable." They can use multiple players in multiple ways, keeping their best players on the field while putting linebackers in a blender. They do that by getting all five guys out in the passing concept. If we include chip blocks, the Niners get all five eligibles out at an 80.1% clip, the highest in the NFL (not including chips, that number drops to 60.8%, second in the NFL behind the Pittsburgh Steelers). Against pressure looks where linebackers are mugged, or showing that they're blitzing, the 49ers like to get everyone into the concept and spread teams out. The Niners (and Bengals as well) are zagging when everyone else is zigging, spreading out and getting five eligibles into the concept against the blitz.

With simulated pressures, there's always a law of equivalent exchange: for every guy you add, a guy has to drop. For most defenses to stay structurally intact while showing these pressures in two-high shells, someone has to drop from the defensive line. Shanahan knows this, and knows he has the personnel to live in five-out looks (we'll get to that later). If we're going to the rock, paper, scissors comparison again, the Niners are choosing to throw paper instead of rock, because if they can find the weakest point in the pressure, they'll hammer it.

This Supreme Court "Deserves Our Celebration, Not Our Condemnation"

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Travel  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

Prof. William Baude on the Supreme Court and the false promise of court reform.

Professor William Baude has an op-ed in the New York Times, "This Is What an Independent, Competent Supreme Court Looks Like," defending the Supreme Court against its critics, left and right, and suggesting that many calls for court reform are misguided.

Noting that the Democratic electoral sweep has bolstered calls for reforming, and perhaps expanding, the Court to alter its course, Baude writes:

The left, understandably, strongly opposes the court's recent rulings on abortion, the Voting Rights Act, the unitary executive and so on. These decisions are a big deal. But court reformers still suffer from a failure of perspective. If Chief Justice John Roberts could write an opinion essay, I would imagine him saying: "What? Ruling against the administration's misuse of the National Guard, sweeping emergency tariffs and attack on birthright citizenship, plus preserving the independence of the Federal Reserve and the integrity of the midterm elections, wasn't enough for you?"

The court has ruled against the administration in high-stakes cases on those issues all in the past year. And it has done so even though six of the justices were appointed by members of the president's party — three by President Trump himself. . . .

Under the circumstances of the second Trump term, the Supreme Court is doing amazingly well. If anything, it deserves our celebration, not our condemnation.

Indeed, the Court's conservatives, on the whole, have shown themselves more willing to constrain Trump's excesses than were the Court's liberals when faced with executive branch overreach by Presidents Biden or Obama.

Baude notes that the Court's willingness to reject Trump Administration priorities has caused some on the Right to question the conservative legal movement's commitment to originalism, and cautions that "what goes around comes around, and principles will be worth more in the long run." Of course, some conservatives criticisms of the Court are for its failure to adhere to the principles some justices espouse, not that those principles are getting in the way of making America great again.

On the question of judicial reform, Baude strikes a cautionary note, particularly if court reform is nothing more than an effort to reverse the Court's current trajectory.

In the abstract, there is nothing wrong with bipartisan judicial reform. The Supreme Court should not be preserved in amber. But partisan judicial reform — reform motivated by frustration with specific judicial decisions and the hope that the reformed court will rule differently — is a dangerous game. Because partisan reform is a tactic equally available to both sides, it accomplishes little in the long run except to accelerate the court's transformation into a political superweapon. Such a tactic might be a short-run necessity in extreme circumstances, but we are far from those extremes.

He also--quite correctly--calls out the obsession with judicial ethics as "superficial." He could add that such concerns are nearly always one-sided and selective. While it may be appropriate to further limit the justices' outside income or ability to accept gifts, we should not pretend that any such reforms would have any effect on the Court's decisions.

The justices' views on big issues are well formed. The justices themselves aren't for sale; book income reflects their increasing celebrity, not any specific quid pro quo. To be sure, it is quite possible that travel and gifts and book sales subtly reinforce the justices' incentives to play to their respective fan bases. But this kind of corruption is quite indirect, and at the same time comes from so much more than money. So ethics reform is unlikely to change much about what the court actually does.

In the end, Baude thinks the best argument for court reform is as a placebo: To convince the public that something has been done so that attention can turn to more pressing issues. As he concludes this point: "If pointless reforms cause many Americans to believe that the court is better — more independent, less corrupt and so on — that would be great for the country."

I largely agree with Baude's piece, though I may be more open to certain sorts of reforms than he reveals in this piece. Reforms that represent genuine political engagement with the work of the courts, such as measures to alter or channel jurisdiction could be quite beneficial. Measures to reduce backlogs, limit forum shopping, constrain unwarranted or overly expansive equitable relief, and expand the Court's mandatory jurisdiction are all worth considering. They also represent the sort of meaningful legislation that Congress should engage in--legislation that requires Congress to take seriously its constitutional responsibilities. (In this regard, I view the willingness to engage with jurisdictional questions in the pending infrastructure reform bill to be a welcome sign, even if I might not endorse every jurisdictional measure the bill contains.)

As Baude ends his essay, courts are not above criticism, and this includes the Supreme Court. It is nonetheless is important to distinguish between criticisms of how the Court operates and performs its job from substantive or ideological disagreement with the decisions the Court reaches.

The "shameful" measles data that lost the US its elimination status

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Measles became endemic in the US months ago—and it’s only accelerating

International health authorities formally announced on Tuesday that the US has lost its measles elimination status, a hard-fought public health triumph won in 2000 after decades-long vaccination efforts. The loss had been widely expected by public health experts for months, and the decision was leaked by the press last week. Still, having the designation officially stripped stings.

After the announcement Tuesday, public health experts, doctors, and political leaders decried the dramatic backslide for the US, calling it “shameful” and a “national embarrassment.” News articles point to the staggering vertical rise of measles cases this year, currently over 5,400 cases, more than any other year since 1991. An explosive measles outbreak in Pennsylvania accounts for over 1,500 of those cases. There have been 10 confirmed deaths across three states, several deaths just in the last week.

But the measles data that caused the US to lose its status—the bits of it that are public at least—paint a darker picture of where the US is in its rematch with measles, the most contagious virus known on Earth. For one thing, almost none of the data from this year—the cases, the explosive Pennsylvania outbreak, the deaths—factored in to the loss. It was lost earlier. What’s happening now is a runaway epidemic.

The US measles elimination status is a designation granted by the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), which is a regional division of the World Health Organization. Last week a committee of experts assembled by the PAHO, called the RVC, met to review measles data for countries across the region, including the US. (RVC is short for the Measles, Rubella and Congenital Rubella Syndrome Elimination Regional Monitoring and Re-Verification Commission.)

For their review of the US data, the RVC heard from an independent committee of experts from the US, called the NSC, which presented measles data collected from January 2025 onward. (The NSC is short for the US National Sustainability Committee for the Elimination of Measles, Rubella, and Congenital Rubella Syndrome.)

According to the PAHO, an elimination status is lost when epidemiological and laboratory data shows that a single viral lineage has spread continuously for at least 12 months within a country. At this point, the virus is considered to be reestablished or endemic. So, to withdraw the US elimination status, the RVC needed to see evidence that a single introduction of a measles virus led to a 12-month-long chain of cases, which requires a lot of data.

The full set of data the NSC presented to the RVC is private for now. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention needs to review and approve such data for public release, which can be a lengthy process that has not yet occurred.

The Department of Health and Human Services, which oversees the CDC, did not respond to a question from Ars Technica regarding when the data may be released.

But Ars Technica spoke with the chair of the NSC, Noel Brewer, who tried to talk through the pieces of data in the NSC report to the PAHO that he could discuss—these are pieces that have already been made public at some point by others, such as by state health officials or researchers. Brewer said the NSC presented the PAHO with genetic data spanning January 2025 to January 2026, and also epidemiological data that extended to June 2026.

In a press briefing Tuesday, officials for the PAHO made clear that only some of the data presented by NSC factored into its assessment of the US elimination status. The RVC only looked at the reporting period between January 2025 and January 2026, which doesn’t include the massive outbreak ongoing in Pennsylvania, which began in April. “The number of cases in Pennsylvania did not actually contribute to the decision that was made by the Reverification Commission,” Rhonda Sealey-Thomas, PAHO’s assistant director, explicitly told reporters Tuesday. For 2025, the US reported 2,289 cases nationally.

Still, there was no ambiguity about the US losing its status; “multiple lines of data show clearly that the US has sustained transmission of measles,” Brewer told Ars.

Brewer, who is also the Gillings Distinguished Professor in Public Health at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, confirmed that the beginning of the end of US elimination was January 20, 2025, in Texas—that date was included in the letter PAHO sent to the US informing it of the loss of elimination status. That Texas outbreak was declared over on August 18, 2025, after 762 cases were confirmed in the state. But the virus carried on elsewhere and is still simmering.

“Since January 20, 2025, the US, Canada, and Mexico have been passing measles cases back and forth across borders,” Brewer said, and the national data shows multiple, “genetically distinct” measles introductions into the US. “However, there is one genomic lineage that predominates across the cases and ties many of them, the majority of them, together,” he said.

For people closely tracking the US measles status, a key question has been whether the big outbreaks that followed the initial one in Texas were clearly linked. If they were, it would be immediately obvious that the US had lost the elimination status; but if not, and the outbreaks were driven by different virus introductions, the US could have kept its measles-free status based on the technicality of how the status is defined.

Data that might hold the answer to this question has been elusive. The measles strain in Texas, D8-9171, appeared similar to the viruses seen subsequently in big outbreaks in Arizona and Utah. But, the strain was also similar to those from Mexico and Canada. Generally, there can be relatively small genetic changes among measles viruses, and genetic data alone is not used by PAHO to evaluate elimination. In June of this year, ProPublica tried to get at the answer itself, conducting its own genetic analysis of measles strains in the US. It concluded that “the measles virus still spreading in Utah as of this May is very closely related to the one that sickened Texans over a year ago.” But the outlet acknowledged this wasn’t a “smoking gun.”

To date, the question lingers. Brewer cannot yet explicitly disclose what the NSC reported to the PAHO about possible links between the Texas outbreak and any of the subsequent large outbreaks in 2025. That includes the one in nearby Utah or one in South Carolina, which began in October 2025 and ultimately tallied 997 cases by the time it was declared over in April, 2026.

For its part, the PAHO did not respond to multiple requests for comment from Ars Technica on linkages between the outbreaks.

But, in a twist, Brewer noted that the answer to the question might be moot; the lineage that started in Texas on January 20 might not be the only one that has been sustained and could have lost the country its status.  There are “one or two that have gone on for more than 12 months,” he said.

With the designation now formally lost, health and medical experts are coming to grips with the reality that measles is once again endemic in the US. Details of how the US should move forward are up for debate; the US is now required to submit a “corrective action plan” to the PAHO, outlining how the country intends to regain its elimination status. But, the fundamental solution is obvious and straightforward: The US needs to increase vaccination rates.

While the virus has been raging in religious communities with low vaccination rates, the entire US has seen slipping vaccination coverage. That makes it easier for the virus to gain a foothold virtually anywhere.

A protective vaccination rate is considered to be 95 percent; according to the latest data, only 92.4 percent of kindergartners were vaccinated with the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine in the 2025–2026 school year. Only 10 states have MMR vaccination rates at or above the 95 percent threshold. Meanwhile, non-medical exemptions from school vaccination requirements have never been higher, at 4 percent. Currently, 24 states have exemption rates at or above 5 percent, meaning that even if they are able to vaccinate every single non-exempt child, they would still not meet the 95 percent target. And this is just the data for young children. The vaccination coverage for adults is another concern, and the data there is scarce. In Pennsylvania’s massive outbreak, 1,055 of the 1,561 cases this year have been in adults ages 18 and older (less than 1 percent of the cases were fully vaccinated).

While some health officials have noted optimistically that 92.4 percent in children is just a few percentage points from the target, the reality of bringing up vaccination rates is more onerous.

PAHO Director Jarbas Barbosa stressed the point in the press briefing Tuesday: It’s “not the national average or the average in the state or even the average in a big city, you need to guarantee that in every community, village, you have this vaccination coverage up to 95 percent. This is very important,” Barbosa said.

The point has been demonstrated in the US. In 2019, the US came very close to losing its measles elimination status. Unlike now—when the virus is on a nationwide rampage, with 47 states and jurisdictions reporting cases—in 2019, the US nearly lost its status from a single, large outbreak that spread in New York. Though it didn’t explode on a national level, it took 11 months to stamp out in those under-vaccinated communities. In that year, the US reported 1,274 measles cases total.

The situation in the US has only gotten worse, and the outlook is bleak, Brewer warns. “The measles outbreaks in Canada and Mexico are winding down,” he noted. “However, in the US, our measles problem continues to accelerate. There is no end in sight.”
