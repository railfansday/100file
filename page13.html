West Ham's London Stadium could undergo a major change after £19m revelation

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

West Ham United's stadium operators have a proposal being put to Newham councillors to try and alleviate the losses that are being made by the club's occupancy of The London Stadium.

Relegation from the Premier League always has financial ramifications, but in the case of West Ham United following their fall from the top-flight iat the end of 2025-26, those costs haven't been limited to the club themselves.

The club moved into their new home in 2016, three years after a deal was signed to get them there on what seemed to be highly favourable terms. With a vastly increased capacity compared to their former home, The Boleyn Ground, it was hoped that the move might move the club towards being regular challengers for Champions League football.

With a sudden reduction in revenue due to a loss of television money, West Ham United's relegation from the Premier League at the end of the 2025-26 season came at a great cost to the club. But the unique circumstances that led to the stadium being available for their use have resulted in The London Stadium becoming expensive for all taxpaying Londoners.

It was reported in July that relegation from the Premier League would add to stadium costs the public, which total £19 million-a-year, with Lynn Garner, the chair of London Stadium LLP, who manage the site, saying: "We are going to finalise the accounts at around a £19m operating loss. This is not unusual. It's been in this ballpark for some time."

London Stadium LLP are taking some quite serious action in order to stem this financial drain. The body have put forward a proposal to Newham Borough Council to drastically increase the number of non-sporting events that are held at the stadium, which is due to be voted upon by local councillors.

They argue that current restrictions make the venue uncompetitive against Wembley Stadium and Tottenham Hotspur Stadium, and their proposals include raising its maximum capacity from 80,000 to 85,000 for eight events a year, raising the noise cap to 75 decibels for all concerts, and reducing permitted sporting events from 87 to 70 annually. The maximum number of non-sporting events per year would increase from 10 to 24.

The current agreement has been roundly criticised by many parties, including the current Mayor of London, Sadiq Khan, who has blamed his predecessor Boris Johnson for not having negotiated a better deal for Londoners. Newham Council's planning officers have recommended approving the application, stating it would boost the tourist economy in Stratford and the wider borough.

One of the more common themes of resentment among West Ham United supporters over the last decade has been that The London Stadium doesn't feel like home. Sight-lines from the backs of the stands are atrocious, while fans have complained about how difficult it is to create an atmosphere there.

With the departure of David Sullivan and Karren Brady earlier this year, protests against the owners of the club have ended, and it was revealed in the summer that Daniel Kretinsky, who is in sole charge of the club on a day-to-day basis now, has big plans to improve the atmosphere inside the stadium on match-days, including the removal of its running track and moving seats closer to the pitch.

But they're not the only people who are unhappy with their occupancy of the stadium. There has long been unhappiness with the terms which allowed them to move in, on a 99-year lease with no break clause for City Hall. It was reported at the end of the 2025-26 season that relegation from the Premier League would cost London taxpayers an additional £2.5 million, and the team's early-season form on the pitch has offered little indication that a return to the top-flight is going to be straightforward.

There will be a certain proportion of West Ham fans for whom The London Stadium will never quite feel like 'home.' The Boleyn Ground was cramped, and by the time they came to leave in 2016, it was outdated too. But the financial losses incurred as a result of their move to The London Stadium haven't helped the club's reputation, and it will certainly help the club if those losses can finally be stemmed and the site made profitable with its current tenants in situ.

Leaving The London Stadium would come at a huge financial cost to the club. Building a new stadium of the same size as the one they currently occupy would cost them hundreds of millions of pounds. It would, therefore, make the most sense for every aspect of The London Stadium as profitable as possible. For the stadium managers, that means expanding what they're able to do there on days when West Ham aren't playing. For West Ham United themselves, meanwhile, this would mean securing that Premier League return as quickly as possible.

Sam, who knows better than anyone how mortified Caroline must be, seems troubled by the notion. But it more or less appears to be the impulse driving Netflix’s The Altruists. The eight-hour series chronicles the rise and fall of FTX and Alameda through the tumultuous relationship between its leaders — and in doing so, deepens the saga’s emotional relatability while also diminishing its stakes.

For most of the season, creator Graham Moore (The Imitation Game) cuts between two timelines. In the halcyon days of 2016, captured by director James Ponsoldt in the warm bright tones and slightly grainy texture of an indie rom-com, Caroline meets Sam on her first day at a New York hedge fund. The two recognize each other almost immediately as kindred spirits, matched in their intelligence (high), their interests (math and winning) and their taste in jokes (charmingly dorky).

In no time, they’re falling into bed, though not quite into a relationship — Caroline prefers to keep it caszh, reasoning that non-monogamy is more “mathematically optimal” for getting off. Instead, they tie their fates together via their careers. Sam first founds Alameda and then FTX, and in short order brings Caroline on board as an exec.

The businesses are rooted in the couple’s shared principle of effective altruism. In theory, EA is a rationalist moral framework that prioritizes doing the greatest good. In practice, like most guiding philosophies, it’s easily twisted into whatever a self-interested devotee wants it to be.

Betraying a colleague, conning oblivious business partners, stealing customer money — why, any of it might be justifiable, noble even, so long as it’s done with the intention of making more money to definitely for sure donate to some worthy cause someday. And so it is that in the show’s other, grayer timeline, it’s 2022 and Sam and Caroline find themselves scrambling to cover up all the grave financial misdeeds they’ve committed in service of some vague higher purpose.

How one timeline could lead into the other is The Altruists’ central concern, though the answer isn’t really much mystery (and not only because it was all headline news a scant four years ago). The differences between the Caroline and Sam we meet in 2016 and the ones we’ll part with in 2022 are ones of degree, not kind. These two have always been aggressive about taking risks, arrogant about their ability to outplay them, skeptical of the rules and gifted in justifying their own bullshit to themselves as perfectly rational, and therefore objectively correct, decisions.

Add in the fact that they tend to augment each other’s worst impulses — backing each other’s wildest moves, one-upping each other for spite or for fun, treating every office disagreement like a proxy for the conversations about commitment or exclusivity they’re supposedly too evolved to need — and FTX/Alameda’s meltdown starts to look almost inevitable. As ex-colleague and ex-friend Trabucco (a very good Alex Lawther) puts it, “Sam was a block of sodium. Caroline was a rushing river.” Put those ingredients together, the MIT graduate explains, and you’ve got an instant fireball.

If the journey contains few surprises, however, it does offer some worthy features. Karan Soni brings much-needed sweetness as FTX engineer Nishad, whose cute will-they-won’t-they with a coworker (Naomi Okada) becomes collateral damage in the implosion. Paul Reiser and Robin Weigert are fun in supporting roles as Sam’s parents, though their appearances are so brief that another character has to explain that while they love their son, they don’t like him. On the other end of the lovability spectrum, Terry Chen drinks up the part of possibly mob-affiliated rival crypto founder CZ with as much gusto as his character does his fancy health tonics.

The real (vegan) meat of the story, of course, is the bond between Sam and Caroline. That, the series understands inside and out. Boyle disappears into the part of Sam, whose boyish arrogance is somehow both his most alluring quality and his most repugnant. He’s outdone only slightly by the chameleonic Garner, who plays the mousy but mighty Caroline like she might see all the gears turning just underneath the surface of reality if she only stares hard enough.

When the two are across a room from one another, even editing a spreadsheet can turn into an act of thrilling flirtation. I hesitate to describe their chemistry as irresistible only because that might suggest we want to see them together. In actuality, the questionably effective but not entirely unpleasant experience of watching The Altruists is to simultaneously believe with your whole heart that these crazy kids are meant for each other and that we’d all have been better off if they’d never met.

The show’s intense focus on their love story distinguishes it, somewhat, from the increasingly crowded field of dramas about scammy and scummy tech founders. The usual rise-fall arc is emotionally complicated by the ménage à trois between Sam, Caroline and FTX/Alameda: The lovers might insist they always put the work first, but The Altruists know well how vulnerable even billionaire geniuses can be to whims of their hearts or loins. (Ahem.)

But as a way into what is still ultimately the story of a tech scam, the romantic angle doesn’t entirely work. Amid all the whirlwind drama, a sense of perspective gets lost. The number $8 billion, as in the amount of money FTX has stolen from its customers, gets lobbed about in every episode. But who those clients are, what impact the crime has in the larger world, what cultural forces allowed SBF’s FTX to get so big so fast to begin with, what consequences are left in the wake of its collapse — all these factors are barely mentioned at all.

You come away with the sense that the real lesson of the whole FTX debacle is what a tragedy it is when two people so intelligent are too caught up in their own heads to simply say what’s in their hearts, to the point that it’s easier to perpetrate financial crimes on a historic scale than it is to simply say “I love you.” In a vacuum, I suppose it’s a worthy lesson. In a broader context, however, it seems an unreasonably expensive one.

‘The Altruists’ Review: Netflix FTX Series Fails to Get a Handle on Crypto Scammer Sam Bankman-Fried

📅 Nov 19, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The collapse of cryptocurrency exchange FTX in 2022 — a spectacular implosion that saw the company evaporate and sent founder Sam Bankman-Fried to prison, along with much of his inner circle — felt epic in the moment. But compared to the frenzy around artificial intelligence that was soon to come, even the Shakespearean downfall of a corporation that could once casually spend nine figures on stadium naming rights feels like a footnote in the intertwined history of technology and finance. The crypto boom now occupies an awkward middle space in the recent arc of the tech industry: After the Obama-era idealism of Facebook, Twitter and other companies that promised to connect the world for the better and before the full-on dystopian rhetoric of post-human labor and a “permanent underclass.” Related Stories How to Watch the CMA Awards (And Taylor Swift's Performance) Live Online 'Grendel' Animated Series Set at Netflix From 'Widow's Bay' Writer Years After Live-Action Adaptation Was Scrapped

The Netflix limited series “The Altruists,” created by Graham Moore (the Oscar-winning screenwriter of “The Imitation Game”) and produced by the Obamas’ shingle Higher Ground, is aware of this gap. “AI is hotter than crypto’s ever been right now,” one character observes in the finale as FTX’s house of cards starts to fall. (Marquee frontier lab and FTX investee Anthropic even makes a cameo.) “The Altruists” could theoretically use our rapidly fading cultural memory to its advantage, dusting off the Bankman-Fried story to deliver a character study that pushes past tabloid headlines about polycules and party mansions to make sense of what happened and a case for why it mattered.

Instead, “The Altruists” opts for the most conventional, least persuasive lens through which to explain the significance of FTX as well as what went wrong: the on-and-off romance between Bankman-Fried (Irish actor Anthony Boyle) and Caroline Ellison (Julia Garner, also an executive producer), the former head of FTX-affiliated hedge fund Alameda Research. The action toggles back and forth between two timelines: the early days of Sam, Caroline and their companies and their swift demise five years later, forecasted in a standard flash-forward to the FBI storming the office.

The erstwhile couple make a certain sense as the show’s focus. They’re a handy metaphor for the messy, inappropriate relationship between their two companies, which culminated in FTX’s use of billions in customer money to cover for Alameda’s trading losses. And, of course, there are salacious details like Ellison’s X-rated “Harry Potter” fanfiction — the kind of low-hanging fruit a writer would have to be foolish to leave on the vine. Sex still sells, even when the sex in question is between socially awkward math geeks whose idea of foreplay is strategic board games.

But “The Altruists,” which works from a series of New York magazine articles by Kevin T. Dugan and Jen Wieczner as its source material, makes Sam and Caroline strangely simplistic, both as individual personalities and larger cultural forces. It starts with the casting: Boyle is a strong actor with a bright future ahead of him; the onetime face of a brash IRA revolutionary in “Say Nothing” is nonetheless an awkward fit for a noted oddball like Bankman-Fried, and Boyle never disappears into the role. His SBF — the boy billionaire’s popular nickname — is motormouthed and visibly anxious, yet the scripts elide some of his strangest tics, like taking interviews while playing video games or sleeping on the office floor. (The bean bag habit is alluded to in dialogue, yet never shown. An odd choice for a visual medium!) Boyle’s performance comes off more like the Mark Zuckerberg of “The Social Network,” a disaffected yet basically relatable nerd who craves validation, than the Mark Zuckerberg of “The Social Reckoning,” a space alien whose inability to grasp human nature has warped the world in his image.

Garner, for her part, dials up Ellison’s tics into a near-caricature of a four-eyed poindexter. Pre-arrest footage of Ellison, who would go on to cooperate extensively with SBF’s prosecutors, shows the same nervous laughter that Garner accurately mimics, but a much more calm and even speaking voice. Amping up an accent and donning a wig are old hat for the star of “Inventing Anna,” another frustrating Netflix project that captured its subject’s eccentricity without delving into their deeper psychology. Were “The Altruists” more of a straight-up satire in tone, Garner’s caricature might work in context. Instead, isolated moments of humor — upon finding out about the fraud, one FTX employee is mad because he’s been urging them to break the law for years and feels excluded — are left to float in an otherwise sincere story of misguided puppy love. (In a striking detail seemingly invented for the show, Caroline is the first woman Sam ever sleeps with.) Perhaps Moore and co-showrunner Jacqueline Hoyt were worried about crossing over into gleeful cruelty. But if perpetrators of a multibillion dollar fraud don’t deserve some mockery, who does?

Sam and Caroline are initially drawn together as young analysts at the Wall Street firm Jane Street Capital by their mutual interest in effective altruism, the “earn to give” movement that gives “The Altruists” its name. (Characters affiliated with EA are frequently identified by their branded T-shirts.) The two encourage each other’s most dangerous instincts, like going all in on every bet because it’s “mathematically optimal” or refusing to communicate discomfort around Sam’s autocratic tendencies or Caroline’s polyamory. The plot frames Sam and Caroline’s slide into criminality as the slow corruption of self-identified “good guys,” though the show is strangely uncritical of the starting premise that the best way to make positive change in the world is by amassing a personal fortune.

For all EA comes into focus, cryptocurrency itself remains stubbornly obscure. It’s not easy to mine drama from complex financial instruments, but “The Altruists” puts frustratingly little effort into explaining the water these crazy kids are swimming in. Without a more defined sense of what makes crypto unique, “The Altruists” is left with a faint rehash of the techno-optimism-gone-awry narrative already explored in series like “The Dropout,” “Super Pumped” and “WeCrashed,” which tellingly dropped within weeks of each other in 2022 (and just a few months before FTX flamed out). Those three titles collectively worked to capture a specific time and place; with its personal focus, “The Altruists” never does the same.

“The Altruists” even gestures at more productive angles on the whole FTX affair than the folie a deux of Sam and Caroline. Potential framing devices, like a Bloomberg reporter’s investigation, are flirted with and abandoned. Nishad Singh (Karan Soni), SBF’s childhood friend turned employee, tells us that Sam’s Stanford Law professor parents Joe (Paul Reiser) and Barbara (Robin Weigert) are the skeleton key to his need to prove himself. “They already love you,” Nishad says of the scholars. “They just don’t fucking like you.” It’s a great line, just not one backed up in any way by the show’s portrayal of that relationship, which is fairly minimal and fails to lay the groundwork for head-scratching decisions like Joe and Barbara’s total credulity toward their son, both before and after his arrest.

It’s not like “The Altruists” lacks time to explore the Bankman-Fried household dynamics. (The resources to convincingly render far-flung locations like Macau and Malta do, however, seem scarce.) At eight hourlong episodes, “The Altruists” is padded out with detours into Nishad’s office romance and the backstory of SBF’s college pal Sam Trabucco (Alex Lawther), who left FTX before things hit the fan. The space would be far better spent building out the crypto universe, shading in Sam and Caroline outside one another’s orbit or even outlandish incidents like FTX’s Larry David-starring Super Bowl ad. Without a stronger center, though, these satellite plots feel like they’re orbiting a vacuum.

Prediction: This Will Be SpaceX's Stock Price by the End of 2030

SpaceX is growing at a staggering pace, but does the stock price already reflect it?

By Prosper Junior Bakiny – Nov 19, 2026 at 3:00AM EST

10 stocks we like better than Space Exploration Technologies ›

If you had invested in Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX +1.27%) shortly after its IPO and held on until now, you would have lost money. The stock debuted on public equity markets at $150, and shares are currently trading at $141 apiece, having risen as high as $225 and dropped to a low of $104. That's some volatility, and investors can expect more of the same from here on out.

But could SpaceX beat the market over the next four years despite the volatility? Let's see what its stock price might be by the end of 2030.

SpaceX made its name by revolutionizing the space launch industry, which has also allowed it to become the leader in satellite-based connectivity. And it has added artificial intelligence (AI) services to its portfolio. All three businesses are doing fairly well, but connectivity and Super Intelligence (SI) are growing much faster right now, and in the third quarter, both were profitable.

SpaceX generated $14.6 billion in revenue in the third quarter, growing by 161% year over year, and CEO Elon Musk said the top-line could grow by about 70% quarter-over-quarter in the fourth quarter. So we could be looking at fourth-quarter revenue of $24.8 billion, and total 2026 revenue of about $51.9 billion.

Here's the best part. Musk also said that SpaceX could generate $1 trillion in revenue by 2030, with a nonzero probability of achieving this milestone in 2029. If SpaceX can really reach $1 trillion in revenue by 2030, it could grow its top line at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 109.6% over the next four years. That would be extraordinary. But how would that translate into profits? Note that SpaceX was profitable in the third quarter.

It generated a net income of $4.4 billion, up from the $1.1 billion net loss recorded in the prior-year quarter. It remains unprofitable over the trailing-12-month period, but at its current pace, it should be profitable next year. And things could get better from then on.

SpaceX's next-gen rocket, Starship, is designed to be fully reusable and could reduce the cost of reaching orbit by 99%, according to the company. Musk has said that once Starship is flying regularly, the company will wind down older rockets, Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy. That could lead to lower expenses and higher profits within its space launch division.

SpaceX will likely continue to spend a significant amount on capex to tap into large AI opportunities while launching its next-gen Starlink V3 satellites, which will help it improve its connectivity business. These initiatives could pay for themselves several times over if successful.

So, in this scenario, SpaceX's earnings per share could grow substantially as well through 2030, perhaps at a CAGR of 50% -- or more. Under these assumptions, SpaceX's current forward price-to-earnings ratio of 49.03 doesn't look that unreasonable.

That said, this scenario looks a bit too optimistic, in my view. For one, Musk's long-term projections for his businesses often don't come to pass. That has been demonstrated time and time again with Tesla (TSLA +0.95%). Second, SpaceX faces considerable uncertainty across both its space launch and SI segments. Regulatory obstacles could weigh on the company, even if it performs well over the medium term. It could also fail to scale Starship launches as fast as it expects, among many other things that could go wrong. Third, competition could intensify over the next few years.

No company is likely to overtake SpaceX's lead in space launch or satellite-based connectivity, but increased competition could force the market to readjust its expectations. Here's one more potential issue: The AI industry may not grow as fast as the company expects. Putting all that together, it seems unlikely to me that SpaceX will generate $1 trillion in revenue by 2030 -- estimates between about $200 billion and $300 billion look much more reasonable, in my view.

Assume about $230 billion in revenue by 2030, and suppose that SpaceX's current price-to-sales ratio of 57.1 drops massively to about 12, as the market adjusts its expectations amid increasing competition and other factors. Then, SpaceX could have a market cap of $2.76 trillion by 2030, for an average annualized return of about 10%. Under these assumptions, the stock could be worth $203 by 2030. Those aren't bad returns, but remember that there is plenty of uncertainty ahead. Only investors comfortable with risk and significant volatility should consider the stock.

Here’s the comparison. In March 2020, the Fed was slashing rates, buying bonds and throwing emergency liquidity at a financial system under immense pressure. Congress approved a $2.2 trillion rescue package to keep households and businesses afloat.

Today, we are watching markets fall while the Fed is possibly making money more expensive.

October’s FOMC minutes weren’t exactly what markets wanted to hear. The Fed held rates at 3.75%–4% during its October meeting, but almost all participants believed further increases would likely be appropriate. The risk is forced selling. Leveraged funds facing losses or investor withdrawals may have to dump assets quickly, potentially pushing prices down further.

Gold has its own problem. A stronger dollar and higher interest rates make it less attractive to investors who can earn a return elsewhere. Bitcoin price, meanwhile, remains vulnerable when traders cut exposure to speculative assets.

The Fed hasn’t declared a financial emergency. Its minutes actually describe the banking system as resilient. But officials are clearly paying attention to risks that could amplify a market shock.

March 2020 was brutal. COVID-19 lockdowns brought businesses to a standstill. Investors rushed for cash, stocks collapsed, and Bitcoin briefly slipped below $4,000 after sitting near $10,000 just weeks earlier.

The Federal Reserve didn’t wait around. It cut rates by 50 basis points on March 3, then delivered another 100-basis-point cut on March 15. The federal funds target range dropped to 0%–0.25%.

On March 23, the Fed announced unlimited purchases of Treasury securities and mortgage-backed securities, promising to buy whatever amounts were necessary to support market functioning. It also introduced emergency lending programmes to keep credit flowing.

President Donald Trump signed the $2.2 trillion CARES Act on March 27. The package included direct payments to eligible Americans, expanded unemployment benefits, and assistance for businesses struggling to survive lockdowns. The Paycheck Protection Program gave small businesses access to loans designed to help them keep employees on payroll.

The response was enormous, but the economy was facing an extraordinary shutdown. Millions of Americans had lost their jobs, businesses were closing, and normal economic activity had practically disappeared.

Markets eventually bounced back. The S&P 500 recovered its pre-pandemic record by August 2020. Bitcoin climbed above $12,000 during the same month.

The current US economy isn’t in the same position as it was during the pandemic. Inflation remains above the Fed’s 2% target, and policymakers are discussing rate increases rather than cuts.

Slashing rates to zero and flooding markets with liquidity might help asset prices. It could also make inflation worse. Another multitrillion-dollar spending package would face an entirely different argument in Congress, especially without a pandemic-style economic shutdown.

The Fed doesn’t have to choose between doing nothing and repeating the 2020 rescue. If banks struggle to fund themselves or credit markets start freezing, it can step in with emergency lending and liquidity operations. That’s a much narrower response than another round of unlimited bond purchases.

Fed’s concerns about leveraged hedge funds and AI financing could become more relevant if investors start selling indiscriminately. A market correction is one thing. Forced selling spreading through the financial system is another.

At the moment, there’s no evidence that we’re facing another March 2020. The banking system remains resilient, and falling Bitcoin prices alone won’t trigger a government bailout.

But the Fed has a problem that didn’t exist in quite the same way during the pandemic. It may need to protect financial stability without giving up its fight against inflation. In 2020, Washington opened the taps. This time, we wait and see, and Bitcoin price will survive.

XRP Flashes Biggest Volatility Warning Among Major Cryptocurrencies

📅 2026-11-19  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

XRP stands out as the cryptocurrency with the most alarming volatility warning among major digital assets, according to recent Coinbase data. The options market indicates that traders are now preparing for price swings that could be nearly double XRP's historical seven-day price move.

XRP's seven-day options-implied price move stands at 1.94 times its historical median. This figure far exceeds similar metrics for major cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin (BTC), Ethereum (ETH) and Solana (SOL).

XRP exhibits the highest premium above its own historical volatility. Solana shows the lowest premium among these four cryptocurrencies, Coinbase remarked.

As the chart below indicates, the options-implied seven-day price move for XRP is around 9.6%, according to Glassnode and Coinbase data as of Nov. 17. This compares to its historical median absolute price move of about 4.9%.

Basically, this means that the options market is signaling a price swing nearly twice as large as XRP's typical seven-day price movement.

Interestingly, Solana's implied price move is also close to 9.5%, almost the same as XRP's. Yet its historical median weekly price move is much higher at about 6.5%.

The key here is that although both cryptocurrencies exhibit similarly large potential price moves, the magnitude of the expected volatility surprise is significantly greater for XRP relative to its usual trading range.

For comparison, Bitcoin shows an options-implied seven-day move of about 6%, versus its historical median of about 3.5%. For Ethereum, these numbers are about 8% and 5.1%, respectively.

Obviously, this latest figure doesn't necessarily imply that XRP is going to crash. It's a sign that options traders expect a big price move regardless of its direction.

Implied volatility is a forward-looking measure. It's based on options prices. The higher the implied volatility, the more traders pay for exposure to potential price movements or protection against them.

The chart from Coinbase is based on a one-standard-deviation estimate. It's derived by converting annualized at-the-money implied volatility into a seven-day timeframe.

For XRP, the roughly 9.6% implied move represents a statistical range. It's not a guaranteed price target. A one-standard-deviation range suggests about a 68% chance of the price staying within this range under typical modeling assumptions.

Thus, the latest figure of 1.94x implies that XRP's volatility premium remains high despite a slight decrease from the previous reading.

From XRP traders' perspective, this can be a very challenging market environment. For traders using leverage, there could be a high risk of liquidation due to potentially sharp price moves.

How Premier League's big six keep getting richer

Ten games into the Premier League season, four of the top six spots are occupied by 'big six' clubs.

Only second-placed Brighton, a club with a model which must be the envy of many of their rivals, and Premier League ever-presents Everton, have muscled their way in.

In pure financial terms, it should come as no surprise.

The six biggest clubs - Manchester United, Liverpool, Manchester City, Arsenal, Tottenham and Chelsea - now generate hundreds of millions of pounds more than the division's other teams.

BBC Sport breaks down how the finances of the big six have exploded, examines new trends and the impact on the transfer market, and asks how much it really matters.

Companies put their money where supporters are prepared to spend it. It is a simple fact of business in every country, a reality of football's food chain.

In England, Liverpool, Arsenal and Manchester United traditionally dominated viewing figures, column inches, shirt sales and sponsorship.

Over the past 20 years they have been joined by Chelsea, Manchester City and Tottenham.

This group of clubs now generate so much money it is hard to imagine how they can fail in the long term - even factoring in short-term performance issues.

We will use Everton, the only club not in the big six to be in the Premier League since its inception in 1992.

This tells a tale of how even one of the league's founding members can be left trailing well behind.

In 2009-10, the gap in revenues between the big-six average and Everton was Â£120m. In 2024-25, that gap had grown to Â£442m.

In large part it is the boom in international TV rights.

Kieran Maguire, professor of football finance at the University of Liverpool, told BBC Sport that it has attracted companies prepared to pay large sums to be associated with the Premier League's top clubs.

"The big football brand clubs have global fanbases and that makes them very attractive to global commercial partners, especially for sponsorship and front-of-shirt deals," Maguire said.

"You combine that with the fact that the Premier League is broadcast to 190 different countries around the world, and especially the international audiences - that allows the clubs to negotiate very good prices."

Only 10% of Everton's revenue comes from commercial deals, while for the big six it is 45%.

It means Everton, and other clubs in the league, are heavily reliant on broadcast income. It makes up 66% of their revenue.

For Everton at least, this should change following the move to Hill Dickinson Stadium.

But that is unlikely to be enough to stop the gap increasing.

It has taken a few years, but Aston Villa and Newcastle have begun to increase their revenue.

This is largely thanks to the financial rewards of Champions League football.

But even Uefa has created a system which entrenches the position of the biggest clubs.

When the European Super League plan was torpedoed, Uefa created a new financial deal called the value pillar.

This reserves 35% of all Champions League revenues, or Â£713m, for the historically most successful clubs.

At the end of the league phase last season City are estimated to have earned Â£37.9m from the value pillar, Liverpool Â£35.5m and Chelsea Â£33.3m. Newcastle received only Â£13.6m.

The good news for Villa is their coefficient has improved greatly, aided by last season's Europa League win.

Their value pillar is projected to be worth Â£25m this season. But that will still be less than the big six clubs in the competition.

The cards are stacked against an ambitious club - not just domestically, but in Europe too.

Take a look at the financial trend of the big six and one thing becomes noticeable - Chelsea are beginning to fall behind.

In the past two seasons, and for the first time in recent history, the Blues recorded the lowest revenues of the big six.

Not just by a small amount. They were Â£74m behind Tottenham, and Â£200m below Liverpool and City.

Stamford Bridge has a capacity of only 40,000, with no easy scope for expansion. The rest of the big six have grounds which hold at least 60,000.

Chelsea generated an average spend per fan of Â£80 in 2024-25, the second highest in the league after Arsenal.

Yet with 20,000 fewer tickets sold than their big six rivals, Chelsea's total matchday revenues are dwarfed.

"Matchday spend has become increasingly important," Maguire said. "There has been a flatlining of broadcast revenues, especially since 2017.

"As a consequence, clubs have had to look at alternative methods of increasing revenues, especially with costs both on the pitch and off pitch rising so fast."

Matchday revenue has effectively stalled for Chelsea, rising only Â£16m over the past 10 years. United's has grown by Â£69m and Spurs, thanks to their new ground, by Â£85m.

"Chelsea are far more reliant upon player sales," Maguire said. "There is a growing divide because the global appeal is very much aligned with three or four clubs.

"Arsenal have re-established themselves with their progress in the Champions League. Manchester United are a brand in their own right. And Liverpool have expanded Anfield from 44,000 to 61,000, with many of those extra seats having gone to commercial."

Chelsea's successful player trading model has so far limited the impact.

But there are signs that, financially at least, the big six could become the big five.

You might recall that Villa and Newcastle were not fans of the old profit and sustainability rules (PSR).

PSR forced both clubs to sell some of their best players to balance the books.

That has changed this season with squad-cost ratio (SCR).

There is a key difference with SCR, in that it allows a club to spend the money they generate on players. PSR limited losses so was more restrictive.

"This does make it a little easier for the likes of Villa and Newcastle to spend money," Maguire said. "But it is still heavily weighted in favour of the big six.

"SCR is different to PSR rather than better. For some people, change is advantageous. For others, it isn't."

The big six generate hundreds of millions of pounds more than the other clubs. So they can spend hundreds of millions of pounds more on their squads.

This makes the big six even more powerful in the transfer market.

"Look at the clubs who voted against SCR - Brighton, Brentford, Bournemouth, Crystal Palace, Fulham and Leeds," Maguire said.

"It's very noticeable that those clubs have traditionally been very good at player sales, while they have tended to have lower matchday and commercial revenues."

Most of the big six - the outlier being Chelsea - are not remotely close to the Premier League's 85% SCR limit.

If you give clubs more money to spend on transfers, the likelihood is they will do just that. And the numbers back that up.

Premier League clubs spent Â£3.5bn - not only a highest total for a summer window, but it almost smashed the record for a whole season, the Â£3.53bn set in 2025-26.

The annual accounting cost of previous transfers, or amortisation, has been rising year on year.

"Over the course of the last decade, transfer amortisation has more than doubled and close to tripled," Maguire said.

"Which means that on average, clubs in the Premier League are now spending, or now have a cost which goes into their SCR calculations of just under Â£100m."

Uefa fears that SCR will make matters worse, and have an inflationary impact across Europe.

In the summer 13 Premier League clubs broke their transfer record.

Brentford, Brighton, Palace, Leeds and Nottingham Forest all spent more than Â£40m on a player - unthinkable until recently.

So we have learned that the big six have more money than ever, and the gap is so huge it is impossible for any other club to compete with them, financially at least.

And this is an important point: other than Villa and Newcastle, the other clubs are not trying to compete with the big six.

Clubs like Brentford and Brighton have shown that smart models are just as effective as fat wallets.

They sign the potential stars of the future, nurture them and then sell them to the big six.

Football's ecosystem recycles the big-six money through the transfer market.

But it is a precarious business for these other clubs.

"The smaller clubs have to get it right on a more regular basis than the bigger clubs," Maguire said.

"The big six can afford to make signings which are Â£60m and Â£70m, who turn out to be relative flops, and still have those players on high wages for several years."

And this is perhaps the key point about the vast finances of the big six.

The commercial power of the big six does not guarantee success. After all, United sit in the relegation zone.

Yet Mauricio Pochettino, their new manager, may well get the chance to revamp the squad despite the club spending Â£945m on players in the past five years.

The richest clubs can try to spend their way out of trouble. Again and again.

But the rest of the league are only a couple of bad transfer windows from catastrophe.

And that is the real gap between the big six and the rest.

As it is, the incumbent Fifa president has weathered an awful lot. In any normal politics, the World Cup privatisation disgrace would have been enough to finish someone. In football, the patronage system has actually ensured Infantino has gathered strength as time has passed. This is why it’s always been so difficult to oust an incumbent. They still oversee how the Fifa money is distributed, no matter the noise.

There is even the estimation that between five and 20 European countries will end up voting for Infantino, out of the same dissatisfaction with Ceferin that sees them politically pressured into stances like "unanimously" backing Diallo. “The Fifa money stands above everything,” one executive says.

As one high-profile football executive bluntly put of the whole Uefa strategy, “it’s a disaster”. And it may leave yet more fall-out for the game.

One view is that they should have forced a Fifa Council meeting when Infantino was most vulnerable. Others maintain that change at Fifa is only ever coming through the courts. Uefa, ironically, may end up facing a similar situation. Then, again, it’s all the same problem.
