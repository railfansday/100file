Five Ways North Korea Breaks Into Crypto Firms—and How Binance Was Hacked

Five Ways North Korea Breaks Into Crypto Firms—and How Binance Was Hacked

Hackers from North Korea are notoriously effective at stealing crypto, with one estimate attributing 60% of all losses last year to DPRK-linked groups. These hackers enlist several different vectors of attack, making it all the more difficult to spot them in the act.

Last week, it took just 20 minutes for alleged North Korean hackers to drain $6.3 billion from Binance, in what is the biggest crypto hack on record. The attackers compromised a Binance engineer's work computer through a fake job interview, used an internal AI tool to gain control of other engineers' computers, and then used stolen credentials to hijack an internal wallet signing service, the exchange said.

In the past, North Korean hackers have been known to get hired at crypto companies under fake identities, attack through third-party infrastructure, and poison widely used code libraries. More recently, they've started using AI to make all of it more convincing.

Here are some examples of the methods that North Korean hackers are known to use in crypto attacks.

Binance chief security officer Jimmy Su told Decrypt in August 2025 that the exchange throws out suspected North Korean resumes every day, and that "almost all the large DPRK hacks," at the time, had involved a fake employee helping facilitate the attack.

Su said there were tell-tale signs in resumes that indicate the applicant was from North Korea, but the exchange was not willing to reveal the specifics on the red flags. Sometimes, though, malicious actors get through to the interview stage, where the attackers use deepfakes to change their face and voice.

"The only real good detection is that they almost always have a slow internet connection," he told Decrypt. "What's happening is that the translation and the voice changer are working during the call … that's why they are always delayed."

Those who do get hired tend to be suspiciously good at the job. Su said North Korean workers are often among a company's top performers, likely because several people may be sharing one role across time zones.

The threat has not let up. Binance said its security team detected and prevented 57 suspected North Korea-linked cyber threat cases in 2026, using techniques ranging from fraudulent job interviews to fake video conferencing apps.

Attackers also flip the scam around and pose as recruiters to target people who already work at high-profile crypto companies. Often, the attacker will claim there's a video or audio issue during the interview and send the victim a link to update Zoom, which infects their device with malware

"The DPRK group will [also] try to schedule calls with the external-facing employees," Su told Decrypt. "Either as a DeFi project or investment firm. Worst yet, they'll be recruiting them for a high-level job, paying twice, three times as much, just to get them onto an interview."

This is how the Binance attack began, according to the exchange. In September, a Binance software engineer was approached through a legitimate job-hunting platform by someone posing as a project director, who set up two interviews. In the first, a purported HR representative built trust over a live video call by sharing a social media account full of personal photos and videos, an approach Binance called "more advanced and deceptive than previously seen."

In the second, held on a chat app, the engineer was told to download an "assignment" onto his work computer. The code repository contained previously unknown malware that ran during setup, swapped a legitimate developer tool for a malicious version and connected to an attacker-controlled server, evading Binance's security tools.

One North Korea-linked group infected at least 30,000 devices in more than 100 countries between roughly December 2025 and July 2026 using similar methods. It took funds or credentials from more than 7,000 crypto wallets and moved around $10.71 million to North Korea.

The group, known as WaterPlum or Contagious Interview, posed as AI, crypto, or NFT companies and set candidates a technical interview or coding task that required downloading malware-filled files. Some used AI face-swapping software during interviews before cutting their video and asking the candidate to do the same, blaming a bad connection.

Binance's Su also told Decrypt that North Korean hackers poison public code libraries, ranking it as the second most common method of attack—behind fake employees.

Node Package Manager (NPM) packages are collections of reusable, open-source code that developers frequently use. Attackers can clone these packages and slip in a small line of malicious code while keeping its original function. That means that a victim could use this poisoned code library and not notice, with the malicious code embedding itself deeper over time as more is built on top of it.

In September 2025, software security firm ReversingLabs found two NPM packages that used Ethereum smart contracts to download malware, which the firm said it hadn't seen before. The packages were tied to a wider network of GitHub repositories, mostly branded as crypto trading bots, with thousands of commits and stars that ReversingLabs thinks were mostly faked.

The tactic formed part of a “sophisticated campaign” of malicious actors attempting to hack users via poisoned blockchain-related public code libraries, a vector of attack that Binance has previously attributed to North Korean hackers.

To counter the threat, Su said, Binance makes employees go through NPM libraries with a fine-tooth comb.

Tampered developer tools also played a part in the Binance hack. In June, a member of Binance's wallet DevOps team downloaded a tampered installation of Homebrew, a popular software installer, while trying to install an AI coding tool he had found through a search engine, the exchange said. Its security team caught the problem, replaced the device and rotated his credentials, but the incident exposed the engineer and his colleagues on the wallet infrastructure team to the attackers.

Before Binance, the unwanted record for the largest crypto hack belonged to Bybit, which lost $1.4 billion in February 2025. Cybersecurity firms revealed that North Korean hackers pulled off the attack by planting malicious code into the infrastructure of Safe—a crypto wallet provider used by the exchange.

The security firms said malicious JavaScript code was directly injected into Safe's online infrastructure. Though, it was unclear how the attackers managed to do this.

Bybit's infrastructure "was not compromised," the exchange told Decrypt, and the hack came purely because a third party was exploited.

The Binance attack targeted the exchange's own systems. On October 29, the attacker used stolen credentials to deploy a container and gain unauthorized access to an internal wallet signing service, which let it initiate transactions outside Binance's normal withdrawal process. On Ethereum, it used the signing service to exploit EIP-7702, a mechanism that lets an account delegate execution rights to a smart contract, giving an attacker-controlled contract partial control over a Binance hot wallet.

Those on-chain authorizations kept working even after Binance suspended withdrawals. Because the hot wallet used multi-party computation, there was no single private key Binance could use to revoke them quickly, the exchange said.

Every single one of the above mentioned attack vectors can be turbo-charged with artificial intelligence tools.

Early this year, Google warned of an AI-powered malware campaign targeting crypto companies coming from North Korea. Similarly, Binance's Su also mentioned that AI tools can be used to increase the effectiveness of attacks.

Concerns around AI-powered hacks have risen in recent months, with Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin saying in October that it's "plausible" AI could break encryption within the next two years. It came amid an explosion in AI-driven maths, with OpenAI claiming to have cracked a 90-year-old maths problem in a matter of days. Experts urged the industry to prepare for AI-powered attacks on encryption.

There are no confirmed reports of North Korean hackers using AI to break encryption. But given how quickly they've adopted AI elsewhere, the industry may have to prepare for that next.

Realty Income Has Paid 676 Consecutive Monthly Dividends. Here’s How Its Earnings Results Fit the Bigger Picture.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Realty Income’s latest results show the cash flow behind its payout and how modest its growth really is.

There's no question that Realty Income (O -0.71%) is my favorite dividend stock. I appreciate the consistency and convenience of a monthly dividend, and I love the company's commitment to dividend growth.

And now that Realty Income reported its third-quarter earnings report on Nov. 2, I see no reason to change my mind.

The real estate investment trust recently declared its 676th consecutive monthly dividend, a track record that stretches back more than 56 years. But the real story isn't how long the company has been paying dividends. It's how Realty Income continues to generate the cash needed to keep those payments coming.

Its third-quarter earnings report offers some encouraging evidence.

Realty Income's third-quarter revenue was $1.62 billion, up 10.3% from a year ago. Net income was $403.9 million and $0.43 per share versus $315.8 million and $0.35 per share last year.

But for mortgage REIT investors, adjusted funds from operations (AFFO) may be even more important. This metric adjusts earnings for items such as real estate depreciation, providing a better picture of the cash-generating performance that supports dividends.

Realty Income's AFFO was up 3.7% year over year to $1.12 per share, up 4 cents a share from a year ago. That might not sound particularly exciting, especially when compared with the growth rates of high-flying technology stocks. But Realty Income isn't supposed to be exciting. It's supposed to produce dependable income, and that's exactly what its business continues to do.

Management raised its AFFO guidance for the full year to a range of $4.45 and $4.46 per share, while reaffirming its $10 billion investment target for 2026.

Realty Income's business model is fairly straightforward. The company owns about 15,500 commercial properties and leases them to tenants, typically under long-term net leases that require tenants to cover many property-related expenses. It has clients in every U.S. state, the U.K., and eight other European countries.

Its diversified client base spans nearly 100 industries, including grocery stores, convenience stores, home improvement retailers, pharmacies, and other businesses that serve everyday consumer needs. Dollar General, its largest tenant, accounts for just 3.3% of annualized contractual rent, while 7-Eleven accounts for another 3%.

Realty Income has also demonstrated remarkable consistency in maintaining occupancy across its properties. Its year-end occupancy rate hasn't fallen below 96% since 1992, including during the financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic.

Meanwhile, the company continues to expand. Realty Income invested $2.6 billion during the third quarter, with its proportionate share totaling $2.2 billion. Those investments had an initial weighted-average cash yield of 6.8%.

The company also expanded its private capital business into Europe through a joint venture with KKR. These arrangements can provide an additional source of capital for investments without relying exclusively on Realty Income's balance sheet.

The appeal of Realty Income has never been the prospect of explosive share-price appreciation. Investors can find plenty of other companies offering faster growth.

What makes Realty Income special is the combination of recurring rental income, a diversified property portfolio, and a long history of rewarding shareholders.

During the third quarter, the company paid $0.813 per share in dividends, compared with $1.12 in AFFO. That's a payout ratio of roughly 73%, leaving room to fund other needs while continuing to reward shareholders.

And that's why the latest earnings report fits so neatly into the bigger picture.

Realty Income doesn't need spectacular earnings growth to deliver on its investment proposition. It needs dependable tenants, disciplined acquisitions, and enough cash flow to keep increasing its dividend over time.

After 676 consecutive monthly dividends, that approach has a pretty convincing track record. And it's why Realty Income remains my favorite dividend stock.

The Last Of Us Season 3 Will Make History After HBO's Past 2 Seasons

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The first two seasons of The Last of Us have hardly been fun and lighthearted, but the show’s upcoming third and final season will be even darker and grimmer than everything that came before. The Last of Us season 3 is switching main characters yet again, this time from Ellie to Abby, as we see the previous three days in Seattle from her perspective.

Abby’s story is relentlessly brutal and intense; she teams up with two young Seraphite runaways, Lev and Yara, and goes through the horrors of war to protect them. Abby and Lev riding through a flaming battlefield while Wolves and Seraphites slaughter each other all around them will make Joel’s hospital massacre look quaint by comparison. Abby’s boss fight with the Rat King will make Ellie and Dina’s struggle against the stalkers look like a walk in the park.

But Abby’s story is just the tip of the iceberg; The Last of Us season 3 will also include the game’s epilogue in Santa Barbara, and that final stretch along the coast is the bleakest part of either of the games. Initially, showrunner Craig Mazin was floating the idea of dedicating the entire third season to Abby and saving Ellie’s California crusade for a fourth season. But now that his co-showrunner Neil Druckmann, the creator of the original games, has jumped ship, Mazin is also looking for the exit. He’s lined up a Baldur’s Gate series as his next big-budget video game adaptation for HBO, so he’ll be speedrunning the rest of The Last of Us in one season.

While The Last of Us season 3 is likely to be the show’s darkest installment yet, it’ll also be the first season to end on a hopeful note. Seasons 1 and 2 both ended on a pretty bleak note, but season 3 will end with a somewhat optimistic outlook.

The first two seasons of The Last of Us both ended with a harrowing twist. Season 1 ended with Joel lying to Ellie about having murdered all the Fireflies and doomed the human race, and season 2 ended with Jesse getting shot in the face and Ellie being held at gunpoint. But when season 3 reaches the epic denouement of the entire saga, after pushing through some of the grimmest, most haunting storylines yet, we’ll finally get to a feeling of hope. No matter how bleak it gets, it never succumbs to a nihilistic worldview.

As important as it is for The Last of Us season 3 to get Abby’s story right, it has to stick the landing in Santa Barbara, too. If it doesn’t capture the right emotions in that sequence, the entire story will fall apart. By the time you reach Santa Barbara in the game, you’re so overwhelmed and fatigued that you just want it all to be over, and that’s very much by design. It’s a brutal slog; you face the most evil faction in the franchise to date, you burn through all your remaining ammo and energy, and then you’re forced to fight someone you don’t want to fight.

The beauty of The Last of Us Part II’s ending is all in the nuances and the ambiguity. HBO’s adaptation already nailed the nuance and ambiguity of the first Last of Us game’s ending. The final scene of The Last of Us’ season 1 finale was a shot-for-shot recreation of Joel and Ellie’s closing confrontation from the game, and Bella Ramsey nailed the uncertainty in Ellie’s eyes as she reluctantly accepts Joel’s lie.

Without spoiling anything specific, The Last of Us games have the perfect ending. All the TV show has to do to have its own perfect ending is recreate that ending shot by shot.

Pokémon games have never really been much of a challenge. I mean, that’s why Nuzlockes are so popular, right? You need to artificially inject some difficulty into the titles to really make the formula work.

However, while most trainers and Pokémon in the series follow the rules, others sometimes break them in one way or another. Intentionally or otherwise, this creates some of the more interesting challenges as the devs try to balance scales that are invariably tipped in our favour.

Let’s look at some of the most interesting examples, and why they make for such fascinating encounters.

This one is pretty famous, especially if you’ve ever Nuzlocked Ultra Sun and Moon! But why does this battle break the rules?

For starters, this battle is more like a trainer battle: you can’t run from this fight or catch Necrozma. No, it must be defeated! And if that wasn’t annoying enough, as soon as you enter the fight, Necrozma gains +1 to every stat.

On top of that, Necrozma has great coverage with its moveset and the ability Neuroforce, making its super-effective attacks even more potent. The thing is, you might have seen inherent stat boosts before, but they were from Totem battles! Necrozma isn’t even a totem Pokémon, and still gets a ridiculous amount of buffs. Getting through this battle without losing a single Pokémon is a massive challenge.

I remember the first time I played Pokémon Yellow and ran into the Ghost. Honestly, the whole vibe of Lavender Town and the Pokémon Tower really freaked me out. But then again, I think I was like 4 or 5! Now this Ghost isn’t a Gastly; it isn’t even a Pokémon… not until you have the Silph Scope at least!

Of course, we all know it’s a grieving Marowak, but oddly enough, even though this really should be a regular Pokémon battle, you can’t catch this Marowak, no matter how hard you try. At least Necrozma gets an excuse; Marowak isn’t even legendary.

The thing is, though, in the original Generation 1 games, you can use a Poke Doll to escape the battle. If you do it this way, the battle is considered over, and you're the victor.

The Pokémon Mystery Dungeon series is pretty infamous for having ridiculously unfair battles that pit you and your partner against a massive number of opponents. Whether it's the entire cast of your Guild or Darkrai and 6 other Pokémon from the Dungeon surrounding you! But you always come out on top!

Well, one of the most annoying things that always seems to happen in these games is that your victory often means very little. For example, in the Grovyle fight at Crystal Lake, it doesn’t matter whether you win or lose; the story plays out as if you lost.

The same is true of the battle against Snover in Bidoof’s Special Episode. Even if you find a way to win, the game assumes you lost, so putting in extra effort and using items in these fights is pretty pointless.

Generation 5 is pretty famous for having Pokémon with stupidly high evolution levels. Whether it’s Mienfoo at level 50, Pawniard at level 52 or Larvesta at level 59. Of course, the most famous of these is Zweilous, the Pseudo-Legendary of the games, which evolves into Hydreigon at an eye-watering level 64.

The thing is, if you remember playing the original Black and White and facing off against Ghetsis at the end, you’ll remember he has a Hydreigon as his ace. And, for some magical reason, it's level 54. On the one hand, I get Game Freak wanting to give cool, powerful Pokémon to trainers and give us a challenge, but 10 whole levels under? Whatever happened to treating your Pokémon with love and respect?

Ah, and speaking of having underleveled Pokémon, we have another cheater in our midst. The thing is, Lance actually commits another assortment of sins alongside having illegal Pokémon.

Let’s ignore his 3 underleveled Dragonite for now, and take a look at his Generation 1 and 2 movesets. First, as part of the Elite 4, Lance has a Dragonite with Barrier, a move it cannot learn in that generation. He shoots himself in the foot a little with this, however, as the battle AI is coded to always use super-effective moves, even if they don’t actually do any damage. So he will always use Barrier against, let’s say, a Gengar, because of its poison typing.

And he commits another error in the next Johto titles, where he has an Aerodactyl with Rock Slide, another move that can’t be learned in Generation 2. Either Lance is a huge cheater, or maybe he just knows some secrets that we don't!

While Generation 8 certainly wasn’t my favourite, it did have some odd yet memorable moments. One of these shows up when you fight Opal, the Fairy-type gym leader of Ballonlea.

This battle introduces some fairly bizarre mechanics, not found anywhere else in the series. As the battle goes on, Opal will ask you questions about herself. If you get them right, then great, you’ll get a nice little stat boost. But if you get them wrong, your Pokémon's stats will actually decrease. Yep, not from a move or an item, but a piece of trivia, mid-fight.

And the thing is, the last of these isn’t even true. You have to lie to Opal, saying she looks younger than she actually is, to get the final stat boost! Of course, this fight wasn’t actually difficult, but it’s still an odd event!

And speaking of Generation 8, we have another major cheater over here. You’ll meet Avery in your adventure through the Isle of Armour DLC for Pokémon Shield.

Avery considers himself a bit of a rival, and as such, he will do anything to win. In the 3rd trial, you’ll battle Avery again; however, in this match, he will set up Psychic Terrain before the fight even begins!

Psychic Terrain is a pretty strong move that boosts the power of all Psychic moves by 50%, and it usually takes a full turn to set up. Unless, of course, Avery had a Tapu Lele… which he doesn’t! He realises this is wrong afterwards and pays the price! Good riddance, eh?

If you have Pokémon Sword, instead of facing off against Avery, you’ll meet Klara, who fills a similar role, acting as your rival as you progress through the DLC.

Like the previous encounter, during the 3rd Trial, Klara will scatter a load of Toxic Spikes on the field before the battle even begins, which feels a bit more devious. And this isn’t just 1 layer of spikes, but 2, meaning that you’ll have the Toxic variant instead of the regular poison! Because Toxic Spikes doesn’t wear off like Psychic Terrain, you’ll need to Rapid Spin these away, or send out your own Poison-type to absorb the poison.

The funny thing about these cheaters is that I actually really like this added challenge in Pokémon games. Lately, the titles have become laughably easy, perhaps because we’re a little older. So having your opponents cheat and get extra turns certainly levels the playing field a little bit.

Pokémon Colosseum and XD are some of my favourite Pokémon games of all time. They brought so much personality to the Pokémon animations, and the world feels much more alive and dangerous than in other titles.

At the end of XD, you’ll face off against Greevil, and realise he actually gets 7 Pokémon instead of 6! First off, you’ll have to face the awesome Shadow Lugia, XD001. If you like, you can end this battle immediately with a Master Ball, but if you want to catch it with something more interesting, like a Premier Ball, it is a rough fight.

Once that’s done, Greevil will battle you directly with a full team of Shadow Pokémon. Oh, and did I mention Shadow Moves are super effective against every regular Pokémon type? And if all that wasn’t enough, he also has the 3 Legendary Birds! Yep, this guy's team is absolutely stacked! What a brilliant fight!

Of course, if you thought that 6v7 was brutal, Volo will totally rock your world. The first time I fought Volo, his team caught me totally off guard. I knew as soon as I saw the Spiritomb and heard that classic music track that this was going to feel like a fight against Cynthia.

Cynthia is famously one of the hardest battles in all of Pokémon, being the Champion of the Sinnoh region. Now imagine if she had a Giratina that emerged after she was defeated… and then you had to defeat that Giratina twice, once in its Altered Form, and once in its Origin Form, all without being able to revive or heal your team. Yep, this was a truly unfair 6v8 battle, and it was glorious.

This is what I meant earlier about trainers who cheat. They make for some of the coolest moments in the series. Why would the main villain let you walk all over them and still abide by the rules? They would try to defeat you by any means possible, right?

So having Volo with effectively 8 Pokémon, plus a Legendary, made for one of the craziest and coolest battles of all time. I’m not sure any other trainer will live up to the awesomeness of that final battle! We will have to wait and see!

5 Best Movies Like The Love Hypothesis

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

There was never any doubt that Lili Reinhart's rom-com "The Love Hypothesis" would become a big hit for Prime Video. Streaming platforms have struck gold by taking romance books and turning them into comforting crowd-pleasers for the small screen. Sometimes this results in films as vapid as "My Fault: London," but often it results in charming movies that take allow us to indulge romantic fantasies via two hot people falling in love.

That's exactly what we got when "The Love Hypothesis" transformed from "Star Wars" fan fiction, of all things, into a novel and then a streaming movie. Ali Hazelwood's book about a biology PhD student who pretends to date her professor, only for the two to fall deeply in love became a hit, and was promptly given a film adaptation in which Reinhart plays the student, Olive Smith, while British actor Tom Bateman plays the ornery professor, Dr. Adam Carlsen.

There were two main tropes to both the book and the film: mismatched lovers who cross a boundary to be together, and the good old fake relationship that becomes very real. Of course, this is far from the first time these tropes have been used in romance movies, which is why it's actually quite easy to recommend films just like "The Love Hypothesis." While there are plenty of golden-age romance movies that used these plot devices (1941's "Come Live with Me" saw James Stewart and Hedy Lamarr fake a marriage only to fall head over heels), we're betting that fans of Reinhart's movie will want more modern examples. With that in mind, here's a list of the best movies like "The Love Hypothesis."

"My Oxford Year" may have a controversial ending but it's got everything you could want from a modern-day streaming flick based on a book. Melodrama and fairytale romance combine in this Netflix movie, which stars Sofia Carson (who's fast becoming the streamer's go-to romantic drama leading lady) as American exchange student Anna De La Vega. Before descending into a life of corporate drudgery, Anna decides to study Victorian poetry at the University of Oxford for a year. Wouldn't you know it, once there she meets an unreasonably charming and handsome English DPhil student and tutor named Jamie Davenport (Corey Mylchreest).

"My Oxford Year" shares much in common with "The Love Hypothesis." Not only are both based on books (though the former actually began as a screenplay before it was adapted into a novel), they both feature a student/teacher relationship that gets off to a rough start only to flourish into a modern-day fairytale. Both also feature strapping English leading men (though Bateman plays an American in "The Love Hypothesis") who start out as jerks only to reveal a tender side as things progress.

It is worth noting that there's a tragic element to "My Oxford Year," so if you're looking for something that wraps up as neatly as "The Love Hypothesis," this might not be the ideal watch. But otherwise fans of Lily Reinhart's movie should fall madly in love with Carson's alternative.

These streaming movies are all well and good but there has to be at least one undisputed classic on this list. There's no better example of the fake relationship trope than this 1990 romantic comedy in which we got to see prime-era Richard Gere and Julia Roberts fall for one another. The movie did what "The Love Hypothesis" and its fellow streaming-age rom-coms did decades before Netflix was even a thing, bringing together impossibly beautiful stars via a fake relationship that blossoms into the real thing.

If you don't know the plot of what might just be Julia Roberts' best movie, I'm not sure what to tell ya. He's a corporate raider, she's a sex worker he asks for directions. So begins a love affair that sees Gere's Edward Lewis paying Roberts' Vivian Ward to spend a week with him and act as his girlfriend. Needless to say, this confected relationship becomes a real one. Ward melts Lewis' cold corporate heart and Lewis draws out the real Ward from behind a wall of dissociation built up over her time spent as a sex worker.

If you're somehow yet to see the film, and are worried the tale of a millionaire corporate raider falling for a sex worker might not have the wholesome charm of "The Love Hypothesis," don't worry. There's a reason Roger Ebert called the film "the sweetest and most openhearted love fable since 'The Princess Bride.'" "Pretty Woman" is as romantic a comedy as they come. But beyond that, it somehow manages to feel grounded thanks to its multi dimensional characters, without compromising the fairytale romance aspect.

Sofia Carson will soon have a monopoly on streaming romance movies. Her 2025 romantic drama "The Life List" dominated Netflix just prior to the similarly successful release of "My Oxford Year." But before either of these movies, Carson made her Netflix romance debut with the 2022 film "Purple Hearts." Guess what — the whole thing was based on a book, Tess Wakefield's 2017 novel of the same name.

In "Purple Hearts" we get a modern day version of James Stewart's "Come Live with Me." This Carson-led love story sees the former Disney Channel star play diabetic waitress and musician Cassie Salazar, who's working herself ragged just to afford insulin. Her salvation arrives in the form of hunky Marine Luke Morrow (Nicholas Galitzine), who agrees to marry Cassie so she can gain health coverage and he can benefit from a pay bump. You can see where this is going.

Much like with "The Love Hypothesis," this is a story about a couple who agree to act as though they're together only to develop real feelings along the way. There's plenty of BookTok-style melodrama on display here, too, which should please anyone who enjoyed the tension of the third act fall-out in "The Love Hypothesis."

It's the film that made Glen Powell a star and proved Sydney Sweeney could make popular stuff outside of "Euphoria." If your favorite thing about "The Love Hypothesis" was watching two attractive people make out, then "Anyone But You" should be right up your alley. In fact, "Anyone But You" is arguably the apotheosis of the modern day two-hot-actors-fall-in-love rom-com. But there's a stronger connection between this film and "The Love Hypothesis."

Lily Reinhart's Olive Smith and Tom Bateman's Dr. Adam Carlsen agreed to fake a relationship for mutually beneficial reasons, and the same happens with Sweeney's Beatrice Messina and Powell's Ben. The duo pretend to be a couple so that Bea's parents will stop trying to repair her relationship with her ex, and Ben can make his own ex jealous. We all knew where it was going from the very beginning but that didn't make it any less enjoyable to watch.

Unlike with "The Love Hypothesis," the guy in this romantic comedy isn't a complete jerk. Full of himself, maybe, but not an anti-social hermit like Bateman's "Dr. Evil." Still, he and Sweeney's law student don't exactly get along, especially after their first meeting ended on awkward terms. That, alongside the fake relationship trope, gives Powell and Sweeney's couple a similar dynamic to that of Olive and Adam in "The Love Hypothesis." So if you somehow missed the unlikely box office smash that was "Anyone But You" back in 2024, now's the time to remedy that.

In 2018, "To All the Boys I've Loved Before" became the new gold standard for teen rom-coms. It also happens to be a nice little companion to "The Love Hypothesis." Based on a book? Check. Fake relationship trope? Check. Academic setting? Sort of check. Rather than college, this tale of teen love, adapted from Jenny Han's YA novel, plays out in high school, where Lara Jean Covey (Lana Condor) writes secret letters to her crushes. Of course, she never intends for any of them to read her missives, but guess what — that's exactly what happens.

Lara Jean's biggest crush is Josh Sanderson (Israel Broussard), and when she sees him approaching with his letter in hand, she kisses another one of the letter recipients, Peter Kavinsky (Noah Centineo) in an attempt to throw Josh off. Soon, Peter and Lara Jean are fake dating. This allows the former to distract from the embarrassment of Josh receiving a letter, and the latter to make his ex-girlfriend Gen (Emilija Baranac) jealous. But you know how this goes. Real romance develops, melodrama ensues, feelings get hurt.

"To All the Boys I've Loved Before" is, however, more than your standard BookTook phenomenon turned Netflix romance movie. The film is surprisingly well shot, retains a heartfelt sensibility throughout, and isn't afraid to embrace its classic fake relationship rom-com influences. It's a touching little high school melodrama that's well worth a watch for anyone who even remotely enjoyed "The Love Hypothesis."

The UK’s Advanced Research and Invention Agency (ARIA) will test servers that use light to run AI models, in a lab it funds, Oxford startup Lumai said on Tuesday.

The Scaling Inference Lab, backed by £50M from ARIA and run by the nonprofit CommonAI, will install Lumai’s Iris servers as its first optical computing platform. It will run them across several machines in a commercial data centre, to see how they cope with real AI models, power and cooling.

Lumai, spun out of Oxford University in 2021, encodes data into beams of light. Its chips do the heavy maths behind AI using light rather than switching transistors, while normal electronics handle the rest. Salience Labs, which also builds on Oxford research, raised $125M last month for optical switches.

The company aims its hardware at “prefill”, the stage where a model first reads a user’s prompt. Ordinary chips would still produce the answer.

“Lumai’s technology is the most mature optical compute platform we evaluated,” said David Lewis, ARIA’s chief science and technology officer.

Lumai claims its approach could cut data centre power use by up to 90%. It also says the system is at least 16 times more energy efficient per request than silicon alone, when paired with standard chips. None of these figures has been independently checked, and testing them is the lab’s job.

The lab already hosts UK startup Oriole, which uses light to link AI chips. Nvidia, meanwhile, has spent billions on light-based connections inside data centres. Another UK inference-chip startup, Fractile, is raising at $6.5B.

Planetary wreckage around a dead star may hint at Earth’s future

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mysterious rings of planetary debris around a dead star suggest that rocky worlds may survive stellar death, offering clues to Earth's distant future.

When a star like the Sun nears the end of its life, it swells dramatically, potentially swallowing the planets closest to it.

Around one dead star, though, the wreckage didn’t settle into a single ring, the way it has around every other dead star with a known gas disc. It formed two rings with a clear gap between them.

The team suspects that two rocky bodies survived the star’s transformation and that each may be supplying gas to a separate ring.

Astronomers call small rocky bodies like these planetesimals.

Nicola Gentile Fusillo, an astronomer at the University of Trieste in Italy, led the study.

“We have known for some time that planets may survive beyond the transformation of their central star, but this is the first example of a complex system with a double debris disc and potentially two surviving planetesimals,” he said.

Neither body has been seen directly. The team inferred both of them from the way the gas around the star behaves.

Near the end of its life, a star like the Sun expands and sheds its outer layers, engulfing nearby planets. Rocky bodies that survive can break apart.

Their pieces end up circling the dead star in a flat disc of dust and gas, and some of that rubble falls onto the star.

What’s left of the star itself is a white dwarf, the dense, burnt-out core. By the team’s estimates, this one holds about half the Sun’s mass in a ball roughly one and a half times as wide as Earth.

This white dwarf, called WD J1328–1450, became one somewhere around 73 million years ago.

The dusty disc around it is one of the brightest ever found around a white dwarf. Only a small share of these discs also contain gas. This one does.

The team studied the star with the X-shooter instrument on the European Southern Observatory’s Very Large Telescope in Chile.

The instrument spreads a star’s light out by color. In a spinning disc of glowing gas, the gas moving toward Earth and the gas moving away split each bright line into two peaks.

All 21 gas discs known before this one show that pair of peaks. This star’s calcium and oxygen lines show four. The team matched them to two rings of gas, one inside the other.

The inner ring stretches from about 60,000 to 147,000 miles (97,000 to 237,000 km) from the star.

The outer ring starts about 234,000 miles (376,000 km) out, close to the Moon’s distance from Earth, and reaches roughly 835,000 miles (1.34 million km).

Together, the rings span a region nearly twice the Sun’s diameter.

The shape of the lines held steady between observations in 2021 and 2023, about two years apart.

Co-author Felipe Lagos-Vilches is an astronomer at the University of Warwick in the UK and at the Universidad Técnica Federico Santa María in Chile.

“The structure we see in this system is particularly intriguing because the two gaseous rings are remarkably stable and remain separated by a gap,” he said.

When gas drifts away from where it formed, it cools and turns back into solid grains. So the gas stays close to its source.

Around another white dwarf, SDSS J1228+1040, astronomers had already picked up a signal in the gas that repeats about every two hours.

Astronomers could explain it only with a dense solid body inside the disc, making gas as it interacts with the dust.

Gentile Fusillo’s team argues the same thing is happening here, twice. Gas made in two separate places would form two separate rings.

At such close distances, the white dwarf’s gravity places severe constraints on the size and strength of any surviving rocky body.

The inner body is probably the size of a typical asteroid, no more than about 107 miles (172 km) across. The outer one could be far bigger, as large as Pluto or even our Moon.

The gas contains calcium, oxygen, magnesium and iron, a mix that matches the rocky material in our own solar system. But magnesium glows only in the inner ring.

If the outer ring had the same balance of magnesium to calcium, the magnesium would have shown up clearly. It didn’t.

The missing magnesium is a clue, because different kinds of rock contain different amounts of it.

Earth’s mantle, the thick rocky layer under the crust, has about eight times as much magnesium as calcium. Earth’s crust has less than twice as much.

The chemical differences suggest the rings could contain material from two different rocky bodies.

Or they may have come from two layers of a single broken planet, with magnesium-rich mantle in one ring and magnesium-poor crust in the other.

“The system is a rare glimpse of the violent, chaotic process by which a planetary system meets its end, and it hints that dead stars can have disc structures far more complex than previously realized,” Fusillo said.

In the far future, the Sun will become a white dwarf too, and that change may destroy Earth. The two rings also resemble the rings seen in discs around young stars, where planets are still forming.

“The system therefore offers a remarkable glimpse of what the future of planetary systems, including systems like our own, might look like,” Lagos-Vilches said.

The team can’t pin down the exact orbits of the two bodies.

Neither of the leading explanations for how gas forms in these discs can fully account for the mysterious gap between the rings.

‘Magic Atlas’ Review: Surreal, Shapeshifting Animation Is a Sometimes Mystifying Technical Marvel

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sun Xun's decade-long project spans different illustration media, different materials, even different dimensions, but it's hard to find a real emotional foothold in the artist's heady and fragmented picture.

There’s an increasingly undeniable hunger for the hand-crafted. As discourse around animation becomes more and more absorbed by anaesthetizing questions about AI, it becomes clearer that people who care about the medium are pushing for tactility and foregrounding real texture, materials imprinted with genuine human feeling. This feels especially true of Sun Xun’s “Magic Atlas,” made over the course of a decade using a refreshingly unrestrained range of different materials and techniques: paint, pen and ink on paper, canvas and newspapers, woodcut, or indeed anything with which the director can bring this strange and dreamlike picture to life. Produced in Singapore by Pi Animation Studio, the film is, to some extent, about a coal empire called Luocha. Ruled by fascist, industrialist despots, it is explored from the perspective of an orphan boy named Xiao Zhi who is granted access to its secrets by an imprisoned magician, incarcerated for giving people some measure of freedom back: the memories taken from them by this government. Plot honestly feels secondary to “Magic Atlas,” which, despite a brief running time, is rather relaxed about getting Xiao Zhi’s story under way, opting to build the mood of the piece through its fluctuating, inky illustrations instead. (In fact, Xiao often disappears from the film, not long after he appears to question the state of the world as he has been taught to view it.)

The appeal of the film is predicated on one’s fondness for the imagery, and perhaps one’s patience for frequently opaque, fragmented, oddly paced storytelling. It opens with the Avatamsaka Sutra, the Buddhist scripture about “the past, the present, the future lying in the present,” followed by a sequence consumed by waves where painted creatures tussle. A narrator (voiced by the director) claiming to be “everywhere, with no beginning and no end” ponders his place in the universe as the style of the art shifts to aged pages and painted backgrounds in black ink. Surreal hybrid animals (including a horse with a camera for a head) prance across the landscape, impossibly, drawn with white lines on a black background.

And then the style shifts again: blue water, a black whale, exploding into more kaleidoscopic paintings which fly through what appear to be homages to Pieter Bruegel. It’s a curious mix of eras, symbols, deities and animals all coalescing in one place, the invasiveness of the regime immediately apparent in how aggressively a factory juts from the more gently painted mountain landscape. Abstract electronic tones in the score create further dissonance in this surrealist fever dream of a picture as the scenery and style change to meet the needs of the moment.

Sun’s piece mostly orbits a monochrome palette of heavy, inky black shading (with white outlines) and a lot of line detail, with broad, impressionistic brushstrokes characterizing the landscape — at least some of the time. But it’s never truly exact or particular about any one style, constantly breaking free of the oppressive obsidian lines that build its authoritarian setting. The next scene may totally turn that palette on its head with an explosion of colour and an embrace of a more “western” painting style, or another segue into pen and pencil on paper. Throughout, cutting back and forth between scheming politicians in backrooms, foreboding factory lines and a natural world suffering both, Xun mixes almost cartoonish expression with the intense detail of painting.

“Magic Atlas” is more compelling for how it looks than for how that look pairs with its story, even as it touches on some impressively odd and grotesque concepts, like a mass surveillance system comprised of giant mosquitoes. There’s little ambiguity about this mix of folkloric imagery with science fiction: The generals’ Orwellian nature couldn’t be more apparent than in the moment they declare, “You have practiced criminally outmoded thinking” — to a child they charge with having “possession of false memories.” (Or rather, the memories the government permits people to have.) The blatant suppression of truth is, at least, an easy theme to relate to in the present moment.

Appropriately, the film doesn’t only dedicate its look to a single idea. A message from the director near the end speaks about how one of the film’s standout sequences was a response to the isolation of the Covid-19 pandemic, made via an international collaboration of artists. The keenest ideas of “Magic Atlas” lie in the moments where it places its malleable style against an idea of pacification through the co-option of art and entertainment. (During the big festival, there is a man in a mouse costume with a silhouette that resembles Mickey Mouse, though this could be confirmation bias — take it as you will.)

It’s perhaps a difficult film to fully connect with emotionally, not necessarily because of its symbolism but more due to its fragmented nature — especially as the story becomes even more metaphysical, even poking fun at the artifice of its own created world. Xun has said before that he’s not particularly concerned if everyone understands everything in the film, a perspective which is more than fair. But the technical merits of “Magic Atlas” prevent it from feeling totally alienating; frequently, the film even approaches the divine.

Understanding the conflict four years on.

Across the former Soviet Union and its satellite states, almost everyone knows The Irony of Fate, a 1976 romantic comedy in which a Moscow surgeon gets drunk at a bathhouse on New Year’s Eve and ends up on a plane to Leningrad. He gives a taxi driver his Moscow address, “Third Builder’s Street,” and arrives at an identical block of flats, where his key opens the door.

The world that the movie describes was once much larger than Russia itself. You could arrive in Kyiv, Tbilisi, or Riga and speak Russian; quote the same Soviet films; laugh at the same jokes; and assume that underneath all the flags, languages, and newly drawn borders there was still some common social and cultural space holding the place together.

When Russia set out to restore that world by force, some of its nostalgic talking points, once confined to state television, found a wide company in the West. Tucker Carlson, who has turned it into a routine, tells his audience that Ukraine is weak, corrupt, and finished. Candace Owens flew to Russia and came back saying, “they are lying to us about Russia.” Others, such as U.S. Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene, refuse to support aid for a country defending itself from invasion.

John Mearsheimer and Noam Chomsky have built long careers explaining that whatever Russia does is somebody else’s fault. Francesca Albanese, a U.N. special rapporteur, has called for an end to arms deliveries to a country fighting against an invader.

The left and the right rarely agree on anything, yet here, some of their thinkers converge: Sooner or later, Kyiv must learn to live with Moscow’s demands. Whether it’s Owen Matthews writing in the Spectator that Ukraine is losing the war or Jeffrey Sachs insisting that Kyiv will never join NATO, the underlying belief is the same. For some people shaped by that world, its disappearance is sad. There is a little of Anton Chekhov’s The Cherry Orchard in them: They are so attached to the estate that they can scarcely imagine a world beyond it.

Leaving Russia for the West did not always mean leaving its imperial assumptions behind. Joseph Brodsky, persecuted and exiled by the Soviet state, greeted Ukrainian independence with a poem full of insults, imagining Ukrainians returning to Russian poet Alexander Pushkin on their deathbeds rather than their own Taras Shevchenko. Pushkin himself, in “To the Slanderers of Russia,” told Europe to stay out of what he presented as a family quarrel among Slavs during Russia’s suppression of the Polish uprising in 1831.

What is striking is how many people who never lived in that world have arrived at the same conclusion. These pundits identify Ukraine’s problems well. Kyiv is short of men, money, and Patriot interceptors; corruption persists; and its allies’ declarations have far outrun their policies. They are right about the diagnosis and wrong about the cure.

They also exaggerate the threat. Russia has advanced at enormous cost to Ukraine, yet a snail set off from its starting positions might have reached parts of today’s front line first. Ukraine won the decisive battle for Kyiv, when Moscow’s aim was a disarmed Ukraine under a government of its choosing. It need not recover all its territory to win. If it remains sovereign, secure, and in the West, then Russia’s larger project will have failed. The only real Russian victory is the one that these arguments point toward—a Ukraine that is left vulnerable and subject to Moscow’s will.

The worst part of this so-called realist take is the claim that it is for Ukraine’s own good. By the forecasts of these observers, Ukraine was supposed to lose in 2022, then every year since. Instead of revising the premise, they move the date forward and wait for history to rescue the thinking.

Even the more serious skeptics concede that Russian President Vladimir Putin has not changed his war aims and that U.S. special envoy Jared Kushner is naive to think that business deals will seduce the Kremlin into peace. If Putin cannot be persuaded, what is Ukraine supposed to negotiate? A concession to a man who demands the maximum will only be a down payment. Georgia learned this the hard way. Its 2008 cease-fire with Russia was followed by the latter’s invasion of Crimea; the Minsk agreements were followed by the full-scale invasion.

Another argument that keeps resurfacing is that the West provoked the war. Mearsheimer and Chomsky describe Russian aggression as a response to a menacing NATO, the supposed existential threat that Putin cites and his Western interpreters obligingly repeat. Yet Moscow was already using force in Moldova and Georgia in the early 1990s, when NATO enlargement was hardly an available excuse. You can call those ethnic conflicts, as some do, but after so many Russian acts of aggression, ignoring the reality takes real determination. The only power that has invaded anyone in the neighborhood is Russia. NATO only prevents it from doing so.

What runs through all this is an old fatalism, very familiar in Russia, in which Russian power is a constant, Western resolve is fickle, and Ukrainian resistance is doomed. It calls itself realism, but it is really pessimism, in which history can change everything except Russia’s behavior. It treats Moscow’s desires as objective reality and Ukraine’s goals as childish aspirations, in the spirit of the Brezhnev Doctrine, where small countries choose freely, provided that Moscow approves.

Soviet power once looked equally permanent; in Alexei Yurchak’s words, “everything was forever, until it was no more.” The Baltic states were told that Russia would never tolerate their joining NATO. They did not move on the map. The policy and debate did, because Western governments decided it should. Yes, they joined while Russia was weak. That is exactly the point.

If Western policy has failed, then the policy should change. If Ukraine lacks air defense, the answer is more air defense. Pressure on Russia should grow until Moscow understands that the West will hold its ground and that the war will end as a strategic loss even if Russia takes the rest of the Donbas, which it does not really need. That means guaranteeing Ukraine’s future security and denying Moscow that lever. Is there a Western consensus on this yet? No. Let’s hope one arrives, because otherwise the problems will end up far closer to home.

Why, then, does the argument keep returning? For some, it is a hatred of the West so complete that every war turns into a story about American wrongdoing. For others, it is audience. Outrage and fringe arguments sell, and Moscow makes a good backdrop for a story about Western decline. And for more than a few, it is simply clear sympathy for Russia, for its strongman, its bigotry framed as tradition, its fantasy of itself as the third Rome against a decadent West.

Then for others, it is fear. In my village in the mountains of Georgia, my neighbor, a man called Valeri, says much the same thing as these commentators, though one should be more forgiving of Valeri. Russian troops already occupy part of his country, and one morning, he may discover that his barn is on the Russian side of the line. When he says that Russia is too big and too dangerous, one can understand. His is a foreign policy of the village bench. But the same worldview, minus the cows, turns up in American studios and newspapers, among people who know that they have more choices.

Whether they echo that view from Moscow or in the West, they will find the Ukraine that comes out of this war even harder to recognize. Young Ukrainians speak more Ukrainian, consume less Russian culture, and understand fewer of the Soviet references that once traveled from Moscow to Mykolaiv. After generations of Russification, the recovery of a national consciousness is not an easy task. It may come with mistakes. But a country bombed in the name of a supposedly common history, that took tyranny and bloodshed to hold together, cannot be expected to preserve that history for the comfort of people who remember it fondly.

Putin can drop more bombs and probably will, but after nearly five years of war, it is hard to imagine Ukrainians deciding that capitulation is the answer. Like other empires, the Russian one will sooner or later collapse, including in people’s heads. Along with it will go the longing to walk into Kyiv or Tbilisi and expect the keys to fit.

A friend tells you she's lost 20 pounds. A coworker shows up looking noticeably smaller. Your sister mentions that she's started taking a GLP-1 medication for weight loss. You recognize that what they do with their bodies is their decision, but before long, you're thinking about your own appearance. Nothing about your body has changed since yesterday, so why are you suddenly feeling dissatisfied with it?

Weight loss is so widely celebrated that congratulating someone for becoming thinner has become a normal and socially acceptable response. Yet weight loss can happen for many reasons, including illness, depression, stress, inadequate nourishment, or an eating disorder. Even when someone has intentionally lost weight and feels good about it, praising the change reinforces the idea that a smaller body is a better body.

We rarely consider what these compliments might mean to someone who later regains the weight. After hearing how wonderful they looked when they were thinner, they may worry that others now find them less attractive or assume they've let themselves go. Nobody has to make a negative comment about their weight gain for them to feel ashamed. The earlier praise has already communicated which version of their body was considered more desirable.

These messages also reinforce the stigma people with larger bodies face, whose appearance is often used to make assumptions about their health, behaviors, and self-discipline. We have become so accustomed to associating thinness with health and attractiveness, and have been told that everyone can and should control their weight and shape. These beliefs end up pathologizing the natural diversity of human bodies.

I think there's more to these reactions than jealousy, particularly for people who have struggled with eating disorders or chronic dieting. Seeing someone else lose weight can bring back memories of when changing your body felt like the solution to so many things you were unhappy about.

Think about all the things you may have missed out on because you felt bad about your body. Perhaps you avoided swimming with friends because you didn't want to be seen in a bathing suit, made excuses not to go to parties because you felt uncomfortable with how you looked, or refused to buy clothes you liked until you went down two sizes. You kept promising yourself that you'd do these things once you were thinner. Looking back, there's sadness in recognizing how many experiences you missed while waiting to feel comfortable in your body.

For someone recovering from an eating disorder, watching another person receive praise for losing weight can bring back memories of the approval they once received. You may remember feeling accomplished when the number on the scale went down, even though maintaining that weight required behaviors that were harming you. It can be upsetting to miss those feelings, especially when you remember how miserable you were at the time.

Eating disorder recovery involves questioning beliefs about food, weight, and self-worth that are deeply embedded in our culture. Sometimes those old thoughts resurface, particularly when the people around you celebrate the very thing you've worked so hard to stop pursuing.

Talk about weight loss has always been difficult to avoid, but the widespread use of GLP-1 medications has brought even more of these conversations into everyday life. People share stories about medications, compare how much weight they've lost, and discuss changes in their appetites. Even a simple dinner can turn into a conversation about portion sizes or how little someone is eating.

In my work as a therapist specializing in eating disorders and body image distress, I hear how frustrating this has become for people who have spent years trying to improve their relationship with food. After working to become less preoccupied with eating and weight, they're surrounded by people discussing medications and the pursuit of smaller bodies. Some describe feeling pulled back into a way of thinking they've worked hard to leave behind.

GLP-1 medications have also changed how people think about losing weight. With weight loss increasingly presented as something that can be achieved through medication, those who have stopped pursuing it may begin questioning their decision. There's a growing sense that if changing your body is possible, you should at least consider it. For someone who has worked to accept their body, that pressure can be difficult to resist.

If hearing about someone else's weight loss makes you want to start dieting again, think about what your previous attempts to lose weight were actually like. Maybe you lost weight and felt good about yourself for a while, only to regain the weight, sometimes more than you initially lost. Each time, you blamed yourself for not having enough willpower or discipline and promised you'd do better with the next diet.

Weight regain is common, in part because our bodies respond to weight loss by increasing hunger and conserving energy. Yet when a diet fails to produce lasting results, people tend to blame themselves rather than question the diet. After years of losing and regaining weight, that repeated sense of failure can seriously affect how someone feels about themselves.

It's worth remembering what those years of dieting actually involved. Perhaps you constantly thought about food, felt guilty after eating, or judged yourself based on what the scale said that morning. If you've decided to stop dieting or are recovering from an eating disorder, you likely had good reasons for wanting something different for yourself. Another person's weight loss doesn't change those experiences.

It's understandable to feel uncomfortable when a friend talks about losing weight, especially if you find yourself comparing your appearance to hers. You might even feel envious of the attention she's receiving and then guilty for having those thoughts. Given how much importance our culture places on thinness, these reactions aren't surprising.

Sometimes the way to cope with those feelings and protect your eating disorder recovery is to first pay attention to what happens afterward. Are you checking your body more frequently, reconsidering foods you've been comfortable eating, or thinking about restarting a diet? Recognizing these changes early can help you avoid slipping back into behaviors you've worked to move away from.

It's also reasonable to limit conversations about dieting and weight loss. You can change the subject or tell someone, "I'm trying to spend less time thinking and talking about weight, so I'd rather hear about what else is going on in your life." You don't owe anyone a detailed explanation of your relationship with food or your body.

We can't prevent other people from pursuing weight loss or control how much attention their changing bodies receive. But we can question why someone else's weight loss has become a reason to criticize our own appearance. You don't have to return to dieting, change how you eat, or become smaller simply because someone else has. Their body is theirs, and yours doesn't need to change in response.

Recessions, scandals and backlash: 10 midterms that hit the party in power

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

These midterm election swings, from 1894 to 2018, show how recessions, scandals and policy fights cost the president's party control of Congress or big blocs of House seats

The party that wins the White House usually pays for it two years later. Since the Civil War, the president's party has lost House seats in most midterm elections. In some years those losses are large enough to hand a chamber of Congress to the opposition and stall a president's legislative agenda.

This list covers 10 of those larger moments, from 1894 to 2018. It is a selection, not a ranking. The elections appear in chronological order rather than by size. The yardstick throughout is the same: the net change in House seats, along with any change in which party controls the House or the Senate. Where historical tallies disagree, the slides describe a swing in general terms rather than settle on one disputed number.

Midterm election swings tend to follow a small set of triggers. Economic downturns are one. A financial panic, a depression or a recession gives voters a concrete reason to hold the party in power responsible. Several elections on this list followed an economic slump. Scandal is another. A president's troubles can weigh on House and Senate candidates who had no part in them. A third trigger is backlash against major legislation or against a president's bid to expand his authority. Voters who believe a party has overreached can use the midterm to register that view.

The midterm electorate also differs from a presidential-year electorate. With no presidential race at the top of the ballot, turnout typically falls. A smaller electorate can shift the balance toward whichever side is more motivated to vote. That helps explain how an opposition party can gain ground quickly while the president remains in office.

The consequences reach beyond the seat count. A House flip gives the new majority the speakership, control of committees and the power to subpoena and investigate. A Senate flip changes who controls confirmation votes on judges and cabinet nominees. Even without a change in control, a large loss can shrink a governing majority enough to give smaller factions more leverage.

Each slide below covers one election: who held the White House, what drove voters and what changed in Congress afterward.

The 1894 midterm gave Republicans control of the House of Representatives. They made a net gain of more than 100 seats as voters punished Grover Cleveland's Democrats for the depression that followed the Panic of 1893.

Cleveland began his second, nonconsecutive term in March 1893. His term had barely started when the panic took hold. The Philadelphia and Reading Railroad had already gone into receivership that February. A wave of bank and business failures followed. Railroads that had overbuilt during the previous boom went bankrupt, and unemployment climbed across industrial cities.

Cleveland's main response was monetary. He called Congress into a special session in 1893 to repeal the Sherman Silver Purchase Act. He blamed the law for draining the Treasury's gold reserve. The repeal passed, but it split his own party. Democrats from the South and West who favored silver saw it as a betrayal of indebted farmers.

Unrest spread in 1894. That spring, Jacob Coxey led a march of unemployed workers, known as Coxey's Army, to Washington, D.C. That summer, the Pullman Strike spread along the nation's railroad lines. Cleveland sent federal troops to Chicago over the objections of Illinois Gov. John Peter Altgeld, a fellow Democrat.

Trade policy offered no relief. The Wilson-Gorman Tariff Act was meant to lower tariffs. It emerged from Congress so weakened by amendments that Cleveland let it become law without his signature.

Democrats went into the November vote carrying the depression, the silver split and the tariff fight. Republicans made heavy gains across the Northeast and Midwest. Thomas Brackett Reed of Maine returned as House speaker when the new Congress met in December 1895.

The result reached beyond a single Congress. The Democratic split over silver carried into the 1896 presidential race. That year the party nominated William Jennings Bryan, and Republican William McKinley won the White House. Republicans then held the House until Democrats won it back in the 1910 midterm.

The 1938 midterm cost Franklin D. Roosevelt's Democrats dozens of House seats, though the party kept its majorities in both chambers. The result strengthened a bloc of Republicans and conservative Southern Democrats that resisted new New Deal programs for years afterward.

The losses came two years after a landslide. In 1936, Roosevelt carried 46 of the 48 states, and Democrats entered 1937 with commanding margins in Congress. That position eroded within months.

The first setback came from the White House. In February 1937, Roosevelt proposed legislation to expand the Supreme Court. The bill would have let him appoint an additional justice for each sitting justice who passed age 70 without retiring, up to six new seats. Critics in both parties called the plan an attack on judicial independence. The Senate sent the bill back to committee in July 1937, and it never became law.

The second setback was economic. A sharp downturn, now known as the Recession of 1937-38, began later that year. Unemployment rose again after several years of recovery. Republicans used the slump to argue that the New Deal had not produced lasting growth.

Roosevelt then tried to reshape his own party. During the 1938 primaries, he campaigned against conservative Democrats who had opposed his agenda. His targets included Senators Walter George of Georgia, Millard Tydings of Maryland and Ellison "Cotton Ed" Smith of South Carolina. All three won renomination. The effort, which came to be called the "purge," succeeded mainly against Representative John J. O'Connor of New York. O'Connor, chairman of the House Rules Committee, lost his primary.

In November, Republicans gained seats in the Senate as well as the House. The incoming class included Robert A. Taft, elected to the Senate from Ohio. Harold Stassen won the governorship of Minnesota the same year. Both men became leading Republican figures in the following decade.

The legislative consequences outlasted the campaign. The Fair Labor Standards Act, signed in June 1938, set a federal minimum wage of 25 cents an hour. It also required overtime pay for work beyond a standard workweek. Few major domestic reforms followed it. After the election, the conservative coalition had enough votes to block or trim many of Roosevelt's domestic proposals. His attention turned increasingly toward the fighting in Asia and the threat of war in Europe.

Republicans won control of both the House and the Senate in the 1946 midterm elections. It was the first time since 1930 that the party had won both chambers. The result left Harry Truman facing a Congress run by the opposition after his first midterm.

Truman had not been elected to the presidency. He moved up from the vice presidency in April 1945, after Franklin D. Roosevelt died, a few months before World War II ended. The 1946 vote was the first national verdict on his administration.

That verdict came as the country shifted from a wartime economy to a peacetime one. Millions of returning service members needed jobs and housing. Wartime price controls were being loosened and fought over, and prices climbed as controls came off. Shortages of meat and other consumer goods frustrated households.

Labor unrest added to the strain. Major strikes hit the auto, steel, coal and railroad industries during 1945 and 1946. Truman clashed with union leaders as he tried to keep those sectors running. Those fights cost him support among workers who had backed Roosevelt.

Republicans built their campaign around that discontent. Their slogan, "Had enough?", asked voters whether they were tired of shortages, strikes and years of Democratic government in Washington.

The new majority formed the 80th Congress. Joseph Martin of Massachusetts became speaker of the House. The incoming class of 1946 also included Richard Nixon, elected to the House from California, and Joseph McCarthy, elected to the Senate from Wisconsin. John F. Kennedy, a Democrat, won his first House race in Massachusetts the same year.

The Republican Congress pushed back against the labor policies of the Roosevelt era. In 1947 it passed the Taft-Hartley Act, which restricted union activity, and overrode Truman's veto to make it law. It also sent the states the proposal that became the 22nd Amendment, limiting presidents to two elected terms.

Truman turned that Congress into his main campaign target. Running for a full term in 1948, he attacked it as the "Do-Nothing Congress." He won that election, and Democrats regained both the House and the Senate the same year.

The 1958 midterm gave Democrats large gains in both the House and the Senate during Dwight D. Eisenhower's second term. Voters cast their ballots after the 1957-58 recession, and the result widened majorities that Democrats already held in both chambers.

That detail sets 1958 apart from most large midterm swings. Democrats had controlled Congress since winning it back in the 1954 elections. The 1958 vote did not flip either chamber. It turned working Democratic majorities into much larger ones for the final two years of Eisenhower's presidency.

The economy shaped much of the result. The downturn that began in 1957 pushed up unemployment, and industrial states in the Midwest were hit hard. Republicans held the White House while factory jobs disappeared. Many of the party's congressional candidates carried that record into November.

Two other events shaped the political backdrop. The Soviet Union launched Sputnik in October 1957. The launch set off a debate in the U.S. over science education and national defense. In September 1958, weeks before the vote, Sherman Adams resigned as Eisenhower's top White House aide. His exit followed disclosures that he had accepted gifts from Boston textile manufacturer Bernard Goldfine.

Labor issues featured in several key contests. Right-to-work measures appeared on the ballot in states including California and Ohio, and unions organized to defeat them. Both measures failed. In California, Senate Republican leader William Knowland gave up his seat to run for governor and lost to Democrat Edmund "Pat" Brown. In Ohio, Republican Sen. John Bricker lost his seat to Democrat Stephen Young.

The Senate class elected in 1958 included several figures who went on to long national careers. Democrats Edmund Muskie of Maine, Eugene McCarthy of Minnesota, Philip Hart of Michigan and Thomas Dodd of Connecticut all won seats that year. Alaska, then on the verge of statehood, elected two Democrats, Ernest Gruening and Bob Bartlett, as its first senators.

The expanded majorities went to a Congress led by two Texas Democrats, Senate Majority Leader Lyndon Johnson and House Speaker Sam Rayburn.

Republicans won back a large bloc of House seats in the 1966 midterm, two years after Lyndon Johnson's landslide win over Barry Goldwater. Democrats kept their majorities in both the House and the Senate. Even so, the result cut sharply into the margins Johnson had used to pass much of his domestic agenda.

The size of the rebound reflected how far Republicans had fallen in 1964. Johnson's landslide that year carried many Democrats into House seats that usually leaned Republican. In 1966, those first-term members ran without a presidential contest at the top of the ticket. Many of them lost.

The Congress elected in 1964, the 89th, had passed a long list of major laws. Medicare and Medicaid became law in 1965. So did the Voting Rights Act and the Elementary and Secondary Education Act. That pace of legislation gave Republicans a record to campaign against. Their message focused on federal spending and the reach of Washington.

Foreign policy also shaped the campaign. Johnson had sent U.S. ground combat troops to Vietnam in large numbers in 1965. By 1966, the war's cost was growing, and it divided the Democratic coalition. Higher prices and unrest in American cities added to voter discontent. The 1965 riots in the Watts neighborhood of Los Angeles were one example of that unrest.

The Republican class of 1966 included several figures who went on to national prominence. George H.W. Bush won a House seat from Houston, his first elected office. In Massachusetts, Edward Brooke became the first Black senator elected by popular vote. Howard Baker won a Senate seat in Tennessee. Mark Hatfield, then Oregon's governor, won a Senate seat in his state. Charles Percy defeated Democratic incumbent Paul Douglas in Illinois.

Republicans also gained ground in races for governor. Ronald Reagan, in his first run for office, defeated incumbent Democrat Edmund "Pat" Brown to become governor of California.

Richard Nixon campaigned across the country for Republican candidates that fall. The results helped rebuild his standing in the party ahead of his successful 1968 presidential run.

In the 90th Congress, Johnson's remaining domestic proposals faced a House with far fewer reliable Democratic votes. Republicans and conservative Southern Democrats held more leverage over which bills advanced.

The 1974 midterm gave Democrats a much larger House majority three months after Richard Nixon resigned over the Watergate scandal. Voters went to the polls on Nov. 5, 1974, with Gerald Ford $F in the White House and Nixon's pardon still fresh. Republicans lost seats in both the House and the Senate.

The scandal had been building for more than two years. It began with the June 1972 break-in at the Democratic National Committee's offices in the Watergate complex in Washington, D.C. Congressional investigators and reporters traced the cover-up back to the White House.

In July 1974, the Supreme Court ruled unanimously in United States v. Nixon that the president had to hand over recordings of Oval Office conversations. That same month, the House Judiciary Committee approved articles of impeachment against him.

Nixon resigned on Aug. 9, 1974, before the full House could vote on impeachment. He was the first U.S. president to leave office by resignation.

Ford took over without having been elected president or vice president. Nixon had nominated him for vice president under the 25th Amendment after Spiro Agnew resigned in October 1973. Congress confirmed the nomination that December.

On Sept. 8, 1974, Ford granted Nixon a full, free and absolute pardon for any federal crimes he may have committed as president. The pardon ruled out a federal prosecution of the former president. Ford's public standing dropped after the announcement. The decision also pulled Watergate back to the center of the fall campaign.

The economy added to the pressure on Republican candidates. The 1973 Arab oil embargo had driven up energy prices. Inflation was high, and the country had slipped into recession. In October 1974, weeks before the vote, Ford launched his "Whip Inflation Now" campaign to rally public support against rising prices.

The election reshaped the House Democratic caucus. The large class of newly elected Democrats became known as the "Watergate babies." Many of them ran as reformers.

Early in 1975, the Democratic Caucus voted to remove three longtime committee chairmen. They were Wright Patman of the Banking Committee, F. Edward Hébert of the Armed Services Committee and W.R. Poage of the Agriculture Committee. Those votes weakened the seniority system that had long decided who led House committees.

The 1994 midterm gave Republicans a majority in the House of Representatives for the first time in 40 years. The party also won control of the Senate. That left President Bill Clinton facing a Republican Congress halfway through his first term.

Democrats had held the House without a break since the 1954 elections. The 1994 result ended that run in a single night.

Republican leaders built their national campaign around the Contract with America. The platform was unveiled on the steps of the U.S. Capitol in September 1994. Its central promise was procedural. If voters gave Republicans the House, the party would bring 10 bills to a floor vote within the first 100 days of the new Congress. The proposals included a balanced budget amendment, congressional term limits, welfare changes and tax cuts.

Newt Gingrich of Georgia, then the House Republican whip, was the plan's chief architect. He became Speaker of the House when the 104th Congress convened in January 1995.

Democrats entered the campaign after several contested legislative fights. Clinton's health care overhaul, led by Hillary Clinton, collapsed in Congress in 1994 without a floor vote in either chamber. His 1993 budget raised taxes and passed without a single Republican vote. The 1994 crime bill included a ban on certain semiautomatic weapons.

The defeats reached the top of the Democratic leadership. House Speaker Tom Foley lost his seat in Washington state. He became the first sitting Speaker defeated for reelection since 1862. In the Senate, Alabama's Richard Shelby switched from the Democratic Party to the Republican Party the day after the election. Bob Dole became Senate majority leader.

The new House majority voted on every item in the contract within the 100-day window. All but the term limits amendment passed the House. That measure fell short of the two-thirds vote a constitutional amendment requires. Several bills later stalled in the Senate.

Clashes over spending between Clinton and the Republican Congress led to two federal government shutdowns between late 1995 and early 1996. The two sides later agreed on a welfare overhaul, which Clinton signed in 1996. Clinton won reelection that November, and Republicans kept their House majority.

Democrats won control of both the House and the Senate in the 2006 midterm, held on November 7 during George W. Bush's second term. The result ended the Republican House majority that began with the 1994 election.

The Iraq War sat at the center of the campaign. The conflict was in its fourth year, and sectarian violence inside Iraq had escalated. Democratic candidates framed the vote as a referendum on the war and on Bush's handling of it.

Scandal added pressure on Republicans. The lobbying scandal surrounding Jack Abramoff produced guilty pleas and put members of Congress under scrutiny. Tom DeLay, the former House majority leader, resigned his seat in 2006 after an indictment in Texas.

Weeks before the vote, Rep. Mark Foley of Florida resigned over messages he had sent to teenagers who had served as House pages. Democrats campaigned against what they called a "culture of corruption" in Washington.

Control of the Senate came down to a handful of close races. Democrats defeated Republican incumbents in Pennsylvania, Ohio, Missouri, Montana, Rhode Island and Virginia. In Virginia, Jim Webb unseated George Allen by a narrow margin. In Montana, Jon Tester ousted Conrad Burns.

The new Senate split 49 to 49, with two independents holding the balance. Bernie Sanders of Vermont and Joe Lieberman of Connecticut both caucused with Democrats. That gave the party a working majority. Lieberman had lost the Democratic primary to Ned Lamont, then won reelection as an independent.

The political consequences arrived within a day. On November 8, Bush announced that Defense Secretary Donald Rumsfeld would step down. He named Robert Gates, a former CIA director, as the replacement. At a news conference, Bush described the results as a "thumping."

In January 2007, Nancy Pelosi of California became Speaker of the House. She was the first woman to hold the post. Harry Reid of Nevada became Senate majority leader.

The election did not end the war policy it was fought over. Bush announced a troop increase in Iraq in January 2007. In May 2007, he vetoed a war funding bill that included a timeline for withdrawing U.S. troops. The new majorities instead used hearings and spending bills to press the administration through the rest of his term.

Republicans won control of the U.S. House in the 2010 midterm, Barack Obama's first. The Tea Party movement and opposition to the Affordable Care Act shaped the campaign. Democrats held the Senate, but the House majority they had won in 2006 was gone after four years.

The election came less than two years after Obama took office with Democrats controlling both chambers of Congress. That majority passed the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act, the stimulus package of early 2009. It also passed the Affordable Care Act, which Obama signed in March 2010. Both laws became central targets for Republican campaigns that fall.

The Tea Party movement took shape in 2009. Its supporters objected to federal spending, bank and auto bailouts, and the health care overhaul. They organized rallies, confronted lawmakers at town hall meetings and backed primary challenges against establishment Republicans. A televised rant by CNBC commentator Rick Santelli in February 2009, aimed at a mortgage relief plan, helped popularize the name.

An early warning for Democrats came in January 2010. Republican Scott Brown won a special election in Massachusetts for the Senate seat long held by Ted Kennedy. The result cost Democrats their 60th Senate vote, the number needed to break a filibuster.

The economy also weighed on the majority. The country was still recovering from the Great Recession. Unemployment stayed high through the campaign.

Tea Party energy reshaped the Republican field. Candidates backed by the movement won Senate seats in several states, including Rand Paul in Kentucky, Marco Rubio in Florida and Mike Lee in Utah. Other insurgent nominees lost races Republicans had hoped to win. Christine O'Donnell lost in Delaware. Sharron Angle lost in Nevada to Senate Majority Leader Harry Reid. Both seats stayed in Democratic hands, and Democrats kept their Senate majority.

The day after the election, Obama described the result as a "shellacking." John Boehner of Ohio became speaker in January 2011, replacing Nancy Pelosi.

The timing extended the effect beyond Congress. Republicans also made large gains in state legislatures and governorships just as states were redrawing congressional maps after the 2010 census. That put the party in charge of drawing district lines in many states for the following decade.

The new House majority quickly clashed with the White House over spending. A standoff over raising the federal debt limit in the summer of 2011 ended with the Budget Control Act. Standard & Poor's downgraded the U.S. credit rating that August.

Democrats won control of the U.S. House of Representatives in the November 6, 2018, midterm election, with a net gain of about 40 seats during Donald Trump's first term. The result ended eight years of Republican control of the chamber and produced divided government for the rest of Trump's term.

Republicans had held the House since winning it in the 2010 election. Going into 2018, the party also controlled the Senate and the White House. That gave Republicans full control of the elected branches in Washington for Trump's first two years in office.

Health care became a central issue in the campaign. In 2017, a Republican bill to repeal and replace the Affordable Care Act passed the House but failed in the Senate. Democratic candidates built much of their message around keeping protections for people with preexisting conditions. Republicans campaigned in part on the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, which Trump signed in December 2017.

Much of the Democratic gain came in suburban districts. Republican-held seats around cities in California, New Jersey, Virginia and Texas moved to Democrats. Several of those districts had sent Republicans to Congress for years before 2018.

The House class that took office in January 2019 included a record number of women. It also included the first two Muslim women elected to Congress, Ilhan Omar of Minnesota and Rashida Tlaib of Michigan. Sharice Davids of Kansas and Deb Haaland of New Mexico became the first two Native American women elected to Congress.

The Senate moved in the opposite direction. Republicans kept their majority and added to it, defeating Democratic incumbents in Florida, Indiana, Missouri and North Dakota. That split outcome set 2018 apart from midterms in which one party gained in both chambers at once.

Nancy Pelosi of California returned as speaker of the House on January 3, 2019. She had first held the post from 2007 to 2011, becoming the first woman to serve as speaker.

Democratic chairs took over House committees in January 2019. They opened inquiries into the Trump administration. In December 2019, the House impeached Trump on two articles, abuse of power and obstruction of Congress. The Republican-led Senate acquitted him in February 2020.

What happens to leftover campaign money? 10 federal rules for surplus funds

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

What happens to leftover campaign money after an election depends on federal rules that ban personal use but allow refunds, charity gifts, party transfers and future campaigns

Fundraising rarely stops as a race nears the finish. Candidates keep asking for donations through the final weeks, and many committees end election night with money still in the bank. Winners and losers face the same question when the results are in. What happens to leftover campaign money?

The candidate cannot simply keep it. Federal law treats contributions as money raised for a campaign, not as a personal fund. The Federal Election Campaign Act bars candidates from converting campaign funds to personal use. The Federal Election Commission, or FEC, enforces that rule and oversees how federal committees handle what remains.

That ban still leaves a candidate with several legal paths. A committee can hold its surplus for a future federal campaign. No federal deadline forces it to spend the money. It can move funds to political party committees or donate them to tax-exempt charities. It can give to other candidates and political action committees, within contribution limits. It can also send money back to the donors who gave it.

Other uses involve settling the business of the race. Campaign money can pay off debts, including loans a candidate made to their own campaign. It can cover legal bills, recounts and the accounting work needed to comply with federal reporting rules. Candidates who win can use it for certain costs tied to their duties in office. A committee that wants to close has to dispose of its funds and clear its debts and obligations first.

This guide covers federal candidates only — people running for president, the U.S. Senate or the U.S. House of Representatives. Their committees answer to the FEC. State and local campaigns follow their own laws, which differ from place to place. Those rules are not covered here. Races for governor, mayor or state legislature fall outside the scope of this guide.

Each of the 10 slides that follow explains one option or restriction for surplus campaign funds. Each slide stands on its own. A reader looking for a single answer can go straight to it. Together they show where unused campaign donations can end up and where they cannot.

A federal candidate cannot keep leftover campaign money as personal income. The prohibition comes from the Federal Election Campaign Act, which forbids turning campaign contributions into personal funds. The ban still applies after Election Day, whether the candidate won or lost.

The rule reaches beyond the candidate. Federal law bars any person from converting a committee's money to personal use, and that includes family members and staff. A surplus stays committee money until it goes to a permitted purpose.

The Federal Election Commission enforces the ban. Its test looks at the expense itself rather than the reason a candidate gives for it. If a cost would exist even without the campaign or the candidate's duties as a federal officeholder, paying it with campaign funds is personal use.

That test explains the FEC's standard examples. A mortgage or rent payment comes due whether or not someone runs for office. So do grocery bills and a child's tuition. The campaign does not create any of those costs, and the committee cannot pay them.

FEC regulations name other expenses treated as personal use in every case. They include health club and country club dues, vacations and tickets to concerts or sporting events with no campaign purpose. Clothing is also on the list. The exception is low-value campaign items such as T-shirts and caps.

Some spending gets a case-by-case review instead. Meals, travel and vehicle costs can serve both campaign and private ends. The committee can pay the share tied to campaign activity. It cannot pay the private share.

Paying relatives draws its own scrutiny. A committee can put a family member on the payroll only for real work at a fair market rate. Any amount above the value of the services counts as personal use.

Penalties range from civil fines to criminal charges. The FEC can pursue civil enforcement. The Justice Department can prosecute knowing and willful violations. Former California Rep. Duncan Hunter pleaded guilty in 2019 to a conspiracy charge tied to spending campaign money on personal expenses.

A federal candidate can keep leftover campaign money in the committee and spend it on a later run. Federal law sets no deadline for using the funds and no cap on how much can sit in the account between elections.

That later run does not have to be for the same seat. The money can support a bid for the same office or for a different federal office. A House member who later runs for the Senate, for example, can draw on money raised for earlier House races.

FEC regulations allow a candidate to transfer funds from an earlier campaign committee to the committee for a new federal race. The main condition is that the candidate is not running for both offices at the same time.

House members face voters every two years. Senators serve six-year terms. A senator's committee can hold a balance through an entire term and put it toward the next reelection campaign. A House member's committee can carry money through several consecutive terms.

A committee that keeps a balance stays registered with the Federal Election Commission. It continues to file regular disclosure reports showing its cash on hand, receipts and spending. Those reports are public, so anyone can see how much a candidate has banked.

Funds carried forward remain campaign funds. The ban on personal use applies the whole time, whether the next race is two years away or 10.

Carrying money forward does not lock in its final use. A committee holding a balance can later choose a different legal path for it. Nothing in federal law forces that decision soon after election day.

"War chest" is the common shorthand for this money. A large balance lets an incumbent start a reelection campaign without first rebuilding funds from zero.

The same rules apply to candidates who lost. A defeated candidate can keep a committee open and use the money if they run for federal office again.

This option applies to federal committees. Money raised for state and local campaigns falls under state law, which differs from place to place.

A federal candidate's campaign committee can move any amount of leftover money to a political party committee. Federal law sets no dollar cap on these transfers. A committee can send its entire remaining balance to a party in one transaction.

The permission covers all three levels of party organization. A committee can send funds to a national party committee, a state party committee or a local party committee. The Federal Election Campaign Act lists these transfers among the lawful uses of campaign money.

At the national level, each major party runs several committees. The Democratic National Committee and the Republican National Committee handle party-wide work. The Democratic Senatorial Campaign Committee and the National Republican Senatorial Committee focus on Senate races. The Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee and the National Republican Congressional Committee do the same for the House.

State and local parties are separate organizations. Both major parties maintain state party organizations across the U.S. Many counties and cities also have local party committees. A candidate's committee can transfer money to these groups on the same unlimited terms as to national committees.

Sitting members of Congress regularly send campaign money to their party's congressional committees. Both parties' House campaign committees set fundraising expectations for their members, a practice commonly called party dues. A member's campaign account is one source of that money.

Once the money reaches a party committee, it belongs to the party. The party decides how to spend it, within the rules that govern party committees. That spending can include voter registration, get-out-the-vote drives and support for other candidates on the ticket.

Both sides of the transaction appear in public records. The candidate's committee reports the transfer as a disbursement on its regular filings with the Federal Election Commission. A party committee registered with the FEC reports the same money as a receipt. Anyone can look up these filings in the FEC's online database.

The absence of a ceiling sets party transfers apart from gifts to individual candidates. A contribution from one candidate's committee directly to another candidate faces a federal limit. A transfer to a party committee does not.

A federal campaign committee can give its leftover money to organizations that qualify as tax-exempt charities under section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code. No cap applies to the amount. The candidate cannot receive compensation from the donation or use it for personal benefit.

A committee can direct its entire surplus to one organization. It can also split the money among several groups. The federal contribution limits that cap gifts to other candidates and political action committees do not apply to charitable donations.

The 501(c)(3) category covers groups organized for charitable, religious, educational, scientific and similar purposes. Food banks, nonprofit hospitals, private universities and many other nonprofits fall into it. The Internal Revenue Service recognizes that status. Its online Tax Exempt Organization Search tool lets anyone check whether a group qualifies. Gifts to these organizations from individual donors are generally tax-deductible.

A candidate cannot route campaign money to a charity that then pays the candidate a salary or fee out of that gift. The donation also cannot serve as a way to deliver goods or services back to the candidate. These limits come from the Federal Election Campaign Act's ban on converting campaign funds to personal use.

Charity also takes the money out of electoral politics. The Internal Revenue Code bars 501(c)(3) organizations from participating in any campaign for or against a candidate for public office. A charity that receives leftover campaign funds cannot spend them supporting or opposing a candidate. The IRS enforces that restriction.

The gift stays on the public record. Campaign committees report their disbursements to the FEC. Itemized entries in those reports name the recipient and the amount. Anyone can search the filings to see which organization received the money.

For a candidate with no plans to seek federal office again, charity offers a permanent exit for the money. Money saved for a future race stays in the committee. A charity gift moves it out of the committee for good. One donation can clear a large balance in a single transaction.

A federal campaign committee can give its leftover money to other candidates and to political action committees. Unlike transfers to party committees, these gifts are capped by federal contribution limits.

Federal campaign finance law lets a candidate's committee contribute to other federal candidates. That gives a candidate with money left after a race a way to support others. A winning lawmaker may back colleagues in competitive seats. A losing candidate may support allies who share the same priorities.

The ceiling sets this route apart from several other ways of disposing of surplus funds. A gift from one candidate's committee to another counts as a contribution under federal law. The receiving campaign must count it toward the limit for that donor, as it would any other contribution.

Limits on gifts to other candidates apply per election. A primary and a general election count as separate elections. A runoff or special election also counts on its own. A committee can give up to the limit for each election in which the recipient is running.

Gifts to PACs work differently. The limit on contributions to a PAC is generally counted per calendar year rather than per election. The FEC publishes the current figures in a contribution limits chart on its website. Some of those limits are adjusted every two years, so the dollar amounts can shift from one cycle to the next. A committee checks the chart for the period in which it gives.

The caps restrict how much of a large surplus this route can absorb. A committee with a big balance would need many separate recipients to spend it down this way. Party transfers and charitable gifts carry no such ceiling. A committee can combine several permitted options to close out its balance.

Both sides of the transaction are public. The giving committee reports the disbursement to the FEC, and the recipient reports the receipt. Those reports appear in the FEC's online database, where anyone can trace money moving from one campaign to another.

A federal campaign committee can return leftover money to the people and organizations that gave it. Under Federal Election Commission rules, refunding contributions is a permitted way to dispose of surplus campaign funds.

A refund sends the money back to its original source. The donor recovers what they gave, and the committee's balance drops by the same amount. No new recipient gets the funds. That sets refunds apart from gifts to a charity, a party or another candidate, where the money moves on to someone else.

Refunds can go to any kind of contributor. Individuals, political action committees and party committees all give to federal candidates. A committee can send each of them its money back.

Refunds are not only an end-of-race decision. Federal rules also require committees to deal with certain contributions they cannot keep. A gift above the federal contribution limit must be refunded, redesignated or reattributed. A gift from a source barred from giving, such as a foreign national, must be refunded. Those corrective refunds follow their own deadlines. They are separate from a choice to return surplus money after an election.

Primary races create another common refund situation. Federal law treats a primary and a general election as separate elections, each with its own contribution limit. Some donors give money designated for the general election before the primary is decided. If the candidate loses the primary, the committee cannot spend that money on a race the candidate will not run. FEC rules require the committee to refund those funds or have the donor redesignate or reattribute them.

Every refund is a disbursement the committee must report to the FEC. Refunds show up in the committee's regular disclosure reports, which are public. Anyone can search those filings on the FEC website to see which committees returned money to donors.

The IRS does not let taxpayers deduct contributions to political campaigns or candidates. A donor who receives a refund recovers money that never produced a tax deduction in the first place.

A federal campaign committee can use leftover money to pay off its debts. Since the Supreme Court's 2022 ruling in FEC v. Ted Cruz for Senate, money raised after Election Day can also repay a candidate's personal loans in full.

Campaigns often end owing money. Unpaid bills to vendors, consultants, pollsters or staff remain obligations of the committee after the votes are counted. Paying them is a permitted use of surplus funds. A committee that still carries debt can also keep raising money to settle it.

Candidate loans are a separate category. Federal law lets candidates lend their own money to their campaigns without a limit on the amount. The harder question has been how that money gets paid back.

The Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002, often called McCain-Feingold, set a ceiling. A campaign could use no more than $250,000 in contributions collected after the election to repay a candidate's loans. FEC rules added a timing requirement. Loan amounts above the threshold had to be repaid with pre-election funds within 20 days of the election.

Ted Cruz challenged the restriction. The Texas Republican lent his 2018 Senate campaign $260,000 just before the election. That was $10,000 more than the cap allowed to be repaid from post-election money. His campaign let the 20-day window pass, leaving that amount unrecoverable under the rules. It then sued the FEC.

The Supreme Court struck down the limit in a 6-3 decision issued in May 2022. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote for the majority. He concluded that the cap burdened political speech in violation of the First Amendment. The majority found the government had not shown that the limit targeted actual corruption or its appearance.

Justice Elena Kagan wrote the dissent, joined by Justices Stephen Breyer and Sonia Sotomayor. She argued that post-election donations used to repay a candidate's loan flow to the candidate personally. Donors giving after the vote also know who won, which she viewed as a corruption risk.

The ruling has the largest effect on self-funding candidates. Those who lend their campaigns large sums can now seek full reimbursement through fundraising after the election. Contributions raised to retire that debt remain subject to the standard federal contribution limits.

Campaign funds can pay legal fees connected to a federal race. They can also cover a recount, an election contest and the accounting needed to comply with Federal Election Commission reporting rules. Each of these expenses can draw on money that remains after Election Day.

Recounts and election contests take place after ballots are cast. A recount retabulates the votes in a close race. An election contest is a formal legal challenge to a certified result. Both can require lawyers and staff. A committee with surplus money can use it to cover those bills.

Legal fees must be tied to the campaign. The personal-use test in the Federal Election Campaign Act applies to legal bills as well. It asks whether an expense would exist regardless of the campaign or the candidate's duties as an officeholder. Campaign money cannot pay for a legal matter the candidate would face anyway.

Compliance costs are a steady expense for any federal committee. Every committee must have a treasurer. The committee's reports to the FEC itemize what it received and what it spent. Preparing those filings takes bookkeeping and accounting work. Campaign funds can pay for that work.

Presidential general election campaigns have used separate legal and accounting compliance funds, commonly called GELACs. These accounts hold money raised for legal and compliance expenses. That money is kept apart from the main campaign account.

Public financing adds another rule. A presidential nominee who accepts a public grant for the general election receives money from the Presidential Election Campaign Fund. Taxpayers direct money to that fund by checking a $3 box on their federal income tax returns. Checking the box does not change the filer's tax owed or refund.

Accepting the grant comes with conditions. The nominee agrees not to raise private contributions for the general election campaign. The legal and accounting compliance fund is the exception. A publicly financed presidential campaign must also return surplus public money to the U.S. Treasury.

Privately raised money left in a committee carries no such repayment requirement.

A candidate who wins federal office can keep spending campaign money on ordinary and necessary expenses connected to the duties of that office. Those costs still cannot be personal expenses.

The permission comes from the Federal Election Campaign Act. The provision, codified at 52 U.S.C. 30114, lists officeholder expenses as a permitted use of contributions. The same section also covers campaign spending, charitable donations and transfers to party committees.

A newly elected member of the House or Senate still controls a campaign committee after Election Day. Under this provision, the committee's funds can pay for some costs of the job itself, not only the costs of running for it.

Campaign money is separate from the official funds Congress provides each member. Those office allowances come from the U.S. Treasury and pay for staff and offices. Campaign funds come largely from donors and stay subject to election law.

The limit on officeholder spending is the personal use rule. A cost is personal if it would exist even if the person did not hold office. A home mortgage stays off-limits for a sitting lawmaker, just as it does for a candidate.

That leaves a judgment for each cost. The question is whether the expense arises from the work of holding federal office or from private life. The FEC interprets and enforces the personal use rule. A committee unsure about a specific expense can request an FEC advisory opinion before spending the money.

Members of Congress also answer to their own chamber. The House Committee on Ethics and the Senate Select Committee on Ethics issue their own guidance on how lawmakers may use campaign funds. A lawmaker spending committee money has to satisfy both federal election law and the rules of the chamber where they serve.

Officeholder spending does not happen out of public view. The committee reports these payments to the FEC as disbursements, the same way it reports any other spending.

A federal campaign committee can close only after it has no outstanding debts or obligations and has disposed of all its remaining money. It then files a termination report with the Federal Election Commission. Until that report is filed, the committee must keep submitting its regular disclosure reports.

The two conditions work together. A committee with cash in the bank cannot simply abandon the balance and close its books. A committee that still owes vendors, staff or lenders cannot close while those bills stand. The debts and the bank account both have to reach zero before termination is possible.

Disposing of the remaining money means sending it somewhere federal rules permit. Refunds to donors, gifts to charities and transfers to party committees are among the allowed channels. A committee that wants to wind down has to move its final dollars through one of them. Only then can it ask to terminate.

Debts can take longer to clear than cash. A campaign can finish a race still owing money to consultants, pollsters, advertising firms or the candidate. Those obligations must be paid off or otherwise resolved before the committee can end. A committee that cannot pay its creditors in full can submit a debt settlement plan to the FEC on Form 8.

The termination report is the committee's final disclosure. It accounts for the last money in and the last money out. It also shows that nothing is left in the account and nothing is still owed. Once the FEC accepts it, the committee's reporting obligations end.

Until that point, the paperwork continues. A committee that has stopped campaigning but still holds money or owes debts remains registered with the FEC. It must keep filing its regular reports on schedule, even when little happens between them.

Federal law sets no deadline for a committee to close. A committee that stays open keeps its reporting duties for as long as it remains registered. Some committees stay on the FEC's books for years after a candidate's last campaign.

The filings themselves are public records. The FEC posts committee reports on its website. Anyone can use them to follow a committee's money from its final campaign through its termination report.

StateBench: Testing how language models track state changes over long documents

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

We’re introducing StateBench, a benchmark for long-context state tracking—how well language models keep track of information about entities in long documents as they evolve.

State tracking underlies tasks from reading code, where variables are assigned and reassigned, to following a story as characters physically move around and objects change hands. For example, if a mystery novel states that “John gave Mary the knife” right before a murder occurs, it’s important to know that the state of each character has changed. However, not all state-tracking problems are created equal: some require only finding the latest update; harder ones require following a chain of changes, where one missed step can throw off the final answer. For example, a harder variant of our mystery state tracking would be if John gave Mary the item in his backpack, with it previously known that Edward put a knife in John’s backpack.

Transformers have well-documented theoretical limits on hard variants of state tracking with long dependency chains, helping motivate recurrent and hybrid architectures such as Gated DeltaNet that carry a running memory forward. Existing state-tracking tests tend to focus on the hardest cases, but as these designs become more common, researchers need to know which kinds of state tracking each architecture can handle. We would like to understand the differences between architectures not just on the hardest kinds of state tracking, but also on the simpler, potentially more common kinds.

To this end, we developed the StateBench benchmark to capture state-tracking problems of different complexity, which we used to evaluate the differences between language models with different architectures. We find that recurrent and hybrid models can generalize much farther than transformers when trained directly on these tasks, while frontier models including Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Opus 5.5, and GPT-6 Sol and Luna still break down on harder forms of state tracking as inputs grow longer.

StateBench consists of four difficulty levels that permit increasingly complex state update operations. For programmers, these levels of state tracking complexity can be understood as allowing different types of variable assignment operations:

These levels can be intuitively understood in terms of variable assignments, but they also represent theoretically motivated distinctions in state tracking rooted in Krohn–Rhodes theory, a branch of algebra that describes how state-tracking problems – which can be viewed as word problems over finite monoids – can be decomposed into atomic primitives. Each level in our hierarchy corresponds to a class of state-tracking problems that can be built out of a different set of primitives.

To compare the four levels fairly, we start with a general finite sequence of variable assignments and derive three easier versions from it, producing four comparable StateBench problems—one at each difficulty level. All four use the same number of variables and distinct values, with assignments appearing at the same points in the sequence; only the type of assignment changes.

We tested a range of open pretrained models on the r-trivial, aperiodic, and general finite levels of StateBench to see where their state tracking breaks down, and whether different architectures fail where theory predicts. Under standard theoretical assumptions, transformers can handle r-trivial robustly (and soluble and aperiodic to some degree) but are predicted to fail on general problems. In contrast, Gated DeltaNet with a more flexible memory update can, in principle, handle all four levels.

We started with open pretrained models, where the training data and context lengths are fully documented. These included two 7-billion-parameter models, Olmo 3 (a transformer) and Olmo Hybrid (which adds Gated DeltaNet layers), along with smaller models with 275 million and 2.7 billion parameters. All of these models were pretrained on the same data; none had seen contexts longer than 64,000 tokens during training, and we tested most of them on inputs well beyond that length.

When we scored each model, almost all performed well on r-trivial problems, lost accuracy on aperiodic ones as inputs grew, and stayed at chance on general finite problems. Problem format mattered here—the 275-million-parameter hybrid and Gated DeltaNet models scored near the ceiling on RULER-style r-trivial problems but were near chance on the same problems framed as spreadsheet edits. That gap is one reason StateBench tests the same underlying problem in several formats.

We then tested four frontier models – Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Opus 5.5, and GPT-6 Sol and Luna – asking them to read each sequence of variable assignments and generate the final value of every variable.

All four performed well on r-trivial and aperiodic problems. But on soluble and general finite problems, accuracy fell sharply as inputs grew longer, dropping to 13% or lower on the longest general finite problems. One likely factor is sensitivity; at these harder levels, many more steps can affect the final answer, leaving less room for a model to miss an update without getting the result wrong.

Next, we investigated whether models’ difficulty with the harder levels reflects limits of the architectures themselves, or whether pretraining simply doesn’t teach models to solve them. Targeted direct training is a more favorable setting that reveals to what degree state-tracking issues come from the underlying architecture and optimizer rather than the data used to pretrain a model.

We trained several 275-million-parameter models from scratch on StateBench: two transformer variants, two hybrids, and two Gated DeltaNet variants with different memory-update constraints. The models trained on sequences of up to 256 assignments and were tested on sequences as long as 64,000 assignments, or 250 times the maximum training length.

The architectures began to diverge as sequences grew longer. Gated DeltaNet and hybrid models generalized almost perfectly on r-trivial, aperiodic, and soluble problems out to the longest sequences we tested, tens of thousands of assignments. Transformers learned the easier levels but degraded on longer sequences, and they never learned general finite problems. On the hardest level, the Gated DeltaNet variant with a more flexible memory update generalized the farthest, staying near perfect out to roughly 10 times the length of the longest sequences it saw during training.

For Gated DeltaNet and hybrid models, the gap points to training rather than architecture; trained directly, they learned to handle state-tracking levels their pretrained counterparts couldn’t. Transformers were different—even trained directly, they could never learn the hardest level.

Some questions remain open. Our new proof shows that Gated DeltaNet with a more limited memory update (non-negative eigenvalues) can solve all aperiodic tasks and certain periodic tasks, such as tracking whether a repeated input has occurred an even or odd number of times, under the paper’s fixed-precision arithmetic with rounding. Its exact placement in the hierarchy remains open, including whether it can solve all soluble tasks and how numerical precision or combining it with attention layers affects its capabilities. StateBench also assumes a fixed number of variables, while real tasks often introduce new entities over time. In a long conversation, for example, new people and objects can appear as it unfolds, and the model has to begin tracking them too. The current framework doesn’t yet cover that setting.

As new flavors of recurrent and hybrid architectures emerge, understanding which kinds of state tracking each can handle could help guide how future systems are developed and trained. We hope StateBench gives researchers a finer-grained way to find where models break down—and to build training and evaluation data for the capabilities that still fall short. More fundamentally, we hope it serves as a North Star for researchers to develop new architectures with more robust state-tracking abilities compared to current models.

How to choose the right gaming monitor for you

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Refresh rates, size and screen technology all play a part.

Choosing the right gaming monitor is a lot like choosing the right outfit. You wouldn't wear the same thing to the gym as you would to the opera, while lifting weights in a tuxedo would inconvenience you and damage the tux. Similarly, a monitor that works great for one PC or console isn't necessarily the best fit for another and gamers who enjoy competitive shooters should look for different options than those who enjoy visually rich single-person narratives.

Only you can figure out exactly which monitor you should buy. To do that, you'll first need to understand which specs best suit your needs. Modern monitors have a lot more to them than resolution and frame rate. Different display panel types suit different uses and picture quality can vary between models. A quick crash course on the basics will get you ready to shop for the monitor you'll be happiest with in the long run.

Before we dive into specs, the first thing to consider when picking a gaming monitor is the existing hardware you'll pair it with. If your PC is decked out with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 or 5080, you can run games on the bleeding edge of performance and have real reasons to consider a top-shelf monitor with a 4K panel and an ultra-high refresh rate. On the other hand, if you're plugging the monitor into an older or lower-powered GPU, consider the performance you'll actually get in the games you play. If you aren't routinely seeing frame rates above, say, 90 fps, there's no reason to invest in a 240Hz monitor. Resolution and refresh rate move in opposite directions, so if you play competitive shooters and esports titles like Valorant, it's best to sacrifice pixels for higher frames, whereas players of visually rich first-person titles like Control: Resonant can make the opposite trade-off or find a balance in the middle.

Once you know the spec range you're looking for, you can move on to other concerns such as panel type, HDR and brightness support, port selection and VRR technologies like G-Sync. Size and resolution are personal preferences. Resolution is tied to graphical performance, but there are reasons other than gaming to consider a 1440p or 4K display. If you do a lot of photo or video editing, or if you watch a lot of video, a higher resolution can be worthwhile. Size is related to resolution, in the sense that the smaller of two monitors with the same resolution will have a higher pixel density  — a finer level of image detail, which can be measured in pixels per inch (PPI).

Esports players often prefer smaller monitors around 24 inches in order to see everything at once, while single-player gamers who value immersion may gravitate toward 27 inches or higher. Larger displays also support multitasking better, but work best when you sit farther back. You'll also need to consider your ideal aspect ratio. A squatter shape is better for productivity tasks, but 16:9 widescreen is the de facto aspect ratio for gaming. Some gamers enjoy ultra-widescreen monitors to fill their peripheral vision, but in games where HUD information is crucial, ultrawide displays can force you to look away from the action.

Refresh rate is often considered the most essential specification when choosing a monitor, and should be the first thing you settle on. As noted above, your GPU should dictate this. Monitors are now shipping with up to 1,000 Hz in lower-resolution modes, at which point you'd question whether you can see the difference even if you could push your hardware that far.

The average PC gamer in September 2026 was running an RTX 5070, 5060, 4060 or even a 3060, according to the Steam Hardware Survey. Accordingly, the sweet spot for the average gamer is likely to land somewhere from 144Hz to 240Hz on a 1440p or 1080p panel. If you have a similar configuration but want to play competitive, lightweight games, you may want to look for a 1080p panel with a significantly higher frame rate. Players of these titles with top-end hardware (such as an RTX 5080 or 5090) can look for 1440p or 4K panels at any refresh rate.

That said, only you can determine the real-world frame rates you're getting on your hardware. One way to do that is to turn on the Steam, NVIDIA, or Xbox in-game overlays while playing and enable the small heads-up frame rate counter they offer. You can also use a benchmarking tool like 3DMark and some games, including Cyberpunk 2077, have built-in benchmark tests. Even if your current monitor can't reach high frame rates, you'll still see a readout showing how many frames per second your graphics card is producing.

After playing multiple games that are representative of your general interests, note where you saw the highest frame rates. Unless you plan to upgrade your GPU in the next several years, there's no need to overspend on a monitor with an unachievably high refresh rate for your system.

If you're looking for a monitor to use with a console, it's worth noting that even the best current-generation consoles like the PlayStation 5 Pro or Xbox Series X don't exceed refresh rates of 120 Hz. That means you're better off putting your money toward a larger or higher-resolution monitor instead of buying a panel with blistering speeds you'll never utilize.

After determining the refresh rate, resolution and aspect ratio ideal to your setup, the biggest choice you'll make is the type of panel your monitor is fitted with. For most gamers, the two best choices are OLED and Fast IPS, in that order.

OLED is well known for its technically infinite contrast ratios, accurate colors and near-instant response times. Because each pixel is self-emissive, it produces its own light rather than relying on a backlight; OLED pixels simply do not illuminate where an image is truly black, meaning they stay black rather than turning dark grey in the way LCD panels do. That makes them ideal for gaming, especially if you value HDR performance.

However, OLED displays have their drawbacks. They require more care to prevent OLED burn-in, a condition in which leaving a static image on screen for too long leaves a permanent ghost of that image on the screen. Newer OLEDs address this with pixel-shifting that slightly moves the picture around and pixel-cleaning cycles that help keep wear and tear even across the display. If you're the sort of person who leaves your monitor on for hours when you're not using it, an OLED will force you to change that behavior.

OLEDs are also dimmer than LCDs and are consequently worse in well-lit environments. Some manufacturers offer matte coatings on their OLED models, which can help, but these displays look best in a dim or dark room. Text fringing is another issue, as is motion flicker and while the latest generation of OLED has come a long way in minimizing them, gamers who also work with documents and spreadsheets should take those issues into consideration before paying the higher cost for an OLED. Thatcost is perhaps the biggest downside, as even entry-level OLEDs start around $400 in late 2026. If you do choose to go OLED, scout for sales.

LCD panels lack the sheer wow factor of OLED, but they are more versatile. There are a range of LCD types, but Fast IPS is generally the best for gaming. IPS panels deliver excellent colors, great viewing angles and they have very consistent performance. However, regular IPS can introduce significant motion blur, which is reduced in a Fast IPS panel. For users who game in bright environments, plan to do productivity work on their gaming PC, or are on a budget, Fast IPS is an easy recommendation.

Gaming on a PC can be uneven, and one of the most frustrating issues you'll commonly encounter is screen tearing. This occurs when the GPU cranks out frames at a different rate than the monitor shows them, so the display shows parts of two frames at the same time. Substantial tearing can completely ruin a gaming session, but there's a surefire fix for screen tearing: variable refresh rate (VRR) technology. NVIDIA G-Sync, AMD FreeSync and the generic Adaptive Sync are all examples of VRR.

For a deeper dive, check out our article on screen tearing and how to fix it, but the bottom line is that you'll want G-Sync or FreeSync on your monitor. By now, it's an industry standard and you won't need to spend much extra money, if any, to get a monitor that has one of those two certifications.

G-Sync can cost extra because it uses a hardware module inside the monitor. However, NVIDIA has certified plenty of monitors as G-Sync Compatible. This means that a monitor doesn't need that module to work with G-Sync. Many FreeSync monitors are G-Sync Compatible.

Console gamers can completely ignore G-Sync, as no console uses an NVIDIA GPU. The Xbox Series X and Series S support FreeSync because of their AMD hardware, while the PlayStation 5 and PS5 Pro support only HDMI 2.1 VRR.

Regardless of your hardware, VRR only works with the right cable. Make sure you're using a cable with at least DisplayPort 1.2a. If you're using HDMI, some HDMI 1.4 and 2.0 connections will support VRR, but the most robust support comes with HDMI 2.1 onward.

For much of his career, Misha Kahn has moved between physical craft and digital experimentation, producing furniture through everything from improvised molds to virtual reality. His earlier work often carried the exaggerated, gravity-defying appearance of objects conceived on a computer screen. This time, he describes a shift toward something more immediate.

‘I think, the past handful of years, I really was doing so much work conceptually, digitally,‘ the artist tells designboom. ‘I think this show was really sort of a studio move.‘ Working partly from his studio in the woods, he describes a spiritual shift toward something ‘really tactile,’ with collage and material experimentation becoming central to the new pieces.

The construction underneath is equally unconventional. ‘We make the frames in the studio, and they’re welded so that the shapes can really flow,‘ Kahn explains. ‘I find that the most satisfying part is sort of the air between the cushions, because we’re so used to seeing a continuous form.‘ Some of the cushions appear loosely attached, raising questions about which parts can actually be moved, while additional pillow elements extend the composition even further.

The video presents an abstract sequence of actions in which materials are repeatedly manipulated and transformed, eventually producing a kind of juice. ‘I wanted to make this video, like, a really abstract version of what it feels like to make objects,‘ he explains, describing the experience of doing something, taking it apart, and putting it back together over and over again.

Kahn designed a series of plastic bottles to accompany the installation, using the familiar manufacturing process associated with commercial water bottles to produce unusual, doubled forms. Cat-sized foxes in Texas are in trouble. Big guard dogs could help.

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 3, 2026 5:10 PM EST

Two guard dogs could be protecting more than just the livestock grazing on the Rita Blanca National Grasslands in the Texas Panhandle. The working canines may be helping increase the odds of survival for the Lone Star State’s smallest fox.

The swift fox (Vulpes velox) is about the size of a house cat. While not federally listed as endangered, conservationists believe there are fewer than 250 left in Texas. They only occupy about 40 percent of their historic range, from Texas north to Alberta, Canada.

“The swift fox relies on the shortgrass prairie ecosystem once found across the Panhandle and into western Texas,” Haley Sloan, a doctoral student at Texas A&M, said in a profile. “Coyotes can take advantage of the cover provided by the taller vegetation that has grown into these areas, resulting in coyote predation being a leading cause of mortality in swift fox populations.”

Sloan is currently studying if livestock guardian dogs paired with a cattle herd can reduce swift fox mortality. The thought is that the dogs will act as a larger predator, and change the way the coyotes behave and use their habitat.

“The hope is that the dogs’ presence will deter coyotes, thereby reducing swift fox mortality and allowing the foxes to start using areas where the coyotes are being excluded,” Sloan said.

The dogs work in northwestern Texas’ Dallam County, the only known county in the state that still has a swift fox population. The swift fox’s small size and diet of insects, plants, and small rodents also means that they don’t pose any threats to cattle, sheep, and goats.

If incorporating these livestock guardian dogs with cattle herds alters the coyotes’ behavior, Sloan said that it offers two potential benefits. It could improve the chances of swift fox survival, while reducing the threat of livestock predation, especially during the vulnerable calving season.

To monitor the animals’ behavior, Sloan and the other researchers put GPS collars on coyotes and swift foxes ahead of this three-year-long project. The livestock guardian dogs were introduced with the cattle herd this summer. While it is too early to report any initial findings, the team has high hopes.

“I’m happy to report right now the dogs are doing great,” Sloan said. “The producer involved in the project hasn’t lost any calves to predation, and I have hope we’ll have similar positive outcomes for swift foxes in the vicinity of the herd.”
