Review: Cumulus Mountlite Down Jacket

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The Cumulus Mountlite is a very warm ultralight winter down jacket designed for winter hiking, ski touring, and climbing. Weighing just 12.9 ounces, it is insulated with 6.3 ounces of highly compressible 900-fill-power goose down, delivering an outstanding warmth-to-weight ratio. An adjustable hood, a #3 YKK zipper with dual sliders, and three zippered pockets round out this impressively warm jacket.

The Mountlite’s key benefit is weight efficiency. The jacket weighs 12.9 ounces in size medium, making it a lightweight option among dedicated winter layers. Of that total weight, 6.35 ounces is 900-fill-power Polish goose-down insulation. This high ratio means that roughly half the garment’s total mass is dedicated directly to trapping body heat. Premium down allows the jacket to compress efficiently and recover its loft quickly after being unpacked in cold environments.

To protect the down insulation from external moisture and wind, Cumulus utilizes 10D Pertex Quantum for the outer shell and liner. This fabric provides a windproof barrier while allowing interior moisture vapor to escape during low-intensity movement. The fabric is thin to minimize total weight but requires careful handling around sharp winter tools.

The hood is shaped to offer close coverage around the face and neck, helping to retain heat during windy stops. The zipper features a standard YKK zipper with 2 sliders that operates reliably in freezing conditions. For draft management, the jacket uses low-profile elastic wrist cuffs and an adjustable hem cinch to seal out cold air from below. The external pockets, one chest and two handwarmer pockets, are positioned to remain accessible while wearing a backpack hip belt or a climbing harness. The jacket is stuffable in its left hand pocket and has an external loop.

The jacket’s insulation is held in sewn-through baffles, which you wouldn’t expect in a jacket this warm. This isn’t the most efficient design because the stitching creates small cold spots with little or no insulation. It may also provide less loft and warmth than a box-baffle jacket. Still, sewn-through construction can be lighter, which is why I suspect Cumulus used it here. I’m honestly amazed at how warm this jacket is regardless of the baffle construction technique, and it is a delight to carry on summit hikes because it packs up so small when compressed.

The Mountlite has an athletic cut, which you should keep in mind when sizing. Its adjustable hood (rear toggle) is helmet-compatible, a useful feature for ski touring, climbing, and mountaineering. Even so, it still insulates very well when used without a helmet. The hood can help reduce heat loss during exposed stops and windy conditions.

The Cumulus Mountlite is an exceptional winter layer for anyone who values serious warmth without accepting unnecessary weight or bulk. Its 900-fill-power goose down, lightweight Pertex Quantum shell, thoughtful hood design, harness-friendly pockets, and compact packed size make it remarkably capable on winter hikes, ski tours, climbs, and summit pushes. Despite its sewn-through construction and delicate shell fabric, the jacket delivers impressive warmth in a package that is easy to carry and quick to deploy when conditions turn cold.

The Mountlite stands out as a highly refined piece of winter gear: exceptionally warm for its weight, versatile in the mountains, and small enough to keep in a pack on every outing. For cold-weather adventures where warmth, packability, and low weight matter most, it is an outstanding choice.

I am very inexperienced with options, and would like to know the less known possible risks of covered calls which I have researched and have concluded to be a low risk strategy.
I'd rethink that conclusion. Assume markets are reasonably efficient. If your strategy is low-risk, you should also expect it to have low returns. If you expect it to have normal equity-level returns, it isn't low-risk.

A covered call strategy trades the equity risk premium for the volatility risk premium. That isn't necessarily a bad thing, but you shouldn't expect to get full equity capital appreciation PLUS a bunch of option income on top of that. You should expect equity-level risk and return over the long term, with returns that will mostly, but not fully, correlate with the underlying ETF's.

The biggest risk of a CC strategy is that you are almost fully exposed to the downside. If the underlying tanks, so will your portfolio. You'll just be down slightly less because you got to keep the option premium.

Munoz, 23, who joined the Reds from Osasuna for £34million in the summer, came on in the 72nd minute at Anfield and made an instant impact.

Just 10 minutes after being introduced, the Spaniard found the back of the net. He picked the ball out on the left wing, played a one-two with Florian Wirtz before slotting past Senne Lammens.

It was a fine finish from Munoz, who now has four goal contributions in 476 Premier League minutes this season. He is becoming a valuable member of Andoni Iraola's squad and could prove to be a bargain signing if he continues to perform for the Reds as he did against United.

Liverpool have got a real gem in Munoz, and he is not being talked about enough. But it isn't a surprise given the impact Rio Ngumoha has, and is having, this season.

Bob Woodward Interviews Justice Kagan, Or Does Justice Kagan Interview Bob Woodward

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

Kagan sheds some light on her view about leaks, Dobbs, and the Court.

Last month I wrote about an excerpt from Bob Woodward's new book that reveals sources. Most of the sources are deceased, so there is little harm outing them. But Justices Kagan and Breyer are alive and well. Woodward reveals a remarkable conversation with the former. As Woodward would observe, it is less clear that he was interviewing Kagan than Kagan was interviewing him.

"Well, the conclusion of The Brethren was that the center of the court was in control," I said, meaning neither the left nor the right was in charge. "Yes, I prefer it that way," Kagan said. "That's exactly what it should be." That's not the way it is now, I noted. It is not, Kagan confirmed. How did you get such detail in the book? We had time, I said. Of course, it was a different era in which Scott and I were doing the reporting in the late 1970s. I started to tell her about Scott and how he had really discovered the Nixon Tapes because he found Alexander Butterfield. Not letting me off the hook, Kagan asked again, "How did you do it?" Well, we interviewed five sitting justices and 170 law clerks. We got documentation. That's extraordinary, Kagan said. How did you get the documents? The justices must have approved the clerks giving you the documents? I shook my head. Not that we were aware. You make clerks now sign nondisclosure agreements, I noted. "Yes," she said.

Over the years, I've speculated that Justices have authorized their clerks to leak information to the press. The premise of Kagan's questions is that Justices had authorized their clerks to speak with Woodward and Bernstein. But no, the clerks went rogue. If the clerks acted alone in the 1970s, perhaps they did so in 2022 with the Dobbs opinion.

I told her that Brennan and Marshall, the liberal justices on the court, would not help us. Well, who did? Rehnquist and White, I said. Kagan seemed surprised. I recounted the story of calling Justice Lewis Powell at the court one morning and him agreeing to meet. I said when and he said now. "I was in my pajamas, but told him I'd be there in half an hour." You must be fast, Kagan said. I was then, I joked. I was 79 now and slow.

Justice Kagan clerked for Justice Marshall, who apparently never discussed the sourcing for The Brethren.

Is all of this known about The Brethren? Kagan asked. I didn't have an answer so instead I dived into Blackmun's role in Roe v. Wade and how he had drafted the opinion at the Mayo Clinic library. "I thought the leak of the Dobbs decision was a public service," I told her, referencing the decision that had overturned Roe the previous year. I can't say I agree with that, Kagan said. Why do you think it's a public service? Well, the court took away a right that had been there for 50 years. The public needed to know so there could be a debate. Kagan didn't say anything.

I find it odd that Kagan said nothing in response. This is not the sort of statement that could trigger a recusal. She might have said "that debate nearly got my colleague assassinated." But she stayed silent.

I said from reporting more than 50 years on 10 presidents, I thought there was such a concentration of power in the presidency. Too much power.

She nodded. It's why Trump was able to get away with so much, I said. Kagan did not respond. Then I said, there's too much power in the Supreme Court maybe. She noticeably reacted to that, her head held a little higher, her posture stiffening. Her only reply was, "That's interesting."

She mustered a response here, but not to the leak question. Interesting?

"Are you happy on the court?" I asked her. No, I'm not, given the situation, Kagan replied. I was surprised at her candor.

Kagan likely didn't think she would be quoted.

As we parted ways, she smiled warmly and said she respected my work and I said I respected hers. Late that night I recorded a detailed voice memo of my conversation with Kagan for my assistant to transcribe. It is hard to tell who was interviewing who.

On Saturday, October 21, 2023, Elsa and I attended a dinner for the 50th anniversary of the Saturday Night Massacre hosted by the former Watergate special prosecutors at the WilmerHale law firm in downtown Washington. The main speaker was retired Supreme Court justice Stephen Breyer, who had been a junior Watergate prosecutor. Elsa sat next to Breyer at the dinner before switching seats with me. He had served 28 years on the Supreme Court and told her he was "forced out" so someone younger could take his place.

No one forced him out. He has life tenure. Instead, Breyer surrendered to the pressure. So much for judicial independence. Justices Alito and Thomas are often charged with being MAGA partisans, but they exhibited robust independence from political forces.

Woodward offers this anecdote about the Thomas confirmation hearing:

Later Gray told me it took five years for Clarence Thomas to recover. Justice Thomas's wife, Ginni Thomas, a conservative activist, approached me at a conference in Washington one day. She said after the hearings her husband was never really a whole person again. He hardened. Many conservatives, including attorney general for George H. W. Bush and for Donald Trump, Bill Barr, have said the hearings pushed Thomas to the hard right.

Van Cleef &amp; Arpels Explores the Secrets and Mysteries of Ancient Egypt

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The maison’s latest high jewelry collection taps into the mystique of the Valley of the Kings

After archaeologist Howard Carter and his team discovered King Tutankhamun’s tomb in 1922, regal diadems, golden collars, and bold cuffs brought a new wave of Egyptian Revival into the Art Deco movement. Van Cleef & Arpels took note and added Egyptian-inspired calligraphy, hieroglyphs, and semiprecious stones into its lexicon of deco bracelets, sautoirs, and brooches, bringing a historic feel to the frostbitten glamour that defined the roaring ’20s. Now, over a century later, the maison’s new Fascinating Egypt high jewelry collection explores more avenues of the region’s mystical splendor.

From opulent collars to sculptural rings, each design harkens back to the arts, values, and beliefs that defined the New Kingdom. But just as Egyptologists explored hidden messages beneath the grandeur of the 18th dynasty, Van Cleef conveys the mystique beneath its history. Shape-shifting necklaces function like secret passageways to new realms of expression, and Mysterioux brooches reincarnate sacred deities in gold and gemstones. Just take the Bennou, a terrestrial vessel for the sun god Ra, whose feathers of emeralds, marine-blue sapphires, lapis, cerulean slips of turquoise, mother of pearl, and drops of diamonds represent the colors of the earth. A blood-orange spessartite garnet with rays of diamonds hangs from a red spinel, like a drop of sunshine.

“There are a lot of real animals that were fashioned into amulets and small statues, likely to imbue the owners with some of their traits, but also to revere the creature and the benefits it provided,” archaeologist Randall Hixenbaugh tells Bazaar. His namesake gallery in Manhattan’s Design District is home to artifacts sourced in accordance with the ethical standards set by the International Association of Dealers in Ancient Arts.

Talismans were worn like supplements to enhance the human experience—cats to spark joy, mongooses to offer protection from snakes, and frogs for fertility. Stones of certain colors were valued for their connotations with abundance and immortality. “The Egyptians prized bright blue and green materials, which were associated with the Nile, the sky, and life itself. Lapis lazuli was rare and valuable and therefore associated with the pharaohs. Its extensive use on King Tut’s gold mask shows how highly regarded it was,” Hixenbaugh explains.

The cobalt stone finds its way into all kinds of Van Cleef’s latest creations. It’s used generously in the “Ornement de Saphir” necklace, whose central totem resembles an evil eye, with an oval-cut sapphire pupil plopped cleverly in an iris of pavé diamonds. Suspended from a panel of lapis lazuli interspersed with slivers of gold and baguette-cut rubies, the pendant is detachable and can be worn like a brooch, while its Corde chain incorporates clasps concealed beneath colorful rondelles that allow it to transform into a pair of bracelets. A concept conceived in the 1940s, the versatile signature speaks to the ongoing conversation between the house’s heritage and Egypt’s mystique.

Some of Van Cleef’s most striking interpretations of the ancient kingdom come through in Art Deco bracelets. The articulated cuff style was a staple of the 1920s, with ample real estate for planes of illustrious motifs to flourish. Instead of a painter’s palette, Van Cleef brought diamonds, emeralds, and rubies together like dots on a pointillist painting through the “mystery set” technique (a manner of setting gemstones sans prongs).

The house patented the method in the 1930s and has continued to enlist it to craft seamless gemscapes, extending into a series of “Paysage” bracelets. Clad in fields of gemstones, the deco-style pieces bring Egyptian royals, celestial skies, and monuments to life. Openwork, a technique that minimizes the use of solid metals, enables light to pass through each bejeweled mural, amplifying the high shine of each cuff. Other inconspicuous elements, like golden hieroglyphic nuggets, rest beneath articulated columns, like artifacts awaiting discovery.

The house’s bold fonts of design can be equally understood through the juxtaposition of lolly-like cabochons and sugar-white diamonds. Amid the sea of platinum and flashy Asscher cuts that defined Gatsby-era decadence, Van Cleef & Arpels brought teardrop emeralds into the mix. Suspended from pillars of diamonds set in platinum, the plump stones hung like enticing lozenges throughout a collaret necklace, circa 1929. The spellbinding masterpiece drew the eye of Egyptian royalty, Her Royal Highness Princess Faiza, who purchased the necklace in 1937. It was a convergence of brilliant facets and vivid, dome-shaped gems that bridged the gradual shift into midcentury modern shapes and matte stones. The provenance of the design influenced the maison’s updated rendition of the crown jewel, featuring luscious emeralds and slender pearls suspended from elongated pendants of white gold and diamonds.

By the 1950s, Van Cleef’s interpretations of Egyptomania embraced organic forms, space-age ellipses, and plots of semiprecious stones. Smooth, matte surfaces of turquoise, malachite, and coral enhanced the realism of biomorphic designs, like a scarab clip with wings of turquoise, circa 1957. (The house would lend the brooch to the Petit Palais for the debut of “Tutankhamun and His Time” nearly a decade later.) Such vibrant stones extended into contemporary aesthetics of the 1980s, such as a collar necklace slathered in slices of jade, stripes of onyx, and birds carved out of coral.

This collection revisited the era through the language of Ettore Sottsass and the Memphis Group, a 1980s decorative arts firm, for spherical shapes and elements of cubism to take flight. Some of Sottsass’s most recognizable pieces include a sterling silver fruit stand with zigzag pillars and a colorblocked bookstand named “The Carlton.” Van Cleef translates this friction between elements smooth and sharp, glossy and matte into pieces like a saucer-shaped Mythe Celeste clip, coated in sheets of lapis and malachite with buttons of coral—elements beheld by the Egyptians as modalities of abundance and immortality. By contrast, a Frise Divine ring juxtaposes an oval-shaped Sri Lankan sapphire with a rigid tile of coral, and rows of diamonds set in zigzag streams of gold.

All creations in the Fascinating Egypt œuvre are united by tradition, a preservation of heritage echoed in the foundations of other art forms, like ballet. In fact, the house’s seamless dances of emeralds, rubies, and diamonds compelled George Balanchine to choreograph Jewels for the New York City Ballet in 1931—a moment that came full circle when the jewelers debuted a collection of ballerina clips and brooches a couple of years later. The nature of their anatomically sound arms, gilded in gold and ribbons of gemstones, extends into the collection’s Muse Éternelle brooches. Modeled after Cleopatra, the palm-sized figures are enrobed in a variety of dazzling threads of gold, strewn with diamonds, sapphires, rubies, and emeralds.

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

As firefighters continue to battle a nearby blaze, Shenandoah National Park is implementing rigid fire restrictions due to exceptionally dry conditions and heightened wildfire risks going into Thanksgiving week.

In a Facebook post park officials explained: “These restrictions protect public safety given the elevated risk of wildland fires throughout the park. Shenandoah National Park, Skyline Drive and other facilities and services remain open as scheduled.”

Under these new restrictions, visitors to the area will have to rethink their cooking plans since they are temporarily unable to build, attend, or maintain open fires anywhere within the park’s boundaries, including in established pits, grates, and in grills. These regulations also apply to picnic areas, shelters, and huts in the park.

These rules also impact backpackers who rely on portable stoves while recreating in the area. Liquid and wood-fuel camp stoves cannot be used within the park until further notice. At the moment, campers can still use canister stoves like Jet Boils and MSR Pocket Rockets.

This announcement came just one day after the White Oak Flat Fire erupted on U.S. Forest Service land about 50 miles southwest of Shenandoah National Park. Since Friday, the fire has grown to 1,800 acres, with 49% containment. So far, 131 wildland personnel have responded to the fire including handcrews, wildland engines, bulldozers and two helicopters. Wildland firefighters are using active suppression strategies to contain the fire. No evacuations have been reported, and a cause for the fire has yet to be released.

The NPS identifies two primary wildfire seasons in Shenandoah that run from February to May and October through December. Wildfires are relatively common in Virginia during the month of November although the frequency and impacted acreage vary by season and seasonal driving factors like low humidity and breezy weather.

Shenandoah park officials expect these restrictions to remain in place until the region receives enough precipitation to reduce fire risks.
