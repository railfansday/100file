Simple Plan Are Meeting a Whole New Generation of Fans, 25 Years In

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Drummer Chuck Comeau talks TikTok, Good Things Festival, and why a farewell tour is "not in the cards"

Simple Plan have been playing the same songs for 25 years. Lately, they’re playing them to a lot of people hearing them live for the first time.

“Every night we ask people, ‘Hey, how many people are here for the first time?’” drummer Chuck Comeau tells Billboard. “Sometimes it’s like 85% have never seen the band.”

The Montreal pop-punk band is drawing fans who weren’t born when “I’m Just a Kid” arrived in 2002, alongside the original listeners who are now bringing their own kids to shows. Some of the younger crowd have even started calling Simple Plan “classic rock.” Related Deep Purple's Ian Gillan Shares Encouraging News After Undergoing Surgery Richard Lloyd, Television Guitarist, Dies at 75 Benson Boone Brings Young Fan Battling Cancer Onstage at Brisbane Concert Explore Simple Plan See latest videos, charts and news

“And you’re like, ‘What are you talking about?’” Comeau says.

“There’s nothing cooler than what’s happening to our band right now,” he adds.

Much of that can be traced to “I’m Just a Kid” going viral on TikTok, where it soundtracked a challenge encouraging users to recreate their childhood photos. Comeau calls the trend a “gigantic gift,” but says meeting fans online has always come naturally to the band, which was filming backstage antics and sharing them through its website and DVDs long before TikTok existed.

“It’s the same thing,” he says. “It’s just a new platform.”

The renewed attention builds on a strong early run. The band’s 2004 album Still Not Getting Any… debuted at No. 3 on the Billboard 200, while “Perfect” and “Welcome to My Life” reached Nos. 24 and 40, respectively, on the Billboard Hot 100.

Next up is a return to Australia for Good Things Festival, where Simple Plan join a bill led by Limp Bizkit and featuring Spiritbox, the All-American Rejects, Bad Religion and MxPx. The festival hits Melbourne’s Flemington Racecourse on Dec. 4, Sydney Showground on Dec. 5 and Brisbane Showgrounds on Dec. 6, marking the band’s third trip Down Under in three years.

“We love Australia. What can I say?” Comeau says. “Any excuse, we come back.”

The lineup mixes pop-punk with nu-metal, hardcore and heavier rock, the kind of bill Comeau admits might once have made him nervous. “Being on this lineup would have been a bit more intimidating 25 years ago,” he says.

He credits streaming with breaking down the scene boundaries that once divided fans, making it easier for younger listeners to move between genres. The band has seen it firsthand at European festivals, sharing stages with acts like Slipknot.

“At first, you’re a little terrified,” he says. “You’re like, ‘How’s that gonna go?’ And then you actually realize that people are stoked.”

He has his own Good Things wish list. Bad Religion, whom he calls his favorite band, are near the top, along with Swedish punks Millencolin, who are playing Pennybridge Pioneers in full. He’s also keen to finally see Limp Bizkit live. “I’ve seen a lot of crazy videos right now that are popping up everywhere,” he says.

Meanwhile, Simple Plan have started writing their seventh studio album, with work set to continue into 2027. There’s no release date, but Comeau says the band has a batch of songs it’s excited about, and no intention of rushing.

“We’ve been around for 25 years and we only have six albums,” he says. “So we take our time.”

The record will follow 2022’s Harder Than It Looks, and Comeau says the aim is to refine the band’s sound rather than overhaul it. “We know who we are,” he says. “We’re not going to turn around and make a black metal record.”

The harder challenge is competing with the band’s own past. Songs that soundtracked fans’ teenage years carry a weight that new material has to earn.

“When you get to this stage of your career, you’re almost playing against yourself,” Comeau says. “You’re playing against your history.”

“You’re not the new shiny young band anymore,” he adds. “So you’ve got to work harder to have people pay attention.”

That’s why he won’t put a deadline on it. “There’s never a good time for a bad record, and there’s never a bad time for a good record,” he says.

Next year also marks 25 years since the band’s debut album, No Pads, No Helmets…Just Balls. Comeau says a special-edition vinyl and a few anniversary performances are on the table, but not a full album tour.

“I think we’re bigger than just one album,” he says. “But that album was really consequential for us. It’s the one that started it all.”

Another milestone arrives March 9, 2027, when the band releases its first children’s picture book, I’m Just a Kid: The Story of Simple Plan, illustrated by Jomike Tejido. Comeau says it grew out of watching the band’s original fans grow up and start families of their own.

“We were with them throughout their difficult moments,” he says, recalling how songs about breakups, school and friendships became part of listeners’ teenage years. “Fast-forward 20 years, 25 years, now they’re starting families, they’re having kids. We want to be there with them in this new chapter of their lives.”

The book explores friendship and perseverance and includes nods for longtime fans. “It’s as much for the parents as it is for the kids,” Comeau says.

For all the anniversaries and nostalgia, Simple Plan have never broken up, gone on hiatus or staged a reunion, which is rare among their peers. Comeau believes the years since the pandemic have been some of the band’s best, with bigger crowds and a sharper focus on the live show.

“When we were young, you were going out and you were partying,” he says. “Now it’s like, no, we don’t do that anymore. We’re all doing yoga and stretching.”

“We’ve always just kept going,” he says. “I feel like there’s a lot of work to be done.”

“There’s no talk about a farewell tour,” Comeau says. “Not in the cards for now.”

The third option is widespread because of its convenience, but there is a hidden side that not everyone is aware of.

According to figures from Deutsche Umwelthilfe, cited by Lavazza in its award submission, around 20 billion coffee capsules are used, and therefore discarded, every year. Germany alone consumes around 3.4 billion, generating approximately 13,500 metric tons of waste annually.

Hailing from Italy, a country where coffee is both a rule and a tradition, the multidisciplinary team, led by Florian Seidl and joined by Angelica Rella, Marco Rotundo and Nicola Bettoni, was well aware of this problem.

Their vision went beyond replacing capsules with a less harmful form of packaging. Instead, they designed a system built around a tab made entirely of coffee. No plastic, aluminum, additives or coatings. Alongside the tabs, they completed the ecosystem by designing a compatible machine, packaging, accessories and a connectivity system.

Lavazza Tablì considers environmental care from production through maintenance and everyday use. The machine is made using post-consumer plastics, and its construction takes repairability and low energy consumption into account. Moreover, because the tabs are made entirely of coffee, they generate only organic waste.

This commitment to sustainability has been key to securing the Special Prize with designboom. The Seoul Design Award’s purpose is precisely to promote projects that have achieved harmony between people, society and the environment.

Most lithium comes from desert ponds or hard-rock mines, and a new lab method skips both

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Engineers pulled lithium from salty water in the lab using a solvent that reacts to heat, a step toward reaching brines that evaporation ponds can’t use.

The lithium in a battery usually comes from one of two places: a hard-rock mine, or a desert pond where the sun bakes salty water for up to two years.

Columbia University engineers have now pulled lithium out of salty water in the lab with neither one. Their solvent takes up lithium at room temperature and lets it go when the temperature rises.

Ngai Yin Yip, an associate professor of Earth and Environmental Engineering at Columbia, did the work with four co-authors. It’s a proof of concept. The team mixed salty water in the lab to copy the chemistry of the Salton Sea, a geothermal region in California.

About 40% of lithium production starts as brine, salty water that collects in large reservoirs under deserts. Nearly all of that lithium comes out through solar evaporation. Mining companies pump the brine into sprawling ponds and wait for the water to evaporate.

Evaporation ponds need a lot of dry, flat land, so they work in places like Chile’s Atacama Desert and parts of Nevada. They also use large volumes of water in places that can barely spare it. Hard-rock mining, the other main source, comes with steep tradeoffs of its own.

“There’s no way solar evaporation alone can match future demand,” Yip said. “And there are promising lithium-rich brines, like those in California’s Salton Sea, where this method simply can’t be used at all.”

The Salton Sea region holds enough lithium, by one estimate, for more than 370 million electric vehicle batteries.

Lithium-bearing brine turns up elsewhere in the U.S. too. In Arkansas, a U.S. Geological Survey-led study estimated 5 million to 19 million tons of lithium in brine beneath the southwestern part of the state.

In Pennsylvania, analysts who studied company data on wastewater from 515 gas wells found lithium in it as well.

The solvent is a liquid chemical that behaves differently depending on temperature. At room temperature, it attracts water. When it warms, it repels water.

The team mixed the solvent with brine at room temperature, and the solvent took up water and lithium together. Then the team warmed it a little.

The solvent now repelled water, so it released the lithium and the water as a purified stream. It returned to its starting state, ready to reuse.

The warming step needs only modest heat. “It can be powered by low-grade heat from waste sources or solar collectors,” Yip said.

In brine, lithium mixes with sodium and potassium, two metals that behave a lot like it, and separating the three is hard. The solvent still preferred lithium to sodium across versions with different chemical structures, by as much as about 10 to 1.

The solvent kept that preference even when lithium was 1,000 times scarcer than sodium or potassium in the water. In the simulated Salton Sea brine, the preference reached about 13 to 1 over sodium and 24 to 1 over potassium. So the solvent still separated lithium when lithium made up only a tiny share of the metals in the water.

Magnesium, another common contaminant in lithium brines, also dropped out as a solid, because the solvent is alkaline, the opposite of acidic. In the Salton Sea mixture, the team removed all of it.

The team reused one batch of solvent for four rounds. Together, the four rounds recovered nearly 40% of the lithium in the simulated brine. The solvent kept its preference for lithium throughout.

So about 60% of the lithium went unrecovered after four rounds. The team hasn’t yet optimized the system for yield or efficiency, so 40% reflects an early version of the method.

The team ran its tests in the lab, in batches, on brines it mixed itself. Real brine from the ground may hold more impurities than the lab mixes did, and the team still has to show how the solvent handles them. The same goes for its performance at the size of a working plant.

Whether recovery rises above 40% once the team tunes the system remains an open question. Columbia has invited companies that want to collaborate on the technology, or license it, to express their interest.

The full study was published in the journal Joule.

Can't Turn Ignition Switch? Check for Locked Steering

Got a service customer complaining he or she can't turn the ignition switch to start the engine? The reason could just be the steering column lock pin is engaged with the front wheels turned. Here's what typically happens:

Some folks use the steering wheel to help support themselves while climbing in or out of the vehicle. This is very common among elderly or disabled drivers. Doing this turns the front wheels, which twists the tires against the ground. The twisted tires apply torque to the steering column. With the ignition switch in LOCK (0), the steering column lock pin is engaged. But the applied torque on the steering column binds the lock pin, making it tough to turn the ignition switch. The ignition switch won't turn to ON (II) if the lock pin isn't disengaged from the steering column.

Tell your customer there's nothing wrong with the ignition switch itself. All he or she needs to do is turn the steering wheel from side to side while turning the switch. This usually releases the lock pin so the engine can be started. In some cases, though, your customer may need to give a good tug on the wheel. This could easily happen when parking on a hill and the wheels are turned sharply before the engine is shut off.
