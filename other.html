Cost per task: Using cheap classifiers to route across Gemini models and thinking levels

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Per-token API prices keep falling, yet engineering bills for autonomous coding agents keep climbing. Price per million tokens isn't the metric that actually governs production economics anymore. The dominating metric is now cost per successful task: total spend across every loop iteration, tool call, retry and internal thinking pass divided by the number of tasks that pass verification.

When an agent calls a high-thinking reasoning configuration for every micro-decision in its harness, you're paying deep-reasoning prices for questions a lightweight configuration already answers accurately. To prevent this unnecessary overhead, consider adding a lightweight routing layer that can intercept routine decisions before they ever reach an expensive reasoning model.That is the premise behind wiring TypeSafe's Jev classifier in front of Google Antigravity's model calls. Rather than generating text token by token, Jev—a specialized structured-output classifier designed for high-throughput decision pipelines—evaluates structured classification primitives in a single forward pass: a Choice from a list, a numeric Score or a Noul, which returns a 0 to 1 probability for a binary statement.

For full disclosure, Google has no partnership with TypeSafe, and this article is not an endorsement of TypeSafe or its products. Jev is used here as one example of a lightweight classifier, and the same routing pattern can be built with other classifiers, including Google-native ones. The Getting started with discriminative models codelab, for example, runs the same kind of classifier on DiffusionGemma, Google DeepMind's open-weights model, in your own Google Cloud project.

We ran a 50-question, auto-graded, coding-agent benchmark against live Gemini and Jev APIs to test whether a near-free classifier can route each task to the cheapest model that still gets it right. Here's what we learned:

The naive way to ask Jev to route a coding task is a single broad Choice question: simple or complex. When we tested that first on 16 coding-agent tasks, Jev routed nearly everything to simple, even tasks requiring an LRU cache trace or modular exponentiation. TypeSafe's documentation warns against this exact anti-pattern. If a question weighs multiple factors or requires a meta-judgment, decompose it into an atomic check.

When we replaced the broad Choice question with one atomic Noul question, it fixed the separation immediately. The question was: does answering this require performing more than one sequential calculation, simulation step or logical deduction, rather than a single direct lookup, format check or one-step classification?

On those initial 16 tasks, Jev routed 13 of 16 in agreement with our hand-labeled difficulty on the first run and 14 of 16 on the second pass. Many engineering teams ask whether they even need a classifier API when they can write a zero-cost regex and prompt-length heuristic in Python, matching words like sequence, modulo and race condition or escalating any prompt over 35 words.

On the 16 development questions used to write those exact rules, the local heuristic scored 15 of 16, or 93.8%. To test whether that heuristic survived contact with realistic coding-agent traffic, we froze its code without changing a character and added 34 unseen held-out tasks across seven coding-agent buckets: fact extraction, schema validation, short classification, single-hop code checks, state simulation traces, math or logic chains and subtle language-semantics bugs.

On the 34 held-out questions, the frozen heuristic dropped 23.2 percentage points to 70.6%. Realistic simple tasks such as extracting a port from a Kubernetes YAML manifest, a file path from a unified git diff header or a line number from a Python traceback exceeded 35 words and triggered false escalations. Conversely, concise multi-step code traces such as mutable default arguments across calls, Python dictionary key hashing on {1: "a", True: "b", 1.0: "c"} and C short-circuit evaluation lacked the heuristic's hardcoded puzzle words and were falsely routed to the simple path. Jev classified all 34 unseen tasks with 100.0% agreement, 34 of 34, reaching 48 of 50, or 96.0%, across the full suite.

A common mistake when building a fast-path router is assuming that setting a low maxOutputTokens cap on a reasoning model will make it spend less on easy tasks. Our 50-question data shows why that assumption fails.

When we compared gemini-3.8-flash with maxOutputTokens=300 against gemini-3.8-flash with maxOutputTokens=4096 while leaving thinkingConfig at the API default, both configurations spent almost identical thinking tokens on the 27 tasks Jev labeled SIMPLE:

Capping the model's output at 300 tokens didn't save any money on easy tasks. The cap is only a ceiling, and easy answers never came close to it. On the easy tasks, the capped version actually cost slightly more than the uncapped one. All the cap did was break 7 of the 25 hard tasks. On six of them, the model ran out of room while still reasoning and never gave an answer. On the seventh, it squeezed its reasoning to fit, finished on time and got the answer wrong.

So a Jev router that sends easy tasks to the capped model and hard ones to normal Flash got all 50 right. It still cost slightly more than skipping the router and using normal Flash for everything, and it was slower. If your cheap option doesn't actually make the model think less on easy tasks, no router can save you money. Not even a perfect one that costs nothing.

The obvious way to spend less on thinking is to ask the model to think less. So we ran a second round of tests with two lighter options: Flash-Lite, which doesn't think at all by default, and Gemini 3.8 Flash with its thinking level set to low. We added those results to the same results file, matched question by question.

Jev's own decision is a small share of the total and comes from the first round. Speed comparisons against default Flash or Pro mix the two rounds, so read those as approximate.

The strongest setup without a router was Gemini 3.8 Flash on low thinking. It solved all 50 tasks. That's the number a router had to beat.

The Jev router beat it. It sends every task to Flash-Lite first and escalates only what Jev flags as complex. It also solved all 50 tasks, and it can help reduce both cost and response time compared with running low thinking on everything. On prompts this short the cost difference per task is small, so for an interactive agent the shorter wait is the bigger benefit.

Why it works. On its own, Flash-Lite answered every simple task in half a second but missed 6 of the 25 hard ones. Jev flagged all 6 as complex with high confidence and escalated them. That brought its cost close to what a perfect router would achieve.

The keyword heuristic was cheaper, but only because it got things wrong. It sent 5 hard tasks to Flash-Lite, and 2 of them failed, for 48 of 50.

Escalating to a heavier setting didn't pay here. Routing hard tasks to Flash on default thinking was cheaper than running default thinking everywhere. It still cost more than running low thinking on everything, which already solved all 50. A heavier escalation target only makes sense when the low setting starts failing.

Limits: one run per setup, 50 prompts with hand-assigned difficulty and simple automatic grading.

Two things in our results only show up once you look past it.

Routing and caching pull in opposite directions. Our test prompts were short, standalone tasks, and on those the Jev router helped reduce both cost and response time. Real coding agents often look different. They send the same big block of context on every turn, such as a map of the repository and the tool definitions. Agent Platform Context Caching stores that block so you pay 75% to 90% less to resend it. The catch is that a cache belongs to one model. If your agent switches between Flash-Lite and Flash from turn to turn, it either keeps two caches or loses the discount. The practical split: route short, self-contained tasks like log parsing or schema checks, and keep long, context-heavy turns on one model.

Match the model to the workload. Gemini 3.1 Pro is built for deep, multi-step reasoning, and it brings that same depth to every request, including short lookups that don't need it. For a workload made mostly of quick checks and extractions, a Flash configuration is the better fit, and Pro is best saved for the tasks that need its reasoning depth. Model configurations can also apply different safety settings to security-related prompts. In our run, the Pro configuration declined two short code-security review prompts that Flash answered. If your agent performs security reviews, test those prompts on the configuration you plan to use.

Other ways to build the router. Jev is one way to get a fast classifier, but not the only one. The Getting started with discriminative models codelab runs the same Choice, Score and Noul questions on DiffusionGemma, Google DeepMind's open-weights model, hosted on a Compute Engine GPU VM or on Cloud Run in your own Google Cloud project. An open-source server exposes the same API in front of it, so the routing code in this article only needs a different endpoint. Our results come from Jev, so measure accuracy and latency on your own tasks before switching, and keep in mind that a self-hosted model is billed by the GPU-hour rather than by the token.

Taken together, the results give three practical rules.

Start with Flash on low thinking before you build a router. It solved all 50 tasks and cost considerably less than the default setting. Lowering the output token limit is not a substitute, because it doesn't reduce how much the model thinks on easy tasks. Set the thinking level directly, with thinkingLevel: "LOW".

Use Jev to route short, standalone tasks. Send simple tasks to Flash-Lite and hard ones to Flash on low thinking. Ask Jev one simple yes-or-no question per factor. In our test, this helped reduce cost and response time compared with running Flash on low thinking for everything, and came close to what a perfect router would achieve. Keep long, context-heavy turns on a single model so caching keeps working. For high-volume warehouse workloads, Jeff Nelson's BigQuery and Cloud Run cascade pattern applies the same idea.

Measure what your agent actually spends. Send your agent's traces and thinking-token counts to Cloud Logging and BigQuery, so you can see which tasks drive your spend and catch truncation or retries that inflate it. The benchmark code will be published after open-source review.

In summary, the most cost-effective way to run an agent is to match each task to the model that fits it. Start with a fast model on a light setting. You'll often find it already gets the job done. Then add a small, almost free classifier like Jev to send only the hard tasks to a stronger model, and ask simple yes-or-no questions rather than one broad one. Judge every option by what it costs per task done right, not per token.

What’s a ‘Winyah’? Fans of This Edgy Folk-Rock Band Can Tell You

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

What on earth is a Winyah? For many, it’s a bay in South Carolina. But for a growing audience of indie-rock fans, it’s the name of their new favorite band.

Named after that very body of water — Winyah Bay — in their home state, the five-piece mixes hard-driving Southern rock with youthful folk stylings to join a rock-edged new wave of bands like Dexter and the Moonrocks, Fox N’ Vead, and Cigarettes at Sunset. Winyah first became an entity in 2023 and played Bonnaroo in 2024, before dropping their debut album, Lot to Learn, the following year. That gave rise to a tour that featured a Lollapalooza showcase and back-to-back sellouts at New York’s Bowery Ballroom. Now, Winyah’s challenge is to harness that momentum with their sophomore LP, Right Here, Right Now, which dropped in mid-September.

Right Here, Right Now is their second LP on Evan Honer’s Cloverdale Records label and features 11 tracks, nearly all of which were written on bass player Stephen Russell’s back porch in September 2025, when the group caught a break between tour dates.

“It’s the first body of work that we ever wrote all together,” frontman Thomas Rowland says. “We came out of it with some songs that we really liked, but it was crazy. You really do have your whole life to write the first album and then such a short time to write the second one. It was a learning experience. It was us figuring out how to write together, and we’re still learning.”

Winyah is Rowland, Russell, keyboardist Robert Buffington Jr., guitarist Luke Gordon, and drummer Jake Riley. They took root playing bars in beach and college towns across South Carolina, and, quite quickly, realized they were on to something. They wrote the songs that make up Lot to Learn to give themselves enough original material to fill a set and, 25 million streams later, are figuring out the rest on the fly.

“Soundchecks, a little bit,” Gordon says of how the band shapes its musical identity. “As time grew between writing the first album and the second album, we all started collectively really enjoying each other’s influences and getting into them a lot more than we were before. “Smoky Steel” Is the Simple Color Technique That Instantly Adds Shine and Dimension to Gray Hair

Lustrous, modern, and endlessly sophisticated

The notion that gray hair lacks versatility couldn't be further from the truth: from “silver framing” to “frosted hair,” innovative color trends keep proving how chic it can be. The latest to join the roster? “Smoky steel.”

With its cool, dark undertones and soft, silvery dimension, this modern take on gray hair makes a compelling case for embracing the shade at every age. Seen to great effect on Andie MacDowell and, more recently, Olivia Colman, smoky steel strikes the perfect balance between sophisticated and modern—whether worn in long cascading curls, cut into a sleek bob, or styled in a polished topknot.

The hue's depth makes it even more appealing. Rather than a classic uniform gray, smoky steel adds density by combining dark, ashy tones with cool silver, creating dimension in the light. “By adding subtle beige, taupe, or mink warmth into gray, the color looks more luminous against the skin as it creates a healthy, diffused glow,” Adam Russell, creative master colorist at Josh Wood, previously told Harper’s Bazaar. “Warm grays also blend beautifully with naturally graying hair, so the growing-out process is far more seamless.”

This is especially true for smoky steel, which sits somewhere between classic silver and ashy brunette. Its softer, multitonal finish blends more easily with natural grays, creating a result that feels both effortless and polished. “These shades balance warm and cool pigments, keeping the color looking chic rather than yellow or brassy,” says Russell.

A skilled colorist can also soften the contrast between sharper roots and lighter lengths, adding shine and dimension with strategic highlights. However, allowing silver strands to emerge without coloring can be just as striking.

Ultimately, smoky steel reminds us that gray hair doesn’t mean compromising on a dynamic, multidimensional look. If you've ever had a reason to reconsider your next hair appointment, this might be it.

Planetary wreckage around a dead star may hint at Earth’s future

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mysterious rings of planetary debris around a dead star suggest that rocky worlds may survive stellar death, offering clues to Earth's distant future.

When a star like the Sun nears the end of its life, it swells dramatically, potentially swallowing the planets closest to it.

Around one dead star, though, the wreckage didn’t settle into a single ring, the way it has around every other dead star with a known gas disc. It formed two rings with a clear gap between them.

The team suspects that two rocky bodies survived the star’s transformation and that each may be supplying gas to a separate ring.

Astronomers call small rocky bodies like these planetesimals.

Nicola Gentile Fusillo, an astronomer at the University of Trieste in Italy, led the study.

“We have known for some time that planets may survive beyond the transformation of their central star, but this is the first example of a complex system with a double debris disc and potentially two surviving planetesimals,” he said.

Neither body has been seen directly. The team inferred both of them from the way the gas around the star behaves.

Near the end of its life, a star like the Sun expands and sheds its outer layers, engulfing nearby planets. Rocky bodies that survive can break apart.

Their pieces end up circling the dead star in a flat disc of dust and gas, and some of that rubble falls onto the star.

What’s left of the star itself is a white dwarf, the dense, burnt-out core. By the team’s estimates, this one holds about half the Sun’s mass in a ball roughly one and a half times as wide as Earth.

This white dwarf, called WD J1328–1450, became one somewhere around 73 million years ago.

The dusty disc around it is one of the brightest ever found around a white dwarf. Only a small share of these discs also contain gas. This one does.

The team studied the star with the X-shooter instrument on the European Southern Observatory’s Very Large Telescope in Chile.

The instrument spreads a star’s light out by color. In a spinning disc of glowing gas, the gas moving toward Earth and the gas moving away split each bright line into two peaks.

All 21 gas discs known before this one show that pair of peaks. This star’s calcium and oxygen lines show four. The team matched them to two rings of gas, one inside the other.

The inner ring stretches from about 60,000 to 147,000 miles (97,000 to 237,000 km) from the star.

The outer ring starts about 234,000 miles (376,000 km) out, close to the Moon’s distance from Earth, and reaches roughly 835,000 miles (1.34 million km).

Together, the rings span a region nearly twice the Sun’s diameter.

The shape of the lines held steady between observations in 2021 and 2023, about two years apart.

Co-author Felipe Lagos-Vilches is an astronomer at the University of Warwick in the UK and at the Universidad Técnica Federico Santa María in Chile.

“The structure we see in this system is particularly intriguing because the two gaseous rings are remarkably stable and remain separated by a gap,” he said.

When gas drifts away from where it formed, it cools and turns back into solid grains. So the gas stays close to its source.

Around another white dwarf, SDSS J1228+1040, astronomers had already picked up a signal in the gas that repeats about every two hours.

Astronomers could explain it only with a dense solid body inside the disc, making gas as it interacts with the dust.

Gentile Fusillo’s team argues the same thing is happening here, twice. Gas made in two separate places would form two separate rings.

At such close distances, the white dwarf’s gravity places severe constraints on the size and strength of any surviving rocky body.

The inner body is probably the size of a typical asteroid, no more than about 107 miles (172 km) across. The outer one could be far bigger, as large as Pluto or even our Moon.

The gas contains calcium, oxygen, magnesium and iron, a mix that matches the rocky material in our own solar system. But magnesium glows only in the inner ring.

If the outer ring had the same balance of magnesium to calcium, the magnesium would have shown up clearly. It didn’t.

The missing magnesium is a clue, because different kinds of rock contain different amounts of it.

Earth’s mantle, the thick rocky layer under the crust, has about eight times as much magnesium as calcium. Earth’s crust has less than twice as much.

The chemical differences suggest the rings could contain material from two different rocky bodies.

Or they may have come from two layers of a single broken planet, with magnesium-rich mantle in one ring and magnesium-poor crust in the other.

“The system is a rare glimpse of the violent, chaotic process by which a planetary system meets its end, and it hints that dead stars can have disc structures far more complex than previously realized,” Fusillo said.

In the far future, the Sun will become a white dwarf too, and that change may destroy Earth. The two rings also resemble the rings seen in discs around young stars, where planets are still forming.

“The system therefore offers a remarkable glimpse of what the future of planetary systems, including systems like our own, might look like,” Lagos-Vilches said.

The team can’t pin down the exact orbits of the two bodies.

Another setup could also produce four peaks. A single disc that bulges upward could shade the region where the gap appears to be, though no existing model of these discs supports that idea.

Two white dwarfs, each with its own disc, would need conditions the team calls extremely fine-tuned.

The star is also taking in rocky material more slowly than its bright disc would suggest. The team suspects the disc may be relatively young, with material falling onto the star for only 300,000 to 600,000 years.

Neither of the leading explanations for how gas forms in these discs can fully account for the mysterious gap between the rings.

For astronomers, that mystery opens a new window into what happens when planetary systems fall apart. Even after a star dies, the remains of its planets may have a complex story left to tell.

StateBench: Testing how language models track state changes over long documents

📅 Nov 03, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

We’re introducing StateBench, a benchmark for long-context state tracking—how well language models keep track of information about entities in long documents as they evolve.

State tracking underlies tasks from reading code, where variables are assigned and reassigned, to following a story as characters physically move around and objects change hands. For example, if a mystery novel states that “John gave Mary the knife” right before a murder occurs, it’s important to know that the state of each character has changed. However, not all state-tracking problems are created equal: some require only finding the latest update; harder ones require following a chain of changes, where one missed step can throw off the final answer. For example, a harder variant of our mystery state tracking would be if John gave Mary the item in his backpack, with it previously known that Edward put a knife in John’s backpack.

Transformers have well-documented theoretical limits on hard variants of state tracking with long dependency chains, helping motivate recurrent and hybrid architectures such as Gated DeltaNet that carry a running memory forward. Existing state-tracking tests tend to focus on the hardest cases, but as these designs become more common, researchers need to know which kinds of state tracking each architecture can handle. We would like to understand the differences between architectures not just on the hardest kinds of state tracking, but also on the simpler, potentially more common kinds.

To this end, we developed the StateBench benchmark to capture state-tracking problems of different complexity, which we used to evaluate the differences between language models with different architectures. We find that recurrent and hybrid models can generalize much farther than transformers when trained directly on these tasks, while frontier models including Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Opus 5.5, and GPT-6 Sol and Luna still break down on harder forms of state tracking as inputs grow longer.

StateBench consists of four difficulty levels that permit increasingly complex state update operations. For programmers, these levels of state tracking complexity can be understood as allowing different types of variable assignment operations:

These levels can be intuitively understood in terms of variable assignments, but they also represent theoretically motivated distinctions in state tracking rooted in Krohn–Rhodes theory, a branch of algebra that describes how state-tracking problems – which can be viewed as word problems over finite monoids – can be decomposed into atomic primitives. Each level in our hierarchy corresponds to a class of state-tracking problems that can be built out of a different set of primitives.

To compare the four levels fairly, we start with a general finite sequence of variable assignments and derive three easier versions from it, producing four comparable StateBench problems—one at each difficulty level. All four use the same number of variables and distinct values, with assignments appearing at the same points in the sequence; only the type of assignment changes.

We tested a range of open pretrained models on the r-trivial, aperiodic, and general finite levels of StateBench to see where their state tracking breaks down, and whether different architectures fail where theory predicts. Under standard theoretical assumptions, transformers can handle r-trivial robustly (and soluble and aperiodic to some degree) but are predicted to fail on general problems. In contrast, Gated DeltaNet with a more flexible memory update can, in principle, handle all four levels.

We started with open pretrained models, where the training data and context lengths are fully documented. These included two 7-billion-parameter models, Olmo 3 (a transformer) and Olmo Hybrid (which adds Gated DeltaNet layers), along with smaller models with 275 million and 2.7 billion parameters. All of these models were pretrained on the same data; none had seen contexts longer than 64,000 tokens during training, and we tested most of them on inputs well beyond that length.

When we scored each model, almost all performed well on r-trivial problems, lost accuracy on aperiodic ones as inputs grew, and stayed at chance on general finite problems. Problem format mattered here—the 275-million-parameter hybrid and Gated DeltaNet models scored near the ceiling on RULER-style r-trivial problems but were near chance on the same problems framed as spreadsheet edits. That gap is one reason StateBench tests the same underlying problem in several formats.

We then tested four frontier models – Gemini 3.8 Flash, Claude Opus 5.5, and GPT-6 Sol and Luna – asking them to read each sequence of variable assignments and generate the final value of every variable.

All four performed well on r-trivial and aperiodic problems. But on soluble and general finite problems, accuracy fell sharply as inputs grew longer, dropping to 13% or lower on the longest general finite problems. One likely factor is sensitivity; at these harder levels, many more steps can affect the final answer, leaving less room for a model to miss an update without getting the result wrong.

Next, we investigated whether models’ difficulty with the harder levels reflects limits of the architectures themselves, or whether pretraining simply doesn’t teach models to solve them. Targeted direct training is a more favorable setting that reveals to what degree state-tracking issues come from the underlying architecture and optimizer rather than the data used to pretrain a model.

We trained several 275-million-parameter models from scratch on StateBench: two transformer variants, two hybrids, and two Gated DeltaNet variants with different memory-update constraints. The models trained on sequences of up to 256 assignments and were tested on sequences as long as 64,000 assignments, or 250 times the maximum training length.

The architectures began to diverge as sequences grew longer. Gated DeltaNet and hybrid models generalized almost perfectly on r-trivial, aperiodic, and soluble problems out to the longest sequences we tested, tens of thousands of assignments. Transformers learned the easier levels but degraded on longer sequences, and they never learned general finite problems. On the hardest level, the Gated DeltaNet variant with a more flexible memory update generalized the farthest, staying near perfect out to roughly 10 times the length of the longest sequences it saw during training.

For Gated DeltaNet and hybrid models, the gap points to training rather than architecture; trained directly, they learned to handle state-tracking levels their pretrained counterparts couldn’t. Transformers were different—even trained directly, they could never learn the hardest level.

Some questions remain open. Our new proof shows that Gated DeltaNet with a more limited memory update (non-negative eigenvalues) can solve all aperiodic tasks and certain periodic tasks, such as tracking whether a repeated input has occurred an even or odd number of times, under the paper’s fixed-precision arithmetic with rounding. Its exact placement in the hierarchy remains open, including whether it can solve all soluble tasks and how numerical precision or combining it with attention layers affects its capabilities. StateBench also assumes a fixed number of variables, while real tasks often introduce new entities over time. In a long conversation, for example, new people and objects can appear as it unfolds, and the model has to begin tracking them too. The current framework doesn’t yet cover that setting.

As new flavors of recurrent and hybrid architectures emerge, understanding which kinds of state tracking each can handle could help guide how future systems are developed and trained. We hope StateBench gives researchers a finer-grained way to find where models break down—and to build training and evaluation data for the capabilities that still fall short. More fundamentally, we hope it serves as a North Star for researchers to develop new architectures with more robust state-tracking abilities compared to current models.

Five-year locoregional control, distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS), and overall survival (OS) exceeded 90%. All but one patient with locoregional failure had recurrences in areas with known residual macroscopic disease. No treatment failures occurred in nodal basins that were omitted from treatment.

De-escalation came in the form of 30-Gy ENI instead of the usual 50-60 Gy dose and omission of low-risk nodal basins, reported Nancy Y. Lee, MD, of Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center in New York City, and colleagues in JAMA Oncology.

"These outcomes were attained across the spectrum of T- and N-stage groups and smoking histories among patients predominantly treated with concurrent platinum-based chemoradiosensitization," the authors noted. "The systematic omission of uninvolved nodal levels IB/V did not result in failures in those areas and provides a simple class solution to reducing irradiated volume and toxic effects. The 30-Gy ENI dose remains the lowest among published approaches, is tailored to HPV-associated disease, and results in the lowest reported gastrostomy tube insertion rate."

"Although low-dose ENI without concurrent chemotherapy has been investigated, recent phase III randomized trials have demonstrated the superiority of concurrent platinum-based chemotherapy for achieving cure in HPV-associated OPC, underscoring the importance of de-escalating ENI within an optimally curative paradigm for HPV-associated OPC," they wrote.

The study showed that focusing on radiation dose is not the only strategy to de-escalate treatment for HPV-associated OPC, said David Sher, MD, of UT Southwestern Medical Center in Dallas, who was not involved in the study.

"While we often focus on the total dose delivered to visible cancer, in actuality, ENI is responsible for a substantial amount of normal tissue irradiation and dose to critical normal structures, such as the salivary glands and swallowing structures," Sher, an expert for the American Society for Radiation Oncology, told MedPage Today. "There has been a progressive recognition that ENI dose can be a significant contributor to quality-of-life outcomes, and there are several studies, both prospective and retrospective, that have shown that the standard dose of 50 Gy can be substantially lowered."

"[The] degree of success is remarkable given the extent of dose reduction, and it is exciting to see such superb outcomes," he added. "Although we do not have quality-of-life data from this study, prior reports were also very favorable."

Providing additional context for the study results, Sher noted that all patients underwent PET-CT imaging and the vast majority had MRI as well. Such advanced imaging may play a key role in identifying smaller lymph nodes that might harbor cancer and require higher radiation doses. Additionally, all the patients received cisplatin-containing chemotherapy, and HPV-associated OPC is cisplatin sensitive. Also, the authors did not report how many "equivocal" lymph nodes were treated with a higher radiation dose.

Results of the current study add to those of a previous phase III trial that used a slightly higher nodal irradiation dose, he added.

"All of this work strongly suggests we are delivering ENI doses that are far too high, and we need to be more aggressive in reducing the acute and long-term side-effect burden of our treatments while preserving oncologic efficacy," said Sher. "For such a result to become a true standard of care, though, we do need a phase III randomized study to confirm non-inferiority in a multi-institutional setting, and hopefully such a trial can be available soon to patients and our community."

Giblet Gravy

📅 Sep 30, 2019  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Giblets may not be your go-to ingredient just yet, but this Thanksgiving, they just might steal the show. Giblets are the edible offal meat of the turkey, typically the liver, heart, gizzards, and neck, that come in a small paper or plastic pouch stuffed inside the cavity of the bird. Adding them to your traditional gravy brings a depth of flavor that takes this classic side to the next level.

This gravy will also make your life a lot easier during Thanksgiving Day—you can make it while the turkey is still in the oven. It also means you'll be able to use every part of the turkey, and you don't have to throw out that little plastic bag that comes along with your bird. We love a zero-waste recipe!

Start by removing the liver from the giblets and chilling in the refrigerator until you are ready to use.

In a large saucepan over medium heat, combine the rest of the giblets, neck, onion, carrot, garlic cloves, sage, thyme, rosemary, and broth. Bring the mixture to a boil, then reduce heat, cover, and let simmer very gently for 1 ½ hours.

Add the chilled liver to the broth and simmer until cooked through, which should take an additional 25 minutes.

Strain the broth and discard the remaining neck and all veggies. You should have about 2 ½ cups stock at this point. Afterward, finely chop all giblets and set them aside.

Melt butter in another large saucepan over medium heat. Slowly whisk in the flour and cook until bubbling and just beginning to turn golden, which should take approximately 2 to 3 minutes.

Whisk in ½ cup of strained giblet stock into the gravy at a time, waiting for the ingredient to fully incorporate into the mixture before adding any more.

Once all stock is in, let the gravy simmer while mixing constantly and scraping the bottom of the pot until thickened. This step should take approximately five minutes. Season with salt and pepper and add chopped giblets back into the pot. You’re now ready to serve!

This gravy can be smothered on turkey, spooned over stuffing and mashed potatoes, or any roasted veggies, but if you have any left over (though we doubt you will), this is the perfect sauce for a leftovers sandwich or for a chicken dinner a couple of days later.
