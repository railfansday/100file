Cyclopia is the most severe form of a rare birth condition in which the developing brain fails to divide into two separate halves. This can also affect facial development, sometimes leaving the baby with a single eye socket in the center of the forehead and severe facial abnormalities. Most babies born with this condition either die before or soon after birth. It is so rare that it occurs in roughly 1 in 100,000 births, so finding physical evidence of it from modern times is difficult, let alone from historical ones.

The President Nominates Circuit Judges, Not Senators

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

Circuit seats belong to the circuit, and not to the state.

There has been a momentous shift in the relationship between the President and the Senate, and most people have missed it.

Let's start with the Eighth Circuit. The Trump Administration interviewed Justice Cody Hiland, but determined he was not up to the task of being a circuit judge, so they offered him a federal district court seat as a consolation prize. Hiland shot back at the White House Counsel and refused the seat. He even wanted to rub it in Trump's face. The White House made a stellar nomination with Judge Lee Rudofsky. Hiland then told an entire room of law students about his experience as a lesson that should be learned. But Hiland had a friend in a high place. Senator Boozman of Arkansas lobbied Senator Tillis of North Carolina and Senator Cornyn of Texas to block Judge Rudofsky.

Apparently there were some discussions between the President and Boozman, and the President folded. Maybe Trump determined that he needed as many judges confirmed before January, so the identity of the judge didn't matter. Maybe Trump didn't want to create more problems with his caucus. Maybe Trump just didn't care. Who knows? Trump withdrew Rudofsky and nominated Hiland. There is no guarantee that Hiland gets a hearing and makes it through the process. We are so late in the game. And frankly, I wouldn't blame some of the other conservative senators from the Eighth Circuit for putting Hiland through the paces at the hearing. It's not clear to me he'll be able to answer all the questions. But regardless of what happens, Senator Boozman won and President Trump lost.

Now, onto my home, the Fifth Circuit. It is painfully clear there was some sort of deal between Judge Southwick and Senator Wicker. In no universe would the White House have picked Todd Butler without that deal. I've yet to hear anyone provide me a single shred of evidence that he is conservative beyond saying "All southern lawyers are conservative. Plus he's a Christian." What about Nathan Johnson and James Talarico? Moreover, Southwick announced on September 29 that he would Senior Status, making it virtually impossible for another nominee to be selected.

Yesterday, I watched Butler's confirmation hearing with interest. He received a few softballs from Senator Grassley, a very warm welcome from Senator Kennedy, and some surprisingly nice questions from Senator Booker about representing police officers. Butler did not seem to understand departmentalism and mis-cited Mabury v. Madison. His answers were not particular articulate--roughly on part with his oral argument before the Supreme Court. There was no depth or curiosity conveyed.  I actually think Butler tripped up on Senator Blumenthal's questions about January 6 and the election, but it didn't matter. He sailed through. Members of the conservative legal movement have raised doubts about Butler. But there were no tough questions asked by Senators Cruz, Lee, Hawley, Schmidt, or others. Only they know why they stayed out. Senator Wicker prevailed, and the President lost.

Then there is the bizarre situation in Oklahoma. Someone yesterday asked me if I knew what Senator Armstrong was doing. My response was, "Who is Senator Armstrong?" He is the temporary senator appointed to fill Senator Mullins's seat. As Mike Fragoso relays, Armstrong is blocking the nomination of Zach West to the District Court in Oklahoma. This is all about some sort of squabble between West and defeated-AG Drummond. Armstrong returned a blue slip but is now trying to take it back. That's not how things work. If Armstrong prevails here, the President will lose.

Circuit seats belong to the circuit, and not to the state. It bothers me that "home state" senators introduce circuit court nominees. That pageantry reinforces the wrong message. Hiland's seat does not belong to Arkansas and Butler's seat does not belong to Mississippi.

Unfortunately, Trump's surrender here has created a terrible precedent. Should the Republicans ever again return to power, I would hope this entire process is reconsidered. Circuit court nominees should be treated as "at large" seats. Senators can make recommendations, but they cannot dictate who is picked. Worse, judges (like Southwick) should not be allowed to make these deals. We should not tolerate incentive structure where motivated actors can control who represents an entire circuit. Maybe the better approach is to start consulting with all the Senators in a given circuit on possible picks, and then pick the best athlete.

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

When you throw five touchdown passes, you get to take home hardware.

That's the case for Houston Texans quarterback C.J. Stroud entering Week 11.

Stroud has been named AFC Offensive Player of the Week following his performance in a 44-22 win over the Los Angeles Chargers, the NFL announced Wednesday morning.

On Sunday, in his return to Los Angeles, Stroud posted a 125.5 passer rating while completing 60.0% of his pass attempts for 384 yards and five touchdowns. The Texans opened the game on an eight-play, 73-yard drive, where Stroud completed five-of-five passes for 53 yards, culminating in a touchdown pass to Xavier Hutchinson for an early 7-0 lead.

The five passing touchdowns tied Stroud's career high and marked the second time in his career that he has thrown for 350 yards while leading five scoring drives through the air. His first came in Week 9's win versus Tampa Bay in 2023, where a touchdown pass to Tank Dell in the final seconds secured a win at Reliant Stadium.

Stroud's 384 passing yards and five touchdowns led the NFL in both categories in Week 9. He recorded the third-highest passer rating (125.5) and finished top five in completion percentage.

Posted November 11, 2026 | Reviewed by Davia Sills

** This post contains possible spoilers. **

“But what is grief, if not love persevering?” (Donney & Shakman, 2021).

It is a famous line from Marvel Studios’ Disney+ series, WandaVision. Ironically, when I first encountered it while watching the series many years ago, I did not think much of it. I might have even rolled my internal eyes in response, thinking that it sounded good, but I had trouble appreciating that the statement could have any meaningful connection to my life.

I heard the same line again a few days ago in the new Marvel Studios’ Disney+ series, VisionQuest. And this time around, it landed differently.

I had always thought that this line was about death and coping with its aftermath. That’s the context under which Vision delivers the words of comfort to Wanda, who is trying to make sense of her brother’s death.

But my understanding of grief—specifically, what kinds of things in life can be grieved—has expanded over time. Grief certainly includes grappling with death, but it is not a process reserved for only interpersonal loss. From this perspective, Vision’s words struck an emotional chord.

I like to think of grief as first recognizing a shift or development, and then taking appropriate action based on the changes. That is, grief helps me to acknowledge that a change has arrived in my life, often abruptly; grief also helps me to adapt to the new reality. “Love persevering” speaks especially to this idea that accepting and making sense of changes can be incredibly difficult, but it is a worthwhile process because of love.

Here is another way to say it: grieving means that you care deeply about something, or someone.

In the rest of this blog post, I wish to focus on grieving the changes in relationship roles. As someone who can be described as part of a sandwich generation in life, in a real sense, I grieve the changes that I observe in the relationships that I have with my children and my aging father. To be clear, the shifts that I will reference are part of normal human development. But just because these are shared human experiences does not make them any easier to go through. Let me give two specific examples.

The other day, my teen daughter prepared a lunchbox and left it in the refrigerator for me to take to work. When I discovered this unexpected gift in the morning, I felt pride, joy, and all the other warm feelings that a parent might have in these unexpected moments of kindness from our own offspring. But at a higher level, I also felt a pang of sadness in being reminded that my baby is no longer a baby (and has not been for a long time!), and that she has entered into a different developmental phase. More specifically, her relationship with me, including the roles that we occupy in the relationship, has shifted.

And then there is my aging father. The combination of his strong personality and Korean cultural emphasis on elevating the paternal role meant that for most of my life, I related to my father in a vertical way, by honoring his preferences and seeing him as the sole decision-maker in the family. But now, as a man in his mid-40s, I see the role shift reflected in many areas of our relationship. Some changes are really acute, directly correlated with declining biology; he can’t read any fine print, so I read it for him. Other changes are more subtle, such as when the hospital worker becomes exasperated, turns to me, and asks that I explain what they are trying to say to my father.

Grief, in sitting with the real changes in front of us; then adaptation, in living into this new space of relating to each other differently.

Are you grieving the changes in your life, especially the interpersonal changes in people that you love? I pray that your love for that person will carry you through the process, even if it's difficult and requires much perseverance. And I hope that through it all, you can find ways to be compassionate to yourself and recognize that these are normal emotions and processes.

What is fueling the brightest gamma-ray star pair ever found?

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A warm gas trail near LMC P3 offers new clues to the extraordinary gamma rays coming from this mysterious binary system.

One pair of stars in a small galaxy next to the Milky Way gives off more gamma rays than any other pair of its kind.

Gamma rays carry more energy than any other form of light, and astronomers have struggled to explain where this pair’s gamma rays come from.

In the cold gas near the stars, they have now found a long, straight streak of warmer gas that lines up with the pair.

The warm streak is strong evidence that the pair, called LMC P3, is shooting out a jet of fast-moving matter. A jet like that could account for the gamma rays.

The work was led by Yasuo Fukui, a professor emeritus at Nagoya University, with colleagues at Gifu University.

“We discovered a gigantic molecular jet,” said Fukui. The team didn’t see the jet itself, though. What they measured is gas they think the jet has heated.

LMC P3 lies in the Large Magellanic Cloud, a small galaxy that orbits our own. When astronomers look toward the middle of the Milky Way, dust blocks much of the view.

The Large Magellanic Cloud has far less of that problem.

One member of the pair is a big, very hot star. The other is a compact object, the crushed core left behind when a giant star exploded.

That core is either a black hole or a neutron star, and nobody has yet confirmed which.

The two circle each other about every 10 days. They orbit inside the debris from the explosion that made the compact object, a leftover cloud called DEM L241.

LMC P3 is the most luminous known gamma-ray binary and the first discovered outside the Milky Way. Astronomers first reported it in 2016.

Starting in 2022, the team pointed the ALMA observatory in Chile at the region.

The array of radio dishes picked up the faint signal of carbon monoxide in the cold gas near the stars.

That signal told the researchers how the gas was spread out, how it moved, and how warm it was.

One streak of gas stood out. It runs about 26 light-years long and three light-years wide, and it lines up with LMC P3.

That length is roughly six times the distance from the Sun to the nearest star.

The streak is still extremely cold: minus 400°F to minus 352°F (33 to 60 K). The gas around it measured about minus 433°F (15 K), which is normal for quiet gas clouds in that galaxy.

Measured from absolute zero, the streak is two to four times warmer than its surroundings.

The team found no nearby hot stars that could have warmed the gas by shining on it.

Fukui said the warm temperatures, spread across a large part of the gas cloud, make “a very strong case” that the gas is being struck by a high-energy jet from the compact object.

That same kind of jet can power gamma rays.

For years, there were two main explanations for LMC P3. In the first, the compact object is a fast-spinning neutron star that blows out a wind of charged particles.

The wind slams into the wind coming off the big star, and the collision speeds particles up enough to make gamma rays.

In the second, the compact object pulls gas off its partner. The gas swirls into a flat, spinning disk around it, which astronomers call an accretion disk.

A disk like that can launch narrow jets of matter moving close to the speed of light.

The second setup is a small version of a quasar.

Quasars, among the brightest objects in the universe, are powered by giant black holes feeding on gas at galactic centers. Some also launch powerful jets.

A star-sized version is called a microquasar. The new streak supports the microquasar idea.

Because black holes generally drive stronger jets than neutron stars do, Fukui sees LMC P3’s huge gamma-ray glow as a clue to what kind of object powers it.

The high gamma-ray output “provides a very strong support for the possibility that its central object is a black hole” rather than a neutron star, Fukui said.

In the paper, the team argues for a jet but doesn’t name the compact object.

Roughly 20 microquasars are known in the Milky Way. Most were found with optical telescopes, radio dishes, or X-ray instruments, aimed at parts of the sky that are mostly clear of dust.

Few microquasars have turned up in the crowded, dusty middle of our galaxy.

The researchers suggest that searching for straight streaks of warm carbon monoxide could turn up microquasars there.

LMC P3 is the second object where this kind of carbon monoxide streak has been found. The first was a gamma-ray source in the Milky Way called HESS J1023-575.

A jet can only build a streak where there’s enough gas in its path. LMC P3 is close to a gas-rich region where new stars are forming.

Around the well-known microquasar SS 433, the gas is thinner, which the team thinks is why only scattered clumps formed there instead of one long streak.

No telescope has detected the jet directly, in radio waves, X-rays, or gamma rays. The researchers wrote that this fits with how sensitive current instruments are.

The streak also shows up only on the north side of the pair, possibly because that’s where the gas cloud is.

The ALMA map didn’t cover the south side, so a matching streak there hasn’t been searched for at this level of detail.

A second, fainter streak runs at an angle of about 16 degrees to the first.

If the jet slowly changes direction over time, it could have made both, but that idea hasn’t been tested.

The team plans sharper ALMA observations that can pick out details smaller than about three light-years.

Those would give better temperature readings and could show whether the jet has been shifting direction.

The full study was published in the journal The Astrophysical Journal Letters.
