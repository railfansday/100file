Ohio State defensive line produces a season-high in sacks vs Northwestern

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Ohio State's defensive line went into the Nov. 14 Northwestern game knowing its pass rush needed to improve.

"We had to get back there. We had to get pressure," defensive tackle Eddrick Houston said. "We had to get sacks and get that pressure off the DBs, who are trying to cover for that long. ... Coverage and rush work together, so we just had to do what we needed to do."

The Buckeyes snapped a two-game losing skid, defeating the Wildcats 28-19 and logging their highest sack total since September 2025 with five.

The defensive line, after failing to record a sack the past four games, accounted for all of the Buckeyes' sacks, which pushed the Wildcats back 25 total yards and led to two fourth downs.

In those games the defensive line went without a sack, Ohio State as a whole had just two, both credited to defensive backs, Jermaine Mathews Jr. and Jaylen McClain. The Buckeyes were ranked 121th in the nation in sacks with 11.

"It's something we've been emphasizing," Ohio State coach Ryan Day said. "Getting more pressure on the quarterback."

Against Northwestern, defensive end Kenyatta Jackson Jr. finished with two sacks despite remaining on the bench for nearly two full drives at the beginning of the game. Has a team-high five.

Alabama transfers Qua Russaw and James Smith Jr., both defensive ends, each recorded a sack, a first for Smith at Ohio State. Will Smith Jr. was the only defensive tackle to sack Wildcats quarterback Nicco Marchiol.

Houston, who has three sacks in his four seasons with the Buckeyes, still took pride in the Buckeyes hitting a season-high in sacks, even though a majority came from the edges rather than the middle of the line.

"It felt great, it all coming together," Houston said. "Rushing four as one, and allowing our ends to eat, but at the same time, while our ends are eating, we can work stuff in the middle to allow us to get sacks as well."

The veteran Buckeye admitted the defensive line had begun to shy away from the pass rush and began to put more emphasis on stopping the run after its performance against Illinois, in which Ohio State allowed 199 rushing yards.

"We've got some great pass rushers," Houston said. "Great guys in there that can pass rush, but we kind of got away from that and focused on stopping the run. Now that we've stopped the run, we've got to get to that quarterback. I just feel like we took that next step."

The run defense was progressing in the three games following Illinois, giving up an average 64.7 rushing yards. But in the losses to USC and Oregon, Ohio State allowed 112 rushing yards per game and conceded five rushing touchdowns, in addition to lacking a pass rush.

Northwestern had been averaging 190 rushing yards per game, the second-most in the Big Ten. On 29 run attempts against Ohio State, the Wildcats posted just 53 yards, marking just the second time they were held to under 100 rushing yards.

The Buckeyes limiting Northwestern's ground game was impactful on the pass rush, especially on long-yardage third down plays, on which Ohio State forced Marchiol into incomplete or short-yardage passes five times.

"When you can put them into a situation where they're one-dimensional, it certainly allows us more [pressure] opportunities," Day said. "There were some times in the game where we struggled on third down, getting off the field, but we got them off schedule a bunch, which allowed us an opportunity to get after the quarterback."

Florida city cancels St. Patrick's Day parade, citing new anti-DEI law

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis (shown here in October) signed the anti-DEI law that Delray Beach officials say prohibits them from putting on cultural celebrations, like a St. Patrick's Day parade. Alex Wong/Getty Images hide caption

One of the first casualties of a new Florida law that prohibits taxpayer dollars from going toward diversity initiatives is a St. Patrick's Day parade in a beach town that has been celebrating its Irish American community for nearly 60 years.

The city of Delray Beach announced this week that it was canceling its popular St. Patrick's Day festivities in compliance with a new law that takes effect on Jan. 1. The city will also withdraw its sponsorship of LGBTQ Pride celebrations in June, officials said.

The law, backed by Republican Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis is part of the state's ongoing crackdown on diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI) initiatives that the governor has called discriminatory and said have "no place" in public institutions.

The city lawyer for Delray Beach, a community in southeast Florida with picturesque beaches and a historic downtown, told the mayor that any government-sponsored celebrations of culture, ethnicity or heritage that are not a national holiday (for example: Independence Day) could run afoul of the new anti-DEI law. So, Mayor Tom Carney said, he made the difficult decision to cancel one of the city's biggest events of the year.

In past years, the parade, an annual event since 1970, wound its way through the downtown core of Delray Beach with spectators lined up several-people deep along the route. Local businesses and organizations sponsored floats and giveaways. School bands played as they marched through the streets. Participants included members of the Ancient Order of Hibernians, the largest and oldest Irish Catholic organization in the country.

"I've said it's engrained in the DNA of Delray and I do mean that. It's one of our signature events," Carney, a Republican, told NPR.

The mayor said he's hopeful that the city can find a path forward — and a way to preserve the event.

If a private sponsor were to volunteer to take over the St. Patrick's Day parade, or the June Pride celebrations, Carney said, the city would gladly hand over the reins and support the continuation of these festivities.

"These types of events we should be celebrating because in this country, people can have these events. In other countries, you can't do this. We have great freedoms here," he said. "And if the community wants something and it's not against public policy then I would support them to do it if they're using private dollars. If we can do it, we should do it."

Carney, who is of Irish descent, said the parade was privately run until about eight years ago — so returning to a private-sponsorship model would be a callback to the parade's origins. His own family, he said, was involved in keeping the parade going during the 1980s and 1990s.

Delray Beach officials said they have been inundated with emails, calls and social media messages from constituents who are angry that the community's decades-long celebration had been axed.

"It was kind of a call to action to me," Carney said. "The community really wants to have it, so we should go ahead and have it, we just need to get it privately funded."

The governor, meanwhile, has accused Delray Beach of weaponizing the new anti-DEI measure to make a political point. DeSantis said the city was "engaging in "malicious compliance" in a message he posted on X following the city's cancellation announcement.

"What they are doing is not mandated by Florida law," the governor wrote. "It's just an attempt to make a political point — a point that legacy media will no doubt try to amplify. We've seen these stunts throughout the years. Rinse and repeat."

The mayor said the city is merely trying to stay out of trouble with the law. The measure, SB1134, asserts that any official that violates the provision could face civil or legal consequences.

"The legislation has unintended consequences. The governor may genuinely believe what he believes and I take him at his word; our city attorney came up with a different conclusion," Carney said. "I'm going to leave it at that."

Calvin and Hobbes’ 5 Greatest Spaceman Spiff Comic Strips

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Calvin and Hobbes wasn't Bill Watterson's first pitch for a comic strip. C&H's protagonists began as side characters in Watterson's comic The Doghouse before his original syndicate suggested that they ought to be the focus. "Marvin" became "Calvin," the rest of The Doghouse's cast was dropped, and while United Feature Syndicate still didn't take the resulting comic, Universal Press Syndicate did.

Before then, Watterson had drawn cartoons about a spacefaring character as early as college. Watterson went on to make that character the focus of one of his rejected comics: a mustachioed, cigar-smoking parody of adventurers like Flash Gordon. Shortly after Calvin and Hobbes began, Watterson reworked "Mort" into Calvin's fantasy alter ego Spaceman Spiff.

Over the course of Calvin and Hobbes' run, Spiff was one of Calvin's most frequent escapist characters. Whenever Calvin was caught in a boring or unpleasant situation, he frequently retreated into his Spiff persona to pass time or even make a hasty retreat. In practice, that gave Watterson a handy excuse to take Calvin and Hobbes' art well outside its usual mundane setting, with tons of gross aliens, impossible landscapes, and great phrases like "eat hot electric death" that don't usually come up in everyday conversation.

Much like the rest of Calvin and Hobbes, Spaceman Spiff really hit his stride in the comic's last three years, when Watterson began doing his trademark "unbreakable" Sunday strips. Some of the most colorful and evocative C&H comics are Spaceman Spiff adventures from the mid-'90s, but Spiff made a few solid appearances before that point. Here are some of our picks for the best Spaceman Spiff comics in Calvin and Hobbes.

When Calvin gets sick during his summer vacation, his first reaction is to get annoyed. Sick days are for the school year. I think we all can relate.

Unfortunately for all involved, Calvin's mom takes him to the doctor. That includes a checkup, an injection, and a retreat into Calvin's Spiff mode, as he imagines himself as a prisoner of aliens rather than a little kid trapped in an examination room.

Calvin's teacher Mrs. Wormwood catches a lot of strays in Spaceman Spiff cartoons. Sometimes I want to see a version of C&H from her perspective. Most of her class are apparently boring normal kids who do the reading and don't get in unusual amounts of trouble. Then there's Calvin, who's as much of a chaos agent as a six-year-old can be. Mrs. Wormwood and Mr. Wilson from Dennis the Menace would have a lot to talk about.

In this early installation, Calvin gets called on in class and seamlessly incorporates Mrs. Wormwood's history question into his daydream. It's not being called on in class; it's being interrogated by the Hideous Zorgs of Planet X-13. (Look at those bug-men. Those are aliens that deserve the word "Hideous" being part of their proper noun.)

The most extreme (in the '90s sense) Spaceman Spiff adventure was a week-long story where Calvin got deeply enough into character that he staged a breakout from elementary school. Called to the board to answer a math problem, Calvin instead decides to shoot his way out (with a rubber band), then run for the door.

Through what one assumes is the relative indifference of school staff, Calvin manages to make it all the way home before his mom catches him. Unfortunately, Spiff can't get Calvin out of the ensuing consequences.

Right from the start, Bill Watterson has said that one of his primary influences on Calvin & Hobbes is George Herriman's Golden Age newspaper comic Krazy Kat, which ran from 1913 to 1944. That was already some inside baseball in the 1980s when Calvin and Hobbes was new, as Krazy Kat’s modern following is almost exclusively made up of historians, fanatics, and other cartoonists. It’s not dead media yet, though, as historians and preservationists have made a point of keeping collections of Krazy Kat in print. Curious readers can check out Fantagraphics' George Herriman Library hardcover collections, which are still in print at time of writing.

If you check out Krazy Kat, its influence on Calvin and Hobbes is obvious immediately, particularly in its colored Sunday comics. Herriman's characters inhabited a cartoonish version of the Arizona desert, and many of Watterson's Spaceman Spiff strips feature him visiting alien planets with similar arid backgrounds. This largely-wordless strip from 1993 is a perfect example, with so much of it devoted to Calvin's imagined spaceship journey from an alien valley to home.

One of the best Spaceman Spiff strips comes in the last year of Calvin and Hobbes. Without dialogue, we see the entire comic from Calvin's perspective, as he's entertaining himself during yet another boring class by imagining himself behind the stick of Spiff's flying saucer.

Suddenly, Mrs. Wormwood interrupts his daydream and breaks his train of thought. As soon as she isn't paying attention anymore, Calvin switches to a new scenario: this time, he's a dinosaur stalking prey.
