Is Current Second Amendment Jurisprudence Premised Upon a "Misquote"?

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Mostly law professors | Sometimes contrarian | Often libertarian | Always independent

A New York Times article attempting to discredit Justice Scalia's Heller opinion misfires.

This past week, the New York Times published an article by Mike McIntire and Eli Murray alleging that a "pivotal legal test" for determining whether gun control laws are constitutional is "based on a misquote." Yet it appears it is the NYT's reporters who misfired.

When the Supreme Court begins weighing the constitutionality of assault-weapons bans next month, the justices will be guided by the court's own precedent: a landmark ruling that such restrictions are allowed only if a firearm is both "dangerous and unusual."

For nearly two decades, judges across the country have invoked this language to strike down gun-control laws. It is a pivotal legal test.

Justice Antonin Scalia, writing for the majority in the 2008 case, District of Columbia v. Heller, attributed the "dangerous and unusual" language to Sir William Blackstone, an 18th-century English jurist whose writings became the basis for much of American law.

But in his seminal 1769 treatise, "Commentaries on the Laws of England," Blackstone referred to weapons that were "dangerous or unusual," suggesting that a gun did not need to be both in order to be restricted.

But is there actually an error in the opinion? Here is the paragraph from Heller in which the alleged misquotation occurs, with the allegedly offending sentence underlined:

We also recognize another important limitation on the right to keep and carry arms. Miller said, as we have explained, that the sorts of weapons protected were those "in common use at the time." 307 U. S., at 179. We think that limitation is fairly supported by the historical tradition of prohibiting the carrying of "dangerous and unusual weapons." See 4 Blackstone 148–149 (1769); 3 B. Wilson, Works of the Honourable James Wilson 79 (1804); J. Dunlap, The New-York Justice 8 (1815); C. Humphreys, A Compendium of the Common Law in Force in Kentucky 482 (1822); 1 W. Russell, A Treatise on Crimes and Indictable Misdemeanors 271–272 (1831); H. Stephen, Summary of the Criminal Law 48 (1840); E. Lewis, An Abridgment of the Criminal Law of the United States 64 (1847); F. Wharton, A Treatise on the Criminal Law of the United States 726 (1852). See also State v. Langford, 10 N. C. 381, 383–384 (1824); O'Neill v. State, 16 Ala. 65, 67 (1849); English v. State, 35 Tex. 473, 476 (1871); State v. Lanier, 71 N. C. 288, 289 (1874).

Note that the claim, with the quotation, is followed by a "See" string cite (followed, in turn, by a "See also" string cite of further authorities).  If they were at all familiar with how legal citation works, this should have given the NYT reporters pause. As others have noted, the Supreme Court's style guide indicates that the "see" signal is not used for direct quotations. Rather it indicates that "the cited authority is basic source material supporting, although not stating, the text proposition" (emphasis added). Blackstone is cited first not because it is the dominant source but because the authorities are listed in chronological order.

What makes matters worse is that it appears the authors of the story were informed of all this before the story went to press. One of the story authors contacted John Bash, who clerked for Justice Antonin Scalia the term Heller was decided and worked on the case, and Bash explained all of this to the reporter, as Bash details in this LinkedIn post.  Bash writes:

This is stunning. A recent article in The New York Times claims that District of Columbia v. Heller--the 2008 Supreme Court case holding that the Second Amendment protects an individual right to own a firearm--contains a "misquote" of Blackstone. The supposed misquote is the phrase "dangerous and unusual weapons" in this passage:

We also recognize another important limitation on the right to keep and carry arms. Miller said, as we have explained, that the sorts of weapons protected were those "in common use at the time." 307 U. S., at 179. We think that limitation is fairly supported by the historical tradition of prohibiting the carrying of "dangerous and unusual weapons."

That passage is followed by the signal "See" and numerous citations, the first of which is Blackstone. The Blackstone source says "dangerous or unusual weapons" ("or" instead of "and"), but a number of the sources that follow say "dangerous and unusual weapons," in addition to sources that use the Blackstone disjunctive framing.

Back in October the New York Times reporter reached out to me about this, presumably because I was the law clerk for Justice Scalia on Heller. I immediately wrote back with a full on-the-record explanation of why there was no misquote. Yet the article does not refer to my explanation at all and asserts with no qualification that it was a misquote.

So if anyone is interested, I have posted my email below verbatim. I will also put in the comments my follow-up when the reporter asked me a question.

Here is the e-mail Bash sent to the reporter working on the story:

Hi Mike, thanks for reaching out on this.

Even if I remembered any conversation with Justice Scalia about this particular point (which I don't), it would not be ethical for me to share private conversations I had during my clerkship wth Justice Scalia about the substance of opinions.

But just as an appellate lawyer, I don't see the issue.  If the historical sources are mixed on the conjunctive or disjunctive framing, then it is true that there is an historical tradition of prohibiting weapons that are both dangerous and unusual, the narrower category that is encompassed by either framing.  The fact that Blackstone is the first citation is just because the treatise citations are in chronological order (consistent with making a point about historical tradition), followed by case citations also in chronological order.  The introduction of the treatise list with a "See" signal (rather than no signal) means that the quoted phrase is not necessarily a direct quote from each source (or any source)—as with the "See also" signal for the cases.  And assuming that it's correct that the conjunctive sources all trace back to a single 18th Century treatise, that would not detract from the point that learned commentators had endorsed or applied that test through early American history, which is the point the text is making about historical tradition.

Here is the first follow-up e-mail Bash sent "in response to the reporter asking if he could quote me and whether the opinion purposefully omitted the Hawkins treatise quoted in the conjunctive-framing sources so that Blackstone would be first":

Sure.  Happy to take a look at the quote.

Can't speak to the thinking that went into the opinion, but I really doubt that.  As I mentioned earlier, I don't think the provenance of the limitation is particularly relevant; the point was that commentators had recognized it throughout history.  For example, in a Fourth Amendment case, a court might make the point that the curtilage rule—that the immediate vicinity of the home is considered part of the home—has been recognized through American history, citing late Eighteenth Century and Nineteenth Century treatises and cases.  Even if that rule could be traced back to a single uncited treatise, that's not really the point. It's that courts and commentators endorsed it and so that history and tradition informs the constitutional analysis.

And all that aside, I just think it is implausible that the paragraph would be understood differently if Blackstone had been the second citation rather than the first—especially since Hawkins was himself a very influential legal commentator (if I recall correctly).  I've never heard of a court or litigant attributing weight to the order of sources subject to the same signal (e.g., "See"), which would seem to me especially unwarranted when the sources are presented chronologically (and so there is an obvious explanation for the ordering other than importance).  As I noted before, I think all the sources together make clear a uniform historical tradition of prohibiting dangerous and unusual weapons, the category encompassed by all of their articulations of the rule.

Interestingly enough, the NYT article concludes citing a scholar affirming Bash's contention that, as the relevant passage claims, "there is an historical tradition of prohibiting weapons that are both dangerous and unusual," or as some would put it, those arms that are "unusually dangerous." Insofar as some lower courts have applied a different analysis, it is not clear to me how that is attributable to Scalia's language in Heller, or the contested passage.

The NYT story ends citing a scholar for the proposition that "relying on centuries-old legal precedents requires understanding how earlier generations used language." One might conclude that those writing on the Supreme Court should heed a similar admonition.

Teachers are often underappreciated and underpaid. In high school, I (EB) had the celebrated teacher and author of Angela’s Ashes (1996), Frank McCourt. And he was as good as they say, if not better. He was inspirational, built my confidence, and taught me to read and write in ways I am forever grateful for. I had less famous, but also fantastic, teachers, who inspired me to learn and grow. At the same time, I have had teachers who filled me with dread. I still remember my kindergarten teacher who, among other things, made me stand, as a 4-year-old, so that the other kids could see the “baby” because I briefly put my thumb in my mouth. Many of us harbor fondness for some of our teachers and anger towards the ones who lacked empathy, even if their behavior did not rise to the level of “abuse,” but rather was adult-sized bullying.

Teachers, and other adults in the lives of children, can treat children in ways that are hurtful. Adult observers are often reluctant to see and act on the warning signs, despite the direct and indirect impact on kids. Some of the warning signs include: insulting comments, even when couched in humor, humiliating remarks or directives, making children cry, publicly calling attention to children’s foibles, and being physically aggressive and controlling, even if it isn’t assaultive. If a behavior would be inappropriate when directed to another adult, it is probably inappropriate when directed toward a child, unless specifically related to normative school-oriented activities. In other words, if an interaction would feel upsetting or humiliating to an adult, it likely would to a child as well. Children may also indicate a problem with a particular adult by telling concerning stories, changing their behavior around the adult, or avoidance of the adult or the activity the adult is involved in.

Adults have more power than children, through their size, their role in the community, and almost every other relevant metric. Because of this power differential, some adults can behave in hurtful ways without realizing the potential impact on children with whom they interact. Why is it that we fail to identify these behaviors as bullying? Some of the reasons include: the privacy inherent in some of these interactions, the wish to avoid confrontation, desire to think the best in other adults, discomfort with the whistleblower role, and self-protection.

Witnesses have to assess whether it is serious enough to have a conversation. When I was a young psychologist, I made extra income by doing psychological testing in a public school. There was a very inappropriate teacher, who anyone, even visiting the school only briefly, could hear yelling and making derogatory comments toward her students. The principal approached me and asked whether I thought there were problems with this teacher. I responded, “Don’t you know?” He nodded. It is hard to take action in such situations, even when it is obvious.

As a psychologist, I continue to hear stories about adult bullying that trouble me. Teachers and other adults in children’s lives are special as they play such a significant role in the lives of the children with whom they interact, most often creating positive experiences. They are afforded status with children which makes the few who are bullies, or otherwise inappropriate, have ample opportunity to use their power to do harm.

Chart Rewind: In 1986, Alabama Had ‘The Touch’ at No. 1

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The band's 1986 LP marked its seventh straight multi-week Top Country Albums leader.

When Alabama edged a spot to No. 1 on Billboard’s Top Country Albums chart dated Nov. 15, 1986, the band demonstrated it was still in touch with country fans’ tastes, delivering the group’s seventh straight non-holiday leader.

The album’s title, The Touch, was apropos. The first single from the package was a cover of The Carpenters’ “Touch Me When We’re Dancing,” which ranked No. 4 on the concurrent Hot Country Songs. That single would rise to No. 1 two weeks later, and the follow-up, “‘You’ve Got’ The Touch,” would reach the summit on April 11, 1987, bringing their run of No. 1 country songs to 21, with an exception for holiday singles and collaborations. That consecutive streak remains a record four decades later.

Bred in Fort Payne, Alabama, the quartet featured frontman Randy Owen, guitarist Jeff Cook, bassist Teddy Gentry and drummer Mark Herndon, selling out arenas while combining country and harmony-based rock influences. True to form, The Touch mixed ballads, uptempo country, old-time rock ‘n’ roll and one track with a touch of New Wave sonics, keeping them at the genre’s forefront even as the New Traditionalist movement was taking over.

The Touch ultimately spent 10 weeks in the pole position on Top Country Albums. Alabama brought its next three releases to No. 1, rolling up 10 consecutive Albums chart-toppers. The band added one more leader with the 2006 gospel collection, Songs of Inspiration. The foursome spent 125 total weeks in the lead on Top Country Albums, the most among groups and a metric that contributed to Alabama’s 2005 induction into the Country Music Hall of Fame.

What's the meaning of the "A" on your USB-A ports?

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Making sense of USB-A and clearing up if it's becoming obsolete.

There's no hidden or extraordinary meaning for the letter "A" in USB-A. It's simply a letter designation for a Type-A connector, which is the familiar, wide rectangular plug found on flash drives and countless charging cables. USB itself stands for Universal Serial Bus, but the letter "A" identifies the connector type and nothing more. The same holds true for USB-B and USB-C connectors.

The reason for differentiating A, B and C goes back to the USB Implementers Forum (USB-IF) and the necessary designators for physical connections and power/data delivery. For instance, with a traditional printer, B is the square device connector and A is the rectangular end that plugs into your PC. In the early days, this was a simple method for informing consumers what goes where and why.

For everyday use, Type A is a connector label with charging capabilities or charging and data transfer. Sure, that's the boring way to describe it, but it really isn't anything beyond that. Alone, the USB-A designation doesn't tell you how quickly you can transfer files with it or how much charging power a USB-A port will spit out. At the end of the day, two ports with the same rectangular opening can represent two entirely different capabilities.

USB-A ports are reassuringly familiar to just about everyone, but the performance capabilities aren't readily apparent all the time. A USB 2.0 connection has a maximum data rate of 480 Mbps, but there are faster USB-A implementations that support 5 Gbps or 10 Gbps. Keep in mind that those are connection limitations that determine why a device may or may not function as expected when you plug it in. If you find a USB-A cable in a drawer, it may have a charging-only capability, or it could be capable of transferring data and charging devices. Without the associated labels and specs, it's just a USB-A cable with unknown capability.

Before you plug a USB-A into a PC port, check the cable's and the PC port's capabilities. There are usually markings beside each port (including a trident symbol), or you can find the information in the PC's manual. There are color differentiators as well, although most aren't universal. Instead, color choices are mostly up to manufacturers. A teal port usually indicates a faster USB connection, for example, but that is not a guarantee. If you're connecting a mouse or a keyboard, they usually don't need the kind of bandwidth that an external SSD will, so choose carefully.

For what it's worth, a USB 2.0 designation is not an automatic qualifier for USB-A only. Even a USB-C can be limited to USB 2.0. Separate the designator from the capability and carefully match them so you know what you have and what you can do.

It's undeniable that USB-A is slowly giving way to USB-C, but that doesn't render USB-A useless. It hasn't gone the way of the rotary phone just yet. It's difficult to deny the shift, however, with most PCs, especially mid- and high-tier, shipping with more of a focus on USB-C ports. USB-C simply has practical advantages in several areas that make it a natural choice for today's devices, though it can be every bit as confusing as USB-A port colors. Most are reversible, feature a smaller connector for saving space on laptops, provide a connector platform for USB4 capabilities and are much more flexible in terms of size and usability.

Ultimately, there's no need for most people to replace a USB-A keyboard and mouse. You don't have to ditch your USB-A flash drive or forego USB-C to USB-A adapters. Granted, most mobile phones are now USB-C exclusive, except for adapters, of course. But PCs and Macs typically ship with USB-A ports, though they often play second fiddle to USB-C. All you need to do is choose the version that takes care of your needs. If you make a new purchase, base it on the ports you already have and use extensively. USB-A's declining presence is a good reason to become more flexible in terms of compatibility, but it's not a reason to get rid of usable devices and cables.

Accelerating Electrification Can Unlock the Full Benefits of Spain’s Renewables Leadership

📅 Nov 15, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

A new IEA report finds renewables are already strengthening energy security and competitiveness while cutting fuel import costs—but faster electrification, grid modernization, and greater flexibility can extend these gains across the economy.

Spain’s rapid expansion of renewable power has strengthened its energy security and competitiveness, but faster electrification across transport, buildings, and industry will be essential to meeting the country’s energy and climate goals, according to a new IEA Energy Policy Review published Oct. 5, 2026.

Between 2015 and 2025, renewables’ share of Spain’s electricity generation increased from 35% to 56%, while emissions declined by around one-fifth. These achievements have been underpinned by the country’s strong energy and climate policy framework and its well-established approach to a just transition, which links clean energy investment with jobs, regional development, and social inclusion.

Spain’s growing share of renewable power is already helping to lower wholesale electricity prices and reduce its exposure to imported fuels. Without the vast expansion of renewable capacity, its natural gas imports for power generation would have been nearly 40%, or USD 26 billion, higher in 2024. Nonetheless, imports still provided almost 70% of Spain’s total energy consumption in 2024, underscoring the need to extend the transition beyond electricity supply.

Accelerating electrification of end-uses should also be a priority, the report finds. Today, electricity accounts for just 25% of Spain’s final energy consumption, well below the country’s target of 35% by 2030. Electric vehicle uptake is relatively limited, notably for corporate fleets, while fossil-fuel heating remains widespread, and the industry faces high upfront investment costs.

A combination of taxes, levies, and network charges are keeping end-user electricity prices high, while fossil fuels face comparatively lower charges in Spain. The country is taking steps to address this, including by abolishing the tax on electricity production in 2026. Further rebalancing domestic energy price dynamics, while protecting vulnerable consumers, could strengthen incentives for households and businesses to electrify and help industry capture the competitive advantages of renewable power.

Spain has launched reforms to accelerate storage, demand-side flexibility, and adapting grid rules to a rapidly changing generation mix. Continued progress on power system infrastructure and management, alongside a more balanced mix of wind, solar, and hybrid technologies, can help Spain integrate growing shares of renewable generation while strengthening electricity security and resilience.

The review also highlights the importance of carefully managing the planned nuclear phase-out to maintain security of supply. Following the recent approval to extend the lifetime operation of the Almaraz nuclear power plant, the report recommends implementing a transparent framework for future case-by-case decisions that considers the costs and benefits for consumers and the wider power system.

Strong progress on renewable power in Spain demonstrates that its deployment can advance energy security, competitiveness, and social inclusion. The report finds that the next test will be to turn this progress into a fully integrated, flexible, and resilient energy system. By accelerating electrification, modernizing and optimizing grids, unlocking flexibility, and carefully managing its nuclear phase-out, Spain can offer valuable lessons for other countries pursuing similar energy transformations across the wider economy.
