What exactly are Flock cameras and how can you spot one?

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Flock cameras are everywhere, and they’re easier to miss than you think.

Basic descriptions of Flock cameras fall woefully short of their true capabilities and potential uses. Most people are familiar with the automated license plate reader (ALPR) version, usually pole-mounted and paired with a solar panel. If that were all there was to it, this would be a very short article. But Flock Safety (Flock Group Inc.) has become deeply intertwined with the defining terminology of ALPR cameras.

In truth, there are more than a dozen manufacturers and vendors of ALPRs; their products just aren't called Flock cameras, and each has its own aesthetic characteristics. For all intents and purposes, however, they are identical. If you see Motorola on the side of one, it likely does much of what a Flock does: read plates and log vehicles.

Most are vertically oval, narrow in width and fairly small, roughly the size of your hand. They're designed to capture your license plate and the time and place you passed by. Most are also accompanied by a solar panel. Leonardo and Genetec make the most visually distinct versions. Leonardo's are blockier, rectangular in shape, with some elongated versions. Genetec's are horizontally oval, with the same narrow width as Flock's variations, only flipped. Motorola manufactures a mix of the two.

The problem is that some Flock cameras can easily be mistaken for a standard traffic camera and vice versa. Because of this, it's difficult to accept the absolute accuracy of a project like DeFlock, which uses volunteers to identify Flock cameras visually. There's no centralized or formal verification system to independently confirm the sightings.

Flock also sells more than ALPRs. Depending on what the customer wants, the system can log details like roof racks on cars and bumper stickers. Some setups have microphones and others can recognize things like clothes and beards.

Flock cameras usually turn up where traffic funnels. They're often mounted on poles at intersections and busy traffic areas. Since many homeowners associations (HOAs) are major Flock camera customers, it shouldn't be a surprise to find them at neighborhood entrances. Shopping centers aren't off the list of potential locations either. The aesthetics aren't irrelevant, but since several manufacturers make them, there are a few other telltale signs to look for as well.

Flock cameras are meant to capture license plates, so look at where they're aimed. Check for Flock's bird logo on the camera and a solar panel above it. Because most are self-powered, there's usually little to no additional equipment nearby. Red light and speed cameras are typically larger and wired to traffic signals or enforcement poles. White, elongated cameras are another lookalike, but they mostly help traffic signals adjust to traffic flow. Some states also mount signs next to speed cameras so they're easy to identify.

Keep in mind that there are other variations of Flock cameras out there. The Flock Condor is a mounted sphere that rotates 360 degrees and tracks people. Naturally, these are mounted near areas with heavy pedestrian traffic. Flock also makes a mobile security trailer, a gunshot detector and a drone-as-first-responder. Because companies are using video integration tools to convert standard IP cameras into plate readers, it will be increasingly difficult to identify Flock or Flock-type cameras.

As a rule of thumb, if it's pointed at you, it's potentially watching you. But for a broader view, check the Atlas of Surveillance, a searchable database from the Electronic Frontier Foundation that shows what technology law enforcement agencies use. Search your town or county to see whether police are using plate readers.

While some services like MapQuest can help you find Flock cameras in your neighborhood, be wary of crowdsourced maps. DeFlock uses OpenStreetMap data collected from volunteers, but there's no official public list, so a missing pin doesn't mean there isn't a Flock camera sitting there.

Pins can also be wrong, since it's easy to confuse a Flock camera with the countless security and traffic camera variations. A Flock camera doesn't have to belong to a public agency. Flock and Flock competitors also sell to HOAs and retailers.

If you want to find every Flock camera in your city, county or state, you have to put in the work. Submit a public records request, attend the next city council meeting or ask your HOA and local businesses.

Meta’s WhatsApp is making a huge change to chat backups, with a new built-in cloud backup option on Android that doesn’t rely on Google, and is free.

Spotted by WABetaInfo, WhatsApp for Android is adding its own cloud backup option. Prior to this, the app relied on Google Drive backup for transferring chats between devices or keeping a backup in case of accidental deletion.

“WhatsApp cloud storage” is showing up in the WhatsApp for Android v2.26.46.4 beta update, rolling out now, and includes 2GB of free storage for cloud backups. Everything is still end-to-end encrypted. Beyond the first 2GB, WhatsApp Plus ($2.99/month) adds 100GB, and Meta One ($7.99/month+) offers up to 4TB of cloud storage. You can still choose to use Google Drive backups instead of the built-in option, but those using devices without a Google account or who are running low on Google storage may appreciate the option to switch.

It’s unclear exactly when this will be available to all WhatsApp users.

Why iPhone Duo wasn’t for me (but might be for you)

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

I’ve had the iPhone Duo for nearly two weeks now, and have officially decided to return it. Here are the three main reasons why the Duo didn’t work out for me, but might for you.

I use my iPhone one-handed all the time, and based on my experience, iPhone Duo is clearly intended to be used with two hands. You can use it one-handed, but for me it’s not nearly as comfortable as the iPhone 18 Pro. It’s too wide and too heavy to be used for long.

Perhaps over time I’d get used to needing two hands for most iPhone interactions. But that would be a pretty significant shift for me, as there are so many situations when I don’t have both hands free.

If you already use two hands with your iPhone regularly—perhaps because you have a Pro Max model—then the Duo’s one-handed experience likely won’t bother you. But the more I used the Duo, the more I found myself missing my iPhone 18 Pro.

The biggest value proposition of a foldable is that it’s a 2-in-1 product, letting you have a standard phone but also a tablet-sized display that fits in your pocket.

But it's also possible that the answer may rest in part on how practically usable an originalist framework is likely to be. And to consider that, it's helpful to have in mind some practical questions. Some are already familiar, for instance questions related to damages liability in libel cases, obscenity law, and the like. This short Essay has aimed to help supplement those familiar examples with some others that haven't been much discussed; I hope others who think about the topic can find them useful.

Senate Poised to Vote on Affordability Package that Hunters, Anglers Worry Is a Trojan Horse for Public-Land Rollbacks

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Published Nov 17, 2026 1:09 PM EST

The U.S. Senate plans to vote this week on a bill aimed at easing America’s affordability crisis and fighting climate change that hunting and fishing groups worry will also erode many of the nation’s bedrock conservation laws.

The complex and winding 400-page Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act focuses on lowering energy costs, something both Republicans and Democrats have championed and and that helped drive Democratic gains in the midterm elections. BAAJA also, however, ties jobs and household bills with curtailing public-lands protections. And in a twist that’s perplexing many conservation groups, the bill is co-sponsored by Sens. Martin Heinrich (D-N.M.) and Mike Lee (R-Utah), two lawmakers known for being on nearly opposite ends of the public-lands spectrum.

BAAJA is largely focused on permitting reform, something the country hasn’t tackled in decades, according to the Bipartisan Policy Center. In practice, the legislation would raise the standard for whether projects approved by one administration can be revoked by a future administration.

That means an industrial solar project permitted by one president, for example, could not be stymied by a future president intent on preventing renewables. It also speeds up permitting processes and cuts red tape for everything from energy projects to landscape-scale changes like prescribed fire.

But sportsmen and conservationists say the bill would not just expedite climate or environmentally friendly projects. BAAJA would also make it much harder — or potentially impossible — for an administration to overturn leases or approvals for projects as controversial as hyperscale data centers on public lands or mines in the same watersheds as sensitive wilderness areas. It’s a bill that prioritizes development, many observers say, at the cost of the very “red tape” that has been responsible for conserving many of the country’s most priceless fish, wildlife, and wild places.

“I understand this is a big, bipartisan bill — it has some things in it I would rather it not have. But I negotiated the deal based on what was actually possible to get signed into law,” Heinrich wrote in an email to Outdoor Life. “Because when I say climate change is an existential threat, I mean it. And I will not let an opportunity to do something about it pass by.”

Conservationists like Ingrid Lyons, executive director of Save the Boundary Waters, understand the need for stability around permitting development. The whiplash between issuing and revoking permits for public land development projects every time an administration changes has likely hurt the U.S.’s ability to move forward with important energy projects, including in renewables like wind and solar.

But she fears this bill could instead cement the creation of controversial projects like a copper-sulfide mine at the headwaters of the Boundary Waters Canoe Area Wilderness or a copper-molybdenum-gold mine in the salmon-rich waters of Alaska’s Bristol Bay. Permits for projects like these have been denied, among other issues, because of environmental risk. If permits are reissued in similar scenarios, legislation like BAAJA could prevent future administrations from stopping development in places that truly need protection.

“What about landscape certainty?” Lyons says. “We’re overlooking in some ways the impact to communities that are relying on protection and not just projects.”

The conservation community has become increasingly vocal in its opposition to the bill since Heinrich and Lee partnered with Sens. Sheldon Whitehouse (D-R.I.), and Shelley Moore Capito (R-W.Va.), to introduce the legislation in late September. Lee has a clear track record of supporting public land sales and undermining existing public-lands conservation, including 2025’s attempt to include federal land sales in the One Big Beautiful Bill.

A lengthy version of BAAJA is before the Senate as of Tuesday. The current bill includes a variety of red flags for hunters, anglers, tribes, endangered-species advocates, and other conservationists.

The Theodore Roosevelt Conservation Partnership acknowledged the need for permit reform but said in a statement that “progress should not come at the expense of the conservation safeguards and public review that give hunters and anglers a voice in how our lands and waters are managed.”

For Lyons, it comes down to the potential unintended consequences of sweeping legislation like BAAJA.

“We need to be exceptionally aware of the implications of this bill, which could be that a lot of these really beloved places could have projects many communities have worked really hard to oppose cemented into place.”

Many in the conservation community were surprised by the bill’s sponsors, given Heinrich’s history as a sportsman and conservationist who regularly defends public lands and sensitive places, and Lee’s general position as a proponent of development and public-lands sell-off.

But in Heinrich’s statement to Outdoor Life, he emphasizes the impact of climate change on our planet, including the places where people hunt, hike, bike and camp. Independent modeling, he said, predicts the bill will lead to a 20 percent reduction in power sector emissions, or the “equivalent to taking 39 coal plants offline for 10 years.”

“You cannot protect a trout stream without paying attention to the warming temperatures and declining flows that threaten it,” he wrote. “You cannot protect wildlife habitat while ignoring the impacts of increasingly severe drought and wildfire. We need to reduce emissions, and that means getting more clean energy onto the grid.”

In an Oct. 30 op-ed in the Albuquerque Journal, Heinrich explained how New Mexico’s SunZia Wind and Transmission project took 17 years to permit and build.

He also pushed back on what he called misinformation about the bill, stating he doesn’t believe data centers should be on public land. The bill also requires large scale data center operators to pay the full cost of electricity consumed by the facility.

The Izaak Walton League’s conservation director, Kate Miller, thinks each of the bill’s sponsors are acting in good faith and trying to do what is right for their constituents. Many projects, including those aimed at restoring forests and wildlife habitat, have been held up by litigation. Something like BAAJA could move those forward.

But Miller would have liked to see these issues tackled individually instead of in a massive bill. And while she hopes that, if the bill passes, the administration will respect existing laws, the past two years has brought an onslaught of changes to most of the nation’s bedrock environmental laws including the Clean Water Act, the Endangered Species Act, and the National Environmental Policy Act.

“We want to make sure what comes out is not something that sacrifices those core protections that allow us to have healthy waters and habitats,” Miller says. “That we don’t sacrifice one for the other.”

‘We don’t need Gen 8’: Kevin Harvick has the solution to NASCAR’s Gen 7 woes

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

NASCAR analyst Kevin Harvick has offered his solution to the Cup Series' Gen 7 woes as fans continue to voice their dismay with the Next Gen regulations.

Introduced in 2022, the seventh-generation Cup Series car has been one of the most polarising cars in NASCAR's history.

The quality of racing is at the forefront of backlash regarding the car, with many under the impression that the parity created on the intermediate speedway circuits simply doesn't make up for the stale product on offer at short tracks, road circuits and superspeedways.

While many have called for series officials to now expedite the sport's next generation of cars in order to move on from the Gen 7 cars, Kevin Harvick has another solution in mind – one he has dubbed "Gen 7.5".

Speaking via a recent episode of Kevin Harvick's Happy Hour, the 2014 Cup Series title-winner highlighted how he believes officials will "have to change things on the race car that they don't want to change" in order to alleviate concerns voiced by fans.

Harvick then agreed with co-host Mamba Smith on the opinion that the formula of the O'Reilly Auto Parts Series shouldn't be touched.

"In order to make all this right, Cup has to be great," the Bakersfield-born former driver asserted. He then doubled down, adding: "O'Reilly Series – good ratings; everything's good. Truck racing – everything's good. Worry about the Cup car!

"Don't worry about screwing up the other two. Like, just stop worrying about trying to make that better. It is what it is. It's good for the time being. The racing is good. Leave them alone. If you need to change the body, change the body, but fix the cup car. That is what you have got to concentrate on."

However, a complete overhaul is not what Harvick believes NASCAR need: "We don't need a Gen 8 car; we need a Gen 7.5." He then pointed to two different areas in which officials need to amend things.

The first port of call should be under the hood, according to the 60-time NASCAR race winner: "Just put some more power in the damn car. They've slowly crept it in there, but quit d****** around with putting a little bit in and put a lot in. Like, just make it violent and crazy to watch."

On top of that, Harvick believes the overall structure of the Gen 7 cars needs to be redesigned, and issued a stern warning in the process: "Structure is going to open up a can of worms that's not going to be fun to deal with, but you're going to have to deal with it if you truly want to make it a lot better."

Ultimately, Harvick's assessment boils down to a willingness to tackle Next Gen's most expensive and politically sensitive aspects, from overhauling the underbody to loosening the single-source supplier model so teams can actually work on the cars again.

NASCAR CEO Steve O'Donnell recently poured cold water on the idea of a major car overhaul anytime soon, so it remains to be seen whether officials are ready to have those difficult conversations on how the sport can be improved in the interim.
