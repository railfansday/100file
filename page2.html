Square Peg announced on November 6, 2026, that it is backing Future Secure AI, whose enterprise AI Co-Workers are designed to complete complex business processes alongside employees and inside customers’ existing systems.

Future Secure AI designs, builds and runs these multi-agent systems. Each Co-Worker has a name, a face and a place on the organization chart, while underlying agents handle intake, judgment calls, system updates and hand-offs. Deterministic logic is used where a language model is the wrong tool, with human involvement where appropriate.

The company calls its target area “Red Zone” AI: workflows that cross multiple systems and teams and require accuracy, security and oversight. Examples include month-end close, tax calculations, reconciliations and customer service, which can sit inside decades-old systems of record and behind strict data rules. That makes completion of the whole operating process, rather than faster responses to individual prompts, the central product requirement.

Delivery is built around executive relationships and embedded implementation. Future Secure AI sells to senior executives, CEOs and boards, opening with their largest business problems. Senior former consultants and engineers then work with customer teams to scope, build and operate the Co-Workers. This forward-deployed model ties the software to a team responsible for fitting it into a customer’s actual processes.

Future Secure AI says its AI Co-Workers execute complex, high-volume workflows instead of only individual tasks, and describes them as LLM-agnostic, resilient and fully auditable. Each is purpose-built for a business role and tailored to an organization’s people, processes and systems. Named examples include Amy AI, a finance month-end analyst for a global investment bank; Ari AI, a fringe benefits tax analyst for a global investment bank; Leo AI, a rostering manager for a medical diagnostic company; and Billie AI, a billing assistant for a global law firm.

Future Secure AI says its technology is not tied to a single large language model, cloud provider or enterprise platform, and is designed to integrate with a customer’s existing environment. Deployments can use single-tenant private-cloud infrastructure and containerized applications inside an enterprise network. Under that configuration, sensitive data remains within the environment and is not used to train public AI models.

The company describes an operating system that coordinates long-running workflows across systems, approvals, decisions and business processes. Its controls include configurable guardrails, monitoring and human oversight, together with operational visibility, explainability and audit trails. Lifecycle management covers deployment, monitoring, support and continuing optimization. Employees can collaborate with Co-Workers through email, voice, Slack and Teams, while connected data access covers structured and unstructured information.

Future Secure AI’s leadership page identifies Mehrdad Baghai and Michael Hunter as co-founders and co-CEOs. Baghai’s biography describes him as a former McKinsey partner and an adviser to CEOs and boards, while Hunter’s background covers entrepreneurship and investment banking at Macquarie Capital.

Square Peg’s account adds that Baghai co-led McKinsey’s worldwide Growth Practice and co-wrote The Alchemy of Growth, later running growth and commercialisation at CSIRO, Australia’s national science agency. Hunter spent more than a decade as a Macquarie investment banker working closely with senior leadership. Those careers help explain why the investor views executive relationships and credibility as part of the company’s delivery model.

Square Peg’s thesis is that enterprise AI adoption will combine products and services, that suppliers must define business problems and own delivery, and that technology alone will not determine who wins complex, regulated workflows where executive trust and delivery proof points are central. That view connects its backing to the founders’ backgrounds and Future Secure AI’s forward-deployed teams, beyond the capabilities of the underlying models.

For Future Secure AI, Square Peg’s backing is an endorsement of an enterprise-AI model built around execution, integration and governance. The central test is whether its role-specific Co-Workers and senior delivery teams can turn those controls into dependable operation across the Red Zone workflows it targets.

A new report finds residential proxies exiting the White House network 236 times in the past month.

As if the Trump administration needed any more bad news this week, cybersecurity researchers discovered several residential proxy services originating from the White House network, exposing a potential security flaw that could allow cybercriminals to route traffic through official government IP addresses.

The security firm Layer3 Intel detected residential proxy activity on AS2650, a network registered to the Executive Office of the President. Another security firm identified software from the proxy server Infatica, a company owned by a Russian entrepreneur who ran a server-rental company used by hackers in attacks on election systems in Arizona and Illinois in 2016.

While there’s no evidence that proxy access was used to carry out attacks on the White House, experts say it could open the door for hackers to exploit vulnerabilities or grant outsiders an unmonitored back door into high-security systems.

“It basically gives them a foothold on a network where previously nobody was getting into the White House network,” Layer3 founder Sergiy Usatyuk told CNET. “From a security policy perspective, it gives you all types of potential pathways to exploit.” (Usatyuk pleaded guilty to federal charges in 2019 for his role in operating websites that launched denial-of-service, or DDoS, attacks.)

Residential proxy services route internet traffic through consumer devices (such as smartphones, laptops or home routers) to make it appear that the activity is being performed by a real person. While proxies can be used for relatively benign purposes — web scraping is by far the most common use case — they’re also popular for activities like malware attacks, identity theft and creating fake social media accounts.

For example, earlier this year, Synthient, another company that tracks proxies and VPNs, documented how one botnet infected more than 2 million devices using a residential proxy server. If a similar vulnerability existed in the White House proxy, hackers could probe the infected device’s internal systems and install malicious payloads.

In its report, Layer3 found traffic from six distinct IP addresses exiting the White House network from at least 28 known residential proxy services between Oct. 5 and Nov. 3, with most activity occurring in the evenings between 5 p.m. and midnight EST. This suggests the culprit may be an employee who connected a personal device to a Wi-Fi network with a compromised app or a proxy SDK (software development kit) running in the background. Third-party SDKs can convert a device into a “proxy node.”

Commercial proxy networks sell access to these nodes, so anyone with a few dollars could rent a trusted IP address — in this case, making it appear as though traffic originates from the Executive Office of the President.

Researchers at Synthient provided more details about the case, posting on X that several White House staffers are running “a Windows 11 machine with InfaticaSDK installed.” They pointed directly to Rave, a popular app that lets users watch streaming services with friends, as the likely delivery mechanism for the residential proxy traffic detected on the White House network.

Riley Kilmer, co-founder of the cybersecurity firm Spur Intelligence, wrote in a LinkedIn post that different types of proxy companies had been discovered in the White House, including one called 9Proxy, which is often used by criminals for banking fraud.

The White House didn’t respond to CNET’s request for comment.

Because smart TVs, connected appliances and home routers usually have minimal user oversight, a single unvetted app or out-of-date firmware update can turn a household of IoT devices into an exit node for commercial proxy networks.

Proxies can generally “glom” onto consumer devices by embedding networking code directly into software, either consensually (users willingly install it) or nonconsensually (by exploiting unsecured device access points).

It starts when app developers look for revenue by integrating those proxy SDKs into their free apps. Then, when you install the app, the fine print of the terms and conditions of service often includes a clause that allows the app to add your IP address to a third-party proxy pool.

A June report from Spur Intelligence found that roughly one in three apps available on Samsung and LG smart TVs were selling users’ IP addresses. To avoid ads, users often accept the terms when downloading the app — a type of unnoticed consent. Once installed, the SDK runs continuously in the background, registering the device’s IP with the proxy provider’s gateway, unless you delete the app or opt out. (The report notes that Amazon and Roku have explicitly banned this practice.)

Using a free VPN could expose you to the same risk, allowing your IP address to be used by someone else for nefarious activities. The FBI’s guidance on protecting yourself against proxies specifically calls out free VPNs as one of the primary ways bad actors gain access to an IP address.

In some cases, you don’t need to download anything to get your IP thrown into a shady proxy pool — you just have to buy the wrong device. A recent investigation by Synthient discovered that more than 1.3 million streaming devices from the China-based device manufacturer MECOOL were infected with proxy SDKs. A proxy service called SocksEscort was charged by the Justice Department in March with compromising thousands of routers to submit fraudulent unemployment insurance claims.

Checking whether your IP address is being used by a proxy service without your knowledge is relatively simple. Layer3 provides a free proxy-checking tool that searches its database of over 70 million proxies to find if yours is listed. GreyNoise, another cybersecurity company, also offers a free IP-checking service.

These tools can tell you if proxy traffic is passing through your network, but they can’t identify which specific device has the SDK running. You’ll have to do some additional detective work to figure out the culprit. Some common sources include free VPNs, gaming apps, “one price, no subscriptions” streaming boxes and smart TVs.

Cybersecurity experts also recommend keeping the firmware on all your internet-connected devices up to date and downloading only from trusted app stores.

A federal court has granted a preliminary injunction to two key groups representing insurance brokers over the Trump administration's moratorium on new broker enrollments.

The District Court of Maryland issued the injunction after determining that the National Association of Benefits and Insurance Professionals and Health Agents for America "have shown that they are likely to succeed on the merits of their claims under the Administrative Procedure Act."

The court also said that the organizations made clear they would likely suffer significant harm if the moratorium stays in place.

NABIP said that the ruling means that it will restart training for new brokers beginning on Nov. 6.

"Today’s ruling is a significant victory for licensed agents and brokers and the consumers who rely on their expertise," said NABIP President Mychal Walker in an announcement (PDF). "With Open Enrollment already underway, every day matters."

"Lifting the moratorium immediately and beginning training tomorrow will help affected professionals get to work assisting individuals and families in finding coverage that fits their needs and budgets," said Walker.

NABIP initially sued on Oct. 15 to challenge the moratorium. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services instituted the moratorium in late September as part of its broader push to combat fraud on the Affordable Care Act's exchanges.

The National Association of Benefits and Insurance Professionals is suing the Trump administration over its moratorium on new brokers entering the Affordable Care Act marketplaces.

The organization, the leading trade group for insurance brokers and agents, said the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services' moratorium impacts close to 19,000 brokers and agents who will be barred from registering ahead of open enrollment.

CMS unveiled the moratorium in late September as part of its ongoing push to root out fraud, waste and abuse. In addition, the agency said it would terminate more than 200 agents and brokers that failed to comply with marketplace standards.

The agency said new brokers and agents were disproportionately responsible for submitting inappropriate enrollments and engaging in behaviors at high risk for fraud.

In an announcement (PDF), NABIP says that it supports the broader effort to combat fraud, but that CMS' moratorium "threatens the livelihoods of thousands of legitimate, licensed professionals who have not been accused of fraud and limits consumers’ access to professional assistance as they navigate Open Enrollment."

The organization argues in the lawsuit that the rule instituting the moratorium violates both the Administrative Procedure Act and the Affordable Care Act. The regulation is unlawful as it is "arbitrary, capricious, and unsupported by sufficient evidence," NABIP said.

The 19,000 agents who would be barred from participating under the rule are part of a critical network of professionals who help consumers find coverage that meets their needs, the organization said.

NABIP President Mychal Walker said that CMS should look elsewhere in its fraud efforts.

"The existence of fraud is not in dispute. NABIP has raised these concerns with CMS repeatedly and has offered concrete solutions to address them," Walker said in the announcement. "What is in dispute is whether the appropriate response is to exclude thousands of legitimate agents based solely on whether they registered last year, without any individualized evidence about them."

"There is a better approach: target fraud where it occurs, strengthen the system’s security and hold bad actors accountable while preserving consumers’ access to trusted professionals," Walker continued.

CMS has taken steps to crack down on brokers as the agency views improper ACA enrollments as a key target in its fight against fraud. CMS officials have repeatedly raised concerns that people are enrolled in ACA plans without their knowledge, or despite being deceased.

Earlier this week, the agency revealed it will purge 189,000 enrollees who are currently listed as enrolled in both ACA plans and other coverage, such as Medicaid or Medicare.

Hong Kong: Joshua Wong Gets Nearly 10 More Years in Prison

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Click to expand Image Joshua Wong speaks to protesters near the Legislative Council in Hong Kong, June 17, 2019.  © 2019 Kin Cheung/AP Photo 

(Tokyo) – The Hong Kong High Court on November 5, 2026, sentenced Joshua Wong, a well-known democracy activist, to 10 years in prison on baseless national security charges, Human Rights Watch said today. Two months of the sentence will run concurrently with his current national security sentence. Wong has been in custody since November 2020, when he was 24, and faces imprisonment until late 2036, when he will be 40.

The Hong Kong government should immediately and unconditionally release Wong. Foreign governments should denounce the sentences against him and other wrongfully detained pro-democracy activists and impose targeted sanctions on officials responsible for Hong Kong’s growing repression.

“The harsh sentence on baseless charges against Joshua Wong is cruel and unjust,” said Elaine Pearson, Asia director at Human Rights Watch. “Young people promoting social change are an asset for any vibrant society, but the Hong Kong government puts them behind bars.”

To keep Hong Kong society subdued, and to reduce the chance of an international outcry, the Hong Kong government appears to have adopted the tactic of stacking charges against influential pro-democracy leaders to ensure their prolonged detention, Human Rights Watch said. Other notable Hong Kong figures who have faced similar tactics include Jimmy Lai, founder of Apple Daily, and veteran democracy activists Lee Cheuk-yan and Albert Ho.

The court sentenced Wong, 30, under the National Security Law for “conspiring to collude with foreign forces.” The authorities accused him of “conspiring” with the activist Nathan Law and other “unknown” individuals between 2016 and November 2020 to urge foreign governments to sanction Hong Kong and Chinese government officials as they advocated for Hong Kong’s “democratic self-determination.” 

The prosecution relied heavily on the international lobbying and media advocacy that Wong and Demosisto, the political party Wong co-founded in 2016, carried out before the National Security Law took effect. Wong initiated online petitions, spoke to international media, and met with foreign officials to draw attention to human rights abuses in Hong Kong. He urged governments to sanction Chinese and Hong Kong officials responsible for abuses and to stop selling weapons to Hong Kong police that they use to carry out rights violations.

Wong had mostly stopped these activities before the National Security Law became effective. Demosisto disbanded on June 30, 2020, and Wong resigned from the group. However, the government quoted social media posts by Wong as ostensible evidence that his alleged “conspiracy” with Law to commit “foreign collusion” continued after the law took effect. In these posts, Wong said he “would not change his views” and that he would “continue to seek support from the international community” in his personal capacity, while Law and others abroad would continue their international advocacy.

The government also cited Wong’s subsequent opinion pieces and media interviews, though none directly called for sanctions, which the National Security Law specifically prohibits. It also cited his reposts of Law’s posts broadly calling for sanctions against Chinese officials and Hong Kong police.

The court ruled that Wong’s offense was of a “grave nature.” The National Security Law sets prison terms of 3 to 10 years for “foreign collusion,” or 10 years to life for offenses deemed to be of a “grave nature.” Wong formally pleaded guilty on September 2. A guilty plea normally results in a one-third sentence reduction. But a sentence reduction cannot bring the term below the statutory minimum.

Because of reporting restrictions on pretrial committal proceedings, when a magistrate determines whether to send a case to a higher court for trial, the public did not know the details of the accusations against Wong until the formal plea. The government enacted a law in 2024 to enable the court, on national security grounds, to deny the defendant’s right to ask the court to lift restrictions on reporting about the case.

By restricting media reporting on the pretrial proceedings in national security cases, the government can control the narrative and undermine the fundamental legal principle of open justice.

Since Beijing imposed the National Security Law on Hong Kong in June 2020, Hong Kong authorities have used the new legal regime—along with existing laws—to rapidly dismantle civil society and detain, imprison, or force into exile hundreds of activists and government critics.

Wong has been in custody since November 2020, when he pleaded guilty to two charges of organizing and inciting an unauthorized assembly linked to the 2019 citywide protest movement. After that, he pleaded guilty to five more charges on separate occasions, including for violating an anti-mask law, participating in an unauthorized vigil for the 1989 Tiananmen Massacre, and breaching a court ban to disclose a police officer’s personal information.

While serving these sentences, Wong was charged with two national security offenses, which carry far harsher penalties. In January 2021, Wong was charged with “conspiring to commit subversion” for participating in an unofficial democratic primary in 2020 and sentenced to another four years and eight months in prison. 

Before Wong could complete these sentences, the authorities brought the “foreign collusion” charge against him in June 2025, ensuring that he would remain in prison.

Wong became one of Hong Kong’s best-known democracy activists as a teenager, after leading a massive 2012 protest against a government educational curriculum widely seen as political indoctrination. He became the global face of Hong Kong’s democracy movement during the 2014 Umbrella Movement and co-founded Demosisto with Law and others in 2016. The party disbanded on June 30, 2020, hours before the National Security Law came into effect.

The National Security Law’s sweeping provisions have been used to punish peaceful protest and expression. Its use against Wong and others violates the right to freedom of expression under the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, which is incorporated into Hong Kong law through the city’s de facto constitution, the Basic Law, and the Bill of Rights Ordinance.

“Beijing has paid no real price for its increasing repression in Hong Kong,” Pearson said. “Concerned governments should press for the release of Wong and other activists, impose targeted sanctions against implicated officials, and review their relationship with China more broadly in light of the deepening abuses.”

The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services on Tuesday finalized its annual outpatient hospital payments rule with a lower-than-proposed rate bump and its major, industry-opposed policy shifts moderated, but largely intact.

The 2027 Hospital Outpatient Prospective Payment (OPPS) and Ambulatory Surgical Center (ASC) Payment Systems rule outlines a 2.3% reimbursement increase for those meeting applicable quality reporting requirements. That’s below the proposed 2.4% increase and reflects a 3.2% projected hospital market basket increase and a 0.9 percentage point productivity adjustment.

The agency is also moving forward with a substantial and controversial cut in 340B drug reimbursements, though not quite to the extent that it initially proposed.

The final rule adjusts these drug payments down from the current bar of average sales price (ASP) plus 6% to ASP minus 28%, as opposed to the proposed 33.4% below ASP. To make the reimbursement change budget neutral as required by statute, OPPS payments for non-drug services will be boosted by 7.5%.

CMS said it tweaked the proposed 340B reimbursement cut based on 340B ceiling price data from the Health Resources and Services Administration, with the baseline reductions informed by a drug acquisition cost survey it conducted earlier this year. Those changes will reduce Original Medicare drug payments by $4.35 billion and the drug payments from beneficiaries by $1.1 billion (or $900 on average per beneficiary who receives an affected drug from a hospital outpatient department) in the policy’s first year, CMS said.

The rate adjustment did little to calm hospital industry groups who’d railed against the 340B reimbursement changes as a threat to safety-net care and a gift to for-profit hospitals that do not participate in the subsidy program but would benefit from the budget-neutral offset. The American Hospital Association, the Association of American Medical Colleges and America’s Essential Hospitals each condemned the finalized policy in reaction statements, with the latter two issuing more forceful rebukes.

“CMS has based its reimbursement to 340B hospitals on the results of a hasty and faulty survey, failing to follow the clear requirements of the Medicare statute,” AAMC Chief Health Care Officer Jonathan Jaffery, M.D., said. “The drug payment cuts disproportionately affect and target non-profit and public hospitals serving low-income patients, while redistributing billions of dollars to hospitals that do not share the same safety-net and academic missions. These cuts threaten access to critical services made possible by the 340B program and will lead to worse health outcomes for patients.”

“Rather than strengthening and preserving safety net funding, CMS is instead prioritizing hospitals that do not serve the safety net and nodding to the pharmaceutical industry's interests,” AEH’s statement reads. “These cuts are landing at a time when essential hospitals and their mission are more important than ever, as millions of people risk losing their Medicaid coverage in the coming months.”

The Federation of American Hospitals, which represents for-profit hospitals that do not participate in 340B, struck a different tone, calling the rulemaking “a win for patients, taxpayers and the hospitals that communities depend on.”

“By bringing Medicare drug payments more in line with actual costs, this reform will lower seniors’ out-of-pocket expenses and redirect billions in Medicare dollars where they’re needed most—supporting access to care for patients served by small and rural hospitals across the country,” Charlene MacDonald, the group’s president and CEO, said in a statement. “We applaud the Administration for advancing a smarter, fairer approach to Medicare payment.”

Additionally, CMS finalized an increase in an annual OPPS conversion factor to non-drug items and services, speeding up the agency’s effort to offset a prior $7.8 billion recoupment to 340B hospitals it was forced to make after previously reducing 340B rates contrary to law. But while this accelerated payback—often described by non-340B hospitals as a clawback—was proposed as an increase from 0.5% to 3%, the final rule brings the conversion factor to 2%.

AHA Senior Vice President of Public Policy Analysis and Development Ashley Thompson described both 340B policy changes together as a “continued assault” on the subsidy program that “will further stress hospitals’ ability to care for their patients.” AAMC’s Jaffery said the combined policies’ impact “will be devastating to patients,” and that his group “remains committed to pursuing all available avenues to reverse these payment cuts.”

Further rankling the hospital industry was the final rule’s continued push toward site-neutral payment policy and a phase-out of the inpatient-only list.

For the former, the agency will apply Physician Fee Schedule payment rates for some imaging services to off-campus provider-based departments. After the SOC Assessment: Common Findings and How to Fix Them

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

_ Got your SOC assessment report back? Here are the findings that show up most often and a practical plan to remediate them._

The report lands in your inbox. Forty pages, a heat map, and a list of findings that wasn't there last week. Now what?

This is the stage where an assessment either pays off or collects dust. Let's walk through the issues that appear most often, how to rank them, and how to turn them into progress. If you're working with a team offering SOC consulting, this is also where good advisors earn their keep.

Every environment is different, but certain patterns repeat.

1. Blind spots in log coverage. Critical systems, cloud workloads, or identity platforms aren't feeding the monitoring tool. Teams often assume they are, because someone set it up years ago.

2. Noisy, untuned alerts. Analysts drown in low-value alerts, so real ones get missed or delayed. Alert fatigue is one of the biggest silent risks.

3. Playbooks that exist only in theory. Documents are outdated, never tested, or unknown to half the team.

4. Weak escalation paths. Contact lists are stale. Nobody's sure who can authorize isolating a server at midnight.

5. Thin after-hours coverage. Strong daytime performance, thin or no coverage overnight and on weekends.

6. Limited detection mapped to real attacker behavior. Rules focus on a few familiar threats while whole categories of technique go unwatched.

7. Inconsistent documentation in tickets. Investigations lack notes, making handoffs and post-incident reviews painful.

8. No meaningful metrics. The team counts alerts but can't say how fast it detects or responds.

9. Tool sprawl and poor integration. Analysts bounce between consoles, wasting time and losing context.

10. Skills and burnout concerns. High turnover, limited training, and heavy workloads.

If you recognize several of these, you're in very ordinary company.

Resist the temptation to start fixing everything. Sort first.

Use two simple lenses: risk (how bad is it if this goes unaddressed?) and effort (how hard is the fix?). That gives you four buckets:

Be honest about risk. Findings tied to detection of serious threats outrank cosmetic documentation issues.

These won't solve everything, but they build momentum and visibly reduce risk.

You don't have to do it all alone. Outside support can make sense for specific needs:

When evaluating remediation help, look for advisors who explain trade-offs and teach your team along the way, not those who create dependency. The goal is a stronger in-house capability or a well-managed partnership, not a permanent black box.

Remediation projects often fade after the first burst of energy. A few habits help.

Name an owner. Someone senior should be accountable for progress.

Track it visibly. A simple board or dashboard works.

Review monthly. Short check-ins catch stalls early.

Celebrate wins. Acknowledge when a gap closes. It reinforces the work.

Retest. Verify that fixes actually work. A closed ticket isn't proof.

Executives want to know three things: how risky is it, what will it cost, and how will we know it's working. Frame your plan around those.

Show the findings in terms of business impact, such as slower detection of account compromise or exposure of customer data. Present a phased roadmap with rough costs and expected risk reduction. Offer a small set of metrics that will demonstrate progress over time.

Treating the report as the finish line. The assessment is the start.

Fixing the easy stuff only. Quick wins matter, but don't ignore the hard, high-risk items.

Buying a tool to solve a process problem. Often the issue is workflow or tuning, not software.

Skipping validation. Test that changes behave as intended.

Blaming individuals. Findings usually reflect systemic gaps, not personal failures.

Picture a healthcare services firm whose assessment flagged missing identity logs, noisy alerts, and no formal after-hours coverage. They spent the first month updating contacts and tuning the five loudest rules. By month three they'd onboarded identity logs and run a tabletop exercise that exposed unclear authority for isolating systems. By month nine they had an on-call rotation and a simple set of response metrics.

Nothing dramatic, just steady, prioritized work. At their reassessment, three domains had moved up a maturity level, and the team could point to numbers instead of impressions.

A report is only as valuable as what happens next. Sort findings by risk and effort, grab the quick wins, plan the heavier lifts, and keep ownership clear. Do that consistently, and the next assessment becomes less of an interrogation and more of a progress check.

The Same TSA Scanner That Lets Laptops Stay In Your Bag Is Quietly Ruining Other Gear

📅 Nov 05, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

While some passengers might not directly notice it, airport security checkpoints across the United States are changing. For most travelers, the most visible benefit is simple: laptops, tablets, and approved liquids can increasingly stay inside carry-on bags, removing one of the most familiar airport security rituals of the past two decades. However, this same technology that makes the checkpoint feel more convenient also creates new problems for some travelers.

The Transportation Security Administration (TSA) has been rolling out advanced computed tomography, commonly known as CT, scanners as part of a major security technology upgrade. In March 2022, the agency awarded two orders totaling up to $781.2 million for base and full-size CT X-ray systems for airport checkpoints, while later procurement added even more units to the program. These machines are designed to improve threat detection by creating three-dimensional images of carry-on bags.

TSA officers can rotate and inspect those images in ways that were not possible with older two-dimensional X-ray machines. For the average passenger, this means fewer items need to be removed from bags, while for travelers carrying analog film, overstuffed carry-ons, or other sensitive items, the story is less straightforward.

The new CT scanning equipment represents a major shift from the traditional X-ray equipment passengers have become used to at airport checkpoints. Older systems produced flatter images, making it harder for officers to distinguish between items layered together inside a bag. This is one reason laptops and other large electronics often need to be removed and screened separately.

The introduction of newer CT systems, which use more advanced imaging and algorithms to create 3D views of carry-on content, is increasingly changing this. According to TSA, officers can view and rotate the image on three axes to identify possible threat items more effectively. The agency has described the technology as both a security upgrade and a way to improve the passenger experience. Today, these scanners are already available at some of the largest hubs in the United States, including Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport (ATL), Chicago O'Hare International Airport (ORD), and New York JFK Airport (JFK), among many others.

For travelers, the most obvious change is the reduced need to unpack their hand luggage at the conveyor belt. TSA has stated that under current screening procedures for CT lanes, laptops are allowed to remain in bags. In some airport press releases, the agency has also noted that passengers screened through CT units do not need to remove 3-1-1 liquids or laptops. Travelers no longer need to place laptops at the top of a backpack just to remove them quickly, and toiletry bags do not always need to be kept in an outside pocket.

The downside is that CT scanners do not treat every item the same way, and their more powerful imaging is useful for security screening, but it can be a problem for undeveloped photographic materials. This is where the new technology creates a completely different experience for analog photographers than for passengers carrying digital equipment. The new CT scanners can damage or destroy unprocessed film rolls, and the stronger imaging can cause damage in a single screening. CT scanners can fog unprocessed rolls, reduce image quality, and cause permanent damage, forcing analog photographers to change how they move through security.

However, this does not mean that every piece of camera equipment is at risk; digital cameras, lenses, batteries, and memory cards are not affected in the same way. The concern is specifically undeveloped film, including rolls carried separately, film already loaded inside a camera, disposable analog cameras, and instant materials. For most travelers, this may sound like a niche problem, but analog photography has become increasingly popular again among travelers who want a different look from digital smartphone photos. Many unaware passengers might also fail to realize there is a risk until after their trip, when the damage is already done.

Airport security technology has changed greatly over the last 25 years.

As a result of the new scanners, the most important rule for travelers carrying analog materials is simple: do not allow it to go through a CT scanner if it can be avoided. TSA’s own guidance recommends placing undeveloped film and cameras containing undeveloped film in carry-on bags, or taking undeveloped film to the checkpoint and asking for a hand inspection.

That means film should not be buried at the bottom of a suitcase. Instead, it should be packed so it's easy to remove before reaching the scanner. It is also advisable to separate unprocessed rolls from cameras where possible and place them in transparent, quart-sized zipper bags on top of the carry-on. That makes it easier for TSA officers to inspect without delaying the process and the entire line behind.

The hand inspection is not especially complicated, but it does take a bit of time, since TSA officers generally take the film to a separate area, visually inspect each roll, swab it for explosive residue, and then manually clear it. The process typically takes three to 10 minutes, with passengers advised to arrive around 15 to 20 minutes earlier if they know they will need the extra step. It is important to note that travelers should clearly tell the TSA officer that they are carrying undeveloped photographic materials and would like a hand inspection.

TSA PreCheck Touchless ID is available at 65 airports nationwide and supports more than 100 participating airlines

The new CT machines have also changed the carry-on versus checked-bag decision process. Many passengers assume that fragile or valuable items should stay in the cabin, but when flying with film, there is another reason to avoid checked baggage. Checked luggage is also screened using similar powerful equipment, and passengers generally do not have the same opportunity to request a hand inspection once the bag disappears behind the check-in counter.

For that reason, film travelers should keep undeveloped rolls, disposable cameras, and cameras containing film in their hand luggage. This does not mean placing them deep inside a trolley that may need to be gate-checked later. The safest option is to keep film in a personal item or small carry-on that will remain accessible through the checkpoint.

There is also a practical difference between domestic and international travel. TSA guidance applies in the United States, where passengers can request a hand inspection. Procedures abroad may vary, and foreign airport security staff may not always handle film requests in the same way. For travelers connecting internationally, that means planning ahead and expecting the same conversation more than once.

The 100ml rule is finally being scrapped after last year's false start.

Besides the potential risk to analog film, newer CT scanning equipment can also create problems for travelers carrying oversized or overstuffed bags, since the scanners have fixed tunnel openings. This means a bag that does not physically fit through the machine cannot be screened in that lane.

That matters because many passengers have become used to a certain amount of flexibility around carry-on sizes. Most major US airlines use a standard domestic carry-on limit of around 22 x 14 x 9 inches (56 x 36 x 23 cm). However, in practice, the strictness of the enforcement has often depended on the airline policy, gate agent, aircraft type, and how full the flight is. Soft-sided bags and expanded trolleys could sometimes slip through, especially when passengers were not asked to place them in a sizing frame.

However, the CT scanners at security lanes are now changing that dynamic, and if a carry-on is too large for the scanner, the issue arises at the security checkpoint rather than at the gate. In that sense, the new scanning technology has become an indirect baggage-size enforcement tool for airlines, and while passengers might not be trying to break a rule, an expanded carry-on suitcase that once passed through older equipment may now be pulled aside before screening can even continue.

The discovery of two live smoke grenades hidden inside a jar of peanut butter in a passenger's checked luggage highlights the importance of screening.

For passengers, the CT rollout at TSA checkpoints is making security increasingly convenient for the average passenger but less forgiving for travelers carrying certain items. Laptops and liquids staying in the bag is a real improvement, as it avoids the need to unpack half a bag in public, but it does not mean passengers can stop thinking about how their carry-on is packed.

A carry-on now needs to be packed not only to meet the airline’s baggage rules but also to fit within the physical limits of the checkpoint itself. For most travelers, the best strategy is to keep laptops and liquids where they fit naturally, while making sure the bag remains within airline carry-on dimensions and is not expanded beyond its normal size. For passengers traveling with analog cameras and photo rolls, it is important they remain accessible and separated where possible. This ensures it is ready to be handed to an officer for a manual inspection before the bag enters the scanner.

The best approach is therefore not to assume every checkpoint will work the same way. Travelers should still arrive prepared for traditional lanes, especially at airports where older and newer machines operate side by side. At the same time, those carrying analog photographic materials or borderline-sized bags should plan around the newer system.

Have you ever thought about how these holdings are taxed though? It's not like a mutual fund or exchange-traded fund, which require a yearly reckoning. As a C corporation, Berkshire's stock holdings are taxed much like an individual investor's are. That is, the capital gains tax bill is incurred for the tax year in which the asset is sold.

Regardless, there's a pretty big future tax liability sitting on Berkshire's books right now. Just don't worry about it if you're a shareholder. It's unlikely you'll even notice when the time comes to sell any of these positions.

As of the middle of this year, Berkshire Hathaway was sitting on $323.8 billion worth of individual stocks, including Coca-Cola, Apple, and American Express. It only paid a total of $106.5 billion for these picks over the course of the past many years, however, translating into unrealized net gains of $217.3 billion that will eventually become realized, taxable gains. For perspective on that figure, Berkshire's total market cap at the time was $1.1 trillion. All of these numbers have changed somewhat since then, although not by much.

Current shareholders don't need to worry too much about capital gains taxes chipping away at the conglomerate's net profits, however. Like his predecessor, Abel moves big positions around pretty slowly, using time to the company's full advantage.

And this raises the question: If its stock portfolio is currently worth $361.3 billion, while the organization's market cap stands at nearly $1.2 trillion, what accounts for the remaining $828.7 billion in Berkshire Hathaway's market valuation?

Another $365.5 billion of it is cash or near-cash equivalents, such as short-term government bonds. That means the other $463.2 billion reflects the perceived value of all the privately owned businesses that are part of the Berkshire Hathaway family, including Clayton Homes, insurer GEICO, Duracell batteries, and Dairy Queen, just to name a few.

His career has never reached the same heights, but the key point is that its trajectory was, in that moment of acceptance, drastically changed.

The old thinking was wrong. That job just wasn’t right for him.

He’s not the only one. There are a few examples, from Graham Potter to Julen Lopetegui, and there’s much more to it than just “backing yourself”.

Unai Emery is at a very different point of his own career, having experienced historic Europa League success  – with three different clubs – over 22 years, but it’s impossible not to wonder whether any of this weighed on his own thinking as he now discusses a new deal with Aston Villa.

Many in football even believe that one reason his club resorted to the absurdity of last week’s legal threat against Jamie Carragher was aggravation about their manager consistently being linked to “a bigger club”.

This, it should be acknowledged, is still Manchester United. It’s Sir Alex Ferguson, Sir Matt Busby and his babes, and so much glory and legacy. Despite all the current problems at Old Trafford, there is a history and consequently inspiration for the future; of what you might be able to do with that.

Others in football believe Emery might previously have been open to joining such a club, primarily because of the will to finally win a major league and the Champions League.

That isn’t to say either are indefinitely impossible at Villa given the club’s rapid recent growth, but their own fans have been frustrated over regulatory constraints.

And yet such strands lead into the nub of this, where this new thinking is warranted.

Emery’s record above Europa League level might raise considerable questions over whether he would suit a club of United’s scale, and those at Arsenal and Paris Saint-Germain have long insisted his outlook didn’t fit.

Far more relevant, however, might be that United just don’t suit Emery.

There are numerous persuasive arguments as to why he should absolutely sign that contract and stay at Villa.

None of them are to deepen these debates about club size, or even Emery’s own career.

It’s more that the 55-year-old is in the very rare modern position of having earned an almost ideal job.

At Villa, everything is built around him, in a way you just don’t really see at this level any more. The only comparison in the Premier League or any of Europe’s wealthiest clubs is at Arsenal, where Mikel Arteta was only given such authority after initially proving himself. Emery has almost complete control, and those circumstances have ensured everything is in tune. The work of a very good manager is consequently greatly amplified, so that Villa consistently over-perform.

That can even be seen in how they’ve responded to the great frustration of the summer. They’re already in far better shape than would have been expected. Tactical shifts can take time, and be painful, as currently being seen at Newcastle United. On Premier League pitches, the game is so systemised and optimised that the margins have become very thin. Hence wild swings in results when clubs aren’t in tune.

At Villa, it’s almost note-perfect for Emery. That isn’t something to be given up easily.

Designed by Renna Studio and architect Angelo Renna, Firenze Little Island is an architectural proposal for a demountable, seasonal public floating platform on the Arno River in Florence, Italy. Sited along the Pescaia weir, where water flow is regulated, the project introduces an amphibious public space designed for cultural gatherings, orchestral performances, and recreation during the calmer summer months between May and September.

The installation, conceived by Renna Studio’s architect Angelo Renna, utilizes a lightweight, reversible modular structure comprising prefabricated floating pontoons topped with a timber beam substructure and finished decking. Encircling the central open-air deck is a segmented, circular planter ring approximately one meter (3.3 feet) high, accommodating 39 mature trees. This planted perimeter acts as both a spatial boundary and an environmental buffer, moderating the localized microclimate through natural shading and air circulation. Engineered for seasonal disassembly ahead of autumn and winter flood conditions, the pontoons, decking modules, and segmented planters are designed to be broken down, transported, and stored, with the root systems maintained in municipal nurseries until reassembly. Originally proposed with municipal backing in 2024 and supported by Heritage Superintendent Antonella Ranaldi in early 2026 before municipal plans paused, the proposal articulates a circular, low-impact strategy for re-integrating the historic riverfront into the civic fabric of Florence.

Chipotle continues to show signs of turning around its business with solid same-store sales growth. However, inflation continues to run high, and its sales growth has not yet reached a level to help it stabilize its restaurant-level margins. This is leading to reduced earnings power.

It is estimated that more than one million Delta passengers may have paid for these seats. A judge has now rejected the airline's attempt to have the case thrown out, following a similar ruling in July that allowed an almost identical lawsuit against United Airlines to proceed.

On Wednesday, November 4, US District Judge Ramon E. Reyes Jr. of the Eastern District of New York ruled that the passengers' breach of contract claims are not preempted by federal law, rejecting one of Delta's central arguments for dismissing the case. Delta, represented by law firm King & Spalding, had argued that the lawsuit should be dismissed on several grounds.

According to Law360, Delta's defense centered on its Contract of Carriage, which it says makes no promise to provide passengers with a window. It also explicitly states that seat assignments "are not guaranteed and form no part of this contract." Delta added that any claims based on seat map labels, tickets or marketing materials fall outside the contract and are therefore preempted by the Airline Deregulation Act (ADA).

On the other hand, the plaintiffs — represented by New York law firm Greenbaum Olbrantz — argued that Delta's use of the word "window" is a commitment to provide an actual window, particularly when it charges an additional fee for the seat. The plaintiffs’ lawyer, Carter Greenbaum, said when the suit was first filed:

"They sold customers window seats and ended up seating them next to a wall."

The case was originally filed on August 19, 2025, as Meyer v. Delta Air Lines, with Brooklyn resident Nicholas Meyer leading the lawsuit. According to the original complaint, Meyer paid extra for window seats on a trip from LaGuardia Airport (LGA) to John Wayne Airport (SNA) in Orange County, connecting through Delta's hub at Hartsfield-Jackson Atlanta International Airport (ATL).

However, when he boarded a Boeing 757-200 for the second sector of his journey, he discovered that seat 23F was next to a blank cabin wall rather than a window. As a result, he spent the roughly four-and-a-half-hour flight without the view he had paid for. The lawsuit states that some seats on Delta's 737s, 757s and A321s have no windows because of air conditioning ducts and other equipment behind the panels, but this was not made clear during booking.

The plaintiffs say they would not have paid for these seats if Delta had made their lack of windows clear. They also argue that Delta could easily prevent the problem by updating its booking system. The complaint points out that the airline already identifies other seat limitations during the selection process, such as restricted recline or a lack of under-seat storage, but does not identify seats without windows.

This is unlike rivals American Airlines and Alaska Airlines, which clearly inform customers during seat selection if a 'window seat' has no window. Southwest Airlines, which introduced assigned seating on January 27, 2026, has also added a warning to its seat selection page.

United clearly believes the lawsuit has no merit.

United must also face a similar lawsuit to Delta's after losing its own bid to have the case dismissed in July. United had claimed that the term "window" describes a seat's position next to the cabin wall, rather than promising passengers an actual view. It also argued that federal law prevented the plaintiffs from bringing their claims.

US District Judge James Donato rejected both arguments, stating that United's ticketing terms "plausibly establish that United expressly agreed to provide a seat with a window." Both lawsuits are seeking millions of dollars in damages, along with changes to how the airlines identify and advertise seats that have no window.

For all that sci-fi television has become much more experimental narrative formulas in the age of streaming, one corner of the genre remains surprisingly attached to tradition: superhero shows. Even the most adventurous and ambitious superhero TV shows seem to eventually fall back into old storytelling habits. Even the series that deliberately lean into strangeness for a selling point do so with the promise that everything will eventually click into place and the true heroes and villains of the story will meet for a final showdown no matter how morally ambiguous they’d been up until that point.

That makes an often overlooked 2017-2019 FX series is deserving of much more recognition. Before Disney+ explored superhero surrealism through MCU shows like WandaVision and VisionQuest and Prime Video lambasted the entire genre with The Boys and Invincible, Legion was already dismantling expectations. Creator Noah Hawley was tasked with making a Marvel adaptation, and instead delivered a series that treats every scene as a chance to experiment. The show’s 27 episodes are dripping with confusion, discomfort, and unpredictability, and it makes for some thoroughly entertaining viewing.

In terms of the show’s premise, Legion follows David Haller (Dan Stevens), a psychiatric patient who discovers his mental health struggles are linked to extraordinary mutant abilities. While the series is loosely connected to Fox’s X-Men movies and draws from Marvel Comics mythology, Legion’s plot stands on its own, and Noah Hawley used that independence to turn what could have been a standard superhero TV show into something truly unique.

From the moment it begins, literally nothing about Legion plays into expectations surrounding its plot and narrative structure. For example, most superhero shows spend time establishing a status quo for reality before introducing something that threatens it. Legion makes reality itself the problem. Memories can be manipulated, seemingly ordinary spaces become mental prisons, and it’s always unclear whether a scene is actually happening or just playing out inside someone’s head. Because David’s mental state means he can’t trust his own experience, the same applies for viewers too.

This combination of atmospheric surrealism and an incredibly unreliable narrator defines how Legion’s story is told. The show’s 27 episodes include some incredibly avant-garde installments. Season 1's “Chapter 7” turns escape from a mental institution into a black-and-white silent film sequence, while “Chapter 10” in season 2 depicts the confrontation between David and central antagonist The Shadow King using dance. This psychedelic approach gives Legion’s psychic conflict its own unique style, making the show’s superpowered struggles more unsettling than a simple exchange of energy blasts or city-leveling fist fight.

It’s not only the scene-by-scene storytelling in Legion that flips expectations on its head. The wider narrative also dismantles a core theme of almost all superhero shows: discovering immense power means inheriting a heroic destiny. David eventually comes to see himself as burdened with glorious purpose, but his self-perception as a savior and the reality of his place in the world couldn’t be more different. His certainty that he deserves love and redemption increasingly justifies the harm he causes others, inverting the traditional message that power, heroism, and responsibility all go hand-in-hand.

Several superhero shows that arrived after Legion have also embraced unreliable perspectives, unconventional formats, and morally compromised "heroes". Few, however, are able to sustain the sense that even their storytelling methods can unexpectedly change at any moment across three whole seasons. Legion may not be the most accessible series out there, but its experimental style shapes the entire experience. Its underlying uncertainty is inseparable from its explorations of what ideas like “truth” and “reality” even mean when everyone’s perception is subjective, and this thematic interest in turn means it breaks almost every narrative rule.

Calling Legion a superhero show is accurate as a description of its source material. The show incorporates X-Men characters such as David himself, the Shadow King, and even Professor Charles Xavier. As a description of actually watching it, however, the “superhero TV show” label is incredibly misleading. Sure, there are genre staples like mutants, secretive organizations, and potentially catastrophic powers, but the show itself has little interest in using these elements to construct a good vs. evil superhero narrative about saving the day.

If one were to assess Legion in a vacuum without considering its X-Men roots, psychological horror is a much more appropriate genre label. David’s mind is a thoroughly unsafe place where childhood memories conceal terrifying intrusions from outsiders, and apparent understanding is often just a trick to disguise more secrets. Alongside psychological horror, Legion also works as an experimental sci-fi series, using concepts like psychic spaces and, eventually, time travel to explore identity, responsibility, and the desire to rewrite painful experiences.

There’s also romance to be found, but once again, this isn’t the kind of love story usually found in superhero shows. David’s relationship with Syd Barrett (Rachel Keller) initially seems like it will be the source of a glimmer of hope, that it’s going to be a tale of two isolated people finally finding connection. However, like everything else in Legion, the love story soon gives way to some unsettling explorations of themes like consent, bodily autonomy, and manipulation. Since David and other characters can violate boundaries in ways ordinary people physically cannot, this leads to some truly disturbing moments.

That makes Legion the perfect superhero TV show for anyone suffering from superhero fatigue. It’s not a superhero story: it’s a deep and complex drama that just so happens to center on characters who first appeared in comic books. Its three-season arc is a psychedelic tragedy that uses its character’s spectacular abilities as tools to explore some truly dark ideas. Legion may technically be a superhero series, but it’s one that feels ideal for viewers who openly balk at them.
