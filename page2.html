Avengers: Doomsday TV Spot Confirms RDJ's Doctor Doom Has A Scarred Face

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Marvel Comics fans know Doctor Victor von Doom is scarcely ever seen without his iron mask — a mask that hides his supposedly horribly scarred face. But with Marvel Studios choosing former Tony Stark actor Robert Downey Jr. to play Doom in the upcoming "Avengers" movies "Doomsday" and "Secret Wars," there's been an expectation that Doom's mask coming off will be a jaw dropping moment.

Now, Chris Evans has claimed that Doom wears his mask for the entirety of "Doomsday." But that could only mean he'll keep the mask on until "Secret Wars." Even then, there's the chance that Doom is so disfigured that his face isn't even recognizable as RDJ. A new TV spot for "Doomsday" does suggest Doom has his scarred face, while also teasing we're eventually going to see exactly what's under his mask ... but not yet.

In this TV spot, Magneto (Ian McKellen) tries to telekinetically rip off Doom's mask — it is made of metal, after all. But Doom does not restrict himself to the laws of science. There's evidently a magical seal on the mask (note there appear to be runic designs across Doom's costume), because glowing green tendrils extend out from Doom's hood and grab the mask, slamming it back onto Doom's face. A brief glimpse of Doom's face, visible through the mask's eyeholes, shows hints of pale macerated skin and scar tissue.

In the "Fantastic Four" comics, it was originally said that Doom suffered his scars during an experiment in college. A machine he built to contact the "nether world" (later revealed to be an attempt to reach his mother's damned soul) exploded in his face, leaving him scarred.

Now, Doom's co-creator Jack Kirby intended that Doom actually only had a thin facial scar — but Doom's ego can't stand even that small imperfection. Later "Fantastic Four" writer/artist John Byrne melded the two ideas. The machine explosion only slightly damaged Doom's face, but then Victor — convinced his face was already ruined — put his freshly forged mask on before it cooled, and that is why he has a horribly scarred face.

The greatest source of Doom's torment is completely self-inflicted. The exemplary 1989 "Doctor Strange and Doctor Doom: Triumph and Torment" goes with Byrne's interpretation. When Doom seeks out the wisdom of the wizard named the Aged Genghis, the sorcerer alludes to Doom's "minor disfigurement." We then see Doom's (barely scarred) face as Victor lashes out at the insult.

It's unclear what origin "Doomsday" will go with, if any of them. Marketing suggests the Marvel Cinematic Universe's Doom will be driven by a lost wife and child, not his mother. What is clear from this TV spot is that Marvel fans can expect to see Doom and Magneto — the two greatest villains of Marvel Comics — clash onscreen. Doom's wizardry here refutes the obvious conclusion that Magneto wins easily because he controls metal and Doom is concealed inside metal armor.

"He is pain, he is extinction," Magneto declares, setting up Doom's famous third person double entendre: "I am Doom!" Ian McKellen's Magneto was the first villain of the Marvel movie boom that kicked off with 2000's "X-Men." Bringing him back to face (and presumably fall to) Doom is some classic carnival barker marketing that would make Stan Lee proud.

"Avengers: Doomsday" is set for theatrical release on December 18, 2026.

Michigan State-Rutgers scouting report: Banged-up Spartans close season

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Looking ahead to Michigan State's final game of the 2026 season:

Matchup: Michigan State (3-8, 1-7 Big Ten) vs. Rutgers (3-8, 2-6).

Where: SHI Stadium, Piscataway, New Jersey.

Out: LT Conner Moore, LB Brady Pretzlaff, QB Alessio Milivojevic, S Devin Vaught, LB Jordan Hall, RB Marvis Parrish, CB Tyran Chappell, TE Jayden Savoury, RB Cam Edwards, FB Jay Coyne, OL Tristan Comer, TE Joe Caudill. Questionable: DT Ben Roberts, DT Eli Coenen, QB Cam Fancher, WR Rodney Bullard Jr., S Michael Richard, TE Brennan Parachek.

Out: QB Sean Ashenfelder, DB Kaj Sanders, WR KJ Duff, DB Zahmir Dawud, DB Chris Jones, OL Kwabena Asamoah. Questionable: RB Antwan Raymond, LB Abram Wright, QB AJ Surace, DB Sage Clawges, LB Joey Kopec. Probable: QB Dylan Lonergan, WR Dyzier Carter.

When MSU has the ball: Alessio Milivojevic will miss his third game in the Spartans' final five to end the season after starting the final four last year and making eight starts as a third-year sophomore. Backup Cam Fancher again is listed as questionable after being a game-time decision but sitting out last week's 45-7 loss to Oregon. If Fancher is unable to play again, Kayd Coffman could make his third start of his freshman year and second in a row. The Spartans got running back Jaziun Patterson back against the Ducks, and he teamed with Brandon Tullis to fight through their individual injuries but struggled in the ground game with left tackle Conner Moore out and multiple moving parts up front. Luka Vincic started at right tackle, with Nick Sharpe sliding over to the left side.

Coach Pat Fitzgerald called Greg Schiano's Rutgers team a mirror image of the Spartans this season, and the offense-vs-defense matchup shows it. MSU ranks 130th nationally in scoring offense (17.7 points) while the Scarlet Knights are 123rd in points allowed (31.5), but they are coming off an impressive 25-24 win at Penn State last week in which they survived giving up 387 yards (161 rushing, 233 passing). Rutgers ranks near the bottom of the Football Bowl Subdivision in total defense (411.2 yards, 119th) and rushing defense (188.0 yards, 119th) while giving up 223.2 passing yards (89th) with a putrid 45.2% third-down conversion rate (124th) against them. Something has to give, as MSU's offense has been equally as troublesome – 134th out of 138 FBS teams in total offense (288.7 yards), 129th in rushing (106.9 yards) and 115th in passing (181.8 yards).

When Rutgers has the ball: The middle of the Spartans' defense will be thin again as they play without their top two linebackers again in Jordan Hall and Brady Pretzlaff, safety Devin Vaught and nickelback Michael Richard. Eli Coenen and Ben Roberts also are listed as questionable, but young defensive tackles Derrick Simmons, Mikeshun Beeler and Carlos Hazelwood have shown promise and production since Roberts' injury at UCLA on Oct. 24. With Pretzlaff and Hall out, and with Dion Crawford and Cam Stodghill each getting nicked up against Oregon, MSU's linebacker corps has been significantly shorthanded.

The Spartans did get cornerbacks Tre Bell and Charles Brantley back, which allowed NiJhay Burt to get more reps at nickelback and Aveon Grose to slide to safety to offset the injuries to Vaught and Richard. With all the injuries, Joe Rossi has managed to piece together a unit that ranks 45th in total defense (344.3 yards) and is 57th against the run (137.8 yards) and 61st against the pass (206.5 yards) but has struggled to get off the field on third downs (38.8%, 118th). MSU's 25.3 points allowed ranks 74th, but Rutgers' offense has struggled to score all season and ranks 100th at 23.4 points.

The Scarlet Knights are averaging just 334.0 yards a game (119th), including 134.1 yards on the ground (101st) and 199.9 through the air (98th) while also struggling to convert on third down (35%, 106th). This will be the final game of the season for both teams, with MSU failing to earn a bowl berth for the fifth straight year and sixth time in the past seven.

Quarterback questions: Like the Spartans, the Scarlet Knights also are dealing with injury issues at quarterback. Schiano has used three different starters this season, with Sean Ashenfelder (left ankle) out, AJ Surace (ribs) questionable and Dylan Lonergan (undisclosed) probable for Saturday. Surace, despite having three broken ribs, started his third game of the season and threw the winning 2-point pass in last week's win at Penn State, Rutgers' first over the Nittany Lions in 38 years. He finished 24 of 39 for 178 yards with a touchdown. Lonergan hasn't played since Oct. 24 against Northwestern after throwing for 291 yards and three TDs in a win over Maryland a week earlier.

Big loss: Whether Surace or Lonergan gets the nod, they won't have Rutgers' top passing target available. Wide receiver KJ Duff had season-ending ankle surgery earlier this month but remains fourth in the Big Ten at 96.8 yards per game (774 yards on just 45 receptions) and is still tied for fourth with nine touchdown catches. Since Duff got hurt against Northwestern on Oct. 24, the Scarlet Knights are averaging just 120.8 passing yards and have just three touchdown catches in those five games.

Ground gains: Though he is questionable with an undisclosed injury, running back Antwaun Raymond leads the Big Ten this season with 911 rushing yards (82.8 per game) on 185 attempts (4.9 per carry). He also has eight rushing touchdowns along with 20 catches for 202 yards receiving with another score. The 5-foot-11, 205-pound junior from Montreal ran 23 times for 101 yards and two touchdowns at PSU last week as well as a critical lateral near the goal line as time expired for the final touchdown to set up Surace's winning 2-point pass.

Not much history: Fitzgerald and Schiano have met just one time before as head coaches, when Fitzgerald's host Northwestern team defeated Rutgers, 21-7, in 2021 for the Wildcats' only Big Ten win that season in Schiano's second season of his second stint with the Scarlet Knights. Fitzgerald won his only game at SHI Stadium in 2018, 18-15, en route to a Big Ten West division title with Northwestern. The Spartans are 8-3 all-time against Rutgers and 5-2 in Piscataway, but they have lost the last two meetings – a 27-24 fourth-quarter collapse in 2023 and a jarring 41-14 blowout loss at home to close the 2024 season.

Punting predicament: MSU senior punter Rhys Dakin enters the final weekend of the regular season averaging 48.0 yards on 38 attempts, which would lead the Big Ten by 0.1 yards over USC's Lachlan Carrigan and 0.9 in front of Washington's Hunter Green. Dakin also would rank fifth in the FBS this season. However, his 3.45 punts per game average falls just below the 3.6 per game threshold for the NCAA's stat rankings, and neither he nor Carrigan have enough to qualify.

MSU 20, Rutgers 14: The Spartans have not quit on the season despite their injuries, and neither have the Scarlet Knights – even after a shocking opening-week at home against Massachusetts. The difference comes down to the defense of Rossi, who was an assistant at Rutgers from 2012-15, and Fitzgerald's motivation to finish strong heading into the offseason.

With help from Matt Damon and Zack Snyder, Ford announces return to Le Mans

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The giant in American automaking announced on Thursday, Nov. 26 that it will return to the iconic endurance race held annually in France with its new Ford GT41 Hypercar.

It's been 60 years since Ford became the first American automaker to win at Le Mans, as three Fords — piloted by Ken Miles, Bruce McLaren and Ronnie Bucknum — finished first, second and third at the race. The 1966 24 Hours of Le Mans and the events leading up to it were dramatized in a 2019 Academy Award-winning film Ford v Ferrari, directed by James Mangold and starring Matt Damon and Christian Bale. The film was nominated for Best Picture and won Oscars for Best Film Editing and Best Sound Editing.

To help Ford with the announcement, Damon provided his voice to a narration over a commercial directed by Zack Snyder, known for films such as 300 and Justice League. The commercial aired on Thanksgiving during the NFL game between the Chicago Bears and Ford's hometown Detroit Lions.

The nearly two-minute long commercial was titled "Go Like Hell" — a phrase that Carroll Shelby wrote on a board to show to driver Ken Miles, telling him to push the car past its limits as they raced at the 24 Hours of Daytona ahead of Le Mans.

"The amazing part about getting a chance to work on this 'Go Like Hell' spot for Ford Racing was the chance to mix the nostalgia of the '66 GT40 with this modern Hypercar and its incredible engineering, as well as this narrative that follows the transporting of the car across America," Snyder said in a statement. "It's during this journey that we get to see what drives Ford to win. And as a filmmaker, those elements inspired me to make some cool images."

Damon added in a statement: "Telling the story of Le Mans '66 was one of the highlights of my career, so getting to lend my voice to the next chapter felt very full circle. Revisiting this one with Ford and Zack, as they take on the world's toughest racing all over again, was a real privilege."

The commercial revisits a familiar filming location, as the hangar used to load the GT41's shipping container is the same location featured in Ford v Ferrari as the site of the Ford GT40 race workshop.

Ford won four consecutive Le Mans from 1966 to 1969. Their second win in 1967 was by a team that featured AJ Foyt and Dan Gurney, two of the greatest American racecar drivers of all time. Foyt won four Indy 500s and a Daytona 500, while Gurney had four Formula 1 Grand Prix wins and five career NASCAR Grand National Series wins.

Six decades later, Ford remains the only American automaker to win at Le Mans. Toyota has won six of the last nine 24 Hours of Le Mans, with Ferrari winning the other three.

"Having completed six European tests, culminating in our first 30-hour endurance test at Aragon, we're now preparing for the next phase of development on American soil," said Dan Sayers, the Ford Racing Hypercar program manager. "We're under no illusions about the scale of the challenge ahead. Our focus remains on developing the car's performance and reliability, while continuing to evolve as a team ahead of our competitive debut."

Ford will also enter the GT41 Hypercar into the FIA World Endurance Championship, which begins in Qatar in March. That race is where the GT41 Hypercar will officially make its debut. Ford Racing said in a statement that it "continues to expand its global racing efforts" to 41 different racing series across six continents.

"We're going back to Le Mans next summer in June to take on Ferrari and the best of the world to win Le Mans outright. It's an all-Ford effort. We're not having another company manage the athletes and the effort — this is all on Ford with some key partnerships," Ford CEO Jim Farley told FOX Business. "We really think of ourselves as America's Race Team."

The Gt41 Hypercar takes inspiration from the classic GT40 and stands 41 inches tall. The engine was designed at Ford's home in Dearborn, Michigan, and the naturally aspirated Coyote-based V8 is powered by an advanced hybrid propulsion system. Ford also said it brought its "discipline learned through our partnership with Red Bull Racing" in Formula 1 to accelerate the process.

Here's the simplest reason to buy Nvidia, and an argument for why it's best to keep the semiconductor stock on a watch list for now.

Heading into this year, some investors were concerned that Nvidia would be unable to maintain its breakneck revenue growth rate and high gross margins. But hyperscalers continued to ramp up AI capital expenditures. And Nvidia has broadened its customer base to other enterprises, AI start-ups, AI labs, and neoclouds.

As a result, Nvidia's earnings growth began outpacing its stock price growth, compressing its valuation. But then the unthinkable happened.

Nvidia's stock price has plummeted despite exceptional results and guidance for 75% revenue growth in fiscal 2028. Nvidia is on track to surpass Alphabet as the world's most profitable company. It now trades at just 15.4 times forward earnings -- making it the cheapest Magnificent Seven stock despite having the highest gross margins and the fastest revenue growth rate.

For context, the forward P/E of the S&P 500 (^GSPC -0.25%) is 17.3 -- meaning Nvidia is now priced like a value stock.

Buying high-quality companies on sale is an excellent way to compound wealth over time. Nvidia doesn't have to deliver blowout results to impress investors. In fact, its growth rate could fall substantially, and it would still be a steal at current levels.

The issue isn't Nvidia's valuation -- it's investors' concerns about where the company could be headed over the next three to five years.

Jensen Huang's sudden resignation exposed Nvidia's extreme vulnerability to key-person risk, governance failures, and an incapable board of directors. Granted, Nvidia is incredibly well positioned to endure a slowdown because of its industry-leading position and rock solid balance sheet. But it could also lead to stagnating or declining earnings in the medium term, which would challenge Nvidia's valuation.

All told, Nvidia simply has too many unknowns right now. Until investors get more clarity on the findings of the investigations, the company's succession plan for Huang, and the extent of its governance overhaul, Nvidia remains a hold.

All 3 The Rings Of Power Seasons, Ranked Worst To Best

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

You'd need to go back to 2003 and The Return of the King to find the last universally acclaimed Tolkien adaptation. Whether it be Peter Jackson's The Hobbit movies and The War of the Rohirrim on the big screen, or Prime Video's The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power on TV, many years have passed since Middle-earth experienced any degree of critical consensus.

The Rings of Power has proven especially divisive. The mere concept of Amazon bringing the Second Age into live-action triggered controversy by the bucket load, and opinions split further thanks to a series of significant deviations from canon: Sauron and Galadriel's romance, the 'orcs aren't so bad' angle, and Tom Bombadil: Defense Against the Dark Arts teacher.

As much as it's difficult to shower The Rings of Power with undiluted adulation, it's also impossible to deny there are elements that J.D. Payne and Patrick McKay's series does very well. This, combined with each season covering distinct periods of the Second Age's timeline, means that The Rings of Power's three seasons released so far vary considerably in quality.

The Rings of Power season 4 is confirmed and, if the showrunners have their way, a fifth and final season will follow sometime thereafter. Until then, The Rings of Power is following a very distinct trend in terms of overall quality.

The Rings of Power didn't exactly put its best foot forward at the beginning. It seems strange now, but the series initially thought the best way to adapt source material almost a century old was by following a 'mystery box' format. The Rings of Power's first season banked hard on questions like, "Who is the Stranger?" and "Who is Halbrand?" Answers to these plot points were handled like huge revelations, but in reality, the actor who looked like a young Gandalf turned out to be playing Gandalf, and the character behaving like Sauron turned out to be Sauron.

Looking back, season 1 kept its cards too close to its chest, and the whole affair ended up more akin to a Lord of the Rings imitator than a genuine Second Age TV series. The Stranger stood in for Gandalf, Adar was black metal's answer to Sauron, Nori and Poppy were your new Frodo and Sam, and Arondir covered the same bases as Legolas. Were it not for the folks in Lindon and Númenor, viewers would have been forgiven for asking whether The Rings of Power was actually a licensed Middle-earth production.

The focus on ambiguity and misdirection took the place of a compelling storyline. Not only does The Rings of Power's first season bear the least resemblance to Tolkien, it's the one season where little of note happens. The Harfoots aren't as interesting as The Rings of Power wants them to be (remember Lenny Henry was a main cast member?), Gil-galad is a shadow of himself, and Númenor is still enjoying some calm before the storm.

It's not all bad, of course. Mordor gets a genuinely fascinating origin story as the Southlands transforms into the volcanic realm The Lord of the Rings fans know and love, while Keeping Up with Khazad-dûm offers some much-needed light relief among all the wistful glances and overwrought foreshadowing.

With Sauron slipping into his Annatar guise, The Rings of Power finally found what it needed most: a canon cornerstone to build from. Wisely spending much time in Eregion, The Rings of Power's second chapter spanned the creation of the Rings of Power themselves, then ended on the Siege of Eregion, one of the Second Age's most memorable battles.

The improvement was palpable. The entire angle hinged on the chemistry between Charlie Vickers' Sauron and Charles Edwards' Celebrimbor, as the pair entered a marriage of blood, fire, and metal. The Rings of Power perfectly captured the nuances of that relationship: Celebrimbor's lofty ambition, Sauron's silky manipulations, and the authentic respect forged between them as they toiled. It also helped that, after an entire first season largely without rings, The Rings of Power finally lived up to its name in season 2, adapting a recognizable story from Tolkien's lore and being more or less faithful.

Tom Bombadil randomly popping up in Rhûn was odd. You'd almost call it fan service, but without the version of Tom fans would know from the books. Galadriel's pining over Sauron also started to grate very quickly, especially after Elrond stepped into the love triangle via a misjudged kiss. And the finale still dropped the 'Gandalf' reveal like the audience still hadn't realized Daniel Weyman wasn't just playing some random wizard.

Generally speaking, however, season 2 was where The Rings of Power started to justify its existence. There was a balance between hard-hitting battle scenes and intelligent character drama, and much less effort spent on trying to keep Tolkien readers on their toes with guessing games. Númenor was a particular highlight, as the tension between Míriel and her bearded cousin boiled over into some rather excellent political backstabbing.

One of the biggest compliments that can be paid to The Rings of Power is that it's still getting better three seasons in. After season 2 ended with the Siege of Eregion, it was left for The Rings of Power's third season to explore the War of Sauron and the Elves, while also handling the Faithful vs. King's Men civil war over in Númenor. The result was Amazon's best season of expensive Middle-earth content yet and, perhaps not coincidentally, the truest to the source material.

By some distance, Sauron shone brightest. Charlie Vickers spent the entire season attempting to construct the One Ring, and his performance exemplified the physical and emotional strain of crafting Arda's most powerful artifact this side of the Silmarils. What Tolkien described of the Ring's creation would fit onto an envelope, and The Rings of Power did admirably to stay within those lines, while also adding logical color between them. It made perfect sense that Sauron needed to know more about the Three before he could control them. And the One Ring's rhythmic method of construction went right back to the biblical opening of The Silmarillion.

The Númenor material continued the good work of season 2, depicting the Faithful as an underground rebellion, and Gil-galad finally turned into a character who vaguely resembles what Tolkien created. One can question whether renaming the Nazgûl was strictly necessary, but watching the likes of Khamûl, Theo, and the two fallen kings in the early stages of their corruption proved an absolute treat. To the east, Gandalf improved immeasurably once his arc dovetailed into the main happenings in Middle-earth.

The Rings of Power isn't quite there in terms of winning over skeptics just yet. Durin's 'wife slept with brother' storyline felt a little too soap opera-y, with very real fears of Disa shouting, "We were on a break!" at one point. The coronation of Thrain contained just a little too much Coronation Street for a Middle-earth series.

It would also be fair to argue that, for a season revolving entirely around two massive wars, The Rings of Power season 3 contained very little war. Opening battle aside, you'd never know conflict was even afoot. Indeed, The Rings of Power twice pulled back from battle scenes at the last minute: once when news of Míriel's wedding prevented the Faithful charging against the King's Men, and then again when Sauron's surrender avoided a clash between his forces and the Númenor-Elf alliance. Hopefully the battle budget is being saved for season 5.

Nevertheless, The Rings of Power sails toward its fourth season with the critical winds at its back, and the gradual improvement seen since the beginning offers reason for optimism that Sauron's trip to Númenor will not disappoint.

Beating the S&P 500 sounds simple until you remember how good the index already is, since it has compounded at about 9.9% a year on a total return basis going back to 1928. So a stock that beats the index over 10 years needs more than a good quarter, it needs a business that can keep growing over the long term.

When I look for that kind of staying power in the consumer world, I aim for three things: a brand that people pay up for, a long expansion runway, and a management team that protects both. Here are three consumer stocks that I think long-term investors should consider for the decade ahead.

Ferrari (RACE +2.96%) deliberately builds fewer cars than people want, and that scarcity is the heart of its economic moat, which is a durable advantage that keeps rivals from stealing its customers.

CEO Benedetto Vigna said in the third-quarter release that deliveries "reflect our clients' ever-deepening desire to make each Ferrari uniquely their own." Those personal touches add profit to every car without adding volume.

Demand isn't the problem. Vigna said on the call, "We continue to experience a healthy demand across all geographies, with an order book that enters 2028."

The risks are a premium valuation, at roughly 36 times earnings, and the shift to electric models like the Luce, which has to win over buyers who love engines.

Hilton (HLT +0.52%) mostly doesn't own its hotels; it collects fees by managing and licensing its brands to owners through a franchise model, which is an arrangement where a business pays to use another company's brand and systems. That lets it grow without spending heavily on buildings.

CEO Chris Nassetta said in the third-quarter release, "We achieved our highest quarter ever for organic openings." He also said on the call, "Globally, Hilton is still less than 6% of supply providing a long runway for continued growth."

Travel is cyclical, though. CFO Kevin Jacobs said, "RevPAR in China declined 5.4% in the quarter," referring to revenue per available room, which is a key hotel measure of how well rooms are filled and priced. Middle East conflict is weighing on that region, too, and the stock trades near 49 times earnings.

Amer Sports (AS +2.56%) owns Arc'teryx jackets, Salomon shoes, and Wilson tennis gear, and every segment grew by double digits in its latest quarter. CEO James Zheng said, "I am very confident in the future outlook for our company"

The U.S. opportunity is what excites me most. Arc'teryx CEO Stuart Haselden noted that the brand has about as many stores in the U.S. as in Canada, which he said "makes it very apparent the potential that we have to expand in the U.S." Management also said Europe's store traffic is a challenge, and higher freight costs are nibbling at margins.

None of these companies will beat the market every year, but I think long-term investors should consider owning all three and giving their growth stories the full decade they deserve. Great brands tend to compound quietly.

What ties these three together is pricing power, which is the ability to raise prices without losing customers, and I think that matters more over a decade than any single quarter's results.

Ferrari's buyers wait years for their cars, Hilton's owners keep signing up for its brands, and Arc'teryx shoppers happily pay premium prices for gear that lasts.

There will be rough stretches, whether it's a travel slowdown, a weak luxury market, or a cold winter for outdoor sales, but investors who hold through those dips give each business time to prove its case. In the end, patience is the edge most investors give up too easily.

Have you ever met a new friend who seemed to think you were too great? You can do no wrong, you’re the first person to ever truly understand them, you’re so much better than their other friends. But then you have a disagreement or miss a birthday party, and they seem to just…turn on you? Now they feel sure that you were only ever kind to them because you wanted something; you’re just like everyone else.

Idealization and devaluation like this are two sides of the same coin—splitting, which is most associated with borderline personality disorder, or BPD. But it, or behaviors that closely resemble it, can also occur in people with narcissistic traits, depression, anxiety, autism, and other conditions. In fact, we all do it to some degree; teenagers are notorious for it. And it happens in all kinds of relationships: friendship, family, professional, marriage or partnership.

Obviously, neither extreme is a complete or fair perception of you. The truth of who you are exists somewhere between the ideal and the devalued, but that liminal space isn’t something your friend has access to right now.

And within that inability to see the space between all-good and all-bad lies the definition of splitting. In all of us, certain cognitive processes go offline when we become dysregulated or lose homeostasis: You don’t communicate well when you’re hungry, you can’t appreciate the beauty of a sunset when you’re angry, and so on. Likewise, when your friend or loved one becomes triggered and splits, their ability to see the nuance of who you are goes offline.

In a way, you each experience a similar betrayal. You’re late coming home from work, and by the time you arrive, your spouse’s overactive threat detection network has them convinced you’re seeing someone else. You don’t really love them. You’ll probably leave. You’re abandoning them.

Of course that’s not what you’re doing. So, you’re likewise blindsided by their response. Your spouse is fuming because you betrayed them, and they have some choice words for you. Every positive aspect of your relationship is read back to you in a negative light. It feels like they hate you.

There is no law that says you must stay in a relationship with this dynamic. However, if you’re committed to it, then equanimity is paramount. Your key to coping is not to internalize either idealization or devaluation. Even though it feels so personal, it’s not about you. It’s about their pain and fear.

The 5 Greatest Bands of the British Invasion, Ranked

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Throughout the 1960s, there was a huge influx of British rock and pop music into American culture. English bands were dominating the charts and the music scene was becoming more globalized than ever before. The overseas relationship was reciprocal – these British bands were reworking American blues and R&B within a rock format, infusing psychedelic and progressive rock. Concurrently, American rock music began to emulate the British sound. This cultural phenomenon changed the face of music forever.

The British Invasion is an interesting and necessary study in the way music reinvents itself – how every musician is influenced by those who came before. This time period introduced this wonderful merging of sound to an international audience, causing a dizzyingly steep learning curve that produced some of the greatest music of all time. Here are the top five most important bands of the British Invasion.

The Animals formed in 1962 in Newcastle upon Tyne. They drew inspiration from American rhythm and blues artists, getting their start with covers by iconic musicians such as Jimmy Reed and Nina Simone. They had a big hand in the genesis of folk rock with their signature song: a cover of the American folk classic “House of the Rising Sun.” Their version is raw and emotional, capturing the struggles of working-class Americans during this time. The band represented a darker and grittier side of the British Invasion canon, the opposite end of the spectrum from the British pop that was also making its way overseas.

In the latter half of the sixties, they changed their lineup and their name to Eric Burden and The Animals. During this time, they delved into the psychedelic rock space, departing from their earlier blues influences. They laid the groundwork for prog rock, influenced punk/ garage music, and inspired artists across many genres (including Bruce Springsteen, The Grateful Dead, and Rod Stewart, to name a few.)

The Kinks also formed in 1962, and their rise in popularity aligned with that of the British Invasion. They released their breakout hit “You Really Got Me” in 1964, quickly achieving international success. They were heavy hitters on the British Invasion circuit – that is, until 1966, when they were banned from touring in America. While the reason has never been explicitly made public, it is believed to be a combination of promoter disputes, chaotic behavior from the band, and visa and permit issues. Nevertheless, they continued to grow in popularity in the UK and America alike.

The Kinks’ satirical single “Sunday Afternoon” was inspired by British music hall – a form of working-class variety theater rooted in Victorian-era taverns and pubs. The track was the biggest hit in the UK during the summer of 1966. The Kinks are often regarded as the original punk band, with much of their music featuring fuzzy guitar distortion and a driving, bluesy energy. They influenced countless bands across rock subgenres, including psychedelic bands like The Doors and Jefferson Airplane, punk rock groups such as the Ramones, and Oasis and other Britpop bands.

The Who popularized many techniques and terms that would become touchstones in hard rock. Their incredible mastery of their instruments allowed them to get super creative with their recording and production. They experimented heavily with synthesizers and feedback, adding layers of texture that were unprecedented at the time. Guitarist and songwriter (and legend!) Pete Townshend had a massive impact on rock music unto himself. He popularized the use of power chords and also coined the term “power pop” in reference to the group’s sound. Townshend wrote the band’s most popular album, Tommy, which was the first time the term “rock opera” was used publicly.

Drummer Keith Moon also had a big hand in the band’s popularity and original sound. His playing was chaotic and unruly – he pushed the rhythm section out of the pocket and onto center stage. Moon was the backbone of The Who’s explosive, energetic live performances. They were the loudest band of the era, and maybe of all time – a quality that was understandably polarizing.

And now we’ve reached the greatest rock band of all time. The Rolling Stones (who are remarkably still together and actively performing) are pioneers of hard rock music. They began their career with blues covers (a common theme on this list) and were greatly influenced by American R&B artists such as Chuck Berry, Muddy Waters, and Fats Domino. The band was drawn to the sheer power and emotional expression these musicians brought to their work.

Eventually, the Stones moved on to writing and recording their own original music – tracks that would, conversely, influence the next generation of American blues and rock artists, a prime example of the circuitous and reciprocal nature of music. These songs were primarily written by Mick Jagger and Keith Richards, a collaboration that sometimes led to tension in the group. Not that it affected them much – they have countless international hits. The Stones’ expansive discography bridged the gap between rhythm and blues and hard classic rock, and also between America and the UK. Their swagger and flamboyant rebellion made them popular in youth culture on both sides of the Atlantic.

Bet you didn’t see this one coming – Beatlemania, baby! Formed in 1960, The Beatles are the most influential band in popular music. They started out with a clean-cut, boy-next-door look and sound – all pop, no grit. With time, they evolved into something much more. They began to travel and broaden their horizons, getting to know themselves and the world on a deeper level. Their lyrics grew deeper and more poetic, and their influences expanded vastly: Indian and Western classical music, pop, psychedelia, and folk were all incorporated. Their evolution as a band is something to be studied – and it has been, quite thoroughly and quite often.

The British Invasion would never have happened without The Beatles – they were the foot in the door to the American popular music market. They were the voice of youth culture and the counterculture, a living and breathing documentation of the Western world from 1960 to 1970. Their constant reinvention and unprecedented levels of success have never been matched or recreated, and likely never will.

Suppose SpaceX acquires Tesla at a 20% premium in an all-stock deal: SpaceX would have to issue a substantial number of new shares, thereby significantly diluting existing shareholders. Tesla's stock might rise, at least initially, while SpaceX's could decline unless investors believe the merger will generate enough synergies to justify the acquisition premium.

There are indeed some reasons to think that the combined entity could create more value than either would on its own. For instance, both Tesla and SpaceX are investing massive sums in artificial intelligence (AI). As a single entity, they could eliminate redundant investments and lower costs, while leveraging their larger size to negotiate better deals with suppliers.

Also, some of Tesla's work, including its energy infrastructure and the Optimus humanoid robot, could be of immense value to SpaceX. So, that could be good news for the business' long-term prospects, although whether the proposed combined entity will, on balance, create more value is by no means guaranteed. However, one major source of uncertainty will likely come from regulators. The deal will probably attract significant attention from lawmakers, at least some of whom may try to stop it.

Whether they succeed or not, it's hard to predict the impact on Tesla and SpaceX. And even as a combined entity, the company will be significantly exposed to regulatory risk across businesses such as space launch, satellite-based connectivity, EVs, and robotaxis.

Putting aside the acquisition talks, Tesla's own business has somewhat attractive prospects. The company's work in the robotaxi industry looks promising. Tesla launched the Cybercab, a self-driving vehicle with no steering wheel, no pedals, and no mirrors, earlier this year. Provided it can scale its Cybercab fleet and capture a decent share of the market over the next five to 10 years, the robotaxi business could meaningfully contribute to the company's revenue and profits.

And Tesla does have an advantage over other robotaxi leaders. The company has a growing number of subscriptions to its Full Self-Driving software, which it offers to EV owners for $99 per month. A growing FSD ecosystem grants the company access to more data to train and improve its software. And as Tesla has pointed out, the FSD software is becoming one of the reasons people buy Teslas. That's great news for the company's future.

But one of Tesla's problems is valuation. At 157.3x forward earnings, the market has already priced in significant success from the company's robotaxi ambition. So, I'd be skeptical about buying Tesla's shares at current levels. I'd prefer a much lower price. The potential merger with SpaceX changes very little, in my view, and actually adds significantly more uncertainty to the mix.

The combined entity (if a merger does happen) will certainly be worth serious consideration, but investors with an average tolerance for risk and volatility shouldn't buy Tesla stock right now, whether or not they expect the company to merge with SpaceX.

All over the world, teachers play a crucial role in their pupils' learning experience. This is especially true for left-handed children, who, for a long time, have been forced to write with their right hand against their will in many countries. Thankfully, the awful practice of handedness relearning is now a thing of the past in the US and many other countries, and today, both left-handed and right-handed pupils are encouraged to write with their naturally dominant hand in school. This makes it important that teachers have some basic knowledge about handedness and also the ergonomic needs of left-handed kids in learning environments. As handedness typically is not a main focus in teacher education, it is, however, largely unknown what knowledge the average teacher has about handedness and the specific learning needs of left-handed children. Thus, more psychological research on this important topic is needed.

A new study entitled “Handedness literacy in teachers: Data from Greece, Serbia, and Belgium”, just published in the scientific journal Laterality, aimed at closing this gap in the psychological literature by focusing on teachers’ handedness literacy (Papadatou-Pastou and co-workers, 2026). Handedness literacy refers to a person’s general knowledge and understanding of handedness.

The study team, led by renowned handedness researcher Marietta Papadatou-Pastou, asked more than 800 prospective and in-service teachers to fill out a questionnaire with various questions about their knowledge of handedness and about themselves. The teachers came from three different European countries: Greece, Serbia, and Belgium.

The questionnaire the teachers had to fill out consisted of four main parts. The first part consisted of open-ended questions about left-handedness, such as “Do left-handed students differ from right-handed students?”. The teachers had to write answers to these questions. The second part of the questionnaire consisted of 18 questions quizzing the teachers about their knowledge of handedness. The third part of the questionnaire contained further questions about handedness, and the fourth part contained some questions about the teachers themselves, such as their own handedness and their age, among others.

The analysis of the data yielded several interesting results about teachers’ handedness literacy.

The analyses of the open-ended questions about handedness in the first part of the questionnaire showed that teachers generally had a supportive attitude towards left-handed pupils. The teachers wrote about different aspects of handedness, such as the underlying neurobiology or specific challenges left-handed pupils face, or their ergonomic and classroom needs. In general, they were in favor of letting children choose which hand they like to write with.

The analysis of the handedness literacy scores revealed that teachers knew some things about handedness, but also had quite a lot of knowledge gaps. As there were 18 questions and the scientists gave 1 point per correct question, the maximum score for handedness literacy was 18. Teachers from Serbia had an average score of 8.11 (45% correct answers). Teachers from Belgium were slightly better as they had an average score of 8.85 (49% correct answers), as were teachers from Greece, who had an average score of 8.90 (49% correct answers).

So, in all three countries, teachers had less than 50% correct answers- if there were a left-handedness test, they would probably fail! One thing that many teachers got wrong was that they thought that left-handed pupils were more intelligent than right-handed pupils. Also, many teachers thought that there were more left-handers in the population than the actual percentage (10.6%). Interestingly, left-handed teachers did not show higher handedness literacy than right-handed teachers.

Many of the teachers who participated in the study stated that they thought that handedness literacy in teachers needs to be improved. The results of the study show that this is actually true. This suggests that teacher education should include more scientific information on left-handedness, so that teachers can optimally educate left-handed kids.

Flying takes longer than it used to – and, retirement of the Concorde notwithstanding, it’s not because planes aren’t as fast. There are more than twice as many commercial flights as there were at the time of airline deregulation, and air traffic control hasn’t been upgraded very much. There’s congested airspace and also congested airports. We also don’t build major new airports – the last one was Denver, 30 years ago. (Austin’s new 1999 airport was a converted military base, and there were others like that in the 90s.) By one estimate flights have gotten 8% longer. That means a two hour flight is 10 minutes longer than it used to be. And the single biggest factor is often tagged as strategic padding of schedules. Airlines are often chided for ‘schedule padding’ – claiming that flights take longer than they should. Often, this just reflects the reality of telling customers when they can expect to arrive – although there’s greater variance than there used to be, which isn’t great for having a gate available when you land early. Airlines do not like this! Though real airfares (even including fees) are down significantly since deregulation, longer flight times are expensive for staffing, fuel and efficient use of $50 – $100 million planes. And longer travel times historically disadvantaged an airline trying to sell tickets, because it meant their flights were displayed lower on the screen that travel agents and later passengers directly saw – which translates into fewer sales. A new study finds that airlines with more “padding” in their schedules tend to charge higher fares. That sounds like passengers paying more for a slower trip. But those extra minutes can also let United hold your plane with ConnectionSaver and get you home that night, and still arrive on time. It also means fewer delays and missed connections. So that ‘premium’ is actually a more valuable product to many consumers. I’ve argued for years that schedule padding often isn’t really padding. Sometimes an airline is finally admitting how long the trip takes. Customers can plan around an honest arrival time. There’s a new chapter by Embry-Riddle’s Jules Yimga, “The Price of Punctuality,” in The Economics of Airline Pricing. It combines 2019 flight time data with the government’s ticket price survey. It compares schedules against different percentiles of actual flight times, then examines how the resulting measures relate to fares. More padding is associated with higher fares, especially when the comparison uses the faster end of actual flight times. The relationship appears in both competitive and concentrated markets. The abstract itself allows that reliability could be a service customers value. (Interestingly, Yimga’s earlier research on schedule padding attributed higher prices primarily to higher costs rather than higher margins.) Even scheduling to the median arrival time means roughly half of trips take longer than published. It’s often better for a customer if an airline underpromises and overdelivers – or arrives ‘on time’ rather than late. Predictability matters when you’re scheduling connections or trying to arrive in time for a meeting. An airline that leaves little buffer is going to appear less reliable, so customers book earlier flights and longer layovers or even fly in the night before an important meeting. Then the buffer hasn’t disappeared, it just changes who’s scheduling it. That’s why I argued American’s investment in more realistic schedules could pay for itself. Avoiding rebooking, mishandled bags and aircraft and crews getting out of position can justify spending running an on-time schedule even before a customer chooses the airline again because the trip met expectations. American’s Dallas – Fort Worth overhaul that began in April combined longer scheduled flight times with a move from nine daily banks of flights to thirteen. Spreading flights into smaller waves reduced congestion that made some of the extra time unnecessary. One plane waiting for a gate can delay its passengers, its next departure, and other flights whose crews are stuck on board. Giving the operation room to recover can prevent that problem from cascading. In January, I covered American network planning chief Brian Znotins explaining that bookings for 40–54 minute connections at DFW were 4% below what its model predicted. Bookings for 55–84 minute connections were 8% above prediction. Customers have actually started choosing longer connections because they feel more reliable and less likely to leave them stranded. When I covered United’s media day in 2019, Greg Hart explained that 25% of its flights each day were expected to arrive ten minutes early. That gave United room to use ConnectionSaver to hold flights for connecting passengers and still arrive on time. Of course there’s such a thing as too much slack. An airline can improve its reported punctuality by moving the promised arrival later without getting anyone anywhere sooner. Excessively long connections waste customer time. And routinely beating an absurdly generous schedule doesn’t make an operation excellent. Not all padding is something customers will pay a premium for. Wizards Is Only Targeting Counterfeit Cards, Not Regular MTG Proxies

There's a fine line between a proxy and a counterfeit card, and Wizards is only interested in eradicating one of them from the game of Magic.

A wave of controversy hit Magic: The Gathering this week after Wizards of the Coast revealed it is taking legal action against a group of proxy card businesses, collectively referred to as 'PrintMTG Enterprise,' that produce and sell unofficial MTG cards directly to players on several marketplaces. The lawsuit is worth $28.5 million, and it includes strict conditions if Wizards succeeds, such as handing over all card-printing equipment used in the business.

The community was immediately up in arms over the announcement, as it felt like Wizards was taking a huge step forward in a war against proxy cards, but the reality is that your average proxy card is safe, and this is actually more about counterfeit cards. Is there a difference? Yes, and it could be a costly one for 'PrintMTG Enterprises.'

The difference between a counterfeit Magic card and a proxy is that a proxy card is clearly not a real Magic card, typically because it has a plain back or doesn't use official Magic artwork on the front. In most cases, proxy cards have a plain white back, so they can't be confused with an official Magic card.

Counterfeit cards are carbon copies of the original Magic design and are intended to deceive players who aren't all that familiar with the minor details that make a Magic card legit. This can also include using the same paper stock as official Magic cards to make them feel authentic in hand, as well as any details meant to fool the eye.

It's entirely fair for Wizards of the Coast to target any seller looking to reproduce Magic cards and sell them in a condition that could be used to defraud others, and this lawsuit shouldn't be confused with Wizards taking a stance against proxy makers or users.

One of the big debates that arose from this lawsuit is why now? It's fair to say that counterfeit Magic cards have been around for a long time, so why is now the time for Wizards to take a stance and look to get them off the market?

The reality is that we'll never know why, but it's a positive move for the secondary market regardless. Proxy cards, when advertised and produced correctly, aren't mistaken for regular Magic cards, and those who buy them are 100% okay with that.

Counterfeit cards become a problem when people look on typically trusted marketplaces for an official card but end up buying a counterfeit version without knowing it, because the seller is trying to make a quick buck.

This isn't to say that the companies producing these counterfeit cards do so with the intent of deceiving players in online transactions, but that's what is happening, and it needs to stop.

I've only ever purchased one counterfeit card in my time playing Magic, and it was a copy of Tibalt's Trickery. It wasn't a very expensive card, but the copy I received was clearly fake, not to the eye, but to the touch. I reported the seller, who had ghosted me after the transaction, and I got my money back, but I wonder how many players that seller managed to fool.

On the proxy front, I only own one set of proxy cards: the Goblin Storm Secret Lair Commander deck. Due to this deck selling out incredibly fast and being scalped for insane prices online, I decided to proxy it instead, but did so from a seller who clearly stated that the backs of the cards would arrive plain white, and that the paper stock used was quite thin (as a warning for when I sleeved the deck). This was perfect, because I planned to use the deck still at my local store, and with my Commander pod, I wanted to be able to say "hey, this is a proxy, does anyone have a problem with that?" as the code of honor among most groups is that, as long as you admit to what it is, there's typically no issue playing with it.

Magic is a very expensive game to get into in most formats, so proxies let you avoid extortionate secondary market prices (especially on land for Commander right now) and still play with limited-availability stuff like Goblin Storm.

Proxies are great, and they should continue to be allowed as long as certain rules are followed. I have no issue with Wizards setting a clear boundary for what a counterfeit card is and why it isn't good for the game.

Andoni Iraola has done a pretty decent job since being appointed as Liverpool's manager.

He's managed to get his team to fourth in the Premier League table with only seven points separating them from first-placed Arsenal.

Obviously, this is still a decent number of points to chip away at but, if the Gunners continue dropping points by drawing games against teams such as Newcastle, the Reds could find themselves back in the title race soon.

Nobody knows what lies ahead for any team but, if Liverpool manage to pull off even half of the transfers they've been linked with, they'll be storming their way up the table.

And to make things even sweeter, the perfect signing for Iraola has just emerged.

Arhcie Brown has a £19.2 million release clause in his contract

So many areas among Liverpool's ranks still need upgrading.

This is a horrible thing to write as, across the last couple of years, well over £600 million has been spent on new players by the Reds.

Some of the transfers that they've made have worked out for the better but, others haven't quite hit the mark.

Milos Kerkez is an example of this because, even though he has had a fair few decent performances, his inconsistency is terrible.

Some days he can be seen as the Premier League's best left-back and, on others, he could end up being the worst player on the pitch.

This has forced fans to ask for a new left-back with Florian Plettenberg now sharing a rather intriguing update on Liverpool target, Archie Brown.

"Archie Brown has a release clause worth around €23m, valid for both the winter and summer transfer windows. The 23 y/o is a regular starter at Fenerbahçe and has also impressed with some very strong performances in the Champions League," he wrote.

"Under contract until 2028, a move is planned, with Premier League clubs already making enquiries."

Archie Brown would fit straight into Andoni Iraola's plans

Not only would Brown be classed as homegrown but, he'd also be one of the cheapest transfers on the market.

Recent links with Liverpool and Alejandro Balde have emerged with Barcelona demanding close to €50 million for the Spaniard.

Others such as Daniel Svensson have also been linked with a move but, again, Borussia Dortmund want over €40 million for him.

Each of these players would be exceptional additions but, Brown for €23 million (£19 million) is a deal that's too good to pass up.

He put in the performance of a lifetime when Liverpool faced Fenerbahce earlier in the season and, if he can replicate that form in the Premier League, the Reds would be onto a winner.

Liverpool signing Brown would also give Kerkez a genuine challenger for his position which might even help him develop his performance.

Since the price for Brown is so good, Liverpool will have to act fast and get straight on with securing his services when the January transfer window opens up, otherwise, they'll end up kicking themselves for missing out on it.

With the release of the prequel The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping, franchise producer Nina Jacobson regrets splitting the Mockingjay films into two parts.

At the time, The Twilight Saga and Harry Potter split their grand finale into two films, and Lionsgate followed suit, breaking up the last Suzanne Collins book of the OG Hunger Games trilogy into two films as well.

“Even though I love the Mockingjay films, if I had it to do over again, I think both Francis [Lawrence, director] and I might try to do it all in one,” she said in an interview with Empire.

She continued, “Mockingjay was such a big book, and it takes you to so many different places. But in the end, I think that Francis and I both felt that splitting them up… even if it did come from an inside-out place of really feeling like we needed two films to tell the story, I think audiences find it cynical. Even if its intentions are earnest, which they were. They feel like it’s a play to make them see two films instead of one.” Watch on Deadline

With Collins’ two prequels like The Hunger Games: The Ballad of Songbirds and Snakes and Sunrise on the Reaping, producers have changed the way they adapt the books.

“We really put the burden on ourselves [now], even when it’s a big book, to transform it into a theatrical experience, to not take advantage of your audience’s patience, to make it as tight as we can,” Jacobson told the publication. “[Sunrise On The Reaping] is the longest by a minute or two of all of them.”

The Top 10 Open World Games

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There was a time when video games were largely linear affairs. You either moved up and down or from left to right in enclosed spaces. As technology evolved, so did the scope of digital worlds. Now players are regularly treated to expansive environments filled with quests, interesting characters, and horizons that stretch further with every console generation. These experiences consume dozens if not hundreds of hours and knowing which titles are worth the time investment has become crucial. To that end, we’ve assembled a list of 10 great modern open-world games available today to help make what can be a paralyzing decision a little easier.

Note that these are listed in release date order and don’t necessarily represent the best games of all time. Rather, this will be an ever-changing list of quality titles to arrive in recent years that you should go out of your way to get lost in.

Grand Theft Auto is arguably the blueprint for open worlds in video games, and its sixth entry is the series' peak. In a universe steeped in satire and cynicism, protagonists Jason and Lucia are instantly likable figures worth rooting for. Their love story is absolutely worth your time, but even if you made the decision to ignore it, you'd be overwhelmed by Leonida's exceedingly vast playground, ripe for exploration. When we say GTAVI's scope is staggering, we are partially talking about the literal size of the map, but we're mainly talking about its density; every corner is meticulously crafted with people to meet and places to rob. It's one of the most believable spaces in gaming history, bolstered by incredible graphics and populated with characters only a little more zany than you'd encounter in its real-life equivalent of Florida. Its dozens of cars feel great to drive, and its dozens of guns are a thrill to shoot, but if you decide to get really into birdwatching instead, that's fine by us. Grand Theft Auto VI is one of the best games of this generation – it's no surprise it made its way onto this top 10 list. | Our review

In an overly simplistic way, Ghost of Yōtei basically took everything that worked about Sucker Punch’s 2020 open world action game, Ghost of Tsushima, and improved it. Exploration, combat, visuals, and narrative are all major steps up from the previous game, which was already fantastic. Atsu’s journey of revenge, fighting and exploring her way through Ezo, is an always-compelling experience, not because it radically re-addresses or changes the mechanics that made Tsushima so great, but rather because they are carefully iterated on and improved in sometimes subtle ways. And as an open world game, it constantly excels at giving the player worthwhile distractions that all appear to the player naturally. You’re not bouncing between points on an overworld map – you’re literally following the wind where it takes you and always ending up somewhere amazing doing something incredible. | Our review

Kingdom Come: Deliverance II is one of the highest-rated open worlds on this list, and for good reason. If Grand Theft Auto VI is the best modern-day open world, and Cyberpunk 2077 the best future-set open-world game, then KCDII is easily the best medieval open-world game in some time. Eschewing the more fantastical likes of the medieval open-world format – which The Witcher 3: Wild Hunt excellently covers on this list – KCDII does all it can to remain grounded and true to the 15th Century Kingdom of Bohemia land it’s set in. You follow Henry, whom you’ll craft through your decisions and upgrade choices, on a journey of retribution, rebellion, and reflection, and you’ll meet an unforgettable cast of characters along the way. They are the grounding elements to an otherwise wide-open adventure that lets you craft a narrative only you can craft. However you invest your time in this massive, sprawling medieval landscape will be reflected in the Henry you play as and how the world responds around you – there’s no shortage of mischief and chivalry to get up to, and across its dozens of hours, you’ll be taken through one of the most rewarding open games you can play today.  | Our Review

FromSoftware proves its punishing yet satisfying style of action RPG absolutely works in an open-world format. Brimming with mystery and danger, Elden Ring allows players to make their own way, providing little guidance or hand-holding, making every discovery feel like a genuinely exciting and well-earned reward. Unlike traditional Souls games, slamming against a difficult boss isn’t the end of the line. You can simply explore elsewhere and fall down the rabbit holes of breaching an impregnable fortress, surviving a treacherous swamp, or taking down dozens upon dozens of other imposing enemies. Quality-of-life additions such as free fast travel and friendlier checkpointing before boss fights alleviate some stress, further encouraging you to hop aboard your spectral mount and uncover every secret of this incredible world. | Our Review

The greatest trick the Forza Horizon series pulls is fooling players into liking racing games by disguising them as open-world RPGs. The latest entry, Horizon 5, serves as the pinnacle of that Trojan Horse-style design. Set in a stunningly beautiful rendition of Mexico, players sit behind hundreds of vehicles to compete in a seemingly endless number of wild races. Whether you're speeding against other cars or a 747 airplane, the sheer variety of events and an over-the-top tone make Horizon more lighthearted compared to most pure racing sims. In addition to traditional races, you can enjoy Horizon 5's entertaining take on battle royale, a zombie-infection mode, and absurd story-driven scenarios (like driving a parade float). A staggering collection of unlockable cars, purchasable homes to store said vehicles, plus cosmetics and goofy dances for your avatar create powerful incentives to score the winner's purse. Best-in-class driving mechanics solidify Horizon 5's first-place standing | Our Review

Though Cyberpunk 2077’s launch was rough (and that’s putting it lightly), developer CD Projekt Red successfully righted the ship and then some. By fixing launch issues and woes, continuing to add meaningful (and free) content like new quests, Cyberpunk Edgerunners items, and more, the game literally got better each year. The team closed out the game with the incredible Phantom Liberty expansion, which adds a heaping dose of Idris Elba and spy-thriller action to Night City, ensuring any V who steps into this dystopian future has hours upon hours of open-world fun to enjoy. Speaking of Night City, it’s one of the most fully realized metropolitan playgrounds on this list. Police react to your actions in real time, as do citizens; futuristic cars and motorbikes await you to live out your Akira slide dreams; and countless weapons and upgrades will help you become the greatest cybernetic warrior gangster this side of the Pacifica. Between side content, the golden path, and the unlisted-but-worth-engaging-in hidden activities spread throughout Night City, you’d be hard-pressed to find a better dystopian open-world shooter than Cyberpunk 2077. | Our Review

Few studios craft worlds that feel as lifelike as Rockstar Games, and Red Dead Redemption 2 might be their crowning achievement. Every inch of this wild west drips with immaculate detail and feels like you’ve stepped into a time portal to the 1800s.
