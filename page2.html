The Midterms Are Over. History Says This Is What the Stock Market Does Next.

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Washington is divided again, and history has some encouraging data for investors.

Democrats won control of both the House and the Senate in the Nov. 3 midterm elections. That means President Trump will spend his final two years in office working with a Congress controlled by the other party. If you're wondering what this means for your portfolio, history offers some useful perspective.

Here's a statistic that sounds extremely reassuring. The S&P 500 has been higher 12 months after each of the 19 midterm elections since 1950, with an average gain of about 15%.

Even the weakest stretch, which included the 1987 crash, ended with a small gain. But the stock market has never been lower a year after a midterm election.

Five presidents since 1950 have served two full terms, and their final two years tell a mixed story. Here's how the S&P 500 did, including dividends:

One key takeaway is that the year after the midterm was positive all five times. The final year was more mixed, with two big declines. But it's worth noting why. The 2000 drop came as the dot-com bubble burst, and the 2008 drop was during the financial crisis. Neither had much to do with who controlled Congress.

Three of those presidents, Eisenhower, Reagan, and Bush, faced a Congress controlled by the other party in their final two years, just as Trump will. The results ranged from excellent to the 2008 crash. Over a longer period, RBC data shows that years with a Republican president and a Democratic Congress have averaged about a 5% annual gain, the lowest of any combination. Of course, that average is based on just a handful of periods, so I wouldn't read too much into it.

In short, history suggests that the year after a midterm tends to be good for stocks, and that a president's final stretch depends far more on the economy than on politics. Earnings, interest rates, and economic growth will drive the market over the next two years. My plan is the same as always. I'll stay invested and keep buying great businesses, no matter who controls Washington.

Once Upon a Farm is cutting its outlook after a toddler snack bar recall

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Free daily briefing on global business news.

The toddler snack maker lowered its full-year net sales forecast to $290 million-$300 million from $327 million-$335 million

Once Upon a Farm lowered its full-year 2026 outlook on Thursday to reflect the voluntary recall of its Tractor Wheels product lines. The company expects net sales of $290 million to $300 million, down from the $327 million to $335 million it had forecast.

The company expects an adjusted EBITDA loss of $4.5 million to $7 million, compared with prior guidance of adjusted EBITDA of $3 million to $4.5 million. Adjusted EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, with additional adjustments. The company said the recall's effect on the fourth quarter drove the update.

For the third quarter, net sales rose 34.9% to $89.1 million from $66.0 million a year earlier. Net loss narrowed to $3.8 million from $11.3 million. Adjusted EBITDA was a loss of $1.0 million, compared with a profit of $0.9 million a year earlier.

Gross margin fell to 36.1% from 42.1%. The company cited trade investment for a national club program, product mix and cooler placement costs. As of Sept. 30, it held $106.4 million in cash and cash equivalents and no debt.

"We are committed to resolving the Tractor Wheels situation correctly, continuing to earn the trust of families and retailers, and executing against the significant growth opportunity ahead of us," CEO and co-founder John Foraker said in a statement.

Foraker told Bloomberg the company anticipates as much as $30 million in recall expenses, spanning written-off inventory, legal fees and product returns. For 2027, Foraker expects the company to reach breakeven or better on an adjusted EBITDA basis, with net sales no lower than $330 million, according to Bloomberg.

The company plans to change the bars' recipe and packaging before bringing them back to store shelves, Foraker told Bloomberg. It has brought in outside specialists in food safety to review the product, according to Bloomberg.

Once Upon a Farm announced the voluntary recall of its Tractor Wheels soft-baked bars on Oct. 30, citing three consumer complaints about a choking hazard. The bars are labeled for toddlers ages 1.5 to 2.5, and the recall covers all lots across three varieties.

Tractor Wheels accounted for about 18% of the company's revenue this year as of the earlier coverage. Once Upon a Farm stock has lost over 40% of its value since it began trading in February.

What we know about the global Microsoft outage

Watch: What we know about the Microsoft global outage overnight

A major global Microsoft outage has caused problems, or in some cases disabled entire services, including emails and artificial intelligence products.

Microsoft has said the outage was not caused by a cyber-security incident, but efforts to fix the issues have so far been largely unsuccessful.

Here's what we know about the services affected and what went wrong.

Schools, GPs and businesses hit by global Microsoft outage as company scrambles to solve issue

Microsoft 365 Services, including Teams, Outlook and Microsoft's artificial intelligence software Copilot, have been affected by "connectivity problems".

Customers are reporting varying levels of access - many have not been able to send or receive emails, view certain files or details on their calendar apps.

Some users have been able to use Teams and are using messaging and online meeting functions without issues.

More than 430 million people use Microsoft 365 apps according to Microsoft's website.

The full scale of the outage has not been confirmed, but monitoring website Downdetector had 140,000 people from 74 countries report issues with Outlook on Thursday evening.

The real figure affected is likely much higher because Downdetector relies primarily on users self-reporting.

Microsoft 365 is also used by companies worldwide and is expected to have a knock-on effect to other services.

Tech giants OpenAI and Anthropic have both reported problems as a result of the outage.

ChatGPT-maker OpenAI said it was investigating an issue affecting its connections to Microsoft's services, which has caused some tools to not work as expected.

From city governments in the US to Australian spy agencies, disruption across the globe has been widespread.

Courts from Los Angeles to Texas warned that communications might be delayed.

In Canada, the public broadcaster CBC reported that a healthcare network running several hospitals in Toronto was experiencing email issues, but that patient care was not affected.

The government of Japan also said its shared IT platform had been disrupted.

Across the UK, GP surgeries, pharmacies, police and schools have been affected by the outage.

Some GP surgeries have urged patients not to submit messages via their website while the problem remains, while several large police forces are urging members of the public to call if they want to make a report.

Microsoft has ruled out a cyber-attack and attributed the ongoing outage to "elevated load" on its infrastructure.

The term refers to when servers are handling a much higher volume of stress than they usually do.

In the case of the Microsoft outage, a large number of servers became "unexpectedly unavailable", the company said, which sent added traffic to remaining servers, straining these.

On Friday afternoon, Microsoft said it had seen "encouraging recovery progress" in Europe and improvement in North America.

The company added it is focused on "addressing the remaining areas of disruption" and warned "some users may still experience intermittent issues as recovery actions continue".

In an earlier statement, the tech giant had said that its attempted fixes were "not taking as expected".

Australian cyber-security expert Troy Hunt told the BBC the outage could have far-reaching consequences.

"Microsoft is a massive part of the internet backbone and reminds us how critical email still is.

"Not only does this outage affect many people's ability to work but also things like the ability to communicate about appointments, purchase things that need verification, create or access accounts and services," he said.

Gergely Orosz, a former leading software engineer at several major tech companies including Skype and Uber, said this could be the "most disruptive" business email outage "of all time".

He said it was still difficult to know just how many people have been affected by the disruption, but added he "cannot recall" an outage over business email ever taking this long to return to normal.

Paul Spain from New Zealand-based advisory firm Gorilla Technology said that with e-mails and services offline, companies have practically "lost a business day", which is "frustrating" users".

Breakfast News: What NVDA’s CEO Exit Means for You

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Huang built a company with the products, people, and customers to outlast him, and we expect Nvidia to come through this.

Jensen Huang resigned as Nvidia's (NVDA -4.34%) chief executive amid controversies over his personal conduct and questions about whether Nvidia's chips reached China through a customer in Singapore.

His exit followed a week of reports about his personal life. The first concerned an affair with an executive at Megaspeed, one of Nvidia's customers in Singapore. U.S. officials suspected the executive of spying for China, which China denied. On Wednesday, a second relationship, with one of Huang's direct reports, came to light. The board met that night, and the company announced Huang's resignation the following morning.

We think investors are right to take Huang's departure seriously. In our view, though, the sell-off prices in more damage than the business has shown so far.

If you've followed Nvidia for any length of time, you know how central Huang has been. He co-founded the company in 1993 and led it for all 33 years. His leadership structure was also known for being unusually flat, as he had 33 direct reports at last count, according to CNBC.

Huang pushed Nvidia as an innovator and technologist. He launched CUDA (software that lets developers program Nvidia's chips) in 2006 and boldly committed the company to artificial intelligence (AI) computing long before it became a mainstream market. He also managed Nvidia's largest customer relationships himself and represented the company in Washington on export rules for China.

That is a lot for one person to hand off, let alone overnight, and Nvidia has no named successor to take it on. For now, CFO Colette Kress, who has worked alongside Huang since 2013 and has joined him on every earnings call, will serve as interim CEO while the board searches for a permanent replacement.

We understand why the market reacted so sharply. A company that leaned this heavily on one person's judgment now has to make its next big decisions without him, and under federal scrutiny. Analyst Dan Ives estimates Nvidia is worth 25% to 30% less without Huang, and some discount is warranted. With shares dropping to $148.80 in Thursday trading and closing at around $166, the market has already applied much of it.

What reassures us is that Huang's exit on its own is unlikely to change Nvidia's revenue much over the next two years. Demand for its chips is still growing quickly. Last quarter, revenue reached $96.2 billion, up 106% from a year earlier, and the company guided to $108 billion for the quarter that ended in October. By any measure, that's a business in good health.

Chips take years to design, so the products Nvidia will sell through 2027 were planned while Huang was still in charge, and customers have already ordered many of them. Even if regulators cut off sales routed through Singapore, we estimate Nvidia would lose no more than about 5% of its revenue.

A broader crackdown would cost more, and the investigations will help decide whether one comes.

Inside the $3.25 Million Pasadena Glass Pavilion That’s Still Studied in Architecture Classes Today

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A three-bedroom midcentury modern home designed by Case Study architect Craig Ellwood has just come on the market in Pasadena, CA, for $3.25 million. It features a notable cantilevered glass pavilion.

Built in 1965, the Kubly House was originally constructed for less than $45,000. The 2,227-square-foot home has since been thoughtfully updated.

“What makes this house special is its architecture and design, and how true it has remained to the original,” says Chris Suarez, who is co-listing the property with Kimberly Benz of Suarez + Benz at Compass. “Nothing was significantly altered. Everything done was intended to restore the house to its original condition. The footprint, the floors, the glass—everything has remained true to the original design.”

Located in the Linda Vista neighborhood, the home has an iconic redwood, cantilevered glass pavilion that opens to a single-level floor plan. The private half-acre lot includes a pool and spa surrounded by lush landscaping with eucalyptus and oak trees.

With exposed post-and-beam construction, clean horizontal lines, and expansive walls of glass, the home was designed to give the illusion of floating above its natural surroundings. The deck was recently restored using specially milled wood.

“It was sourced from northern Washington and brought down here,” Suarez notes. “Everything was done painstakingly to avoid altering the house’s integrity, architecture, and design. That being said, significant work has been done, including updates to the roof, infrastructure, air conditioning, and electrical systems. All the major systems have been updated, but you wouldn’t necessarily know it because the work was done so carefully.”

Originally commissioned by ArtCenter College of Design alumni and educators Don and Sally Kubly, the home represents their understanding of design and the culture of the college community. It is still studied in classes there.

“Don was ArtCenter’s first president in Pasadena,” Suarez explains. “There’s also a connection through Craig Ellwood, who designed ArtCenter’s main campus building. The main campus is an incredible steel-and-glass building designed by Ellwood. Don played a significant role in moving the school from Los Angeles to Pasadena, where he served as president for many years. He’s very well known within that community, and this house is significant to the college’s history and culture.”

Based on available architectural records, the Kubly House is said to be Ellwood’s only documented residential project in Pasadena.

After remaining in the Kubly family for nearly six decades, the home changed hands for $3.6 million in 2022. Since then, the sellers have undertaken a preservation-minded restoration with Carl Bronson of Bronstruction, updating major systems to contemporary standards.

“A house that helped usher in much midcentury modernism was the Glass House, designed by Philip Johnson around 1948 or 1949,” Benz notes. “That design was definitely something Ellwood loved. You can clearly see that house’s influence here, which is important because it reveals some of the influences behind Ellwood’s work.”

Benz adds that the home’s design was also influenced by Ellwood’s peers, including midcentury designer George Nelson.

“A furniture plan developed for the house clearly referenced Mies van der Rohe and George Nelson, both important figures in midcentury modern design,” she says. “Even the deck and the steps leading to the house resemble a George Nelson bench. There’s actually a bench built into the concrete wall near the pool that resembles a George Nelson design. I don’t know whether Nelson designed or made it, but the influence is certainly there.”

The house is special because many of the original details have remained intact, she adds.

“You can see the influence of the people Ellwood admired throughout the house,” Benz says. “That significance is what makes this house important when you look at the midcentury modernist movement. All these examples are visible in the house and remain there.”

Some of the built-in furniture has a distinctive style, too.

“There’s a stunning floating credenza that looks like a George Nelson piece,” she notes. “We think it might be one of his designs. It’s in the living room. There’s also a built-in console that might be a George Nelson design as well. So, some of the furniture is actually built into the architecture.”

While some pieces are originals and others are reproductions, Benz says, “the most important thing was following the original plan. The history of this house is everything.”

When staging the home, she says they were “fortunate to discover an original interior design study among the blueprints, plans, and documents that came with the house. We were able to look at that study, and it became a guide and a road map for staging the property. Sourcing the furniture was a challenge because we had to find the specific pieces.”

The city of Pasadena has also identified the property as eligible for individual listing on the National Register of Historic Places.

A hopeless thriller is one that keeps you grabbed by the neck but still ends in a painful manner. Someone in a thriller says, “We just need to find the truth,” and I immediately start worrying about them. Because finding the truth is one thing. Surviving what it does to your life is another. I've watched characters sacrifice everything to uncover a secret, only to discover that knowing the answer doesn't give them the slightest power to change it. It's infuriating. It's heartbreaking. And when a show earns that feeling, I cannot look away.

I think helplessness becomes truly frightening when you can understand every decision that leads somebody there. You wouldn't necessarily have behaved differently. You might have trusted the same person, followed the same evidence, or convinced yourself that one more sacrifice would finally put things right. These six thrillers are merciless about that very human tendency.

Sarah Linden (Mireille Enos) is preparing to leave the Seattle Police Department when the disappearance of teenager Rosie Larsen interrupts her plans. She works alongside detective Stephen Holder (Joel Kinnaman) and investigates a murder that tears through Rosie's family and becomes entangled with a political campaign. The Killing makes the investigation painfully personal, particularly for Rosie's parents, Stan (Brent Sexton) and Mitch (Michelle Forbes).

What gets under my skin is how little the truth can actually repair. Mitch can barely function inside the home where her daughter should still be living. Stan's grief keeps twisting into anger, leaving him vulnerable to decisions that could destroy what's left of his family. Meanwhile, Linden's inability to abandon the case damages her relationship with her own son. Everybody wants justice, but nobody can promise that justice will make living any easier. I find that distinction absolutely devastating.

Paul Spector (Jamie Dornan) has a wife, children, and a job as a grief counselor. He also stalks and murders women in Belfast. Detective Superintendent Stella Gibson (Gillian Anderson) arrives to investigate the killings, gradually closing in on a man who appears disturbingly ordinary outside his crimes. The Fall makes no secret of the murderer's identity. We know who he is, and the awful suspense comes from watching him move through a city full of people who don't.

Dornan's most disturbing moments aren't necessarily the violent ones. Sometimes Paul is simply behaving affectionately toward his children, and I want him as far away from them as possible. His domestic life offers camouflage without making his affection straightforwardly false, which is a sickening contradiction. Gibson is formidable, but intelligence and determination cannot undo what his victims have experienced. Even catching him means confronting the lasting damage he's inflicted. This is a thriller where the prospect of victory never feels remotely satisfying enough.

Mr. Robot begins with a thrilling fantasy of rebellion. Elliot Alderson (Rami Malek) works in cybersecurity while secretly hacking people and organizations he believes deserve exposing. When the mysterious Mr. Robot (Christian Slater) recruits him into a movement determined to destroy the financial power of E Corp, Elliot sees an opportunity to strike back at a system he despises. The movie gradually questions whether Elliot understands the consequences of his own actions.

I remember being almost embarrassingly excited about fsociety's ambitions. Then the economic damage starts spreading, ordinary people suffer, and the promised revolution becomes another nightmare. But Elliot's loneliness is even harder to endure. He can access almost anybody's private information while remaining terrified of genuine human intimacy. Sam Esmail, the show's creator, pushes that contradiction to its most painful extreme during Elliot's conversations with therapist Krista. The man determined to expose the world's hidden cruelty has spent years struggling to confront his own suffering. There is compassion here, thankfully, but very little comes without a terrible emotional cost.

Will Graham (Hugh Dancy) possesses a remarkable ability to understand how murderers think, making him invaluable to FBI investigator Jack Crawford (Laurence Fishburne). The second season's confrontation at Hannibal's home is among the cruelest emotional disasters I've watched on television. What destroys me is that neither man can simply dismiss their attachment afterward. Understanding one another has become another form of imprisonment.

Facing The Numbers by Kieran Satour and Tyson Perkins is one of the most unique propositions to come out of Australia in some time.

Described as a “social impact campaign and film project,” the feature-length documentary explores the significant disadvantages First Nations people experience in Australia’s legal system.

Recent data show that First Nations people experience disadvantages across all areas of Aussie life, including child protection, justice, health, housing and education. “The statistics tell one story. We tell another,” the film’s opening frame reads.

Structured as a six-part anthology, Facing The Numbers explores these disparities through the real-life stories of First Nations people using a bold mix of dance, poetry, and spoken word.

Satour and Perkins wrote and directed the film together. Perkins also served as the DoP. Satour produced alongside Ramona Telecican. The film debuted earlier this year at the Melbourne Film Festival before screening across the Aussie circuit and premiering at the Sydney Opera House. The doc will screen on NITV in Australia on November 15 and will later be repackaged for distribution on social platforms.

Below, Satour and Perkins speak with Deadline about how they developed the anthology film and their bold plans for its release.

DEADLINE: This film has a very unique structure. How did you develop this approach to the material?

TYSON PERKINS: This kind of subject matter is often portrayed in a very academic way on film. It tends to take the form of talking-head interviews and fairly basic re-creations, which don’t get close to the emotional point of view—or the emotional suffering—that many victims of police brutality go through. We started with that as our aim, and this little, unusual thing came out of it.

DEADLINE: I read this started as a short project and then expanded. Is that right?

PERKINS: It was as much about treating the subject matter in a fresh way as it was about using a fresh format. For us, that format was short-form and shareable online. It came as a response to the Black Lives Matter movement. We were watching what was happening in America from Australia and the artistic responses that came out of it—not the news coverage, but the things that appeared on our Instagram feeds. It prompted Kieran and me to think about Australia. Some of our statistics on incarceration, youth incarceration, male suicide, and deaths in custody are among the worst in the world. We wanted to take some of those subjects and statistics and frame them through the short-form, viral storytelling many African American artists in the U.S. were using. By “viral,” we didn’t necessarily mean that it had to go viral; we meant something short, shareable, and to the point.

As happens with films, once we delved into the individual subjects and the stories, they expanded. We realised there was a way to tell the story of this country—its institutional racism, its history of oppression and its ongoing oppression—through these personal, first-hand stories. We thought we had six separate subject films, but when we put them together, they formed a truthful picture of what Aboriginal people face in this country.

DEADLINE: You incorporate several different art forms in the film. There’s dance, spoken word. But it feels seamless. How did you get it to work?

KIERAN SATOUR: One of the amazing things about Facing the Numbers is that so many First Nations artists came together to tell this story. The spoken word, poetry, and dancing feel seamless because everyone is telling the same story. We’re telling multiple separate stories, but they represent a collective Aboriginal experience. Tyson and I were lucky to work with all these incredible creatives.

DEADLINE: How have people been responding to the film, especially in Australia?

SATOUR: People have been responding really well. We premiered at the Melbourne International Film Festival and had a great turnout. About 75% of tickets were sold for both screenings. We had standing ovations, and the Letterboxd reviews came in almost immediately after the first screening. Nothing was under four stars, and the reviews were glowing. We screened at the Darwin International Film Festival. My sister lives in Darwin. She’s also a co-founder of the production company and an executive producer on the film. She told me that people have been stopping her in Darwin to talk about Facing the Numbers because they’d been at the screening. It’s been amazing.

DEADLINE: Where did you find your participants?

SATOUR: During the global movement after George Floyd’s murder, people were sharing their stories online, and we found some of these stories through social media. Geoffrey Johnson is one example. Otherwise, we were doing research and development, trying to find stories that spoke to this collective experience. It came down to meetings and phone calls.

PERKINS: It’s important to mention that it would not be difficult to find thousands of stories like these. Thousands of Aboriginal people have direct descendants who were taken during the Stolen Generations or sent to schools like Kinchela Boys’ Home. Probably every Aboriginal family knows someone who has been a victim of police brutality, whether in their own family or a family they know. The stories we chose are told by extremely courageous people. It takes strength to repeat your story on camera, allow it to be filmed, and make it public. But one of the key messages of the film is that these experiences are extraordinarily common—in people’s lives and statistically.

DEADLINE: How did you build this production? I can’t imagine the Australian public funding system was jumping to invest in something like this. Or maybe I’m wrong.

SATOUR: It was funded through a digital-series, online-production fund at Screen Australia, our national public screen-funding body. That fund doesn’t even exist anymore. The project was never meant to be a feature film. We made the anthology into a feature partly because that helped us unlock extra funding and become eligible for a significant tax offset. Production went through a few phases. At the end of the first phase, we had two strong rough cuts, which we used to secure more funding. We received a little more from the national broadcasting funding body, then a broadcaster attached through a pre-sale, and we brought in philanthropic support. It was a struggle, but we got there. A film like this doesn’t get made without the goodwill of philanthropists, but also without all the work everyone put into those first two rough cuts. They were strong, and I think the project sold itself from there.

DEADLINE: How will it be distributed? I know you’re screening at the Sydney Opera House, which is wonderful. Do you have plans for international screenings? In the UK and U.S., people involved in solidarity movements would be intrigued to hear from you and see a film like this.

SATOUR: Totally. In terms of craft, we were inspired by global Black filmmakers. Steve McQueen’s Small Axe was a huge reference for us, as was Spike Lee. We both admire the American director Khalil Joseph. We’re connecting with global Black and brown movements because we’re facing similar issues everywhere: over-policing, incarceration, and child removal. We’re still waiting to find out where the international premiere will be. In Australia, we’re doing a film-festival run, and then the film will be broadcast on NITV, the National Indigenous Television Network, which sits under SBS, our multicultural public broadcaster. The digital release will follow once the holdbacks for the national TV broadcast are over. The films will also be released online through a microsite.

A key partner is Common Ground, an Aboriginal-run and Aboriginal-led organisation focused on education. They’re running our impact campaign. The organisation’s founder, Rona Glynn-McDonald, is Warwick Thornton’s daughter, so filmmaking is in her blood. We’re excited to get these stories out digitally and reach younger people who may not watch television or attend film festivals, and to connect with an audience that isn’t already converted.

PERKINS: For me, the online release is what I’m most excited about. In film and television now, you see people who have built audiences on YouTube or Instagram bringing those audiences into more traditional film and TV. The ideal outcome would be for some of the storytellers to experience justice—whether for a family member’s death or, in Uncle Widdy’s case, by having more of the public learn what happened to him at Kinchela Boys’ Home. Not many people know that thousands of children went through that school or what happened to them. The survivors are trying to reclaim the land where the school stood. Once the films have been shared and completed their festival runs, I hope some of those outcomes can begin to happen. I’m really excited about that.

Three central banks have completed tests of a cross-border foreign exchange settlement system connecting traditional payment infrastructure with distributed ledger technology.

In a joint statement on Friday, the Bank of England, Monetary Authority of Singapore, and Bank of Thailand said they conducted the experiments using a prototype developed by Partior, which coordinated payment-versus-payment (PvP) settlement and multilateral net FX transactions across their test environments.

The three central banks are working under Project Meridian FX APAC, announced last November. The project encompassed multiple currencies, technologies, settlement infrastructures, and time zones, with the Reserve Bank of Australia and Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas also participating as observers, the banks said.

According to the statement, Partio's system matched settlement instructions, coordinated the reservation of funds, monitored payment readiness, and initiated settlement.

The experiment also examined multilateral netting, which allows banks to offset payments against one another to reduce the liquidity needed for settlement.

However, the accompanying policy report identified questions around operational resilience, settlement finality, governance, liability, and cross-border data sharing. It also warned that additional functions, including holding funds or calculating net positions, could change an operator's regulatory responsibilities.

Tom Mutton, the BOE's executive director of payments, innovation and fintech, said the project provided "practical evidence of how synchronized settlement could help reduce risk and improve efficiency in cross-border FX transactions."

The BOE plans to launch a live synchronization interface in 2028. Findings from Project Meridian FX APAC will inform further work on synchronized settlement.

It’s a cruel fact that it’s the plants with the biggest or boldest leaves that struggle most in the dry air of our winter-heated homes: adapting to life in a rainforest is exactly why the foliage is so fabulous in the first place. Beneath a dense forest canopy, sunlight is a precious commodity so some plants like Elephant Ear (Alocasia) grow huge leaves to catch as much light as possible. Others such as peace lilies has foliage densely packed with chlorophyll to absorb as much of the scarce light as possible, and that's what creates their intense, deep green hue. Some like Calathea have purple or red undersides to their leaves precisely so that precious light gets bounced back into the leaf.

So, if you want to enjoy these dramatic leaves at their best, you have to provide these houseplants with the humidity level they need to flourish. Any plant likely suffering from a lack of humidity at this time of the year is easy to spot, starting with any houseplants with brown leaf tips or edges, smaller or discolored leaves, or slow growth. Now, to restore these ailing houseplants to lush, glossy growth, you could treat them to a small humidifier like the Homedics Humidifier from Amazon. Or you could simply DIY a humidity tray with stuff you’ve probably already got in your backyard.

A humidity tray, also called a pebble tray, is a shallow container that’s wider than the plant pot it serves, filled with small pebbles and water. The plant pot sits on the pebbles above the water level so instead of water being pulled up into the potting soil, it evaporates into the air. The higher the heat in your home, and so the drier the air, the more water evaporates to create a humid microclimate around your houseplant.

An old plant saucer is perfect, or if you’d like something more decorative for your houseplant display, look in your kitchen cupboards for a deep plate or shallow dish. Fill this with fine gravel or small pebbles – pea gravel is perfect and you can buy bagged pea gravel pebbles from Home Depot very cheaply if you don’t have a gravel path or driveway to raid.

Then simply stand the plant pot on top of your DIY humidity tray and keep the water topped up, making sure the water level stays below the base of the pot.

Grouping your houseplants together will also help to raise the humidity. Clustering plants together creates an indoor microclimate because the water vapor that’s lost through the leaves diffuses more slowly – and this effect is amplified if even one or two of the grouped houseplants have humidity trays.

These plants all thrive in humidity levels of 40-60%. While that's likely within the average humidity of a home in summer, it's a different story in winter. Leaves with brown tips and outer edges are the most common sign that a houseplant is struggling in low humidity levels and is losing moisture from its foliage faster than the roots can replace it. All plants release water vapor from tiny pores on their leaves and, in dry air, that moisture is sucked out at a faster rate. The plant parts furthest away from the roots supplying the water are the first to run out of moisture, which is why leaf tips are the first to dry out to a crispy brown. Misting can also create a breeding ground for fungal and bacterial disease by leaving water sitting on foliage.

At the other end of the scale, if you live in a high-humidity climate like Louisiana, Mississippi or Florida, only the houseplants that are highly dependent on humidity need a pebble tray – unless your home is equipped with an efficient air-conditioning system that dehumidifies, and dependant on your winter home-heat level, of course. Unnecessary standing water in a warm, damp home puts plants at a higher risk of pests and fungal disease.

Keep the trays topped up with water for maximum humidity, and gradually reduce if needs be once your home's heat is turned off. It shatters the tone of an idealistic, progressive politician navigating the red tape of city hall, highlighting Wasicsko’s egotistical knack for defining his self-worth through his career as a damning fault, while also illustrating the callous, arduous nature of true systemic change.

The Leftovers is one of those series that feels like everything changes every episode. While it is a slow and meditative show, the depth it invests in its characters constantly brings new revelations to the fold, not only in their own lives as they navigate heartache, frustration, and rage, but in accordance with overarching story themes of grief, trauma, and the human condition as well. Overall, The Leftovers unfolds in a world reeling from the “Sudden Departure”, an inexplicable event in which two percent of the world’s population vanished in an instant.

The series finale, “The Book of Nora,” transpires years after the rest of the series, exploring an uneasy reunion between Nora (Carrie Coon) and Kevin (Justin Theroux). It revels in the surrealist intensity and compassionate dramatic might that defined the series at its best, ending on an unproven explanation as to what caused the Sudden Departure, then leans on empathy and choice in its final moments, rather than a traditional, plot-centered resolution. It’s a bold decision, but it perfectly suits the series’ overarching tone and brings touching closure to Kevin and Nora’s relationship.

Blending corporate satire with engrossing, high-stakes drama, Succession fast became one of HBO’s greatest ever series in its exploration of the vying for power that erupts between three siblings when their father, the aging founder and head of a multi-billion-dollar multimedia company, signals his intent to retire. It is a manic fight for power and position with no blow being deemed below the belt, and yet the fourth and final season still ends in a manner that leaves viewers numb and shocked.

The kinship the Roy siblings display as they stand against Lukas Matsson’s (Alexander Skarsgård) efforts to buy Waystar RoyCo gives way to total desperation and betrayal. It is quite brilliant how a series that features such unlikable and conniving characters leaves the audience feeling hollow, if not completely rattled, at their failure.

Based on Gillian Flynn’s novel of the same name, Sharp Objects is a methodical, meandering crime thriller that anchors its drama in Camille Preaker’s (Amy Adams) investigation of the murder of two young girls and the inner turmoil she experiences as she returns to her hometown of Wind Gap and is confronted with the ghosts of her traumatic childhood. It holds a captivating Southern Gothic atmosphere, a litany of exceptional performances, and a macabre balance of character drama and murder mystery, but its greatest asset is the festering sense of dreadful unpredictability it exudes throughout the entirety of its eight-episode run.

Even with viewers accustomed to its volatile plot twists, Sharp Objects completely changes the nature of its story in its final episode. In fact, it does so many times. The most striking of which comes in the final minute of the series, revealing a horrifying truth that shatters Camille’s sense of self-growth and safety. Accompanied by a credits sequence intercut with shots that force the audience to bask in the terrifying truth, Sharp Objects delivers an unforgettable ending that elevates it from a gripping psychological thriller to a masterclass in nightmarish suspense.

Six Feet Under is essentially a tale of mortality and the human condition wrapped up in a dark comedy about the dysfunctional Fisher family and their management of a funeral home in the aftermath of their patriarch’s death. It’s funny, often poignant and sad, always thought-provoking and compelling, and it ends with what is arguably the greatest series finale in the history of television, a feat of storytelling steeped in catharsis, closure, and beautiful thematic touch.

Within the context of the series itself, it recalibrated all the death and drama as being a simple fact within the wonder of life, transforming the show from being an acidic satirical dramedy to a powerful ode to family and existence itself. It should come as no surprise that The Sopranos’ finale altered the course of television history. After all, the series itself did so from its very first episode. Whether it was through presenting such a complex and challenging antihero in Tony Soprano (James Gandolfini), pioneering long-form serialized drama on the small screen, or simply through its stronger appetite for graphic material, The Sopranos revolutionized the modern age of television drama, and its finale marks another major evolutionary step for storytelling in the medium.

It is iconic in its own right. The diner, the uneasy tension, Journey’s “Don’t Stop Believin’”, and, of course, the cut to black. It was divisive at the time, but it has come to be heralded as a stroke of genius. Almost 20 years on, people still discuss what they think happened to Tony, but from the perspective of television drama, it proved that series didn’t necessarily need to end with neat, conclusive closure to be rewarding. It proved ambiguity had a huge part to play in 21st-century television; it redefined how audiences engaged with television (especially as it coincided with the rising popularity of the internet and social media), and it remains one of the best and boldest TV finales of all time.

Gut bacteria cannot see the sun so they use your meal times to control how your body absorbs nutrients

📅 Nov 13, 2026  |  Category: Food  |  Read Source →

Gut bacteria in mice read mealtimes, not daylight, and tell the intestine when to absorb, helping underfed mice grow and overfed ones get fat.

The body clock is supposed to run on light. Sunrise wakes the brain, and the rest of the body falls in line. But the bacteria in the gut live in the dark.

In mice, a common microbe keeps time another way: it reads when food arrives and tells the cells of the intestine to start absorbing.

The signal is a small molecule the bacterium makes while it digests phytate, a compound in grains, nuts and seeds. The molecule switches on genes that pull amino acids and fats out of a meal.

Give it to undernourished young mice and they grow. Give it to mice on a high-fat diet and they get fatter.

Zheng Kuang, an associate professor of biological sciences at Carnegie Mellon University, led the work from his lab. Colleagues at the University of Pittsburgh, UT Southwestern Medical Center and the Quadram Institute in England took part.

All of it was done in mice and in human gut cells grown in a dish.

In an interview with Earth.com, Kuang said what struck him most was that the bacteria convert the timing of a meal into chemistry.

“Their absolute numbers did not need to change substantially across the day – their metabolic activity changed,” he said.

The bacterium is Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, one of the most common residents of the human gut.

Kuang’s team raised mice with no gut microbes at all, then gave some of them this one species and nothing else. They sampled the lining of the small intestine around the clock.

Mice eat at night. In the hours after dark, the colonized mice carried more of the bacterial signal molecule in their gut lining than they did in the morning.

Germ-free mice showed no such rise, and neither did mice given a mutant strain missing the enzyme that breaks down phytate.

The bacteria themselves were no more numerous at night than in the morning, Kuang’s team found. What rose was the activity of that one enzyme.

“We are not claiming these bacteria have their own internal clock,” Kuang told Earth.com. “Rather, they respond to feeding cues and produce molecules called inositol phosphates, which activate a protein called HDAC3 in intestinal cells.”

That protein, HDAC3, works inside the cells lining the intestine and helps decide which genes are on.

The bacterial molecule makes it grip a partner protein more tightly, and the pair then turns on a set of transporter genes that carry nutrients across the gut wall.

In colonized mice, 14 amino acids and 14 fatty acids rose in the blood during the feeding hours, while in germ-free mice they stayed low.

Of 248 transporter genes the team tracked, 122 cycled more strongly with the bacterium present, and most of those handle amino acids, sugars or fats.

The body’s master clock genes kept their normal daily rhythm either way. The bacterium didn’t reset the clock; it strengthened the daily rise and fall of the metabolic genes that follow it.

The team then fed mice only during the day, which is their normal sleeping time. The signal molecule fell and the gut’s daily rhythm in gene activity flattened, though the animals ate no less than before.

Mice allowed to eat only at night looked like mice allowed to eat whenever they liked.

The feeding experiment is the result Kuang pointed to when asked what people should take from the work. “The key message is to align your feeding schedule with the natural day-night cycle – eating during your active, daytime hours,” he said.

Artificial light has pushed human eating into the night, he added, and the study suggests that misalignment disrupts the bacterial signals.

Kuang said there was likely a metabolic cost to that disruption. The feeding experiment itself tracked the signal and the gene rhythms, not the mice’s weight.

Young mice were weaned onto a diet with the usual calories but little protein or fat, a model for childhood malnutrition.

Those given the bacterium grew longer bodies and longer thigh bones than those given the mutant strain. They had heavier organs, more amino acids in their blood and better blood sugar control, and they ate the same amount.

The signal molecule on its own did the same, in mice with and without a normal gut community. In mice bred to lack HDAC3 in their gut cells, neither the bacterium nor the molecule helped, and a drug that blocks HDAC3 blunted the effect too.

In a public dataset of children’s stool samples, the bacterium was among 19 species more common in healthy children than in undernourished ones.

Adult mice fed a high-fat, high-sugar diet for 12 weeks gained more weight with the bacterium than with the mutant strain.

They carried more belly fat and liver fat and had higher blood fats and worse blood sugar handling. They absorbed more fat from food and passed less of it in their stool.

Again, the mice lacking HDAC3 in their gut cells showed none of this, and food intake didn’t differ.

Kuang said that double edge is the finding he most wants understood. “This illustrates why it is too simple to label a bacterium as inherently ‘good’ or ‘bad’ – its effects depend on diet and the metabolic state of the host,” he explained.

The team’s reading is evolutionary. A pathway that extracts the most from every meal was an advantage when food was scarce, and the same pathway can work against a body surrounded by high-fat diets.

Whether this bacterium even lives and works in the human small intestine is an open question. The team also could not prove that the signal molecule in the gut cells came from the bacteria rather than from the cells themselves, which make related molecules.

And phytate intake varies from one diet to another, so the signal’s strength probably does too, the authors wrote.

Whether the same signal shifts in people who eat on a jet-lagged or night-shift schedule is a question the study does not reach.

Asked how soon the bacteria could be tested against malnutrition, Kuang said it is too early to give a reliable timeline for human testing.

The next step, he said, is to look for the pathway in human intestinal samples and in children with undernutrition. Until then, the case that mealtimes talk to the gut through its bacteria rests on mice.

The full study was published online in a publication of the Carnegie Mellon University.

In recent years, United's managerial merry-go-round has alternated between European coaches with their own fixed styles of play, and former players from the Ferguson era.

But between his tactics of choice and his adoration for the former United boss, Pochettino may be uniquely placed to bridge the gap between both camps.

Pochettino a decade in Man Utd's making - but Carrick deserves better

Michael Carrick's United produced a series of good results to finish last season with a run unmatched by any other team in the league. They looked great, but it was a greatness built on attacking in a transition-heavy manner.

Under Carrick, in games they had less than 50% of the ball, they only lost once. In those 13 games, they boasted a win-rate of 62%. This dropped to 36% in games they had the majority of possession.

This style of play suited their direct attackers and protected their defenders.

At the same time, Casemiro's set-piece prowess provided United a reliable way to break the deadlock against deep defences - something they have struggled with this season.

With United a goal up, opponents stepped forward, giving Carrick's men more space. They could play to their strengths again, attacking quickly. It worked.

But teams at the top are reluctant to play in this way. Carrick's vision for United was a side that played slick football, with short passes in build-up intuitively, referencing Paris St-Germain's midfield three as a source of inspiration.

Building up in this manner, to entice and escape from pressure, before looking to find the forwards quickly, was the plan Carrick was working towards.

This didn't come to fruition and trying to take United from a successful counter-attacking side to one that could reliably beat teams while keeping the ball was the same hurdle that undid Ole Gunnar Solskjar and Erik ten Hag.

Pochettino gives United an interesting solution to this problem.

In his own words, he has described his sides as needing "to be protagonists, always press high, work hard and be dynamic". He refers to himself as a "disciple" of the legendary Argentina boss Marcelo Bielsa, who he played for.

If you wanted an immediate comparison in the Premier League to a coach who resembles Pochettino's ideas in possession, it would be worth looking at Liverpool's Andoni Iraola, who similarly cites Bielsa as his reference.

These are coaches who, while taking the initiative, encourage vertical football. For as different as the clubs are, both the fans of United and Liverpool have enjoyed success when their teams have played high-octane football.

This summer's World Cup was a strong showcase of Pochettino's style of play and his recent tactical adaptation.

Rather than a fixed, positional style of play - akin to the work of somebody like Manchester City's Enzo Maresca - Pochettino's USA had only certain players being tasked with holding their position.

This approach posed problems for teams who defended in a zonal manner - defending specific areas rather than individual players.

The US midfielders being able to move freely meant they could step into areas of the pitch between the zones that were being defended, which resulted in their players often being unmarked with time and space on the ball.

By playing in close proximity to each other in the middle of the pitch, the US found another benefit.

Small-space passing combinations helped draw the opposing team up towards the ball and combined with clever off-ball movement to stretch the pitch, allowed the US to find their striker with few defenders around him.

Pochettino's men looked to thread through-balls in behind and early after enticing the opposition's press and the speedy Folarin Balogun was able to turn those chances into goals.

With a four against three in that area of the pitch, USA are best placed to play quick combinations before looking to find their attackers in space.

Here we can the swapping of left-winger Pulisic and left-back Robinson. In this example, Robinson inverts into the fluid midfield four with Pulisic positioned on the touchline for balance. When out wide, Pulisic was able to showcase impressive one-against-one dribbling throughout the match too.

This combination of a fluid midfield, small-space passing and direct through-balls doesn't sound dissimilar to what Carrick was trying to move towards implementing this season.

And although that may worry United fans, the similarity in principles will benefit the players.

"My philosophy is to give a freer style with a good organisation," were Pochettino's words in 2014.

Although philosophically similar, that latter part - a clearer organisation to his freer style - is likely to be what Carrick was unable to reliably offer his players.

For fans of top sides, fast-paced, vertical football tends to be accepted when it comes from a place of being proactive - think Ferguson's United or Jurgen Klopp's Liverpool - wanting to keep the ball, but not passing it for passing's sake.

Fast-paced, vertical football tends to be frowned upon when it comes from a place of conceding possession and defending your own goal for long periods of the match.

The first half of Carrick's stint did appear proactive, while his 4-2-4 press worked well. Leaving four players high was a risk that allowed them to win the ball back and score quickly, but as teams figured this out, United failed to adapt.

There also appeared to be a drop in the team's fitness the longer United went from the departure of ex-boss Ruben Amorim, who left United well conditioned, albeit on the back of a schedule without European football.

Hiring Pochettino will immediately begin the process of turning United into a well-drilled side, capable of applying intense pressure, likely in a co-ordinated manner.

Pochettino enjoyed relative success with Spurs, built on this intensity and co-ordination, that differentiated him from the rest of the league.

With that unique selling point more common now than a decade ago, how he builds on it will dictate how his tenure unfolds.

But watching Brighton this season, he can be assured that an approach founded on intensity off the ball can work in the Premier League in 2026, with Fabian Hurzeler's men second in the league, with the fourth highest average possession, 10 games in.

Pochettino, from a footballing point of view, feels extremely aligned to the values of Ferguson's United, and as a result will implement numerous similarities to the things Carrick wanted, but ultimately failed, to deliver.

His wealth of experience make him a more proven entity than the departing United manager and if he can get his team fit and pressing high, in typically Pochettino fashion, they should see an upturn in form.

Expanding on his managerial approach, while managing Spurs he explained how "the most important thing [was] to create a good shape for players to be comfortable and to show their real qualities".

Rather than relying on pre-defined pattern, he looks to empower individuals to solve attacking problems. Taking this approach means recruitment of top talent matters more than ever.

"He [gave] me the confidence and licence to go where I wanted on the pitch, where I [could] use my strengths," said Cole Palmer, who enjoyed his most productive Premier League season under Pochettino at Chelsea.

United have good attackers but they will need to take the same initiative Palmer did, if platformed to their strengths - something Pochettino does well.

If they don't, and if Pochettino is to stumble, it will likely be in United's inability to break down stubborn defences when asked to dominate the ball - in what would feel like Groundhog Day, again.
