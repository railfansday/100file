A Used Cybertruck Is Selling A Month Faster Than A Ford F-150

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Browse this and many other websites without banner ads, personalized tracking and video ads.

If you had to guess which used pickup found a buyer fastest in October, the Toyota Tacoma would be a sensible bet. It’s America’s best-selling midsize truck and has a reputation for holding its value. Or you might pick the super frugal Ford Maverick Hybrid. But the truck that beat them both was the Tesla Cybertruck.

The data from the new iSeeCars study covers 1.1 million used vehicles that changed hands in October, so this was before Tesla announced it would discontinue the Cybertruck in 2027. The study looked at one- to five-year-old vehicles and ranked models by how long they’d been listed before selling.

The Cybertruck surpassed all other pickups, averaging 39.1 days, compared to 65.5 days for used pickups overall. The Maverick Hybrid came closest at 43.3 days, followed by the Rivian R1T at 45.2 and the F-150 Lightning at 47.4. The Tacoma came in fifth position.

The gap between the two electric pickups at either end of the list was even more striking. A used Chevrolet Silverado EV took 107.5 days to sell, putting it last among the 24 trucks in the study. That’s a 68-day difference between two American electric pickups. Across the wider used market, EVs averaged about 46 days to find a buyer, around nine fewer than hybrids and 15 fewer than gas vehicles.

How quickly a used Cybertruck sold seems to have changed a lot since May 2024, when some sellers were cutting as much as $30,000 from their asking prices after listing their trucks for close to $200,000. By December of that year, we reported that used Cybertrucks were piling up on dealer lots, with data at the time showing they stayed for 75 days, up from 27 in May.

Then owners got a look at what Tesla itself would pay for a used Cybertruck trade-in. One year later, in May 2025, one owner of a $99,990 dual-motor Foundation Series truck got a trade-in estimate of $65,400 after driving it just 6,211 miles.

So even though the Cybertruck was the fastest-selling used pickup in iSeeCars’ new study, it didn’t report what buyers paid, so we don’t know whether owners got their asking price or had to lower it to sell.

Tesla will build the last Cybertrucks at the end of summer 2027, and this will reshape their used market. Elon Musk originally announced production would end around the middle of the year, but then extended the run to fill more orders. And if you order a Cybertruck now, the only one you can get is the $110,000 tri-motor Cyberbeast, so anyone who wants a less expensive dual-motor truck has to look for a used example.

Once the last new Cybertruck is sold, I’d expect collectors and everyday truck buyers to look at used ones differently. Someone after a clean Foundation Series truck with low miles might pay a little extra for one close to home, especially since it could be months before another comes up for sale nearby.

At the same time, someone who just needs an electric pickup to tow a trailer has more options. If a used dual-motor Cybertruck costs about the same as a Ford F-150 Lightning or a Rivian R1T, I suspect that buyer will care more about mileage, condition, and how much battery warranty is left than when Tesla built its last truck.

Is Anthropic Joining the S&P 500? Here's What Investors Need to Know.

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Don't expect major changes to your ETF portfolio.

By David Dierking – Nov 26, 2026 at 8:03AM EST

10 stocks we like better than S&P 500 Index ›

Anthropic (ANT +1.08%) just completed the largest IPO in history, raising around $93.5 billion at $83 per share. The stock closed Nov. 20 at $88.91.

Given that it's now one of the largest publicly traded companies in the world, you might assume its addition to the S&P 500 (^GSPC -0.25%) is imminent. It isn't, actually, and won't be for a while. But the stock will show up in several other major indexes within days.

When Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX +1.30%) debuted earlier this year, there was a lot of discussion around how the major indexes would handle it. Typically, companies go public at much smaller market caps, not with market caps over $1 trillion. Index committees needed to decide whether to "fast track" these mega IPOs into the indexes sooner, given their outsize influence.

Several providers made changes. But Standard & Poor's did not. It elected to keep the existing 12-month waiting period and generally accepted accounting principles (GAAP) income requirements -- companies need to be profitable in the most recent quarter as well as the sum of the past four quarters in order to qualify.

Anthropic, so far, falls far short on the GAAP income requirement, reporting a net loss of roughly $104 billion in the first nine months of this year.

Given these requirements, the earliest we're likely to see Anthropic in the S&P 500 is late 2027.

The S&P 500 will actually be the exception, not the rule, when it comes to adding Anthropic.

The Nasdaq-100 index updated its inclusion criteria as a result of the SpaceX IPO. Now, if a company's entire market capitalization ranks within the top 40 current constituents in the index, it can be added to the index after just 15 trading days. Since Anthropic is listed on the Nasdaq, it could show up in the Invesco QQQ ETF (QQQ -0.65%) before the end of the year.

The Morningstar indexes, which acquired the widely-used CRSP indexes, could add Anthropic after the close of the fifth trading day. The Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF (VTI -0.18%) would be one of the biggest funds impacted by this. FTSE Russell works on a similar timeline.

A lot of index funds will weight stocks according to the number of publicly available shares, not necessarily the total market cap. Since only about 5% of Anthropic's total market cap is now traded publicly, its starting weight will likely be small. Vanguard has projected only a 0.055% to 0.15% weighting for Anthropic in the Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF.

When and if additional shares become publicly available, the anticipated weighting of these stocks would likely grow along with it.

If you really want to own Anthropic, you should probably just buy Anthropic.

The stock won't show up at all in the S&P 500 for the foreseeable future. For many of the major large-cap indexes and corresponding ETFs, the initial weight probably won't get much above 0.25%. The exception might be the Nasdaq-100, the Invesco QQQ ETF, and the Invesco Nasdaq 100 ETF (QQQM -0.66%). They could see an allocation closer to 1% given the more concentrated nature of that index.

It'll likely be a similar story to what we saw when SpaceX went public: a lot of hype but minimal impact to the ETF marketplace and your portfolio in general.

Long-term investors almost certainly shouldn't be making big asset allocation changes based on this. Even if you want to overweight tech based on a belief that Anthropic might spark the sector, your tech ETFs aren't going to meaningfully change in the future.

Three Ancient Cosmic Collisions May Explain Why the Moon Has Two Faces

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Scientists have narrowed down when three massive collisions reshaped the Moon, bringing them closer to understanding why its Earth-facing side looks so different from its far side.

Billions of years ago, a collision powerful enough to melt enormous quantities of lunar rock created the Moon’s largest impact basin. Subsequent collisions broke apart and scattered some of that ancient material across the surface, leaving behind a geological record that scientists are now piecing together.

Located on the Moon’s far side, the South Pole-Aitken (SPA) basin is one of the oldest and largest impact structures in the solar system. Understanding its history could help explain why the Moon’s near and far sides are so different, what lies beneath its surface, and when major collisions occurred during its early evolution.

An international research team led by Professor Guochun Zhao, Mok Sau-King, a professor in the Department of Earth and Planetary Sciences (DEPS) at the University of Hong Kong (HKU), and Dr. Yuqi Qian, the study’s first author, has identified two types of magnesium-rich rocks with remarkably different origins. Published in Science Advances, the research also establishes a clearer timeline of the major impacts that shaped the region.

For decades, the basin remained largely unexplored. That changed in 2019, when Chang’e-4 completed the first successful landing on the Moon’s far side, followed in 2024 by Chang’e-6, which brought the first rock samples from this largely unexplored region back to Earth.

Using Chang’e-6 samples allocated to HKU DEPS and surface measurements from both missions, the researchers obtained the first direct geological evidence from the two landing sites for reconstructing the basin’s impact history. Satellite observations and numerical simulations helped them trace the origins of rocks across the wider basin.

One group of rocks, known as moderately magnesium-rich norites, formed in the aftermath of the original SPA collision. The impact generated a thick sheet of molten rock that gradually cooled, with different minerals crystallizing at different stages. Later collisions exposed these ancient rocks at the central peaks of large craters. Some were eventually blasted toward the Chang’e-4 landing site in Von Kármán crater by the impact that formed Finsen crater.

The second group contained considerably more magnesium and likely formed through a separate process called magnesium-suite magmatism, a type of magma activity associated with the original SPA impact. These rocks were exposed along the western ring of mountain peaks within the Apollo basin before the Chaffee S impact scattered some of them toward the Chang’e-6 landing site.

Earlier research by the HKU-led team had already uncovered evidence of prolonged volcanic activity and previously unrecognized magma activity in the region. Those studies also suggested that underground accumulations of titanium-rich minerals, known as ilmenite-bearing cumulates, helped account for the contrasting volcanic histories of the Moon’s two sides.

The latest findings suggest that the South Pole-Aitken basin formed approximately 4.34 billion years ago. Another major collision created the Apollo basin around 4.2 billion years ago, followed by the formation of the Orientale basin approximately 3.87 billion years ago. Together, these three events reveal how successive collisions reshaped the Moon’s largest impact basin over hundreds of millions of years.

Reference: “Postimpact evolution of the South Pole–Aitken basin revealed by Chang’e-4 and Chang’e-6” by Yuqi Qian, Wenlei Song, James Head, Mingyu Tian, Zhuang Guo, Harald Hiesinger, Carolyn H. van der Bogert, Le Zhang, Jingyou Chen, Wanfeng Zhang, Ziqing Li, Lingzhi Sun, Xiangsong Wang, Wei-Ran Li, Jiacheng Liu, Nicolas Dauphas, Yi-Gang Xu and Guochun Zhao, 18 November 2026, Science Advances. From the CD changer to the iPod and aux jack, the decade saw audio options shift—for better or for worse.

And I’m going to be honest with you: Results have been mixed. The seeds of our collective solitude were planted in the decades before, and the worst of it wouldn’t hit us until the years after, but 2000–09 was the era when the monoculture really began to fracture and fragment into microcultures. The great American mainstream was dwindling in the Aughts, a narrative expressed through our music. New technology changed the way people across the world listened. Once we shook off Y2K anxiety, the new millennium began as normal. Fueled by boy bands, blockbuster hip-hop, and, somehow, Limp Bizkit, the recording industry was flush in 2000. What was big was huge, and whether you liked the songs or not, you knew them. My main musical memory of the year 2000 took place in a rental car, on a drive up to the mountains with my roommates. The mid-size sedan had a mere cassette player, so we made do with the radio. On that October afternoon, some station played the comeback single from a band that hadn’t released a studio album in three years: U2’s “Beautiful Day.” We turned it up, we nodded along, and we were impressed that a band could sound this good despite having members who were now 40. Best of all, we shared the experience with however many strangers were also tuned in to this frequency.

As the Aughts progressed, I found myself sliding into my car’s CD slot fewer store-bought discs and more homemade mixes I burned using my iBook after downloading music from iTunes. I recently found a box of these discs in my garage, and each one is packed with music I have not thought about since I put them there. Then there was the iPod. Other MP3 players predated it, but the iPod was sleek and sexy and held up to 1000 songs, which I swear sounded like a lot in 2001. The forward-thinking among us might carry a special cord in our bag in case we found ourselves in a car with an auxiliary input. Traditional radio stations suffered as we ditched the DJs and did their job for free.

Satellite radio launched an effort to fill the void—XM in 2001, Sirius in 2002, then SiriusXM in 2008, with hundreds of hyperspecific-genre stations on the digital dial. Satellite radio broke us in to the concept of paying a monthly fee to listen to music we don’t own, and Spotify and Apple Music would run with that idea in the decades ahead. Now we all have access to virtually every piece of music ever recorded, but who is guiding us through it? It’s chaos. I find myself longing for those days before endless choices, before Bluetooth or aux cables, when I’d trust a stranger to play something for me while I was driving. When I’d be on a road trip and an unknown station would fuzz in and play a country song and a public service announcement, when a human being in a studio said something that streaked on radio waves across the desert air, and we were connected, until the station fuzzed out.

Of course, I can still do that. But the convenience culture that has continued to creep in since the Aughts has conditioned me to pull out my phone, wirelessly connect it to the car, and put on a mix or a podcast. I actually have to remind myself that those days were real, when the radio was once our go-to.

I miss those times. The new stuff from U2 is pretty good, though.

From Saturn's magnificent rings to the waning gibbous moon, here's how to turn your Thanksgiving celebrations into a family skywatching adventure.

If your household is like most American families, then on Thursday you likely will all be sitting down with family and friends for a traditional Thanksgiving turkey dinner during the mid-to-late afternoon hours.

Then as darkness falls, some will probably migrate into the living room to catch some football or other holiday fare on television. But if you have a large gathering of family and friends at your home — and if your skies are mainly clear — why not invite everyone outside to gaze at the evening sky?

If you have binoculars, or better yet, a telescope, you just might end up turning this into a memorable family tradition of its own.

If I may paraphrase the mysterious voice from the 1989 movie, "Field of Dreams": "If you set up your telescope, they will come." And you'll get a big kick out of your Turkey Day guests uttering all the "oohs" and "ahs" or exclamations of "Wow!" with just one peek through the eyepiece.

As darkness falls, Saturn will glow at first magnitude, shining with a sedate, yellow-white light and appearing noticeably brighter than the star Fomalhaut, about 40 degrees (the equivalent of four fist-widths) to its lower right.

Its famous ring system is now tilted only about six degrees toward Earth but may be visible with steadied (or image-stabilized) high-power binoculars or through the eyepiece of a telescope magnifying over 30-power. The rings will seem to glow with an inner light and for those who have never had the pleasure of seeing them for themselves, that first view always elicits astonishment; kids especially most often will say: "Awesome!" or "No way!" I've even had people accuse me of using a slide of Saturn, to which I, of course, reply with, "No . . . that's the real thing."

Then, along about 6:25 p.m. local time, the waning gibbous moon, 2½ days past full phase and 91% illuminated, will begin to emerge from beyond the east-northeast horizon. Give it another hour and it will stand about 10 degrees above the horizon — equal to the width of your clenched fist held at arm's length. By then, the moon will be in a better position for evening study.

Even in binoculars, the moon is a striking sight to see. It's such a special object because it doesn't matter whether you live in a rural area or in the middle of a brightly lit city, for the moon can always be readily observed. It always looks spectacular whether you're using binoculars or a telescope. It never looks precisely the same no matter how often you view it and can be observed even on hazy or partially cloud-covered nights.

Pay particular attention to the line of darkness — called the terminator — features near that border stand out in bold relief; the shadows become stronger and details are more easily seen. Take note on this night of the circular dark area, bisected by the terminator: Mare Crisium — the "Sea of Crises." Immediately bordering above it is a 50-mile (80 km)-wide ring of very rugged mountains, rising as high as 14,400 feet (4,389 meters), which now resembles a huge crater partially in shadow when the sun is low.

Probably the one object that your Thanksgiving guests will want to see is the biggest and brightest of all the man-made objects orbiting the Earth: the International Space Station (ISS). Except for those living across the southern US, for most everywhere else, the ISS will be making visible passes on Thanksgiving evening.

Circling the Earth at an average altitude of 260 miles (420 km) and at a speed of 17,500 mph (28,200 kph), it can appear to move as fast as a high-flying jet airliner, sometimes taking as much as six or seven minutes to cross the sky. It can easily be confused with aircraft lights. Nominally, it appears white with a slight yellow tinge and at its very brightest it can sometimes appear to shine as bright as magnitude -5.6, which is twice as bright as the planet Venus! A vibration too faint for the human ear to detect. A temperature variation that is difficult to observe directly. An unusual pattern hidden within operational data.

Turning 38 Years of Engineering Knowledge Into Industrial AI

The global artificial intelligence industry is advancing rapidly, with companies competing to develop increasingly capable models and applications.

Yet applying AI effectively in real industrial environments remains a fundamentally different challenge.

A factory cannot be fully understood through information learned from the internet alone.

Similar vibration patterns may indicate entirely different mechanical problems depending on equipment design, operating conditions and the physical mechanisms involved.

For plant operators, an alarm indicating that something is abnormal is rarely sufficient to justify stopping a critical production line.

FutureMain developed its flagship ExRBM platform to address precisely this challenge.

Rather than relying solely on statistical anomaly detection, the platform is designed to identify specific failure mechanisms, explain the evidence behind its assessments and recommend appropriate responses.

According to company disclosures, ExRBM can automatically identify more than 85 machinery failure mechanisms, including bearing damage and shaft misalignment.

Decades of experience analyzing normal equipment behavior, abnormal operating conditions and mechanical failures have produced a specialized body of knowledge that is difficult to replicate through software development alone.

By transforming this expertise into AI-driven diagnostic capabilities, FutureMain aims to make advanced industrial knowledge more accessible, scalable and actionable.

ExRBM 2.0 takes a more advanced approach.

Newly attributed Titian self-portrait, bought for almost $70,000 two years ago, on sale in London

The newly discovered Titian self-portrait

“Both Rubens and his erstwhile assistant Van Dyck venerated Titian as the very prince of painters.” So writes the Titian expert Peter Humfrey in the catalogue for Dickinson’s impressive new exhibition, Titian, Rubens, Van Dyck, which opened last night and runs until 18th December at 58 Jermyn Street, London.

Simon Dickinson and his son Milo have brought together two paintings by Titian, two by Rubens and nine by Van Dyck for the show, all from private collections and most for sale, priced between around £400,000 and over £3m. At the heart of the show is the relationship between the three painters, principally the influence of Titian on the two younger, Flemish painters.

The exhibition, one of the most ambitious staged by the gallery in its 33-year history, is accompanied by an extensive catalogue including essays from Humfrey alongside the Belgian art historians Koenraad Jonckheere, a professor in Northern Renaissance and Baroque art at Ghent University, and Katlijne Van der Stighelen, a Rubens expert.

While works by Titian, Rubens and Van Dyck are “very, very difficult to find”, Milo Dickinson says, there are still some at large thanks to all three being extremely prolific. “Titian was this great role model to Rubens and then Van Dyck, both as a man who was a successful artist throughout his life, courted by major patrons, but also as a painter: the colours he used, the compositions he devised,” Dickinson says. Rubens, for instance, “spent several months just copying paintings by Titian in Madrid, which had been bought by Phillip II of Spain.” As for Van Dyck, the sketchbook he took around Italy when he was a young man is full of annotated studies of Titians.

The exhibition includes several recent rediscoveries, most made after cleaning, with the catalogue outlining the arguments in favour of the attributions made by various experts.

Peter Paul Rubens, Study of the young Anthony van Dyck (around 1616-7)

Among them is what is thought to be a late self-portrait by Titian, variously dated by scholars to between 1546-47 to 1560. The attribution has been long debated by scholars, Milo Dickinson says. Everett Fahy, the American art historian and former director of the Frick who died in 2018, first proposed the attribution to Titian in 2009 when the painting emerged—filthy—from a French private collection. The oil on canvas, small at 44cm by 33cm, was sold for $69,850 at Sotheby's New York in 2024, catalogued as “attributed to Titian”. It has subsequently been cleaned, X-rayed and further studied. Now, Dickinson says, “various scholars—Peter Humfrey, Nicholas Penny and Ian Kennedy—have written on it and debated the dating of it because [if the attribution is correct] it would make it one of only three accepted self-portraits by Titian”, alongside those in the Gemäldegalerie in Berlin and that in the Prado, Madrid. The painting, which has been in a UK private collection since 2024, is now priced at £1.5m.

Another find is a Rubens head study which is now thought to depict a young Anthony van Dyck, based on dating, the sitter’s distinctive features and comparison with van Dyck’s self-portraits. The oil on panel, which measures 43cm by 35cm, was bought by the current owner at Sotheby’s Paris last November as part of the sale of the collection of the British property billionaire Manny Davidson. Catalogued as a Head Study of a Boy by Rubens, it sold for €744,000. With fitting circularity, Davidson had bought the painting as “attributed to Rubens” from Simon Dickinson in 2007, snapping it up after this attribution was questioned by the vetting committee at Tefaf Maastricht—Simon Dickinson spent several years trying to prove it was by Rubens.

The study had been thought to date to around 1614, when Van Dyck would have been only 14 or 15 years old, younger than the boy depicted here with his wispy stubble. But the Louvre, which holds the other half of this double head study, has recently argued that its sketch probably dates to 1616-17. “That got us thinking,” Dickinson says. “Once you've seen it cleaned, you realise the hair colour, the distinct shape of the face, the lips, the shape of the nose, they're totally concurrent with what Van Dyck looked like in his self-portraits.” He adds that Jonckheere and Van der Stighelen support the identity of the sitter as being van Dyck, alongside other scholars. The head study is for sale, price undisclosed.

Brunaille equestrian study by van Dyck (around 1629-32)

Yet another post-clean rediscovery is a study by van Dyck for the equestrian portrait of Albert de Ligne, Prince of Arenberg and Barbançon (around 1629-32) which hangs in Holkham Hall, Norfolk. It is one of several similar brunaille studies, one of which is the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York. Another of these sketches, presumed to be the original, had been in the De Craecker collection in the 19th century but had since gone missing. “Then this one appeared in an American private collection and it was pretty clear, upon seeing it cleaned, that this was the original and the other versions were copies done in Van Dyck's studio by assistants.” Dickinson says.

He adds that “the sheer quality, the way that he uses highlights, the abbreviated forms” in this version are superior when compared to the others. The pentimenti (revisions) in this, typical of Van Dyck, have also become clearly visible over time, Dickinson says, particularly the adjusted position of the horses head and front legs. The American collector, who bought it in 2014, is now selling the sketch through Dickinson, with an asking price in the region of £500,000.

Lisa Marei Schmidt appointed to lead Kunstmuseum Bern and Zentrum Paul Klee

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Lisa Marei Schmidt has worked at other institutions including Kunstsammlung Nordrhein-Westfalen in Düsseldorf and Berlin's Hamburger Bahnhof

Photo David von Becker/Kunstmuseum Bern – Zentrum Paul Klee

Lisa Marei Schmidt has been appointed as the new director of two major museums in Bern: Kunstmuseum Bern and Zentrum Paul Klee. The board of trustees of the foundation that oversees both institutions selected Schmidt from 97 applicants following an international search, according to a release. She will lead on artistic direction and also chair the museums' management board.

Schmidt has directed the Brücke-Museum in Berlin, home to the world’s largest collection of works by artists from the German Expressionist group Die Brücke, since 2017. During that time she has co-curated exhibitions exploring complex and sometimes dark aspects of the history of German Expressionism, such as Escape into Art? The Brücke Painters in the Nazi Period (2019) and Whose Expression? The Brücke Artists and Colonialism (2021–22). Under her direction, the museum has also strengthened its work in provenance research, restoration, art education and digitisation. The German Association of Art Critics (AICA) named the Brücke-Museum as its museum of the year for 2023.

Born in 1978, Schmidt studied art history in Marburg, Amsterdam and Berlin, and curating contemporary art at the Royal College of Art in London. Before joining the Brücke-Museum, she worked as a research trainee at Kunstsammlung Nordrhein-Westfalen and served as a curator at Berlin's Hamburger Bahnhof from 2012 to 2017. In 2020, she curated Bonzo's Dream by Swiss artist Vivian Suter, which received AICA's Special Exhibition award.

“To me, museums are places where art and people meet,” Schmidt said in a statement. "They provide space for encounters, new perspectives and dialogue across society." She said she looked forward to getting to know the teams in Bern and building on the distinctive qualities of the two museums, which have shared strategic leadership under the umbrella foundation Kunstmuseum Bern – Zentrum Paul Klee since 2015.

Schmidt will also inherit a pressing challenge at Kunstmuseum Bern. In June, voters in the canton rejected a CHF15.7m (£14.4m) planning credit, or budget allocation, for a project to renovate the museum's historic building and replace its 1983 extension, with 51.8% voting against it. The museum says its ageing buildings still urgently need repairs.

Schmidt succeeds Nina Zimmer, who led the institutions for a decade and will become director-general of Vienna's Belvedere in January 2027. The foundation’s commercial director Thomas Soraperra will serve as interim director until Schmidt arrives.

Yoro was sent off eight minutes into United’s trip to Anfield for a last-man foul on Isak, which referee Sam Barrott determined fit the criteria for Denial Of A Goalscoring Opportunity (DOGSO).

The young United centre-back was shown a straight red card after VAR doubled down on the day, insisting that no ‘clear and obvious error’ had been made.

United successfully appealed the decision through an independent panel and the lengths they went to for their evidence submission has now come to light.

Bosses at United included three high-definition video clips of the incident in question, which used multiple different angles as well as speeds in slow-motion and in real time for the panel to consider.

Coca-Cola Is the Most Boring Stock I Own. After 64 Years of Dividend Raises, I'll Never Sell.

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

It's a blue chip dividend stock that you don't have to second-guess.

By Stefon Walters – Nov 26, 2026 at 7:39AM EST

10 stocks we like better than Coca-Cola ›

I consider myself adventurous, but I often prefer boring when it comes to the stocks I own. I have my fair share of growth stocks, for sure, but my staple "boring" stock is Coca-Cola (KO -0.03%).

Although Coca-Cola is up over 40% this year (as of Nov. 24), its main appeal has long been its dividend. It has increased its annual dividend for 64 consecutive years, making it one of the market's premier Dividend Kings ( companies with at least 50 consecutive years of dividend hikes). Considering that and its sustained success, it's a stock I'll never sell.

You can count on one hand the number of brands more well-known globally than Coca-Cola. Its products (especially its namesake soda) are available in virtually every part of the world, and its asset-light business model enables it to operate efficiently.

Usually, if a company wants distribution somewhere, it needs plants, warehouses, and delivery fleets in that market. In Coca-Cola's case, it relies on bottling and distribution partners to do so. Instead of owning factories and delivery fleets around the world, Coca-Cola sells concentrates and syrups to its partners and lets them do the heavy lifting.

Keeping its business asset-light lets Coca-Cola maintain high margins and an ultra-safe dividend. You don't get to 64 years of dividend increases without consistent, high-margin cash flows.

Not many companies fit the "recession-proof" narrative, but Coca-Cola is one of them. Even when the economy slows and money is tight, people still buy their favorite Coca-Cola brands. That's the beauty of its brand moat.

And despite being the undisputed leader in its space, Coca-Cola hasn't gotten complacent with its drink portfolio. It constantly refines its lineup to adjust to changing consumer preferences. Just six years ago, it had over 400 brands in its portfolio. Today, it has around half of that.

When you have an option for virtually every occasion, you keep customers in your ecosystem. It's how the company has sustained success for so long.

Coca-Cola is a premium business that's priced like one. At the time of this writing, it's trading at 28.9 times its earnings, which is higher than its average over the past few years and higher than the S&P 500's (^GSPC -0.25%)23.7 P/E ratio. The stock is still a buy, but after a 40% run-up this year, the upside may be more limited. Luckily, that doesn't affect its dividend.

Coca-Cola is always a good long-term investment, but with bear-market fears lingering, a short-term silver lining is that the stock typically outperforms the market during sell-offs. Past results don't guarantee future performance, but Coca-Cola has strong momentum at a time when the tech sector is dragging down the broader market.

A Mark Cuban-backed AI startup is helping families turn Thanksgiving conversations with their elderly relatives into lasting memories

📅 Nov 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As 81.5 million Americans travel at least 50 miles from home for Thanksgiving, conversations around the dinner table could present a rare opportunity for people to learn more about their aging loved ones. A new class of AI startups wants to help them preserve those conversations forever.

Charlie Greene, the cofounder and CEO of Remento, first learned about the importance of recording memories when his father, Don Greene, died during the hijacking of United Airlines Flight 93 on 9/11.

A 10-year-old boy at the time of the terrorist attack, which resulted in his father’s plane crashing in Shanksville, Penn., the now 34-year-old Greene mostly has a collection of old home videos to remember him by.

So when his mother Claudette, 74, was diagnosed with stage-three lung cancer, he quickly acted to record her memories. With the intention of conducting an oral history interview, Greene searched “questions to ask a parent,” which Google morbidly autocompleted with “before they die.”

When he started asking her the questions like “How did you get to elementary school as a kid,” she lit up, surprised he was interested at all.

“The thing that blew me away about that experience was how unmorbid it felt,” he said.

Claudette is in remission, but her battle with cancer still left Greene “unhealthily fixated” on the idea of how to best preserve loved ones’ memories. In October 2023, he launched Remento, which sends older “storytellers” a weekly text or email reminder prompting them to respond to photos or questions chosen in advance by their family members, such as, “What was the hardest thing you had to overcome as a child?”

After gathering a maximum of a year’s worth of responses, the AI converts the raw transcript into a naturally flowing narrative that goes into a physical book complete with images and QR codes linking the text to the original recordings.

Greene told Fortune Thanksgiving presents a great opportunity for people to discover new memories or learn new twists on well worn stories from their relatives.

“One of the things I want for my daughter is the chance to sit next to her grandmother and hear her say ‘Believe it or not that’s me.’ Thanksgiving is a pretty good excuse to pull an old album off the shelf and make room for the two of them on the couch,” he said.

Last year, Greene secured a $300,000 investment from billionaire Mark Cuban during an episode of Shark Tank in exchange for a 10% stake in his company. He has raised a total of $4.3 million since launch.

Greene said he understands some people’s skepticism of AI, because “A lot of what AI is proposing to the world doesn’t feel actually that good to our lives right now.”

Widespread anxiety about AI replacing human workers as well as concerns over its environmental footprint have contributed to some people’s increasing skepticism toward the technology. A March study of more than 3,000 people by the nonprofit Jobs For the Future found that 38% of respondents said AI was doing more harm than good, while less than half said the technology was a net positive.

Yet Greene hasn’t gotten as much pushback about the company’s use of AI as he had expected because Remento is using the technology to provide its users with something deeply personal, he added.

“Investors want to invest in AI, because it’s an exciting place to be. But I think what we’re experiencing is people have problems and they want them solved, and they want consumer technology to be able to solve those problems,” he said.

Kindred Tales, a rival platform cofounded by Nick Hern and his wife Rebecca Hern, has seen a similar trend with their customers. The company has both AI and non-AI versions of its product.

While the non-AI version requires users to type out their responses to weekly prompts, the AI version helps by providing speech-to-text transcription and some light editing. It can also write a first draft of the interviewee’s story, if they want.

Before 2025, just over half of the company’s customers chose the AI version, but that number jumped to 80% after 2025.

“Customers increasingly see AI as a meaningful part of the storytelling experience, not just an extra,” Nick Hern told Fortune.

Part of the reason is that the company’s AI chatbot, called “Ali,” gives the interview process a conversational feel by teasing more out of users with follow-up questions crafted to their responses.

AI has helped Kern, who also works full time for Thomson Reuters, run the company as well. He said he has used the technology to help filter emails, as well as slowly replace freelancers that he previously hired to help him craft marketing and social content.

At a time when companies are spending billions to bring AI to every American, Greene, said, despite its flaws, AI has allowed Remento’s customers’, as well as himself, to record moments with family members that are invaluable.

“AI in our company is being used to get people to do things that they’ve always wanted to do but haven’t been able to, and to make that experience as seamless and humanlike as possible,” he said.
