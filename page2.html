Ford Has A Dedicated Battery Abuse Team. It Obsesses Over How Cells Fail

Electric vehicle batteries live a harder life than the ones in your phone or laptop. They spend most of their time outdoors, deal with extreme heat and cold, and can take a beating from road debris and potholes.

That means for battery makers, engineering for durability is a priority right up there with energy density and charging speed. And to make sure its cells can handle the abuse of daily life as an EV, Ford has a team dedicated to understanding how batteries fail—and how to stop it.

“That team is 100% focused on battery abuse, design, and testing as a function,” Wesley Thibault, the global chief engineer of battery product development at Ford said last week during a media roundtable at the automaker’s new battery plant in Marshall, Michigan.

Ford is new to the EV battery business. Last week, it started cranking out lithium iron phosphate (LFP) battery packs at BlueOval Battery Park Michigan, using technology licensed from China’s CATL, the world’s largest battery maker. The batteries will power the all-important Ford Fathom pickup, which the automaker says will enter production toward the end of next year and kick off a new generation of vastly better and more profitable EVs.

Despite the licensing deal, Ford officials said they were involved in developing the batteries from the outset, with a focus on durability.

The job is not as simple as it sounds. The battery abuse team isn’t tasked with destroying fully made cells and then finding ways to make them indestructible. Instead, Thibault said, it is involved in the design process from the start.

“They want to understand down to the physics level, how a cell fails and why, and when it does fail, how we can prevent it from creating harm to the occupants,” he said.

Quality operator at Ford's BlueOval Battery Park Michigan.

For example, the team measures how cells vent, a crucial process that keeps batteries from catching fire or damaging themselves when they overheat. When temperatures inside a cell get too high, the electrolyte, the liquid chemical that shuttles electrons between the anode and cathode, gets hot and releases gases.

If those gases stay trapped, they can cause short circuits or thermal runaway, so they need to escape in a controlled way. Thibault said his team can measure all of this analytically—using computer models—before the cells are validated.

Production adds another layer of scrutiny. At various stages, the materials inside the cell, as well as the finished cell itself, go through thousands of quality checks, Ford engineers said. Those include CT scans, X-ray machines, lasers, camera inspections, and AI systems.

For example, when the anode slurry is coated onto a copper foil, every millimeter is scanned by lasers for wrinkles and other anomalies. All employees have the ability to pause production if they spot any problems.In case of any issues, they quarantine the cells affected during a specific time period. Ford also claimed that impacted components can be traced all the way from production to the vehicle they end up in.

To be sure, not all of this is unique to Ford. Strict quality checks like these are common among major battery makers, who all aim to minimize scrap rates for costly batteries and maximize the amount of material that actually gets sold.

Ford engineer operating a winding machine, which brings together the anode and cathode, adding a separator in between.

Ford continuously tests the manufacturing process in other ways, too. Engineers said they intentionally feed damaged components and cells—which they call “red rabbits”—into the production machinery to confirm the quality systems catch them and are working as intended.

When cells are almost ready, they’re blasted with helium gas to check for leaks. Once a pack is assembled, it’s mounted on a hydraulic shaker that mimics thousands of miles and years of real-world road use. The shaker can twist, dive, squat, contort, yaw, and replicate the other movements of a car, putting the pack through everything it would see on the road.

Ford’s EV batteries have fared well, by and large. High-mileage Mach-Es with 100,000 or even 200,000 miles on the odometer show little range loss compared to their original capacity, according to data the company released this month.

On the other hand, Ford’s emphasis on battery quality isn’t exactly surprising. The company has grappled with high warranty costs in recent years. And both the Mustang Mach-E and the F-150 Lightning have had issues linked to their high-voltage batteries. Some early Mach-Es were recalled because their battery contactors would overheat during DC fast-charging. A small number of Lightnings were recalled in 2023 due to defective battery cells.

Ford will want to avoid any similar quality issues as it rolls out the high-stakes Fathom, which it hopes will outsell its previous EVs and set the stage for an electric comeback. It will be years before we know how the Fathom's batteries hold up in the real world, but if the testing so far is any indication, the early signs look promising.

Justin Jefferson’s Latest Tunnel-Walk Fit Is an Homage to Mexican Fashion

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Ever since the NFL season kicked off back in September, the Minnesota Vikings’ star receiver Justin Jefferson has been going full-tilt. Last night, his team squared off against the San Fransisco 49ers in Mexico City (the NFL has been scheduling more away-gamers outside the U.S. to try to expand their viewership) in one of the marquee matchups of the season, and while the 49ers ended up winning that one, Jefferson remains inspired by the Vikings’ 7-3 record so far. “I’ve been great,” Jefferson says. “Everything’s been going really well, and I’m feeling good.”

Preparing for such a big game is no small feat, of course—even if a pro like Jefferson makes it look easy. Ahead of hitting the field, you can often find him getting ready both physically and mentally. “Preparation is everything: I want to make sure I'm confident, focused, and ready to perform at my best,” he says. How did he prepare for Mexico City? “A big part has been making sure I'm physically and mentally ready for the challenges of playing at a higher altitude,” Jefferson says. (Mexico City is about 1.4 miles above sea level—much more intense than even Denver’s Mile High Stadium.) “I've been using the altitude chamber to help my body acclimate. Mentally, it’s about studying the plays, understanding the different schemes, and making sure we're approaching the game with the right mindset.”

A more unexpected way Jefferson gets game-day ready, however: With his intentional tunnel-walk outfits. Yesterday, before the athletes even hit the field, Jefferson wanted to rock a meaningful look that would put him in the right mindset for his Mexico City matchup—so he enlisted one of the city’s most well-known designers, Patricio Campillo, to craft a custom look for him. “I've always tried to make my tunnel looks reflect where I am in the world, especially when we're playing internationally,” says Jefferson. “For me, fashion is an opportunity to connect with different cultures and showcase designers who bring something unique to the table.”

A very cool moment for me: Meet the designer—Patricio Campillo—behind the look.

Wanting to rep a design that represented the local culture, Jefferson called up Campillo to start ideating his custom look. He was drawn to the designer for his unique craftsmanship, creativity, and attention to detail. “Choosing Campillo felt like a natural decision—I was genuinely impressed,” says Jefferson. “You can tell how much time, passion, and hard work goes into creating these looks. Being able to represent that craftsmanship on an international stage is something I think is really special.”

Together, they landed on doing a cool, modern take on tailoring. Campillo created a cropped, boxy-style jacket with brown leather trousers—a signature element of the label that the designer wanted to introduce to a whole new audience. “It’s exciting to see someone of Justin’s caliber embrace Campillo,” he says. “I’ve always believed that fashion has the power to bring together people from different backgrounds, so seeing an athlete like Justin connect with our work is especially meaningful.”

He accessorized with pieces such as a horsehair bag, Oakley shades, and his signature chains. When the wide receiver put on the final look for the first ime, he was immediately drawn to the finer details. “One of my favorite details was the horsehair incorporated into the bag,” says Jefferson. “It’s such a unique touch and gives the piece a lot of character.” He also appreciated the hand-craftsmanship. “Knowing how much time and effort goes into creating each piece makes you appreciate the look even more,” he says. “I love wearing things that have a story behind them, and this outfit definitely does.”

The fit, custom made by Patricio Campillo.

It’s not the first time that Jefferson has flexed a fashion statement for his tunnel walk. During past games, he has worn everything from Off-White to Louis Vuitton and Chrome Hearts. What he wears and experiments, of course, depends on the setting. “My tunnel looks are really an extension of my personality, and how I’m feeling that day,” he says. “There are a few things I consider when putting a look together: my mood, who we’re playing, and the brands or designers I want to showcase. Fashion has become another creative outlet for me, and I enjoy stepping outside of what people might expect.”

This fluid approach to dressing applies to his off-duty wardrobe, too. Jefferson says he enjoys playing around with fashion even on his days off. “Right now, I'd describe my personal style as evolving,” he says. “I'm really focused on experimenting with different looks and exploring styles I might not have considered earlier in my career. I like stepping out of my comfort zone and being open to trying new things. Fashion is all about self-expression, and I think the more willing you are to take risks, the more you learn about yourself and what you like.”

What does this mean for the rest of Jefferson’s NFL season? You can expect tons more tunnel-walk outfits, for starters. “Every week is another opportunity to bring something exciting to the tunnel,” he says. “We love finding ways to surprise people, turn heads, and keep things interesting.” Start taking your bets—fashion bets, not sporting bets—on what runway outfit or custom creation he will don next. “We’ve already had a few outfits this season that have gotten a lot of attention, and that’s always fun to see,” he says. “But for me, it’s not just about getting people talking—it’s about continuing to discover new brands, experimenting with different styles, and finding creative ways to express myself through fashion.”

Pottery Barn Jake Leather Sofa Review, Tested by AD (2026)

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

I’ve wanted a leather sofa for many years, but I could never find a color I liked—a detail just as important to me as the material. I wasn’t interested in the usual black, brown, or cognac options, instead preferring something less traditional but not squarely trendy. And then I discovered Pottery Barn’s Jake Leather Sofa in Vintage Hunter, which hit my sofa sweet spot, exuding a deep confidence that reads as rich, grounded, and classy. Its sophisticated presence anchors my living room without overpowering the space.

If you don’t love the stately green hue as much as I do, that hardly means the Jake isn’t your room’s match. The sofa’s customization options—another major selling point—are robust: In addition to being able to choose from a broad range of leathers and upholstery colors, I could also select the style of the seat and back cushions, adding up to a piece of furniture that feels more personal than an off-the-sales-floor purchase. With its slim arms, low profile, plush cushions, and bronze-finished metal legs, it strikes a balance between streamlined and inviting.

How much does it cost? Are there payment plans available?

The cost of the Jake depends on the size, material, and cushion configuration you choose, falling somewhere between $2,000 and $6,000 for most designs. To save yourself from shopping-cart sticker shock, price out the exact model you want, since the leather grade and sofa width can meaningfully change the total. For instance, my 86-inch-wide, 36-inch-deep model in Vintage Hunter retails for $3,999, but a 70-inch-wide, 36-inch-deep loveseat in a Chocolate Performance fabric costs $3,099, and is sometimes on sale for less.

What are the upholstery or customization options? Did you choose any?

There’s little you can’t choose with the Jake. Available in an extensive selection of leather colors and finishes, you can also pick the number of back cushions you’d like (two or three) and style of seat cushion (two separate or a single bench). For my vintage leather sofa in Hunter, I decided on one bench-style seat cushion and three back cushions.

What is the lead time for customization, manufacturing, and delivery?

It took about six weeks for my sofa to arrive at my home after I ordered it, which felt reasonable for a piece with customized upholstery and cushion selections.

What kind of couch or seating arrangement did you have before, and what drew you to your new couch?

My previous sofa was a smaller turquoise felt sleeper from Mopio that I bought on Amazon; its blue undertones made it feel brighter and more casual. I loved the feel, but dog hair got caught in it easily and was hard to vacuum. This Hunter shade is deeper, moodier, and much closer to the sophisticated design I had envisioned for the room.

What was the delivery process like? What was the assembly process like? How long did it take, and how many people did it require?

Receiving the sofa could not have been easier, thanks to Pottery Barn’s included white-glove delivery service. Two people brought the sofa into my home, placed it where I wanted it, and assembled it on the spot. The entire process took about 10 minutes. For a substantial piece of furniture like this sofa, the luxury delivery experience was a major advantage.

What are the dimensions? How does it fit in your space? Would it be better suited for a certain type of home?

I chose the 85-inch-wide version, which measures 36 inches deep and 32 inches high, and it fits my apartment almost perfectly. It feels generous enough to function as the room’s main seating option without commandeering the full focus of the room. I would recommend this size for a midsize living area, provided that the access points can accommodate its width. As always, measuring the doorway, elevator, stairwell, and turning radius before you click “buy” is just as crucial as measuring the piece’s final spot.

Sit test: Is it comfortable? Does it feel well-made? Does the couch have a tendency to sag?

Comfort is where the Jake has exceeded my expectations. It feels supportive and substantial, and the cushions have held their shape without developing an obvious sag. In the four to five months I’ve had the Jake, several visitors have already stayed over and shared that they’ve experienced restful nights on this couch.

The only recurring quirk I’ve experienced is that the seat cushion occasionally slides forward a smidge. It is easy to nudge back into place and has never become a serious annoyance. It’s also entirely possible that this funky detail has more to do with my sitting tendencies than the sofa’s construction.

Is the sofa suitable for particular decor styles and if so, which?

The Jake naturally fits with modern and contemporary interiors. Its low, linear profile, narrow arms, and metal legs provide a clean architectural quality, while the Hunter leather adds depth and character. However, with the right design elements in place, the sofa would also look at home in decidedly relaxed spaces, with warm woods, woven textiles, vintage pieces, or more traditional accessories.

Is it durable and practical in a modern home?

Despite its sophisticated appearance, this sofa isn’t your grandfather’s precious showpiece that exists for looking at and not sitting on. Rather, I’ve found it to be durable, practical, and comfortable to actually use daily.

But that’s just for humans; pets are the bigger consideration here. I have a dog, and a few scratches have already appeared on the leather. My current remediation system is to cover the sofa with a sheet when we are home and remove it before guests arrive. It works, but anyone hoping for completely carefree pet ownership should be realistic about the care needs of a leather sofa before purchasing this polished piece.

The Jake might fare better with children than with a dog, since typical spills are often easy to address quickly, while claw marks are harder to reverse (if possible to do at all). I am planning to try a leather conditioner to soften the appearance of the scratches, though I have not tested it yet. Before applying any product, I would follow Pottery Barn’s care instructions to spot-test it in an inconspicuous area first. Ultimately, the Jake is probably better suited to a pet-free home, or to any owner willing to go whatever lengths necessary to protect and maintain the leather.

Do the photos do this product justice? Was there anything you were surprised to see IRL?

The sofa looks remarkably similar in person to how it appears online, including the depth of the Hunter color and the overall proportions. There was no unpleasant reveal on delivery day; the sofa I had spent weeks picturing was essentially the sofa that arrived.

Would the couch be easy to move into another room? Would it be easy to move to another apartment or home by yourself?

While it’s more manageable to move than other full-size leather sofas may be, largely in light of its removable legs and loose cushions, I wouldn’t call the Jake a portable sofa. It’s not modular, but rather comes in a single piece, so moving it to another room or home is certainly not a single-person job. However, assuming the doorways and corners cooperate, rehoming its 85-inch span should be possible, so long as you have help on hand.

Industrial Serial Gateways Broadcast That They're Insecure

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Critical OT access devices will often tell you, and anyone else, whether they're protected by authentication — and many aren't.

Internet scans indicate that some substantial fraction of serial device servers deployed at operational technology (OT) sites worldwide not only lack authentication but broadcast the fact publicly.

We all have a friend or a family member who's deeply insecure, and it's obvious as soon as they start talking. The same can be said of serial-to-IP gateways. Recently Adrian Cheek, senior cybercrime researcher for Flare, probed the wider Internet for these sorts of devices, finding that a substantial chunk of them are discoverable. Worse, they often responded to straightforward requests by saying whether they were or were not protected by authentication — and many were not.

An insecure serial-to-IP gateway is a serious risk to anyone downstream of it. These devices are responsible for translating information between the Internet and industrial machinery. So in a cyberattack, a reachable and unauthenticated one can inadvertently translate an Internet-based threat into one that affects major, safety-critical machines.

Related:How to Assess OT Incident Response Preparedness

Using a specific vendor's UDP discovery service, rather than probing all vendors' devices, Cheek found 2,474 serial-to-IP gateways exposed on the Web. They seemed to map to real devices rather than honeypots, with half of them located in Russia.

For some reason, in response to the researcher's unsolicited probes, around half of the devices — 1,265 — proffered their authentication status. Of those that did, 973 reported that authentication was disabled. In other words, 39.3% of all Internet-exposed gateways visibly lacked authentication, and among those whose status could be gleaned, that figure was 77%.

"These devices ship with authentication off. An integrator sets one up on a bench, gets the meter or the drive or the Remote Terminal Unit (RTU) talking to the SCADA host, and moves on to the next site," Cheek laments. "The device then does one job for 10 or 15 years, and nobody logs into it again. In many cases, the operator doesn't even know it exists because it went in as part of someone else's equipment."

There are also understandable, if not entirely justifiable reasons why operators fail to conceal and lock down their serial device servers. Cheek cites a multitude of reasons, including that enabling such scurity features can break software on the host side.

"Older SCADA packages and virtual serial port drivers may not support the secured modes, so an operator who switches them on can lose the link to the field," he says. "Then someone has to drive to an unstaffed site to fix it. Firmware updates carry the same risk at a remote site with no spare on hand. People who have been burned by that once are slow to touch a working device again."

Related:Why Europe's Freezer Hacks Spread So Far, So Fast

Still, an exposed, unauthenticated serial gateway is a welcome mat for bad actors. Not only does it give arbitrary actors entry into sensitive OT systems and eliminates the need to exploit any kind of vulnerabilities, it also allows attackers to use the vendor's own supplied tools, which could blend malicious activity in with normal IT-OT traffic.

Cheek made special mention of the risk to mobile network operators and, even more specifically, private access point name (APN) service providers. Last year's Polish power grid attack used a private APN to pivot from a wind farm's network to the power plant where the attackers ultimately shut down a steam turbine, water treatment system, and seven serial-to-IP devices. According to Flare, 322 of the 2,474 discovered devices in the study lived in address space associated with known mobile network operators in Europe and the Middle East.

OT operators can begin to secure their OT access layer by first doing an inventory of the devices they're running. "For every cellular unit, find out what plan the SIM is on and whether it gets a public address. Then ask your carrier in writing whether devices on your private network can reach each other or devices belonging to other customers," Cheek notes. "Don't assume the managed product isolates them. In the Polish incident, that missing isolation is what let the attacker move between two unrelated organizations."

Related:Fresh European Fridge Attacks Put Cold-Chain Security in Focus

Next, he advises, "Lock down the data ports as well as the login. Most of these devices let you restrict which IP addresses can open a connection. Configure them so only the SCADA host can reach the serial ports and only an engineering workstation can reach the management interface. Switch off the services you don't use, which on most units means the discovery service, Telnet, older SNMP, and often the Web interface. Remove SSH and Telnet entirely from anything that faces the carrier network," and plop a strict firewall between the gateway and control network.

There are also general practices organizations can put in place, like ensuring that the next time they purchase serial device servers, a person or team is assigned to be responsible for the devices, and that they have necessary credentials, configuration, and network details from system integrators.

At the end of the day, though, nothing compares to preventing an attacker's ability to find and speak to a device in the first place.

"Even a fully patched gateway with authentication on is usually connected to equipment on the serial side that has no security of its own," Cheek points out. "A meter, a drive, or an RTU will accept any correctly formed command that reaches it. So the useful question is who can reach the gateway at all, because whoever can reach it gets whatever is behind it. Authentication and patching both matter, but restricting what can reach the gateway does more good than either."

Supertake raises $20M to let AI agents turn your market predictions into stock portfolios

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AI investing startup Supertake has an interesting premise: What if your “takes” on the market could make you money without you having to pick stocks yourself? The service lets people turn their ideas into real investment portfolios, using frontier AI models and trading agents.

Now, the company has raised $20 million in seed funding to grow its business. The round was led by venture firm Adjacent (Nico Wittenborn), with participation from Coinbase Ventures and Union Square Ventures (USV). Numerous angel investors also joined, including Nat Friedman, who’s currently head of product at Meta Superintelligence Labs; Vercel CEO Guillermo Rauch; renowned product manager Nikita Bier; investor Scott Belsky; and Nemil Dalal, a former Coinbase exec who is now a visiting partner at Y Combinator.

Michael Mignano originally incubated the startup at USV. He is the co-founder and former CEO of the podcasting platform Anchor, which Spotify acquired in 2019 for over $150 million. After leading Spotify’s podcasting business as “Head of Talk” for a few years, he joined the team at Lightspeed as a venture partner, betting on AI companies like Suno and Granola. He also co-founded Oboe, an AI education startup, and joined USV as a general partner in April.

“We really believed there was an opportunity to make investing more democratized…and give everyone and anyone the ability to invest in their own insights and beliefs and ideas,” Mignano said. While he will continue in his role as founder and executive chairman, he’s now brought in a team to run the company day to day.

The team includes co-founders Lincoln Murr (CEO) and John Peterson (CTO). Both joined from Coinbase, where, Mignano notes they were the heads of AI product and engineering, leading launches like Coinbase advisor, Coinbase for agents, agentic wallets, and others.

The startup arrives as AI continues to make inroads into financial services, but allowing AI to take over people’s actual investments requires a new level of trust. Supertake is betting that people will ultimately be willing to take that leap, given recent improvements in frontier AI models.

Currently, Supertake works with Coinbase and Robinhood, and the company intends to expand to more brokerages over time. The idea is that you can explain, in broad, natural language, what you believe about how the world is going to change, and Supertake’s AI will iterate with you back and forth to turn that idea into a “take” that’s investable.

Under the hood, the startup is leveraging frontier AI technology and tools, combined with its own proprietary software that directs and limits what the AI can do.

Supertake’s users’ ideas are tracked on a leaderboard on the site, so others who agree can invest alongside them. For instance, there’s currently an idea on the leaderboard about looksmaxxing and longevity becoming more of a trend, where the portfolio includes certain pharma and cosmetic companies that could potentially benefit.

For now, these portfolios consist of stock picks, but the team wants to expand the service over time to handle other types of transactions, like shorts, derivatives, perpetuals, long exposure, or even prediction markets — the latter handled through partnerships with companies in that space.

Despite these “takes” seeming like bets on what could happen next in a given market, Murr stresses that this doesn’t make Supertake itself a prediction market.

“We think it’s a novel and very much improved way to create what was previously considered the robo-advisor experience,” he says. “When that came out, it had this amazing vision and direction of allowing people to have digital asset management. We think now is actually the time for that, with AI agents being so widely proliferated.”

“I don’t think this is necessarily a prediction market by any means. The idea of natural language translation into an underlying set of assets feels different in that regard,” he says.

Still, the startup could wade into short-term predictions if a user’s take involves something they believe could happen in the near future, like who might win an election.

“We’re primarily focused on the longer, medium-term holdings today. But that being said, we don’t want to preclude people from building out very hyper short-term strategies that might exist,” Murr adds.

To address people’s concerns over AI managing their money, Supertake’s team says it’s implementing certain guardrails and spend limits. For instance, the AI has no ability to deposit or withdraw funds on a user’s behalf without their explicit consent. There are also drift limits in place so the portfolio stays balanced, plus allow and deny lists (meaning which assets the AI may and may not trade), real-time monitoring for anomalous activity, tools to set trade size limits, and a restriction that keeps agents from seeing the user’s brokerage credentials.

“We view it as somewhat analogous to self-driving cars, actually, where there is a spectrum here of both trust and technological innovations that are happening,” says Murr. “Where a few years ago you may not feel comfortable getting in a car that has lane assist, but today you get in a Waymo, and it’s like nothing. And we think that there’s a very similar progression happening with the agentic finance space, and we’re far further along in the technological spectrum than people think.”

Access to Supertake is still behind a waitlist while in beta, but the founders said they’ve put their money where their mouths are. Murr and Peterson are letting it manage “tens of thousands” and “thousands” of dollars, respectively.

Mignano, meanwhile, says he has Supertake managing “hundreds of thousands” of dollars.

With the new funding, which follows a $2 million pre-seed round, Supertake aims to expand its team in areas like engineering, design, growth marketing, and others. Mignano and Murr are in New York, while Peterson is in the Austin area but commutes to New York at times. The team says it hopes to figure out a hybrid work model as it grows.

‘The Gilded Age’ Just Used a TV Trope We Really Need to Retire

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Spoilers for The Gilded Age season 4 ahead.

Oh, Marian. Marian, Marian, Marian. We finally got to see her letting loose, and then it all came crashing down. Hard. As you may have seen coming from a mile away, on Sunday night’s fourth episode of The Gilded Age season 4, Marian realized that she was pregnant (at least she assumes she is). After a one-night stand. I think I’ve seen this show before—and it was created and written by the same person.

The season 4 premiere shows Marian Brook going out on the town with her ex-fiancé Larry Russell following Enid and Oscar’s wedding. They have a great time dancing, and then they have a great time at a hotel after bad weather means they can’t make the trip back uptown. Marian literally lets her Victorian-era hair down and loses her virginity to the man she loves—or at least, loved.

The aftermath is not fun, though. Marian thinks of herself as a ruined woman for having premarital sex, and Larry offers to get re-engaged to her—a fine idea considering they both still have feelings for each other—but she sees it as a pity re-proposal and refuses him. A few episodes later, she’s having nosebleeds all over the place and admitting to a colleague at the settlement house that she hasn’t had her “monthly courses.” (We’ll get to more on why I don’t like this storyline, but I will give the show a shoutout for using the underrepresented pregnancy symptom of nosebleeds rather than the classic barfing everywhere.)

This could have been different. Marian, a character who has had happiness snatched out of her hands time and time again, could have just stubbornly continued to turn down Larry and devoted herself to her work, only to realize by the season finale that she actually is still in love with him. I wouldn’t have minded a good old slow burn here! Much of this show moves so fast. Marian and Larry’s romance could’ve kept things more grounded.

But, instead, we get the classic TV trope of “woman gets pregnant after having sex with someone once.” And this particular situation has the added bonus of “and she was a virgin.” Shows as wide-ranging as Friends, Mad Men, The Secret Life of the American Teenager, Reign, Ginny & Georgia, and The Originals have featured one-night stands that lead to pregnancies to varying levels of success. The trope is supposed to make us gasp, but at this point, it’s just kind of cheap and predictable.

Most notably, this already happened on Downton Abbey, which, like The Gilded Age, was written and created by Julian Fellowes. The character Edith—who is very similar to Marian in that she repeatedly gets into doomed relationships with men and devotes herself to work at a time when that’s not the norm for aristocratic women—gets pregnant after sleeping with her love interest, Michael Gregson, one time. As with The Gilded Age, given the time period of the show, this was not happy news and was just another blow to a character who kept getting the short end of the stick. (Speaking of the short end of the stick, Michael Gregson goes out of the country following their lovemaking and dies while away, so Edith never even gets to see him again!)

The whole “Poor Edith” thing became something of a running joke for Downton fans and on the actual show, which was entertaining, but seeing something so similar happen again on The Gilded Age just makes me wish this had gone in a more creative direction. Maybe Marian could’ve kept hooking up with Larry despite the side of herself that knew it was a bad idea. Maybe Marian could’ve sworn off of men altogether. (Honestly, it’d make sense at this point! I mean, damn, that’s three failed engagements!) Anything but pregnancy.

So, where does Poor Marian go from here? My guess, it’ll either be: 1. She isn’t actually pregnant, but the stress from her guilt caused the nosebleeds and missed “monthly courses.” 2. She has the baby and secretly puts it up for adoption a la Edith from Downton. 3. She gets back with Larry, has the baby, and Bertha does some of her conniving to get society to not ask questions about the timeline. 4. She has a miscarriage.

Are any of these options that great? Is this going to be worth it? A miscarriage would mean it was just for the shock (or, “shock”) of the episode 4 cliffhanger. A baby with Larry would be forcing them together more than the guilt of the one-night stand.

Despite my critique of this particular storyline, I love this show, so I am interested to see how it turns out. The creativity might not have been there when it came to using this trope again, but hopefully, it’ll be there in the outcome.

The Billion-Dollar Companies Behind Half Of The World's Commercial Aircraft

While household names such as United Airlines and Lufthansa dominate the skies in terms of fleet count, there are many companies in existence that have similarly sized fleets that do not carry passengers or cargo, nor do they operate charter missions. These companies never sell a ticket, operate a route, or pursue significant branding campaigns.

Some of these firms control fleets worth tens of billions of dollars, and one owns more aircraft than many of the airlines the public recognizes. The airline industry is composed primarily of two main financial systems: ownership and lease.

Many companies such as Delta Air Lines are known for owning a high percentage of their fleet, notably their older Boeing 717 or Boeing 767 fleets. However, the majority of airlines lease a portion of their jets, as many aircraft with long order backlogs at the manufacturer itself can be hard to obtain. Leasing is a common way to bypass delivery delays.

Most people assume airlines buy the aircraft they fly. Increasingly, that is not the case. According to the International Air Transport Association (IATA), leasing now accounts for more than half of the world's commercial fleet, making aircraft leasing one of the most important yet least visible sectors in aviation.

Rather than purchasing aircraft outright, many airlines rent them under long-term agreements from specialized leasing companies. The scale of those firms is difficult to appreciate until their fleets are examined. AerCap, the world's largest aircraft lessor, reported owning 1,501 aircraft and managing another 148 at the end of 2025, with an additional 283 aircraft on order.

Those figures make AerCap a larger aircraft owner than many of the world's biggest airlines. Meanwhile, competitors such as SMBC Aviation Capital, Avolon, BOC Aviation, and Air Lease Corporation collectively control thousands more aircraft worth hundreds of billions of dollars.

However, ownership and operation are not the same thing. If airlines do not own many of the aircraft they fly, why would they willingly hand that responsibility to someone else? While there are many sides to the discussion, the answer relates to the complexities of operating an airplane to begin with, which is far more involved than car or boat ownership, for example.

A new Boeing 787-9 carries a list price exceeding $300 million, while even narrowbody aircraft represent investments worth tens of millions of dollars each. Purchasing hundreds of aircraft outright would require enormous amounts of capital that airlines often prefer to dedicate elsewhere. Dry leasing is the most common and most cost-effective way to accomplish this cost benefit.

Aircraft leasing allows airlines to expand fleets without making the full upfront purchase themselves. Instead, leasing firms acquire aircraft from manufacturers such as Boeing and Airbus and lease them to airlines over periods that frequently range from six to twelve years. Airlines gain access to modern aircraft while preserving cash and reducing financial risk.

While having direct operating authority of 1,066 aircraft, United Airlines' fleet also includes a subsection of leased aircraft, according to the SEC. This figure comprises 958 owned and 108 leased, at the end of 2025. This number is significantly less than AerCap's total number of active leases, which, on paper, causes AerCap to resemble a major international airline.

In practice, passengers rarely notice that a different company owns the aircraft they are flying on. Under a dry lease, the lessor provides the aircraft while the airline supplies its own pilots, maintenance program, insurance, and operating certificates. To passengers, the aircraft appears no different from any other jet in the airline's fleet.

One recent example comes from Avolon's reported October 2025 sale-and-leaseback agreement with TAAG Angola Airlines involving 787-9 aircraft. In that arrangement, the aircraft are operated by TAAG, but ownership remains with the leasing company. Similar transactions occur throughout the industry and frequently involve new aircraft deliveries from Boeing and Airbus. An aircraft may spend its entire operational life flying for airlines that never technically own it.

A wet lease is an entirely different form of leasing, provided by companies like Heston Airlines, Hi Fly, or Wamos Air. The aircraft is frequently in the livery of the lessor, although not always. Further, the in-flight service, cabin crew, and standards are all those of the lessor company. Wet leases generally have a higher cost than dry leases, and are generally shorter in term.

The influence of lessors extends far beyond simple ownership. Because companies such as AerCap, Avolon, and SMBC Aviation Capital place enormous aircraft orders, they have become some of Boeing's and Airbus' most important customers. Lessors routinely order aircraft years before identifying the airlines that will eventually operate them.

This purchasing power became especially significant during recent supply chain constraints. CAPA reports that aircraft shortages and production delays have strengthened lessors' market positions by increasing aircraft values and lease rates. Airlines seeking growth often cannot wait years for new direct deliveries, making leased aircraft one of the few available solutions.

The result is that lessors frequently influence what aircraft manufacturers build, how quickly airlines can expand, and even which carriers gain access to modern fleets. A major leasing company may place an order measured in hundreds of aircraft, a scale that rivals the purchasing power of many national airlines.

Orders placed by airlines themselves to manufacturers such as Boeing or Airbus are typically more noteworthy. They represent a larger upfront financial commitment and a known destination for production aircraft. Orders placed by lessors are less noteworthy, but still significant from an operational standpoint.

While the end user of the aircraft is not known when, for example, AerCap placed an order for 100 Airbus jets, such an order boosts the inventory of a lessor. This not only provides options for customers, but an entirely different, and at times, more accessible way of expanding a fleet.

Leasing an aircraft engine is expensive, especially in today's industry.

The growing importance of lessors is increasingly tied to aircraft scarcity. Airbus and Boeing continue to work through substantial backlogs measured in thousands of aircraft. For airlines needing capacity sooner rather than later, leasing companies often represent the fastest available path to fleet growth.

Leasing companies also offer smaller airlines and startup carriers alike a lower barrier to entry to operation than buying an aircraft outright from the producer. AerCap provides one of the clearest examples. The company website published an order book of 283 aircraft entering 2026 while simultaneously managing thousands of aviation assets, including engines and helicopters.

That position effectively allows it to act as a bridge between manufacturers producing aircraft and airlines seeking them. Manufacturers in the aviation industry have the greatest amount of market control for their product. However, some of that power can be relinquished when a customer also acts as a distributor. The relationship between the manufacturer and the lessor creates an unusual balance of power.

Manufacturers remain responsible for designing and producing aircraft, but the largest lessors have amassed order books so large that they have become some of Airbus' and Boeing's most important customers. Companies such as AerCap regularly place orders measured in the hundreds of aircraft, allowing them to negotiate from a position of strength while shaping fleet decisions across dozens of airlines simultaneously.

The future significance of aircraft lessors will likely depend on one simple metric: access to aircraft. Airlines can only grow as quickly as new aircraft become available, and both Airbus and Boeing continue to hold order backlogs extending years into the future. Major aircraft manufacturers, while experiencing high backlogs of orders and record customer interest, can lose control of the end use of their product when lessors place massive orders.

That reality places unusual attention on companies such as AerCap, Avolon, and SMBC Aviation Capital. Their order books, delivery positions, and financing capabilities may determine which airlines can launch new routes, replace aging fleets, or enter emerging markets first.

In an industry built around aircraft availability, the companies holding the keys to future deliveries may become as important as the carriers operating them. The next major test will come as manufacturers work through production constraints and thousands of aircraft currently sitting in order backlogs move toward delivery.

Whether airlines continue shifting toward leased fleets or return to greater ownership could depend on how effectively Boeing and Airbus satisfy demand over the remainder of the decade. For passengers, the aircraft parked at the gate will probably continue wearing airline colors. The more interesting question is whether the real power behind those fleets increasingly belongs to companies whose names never appear on the boarding pass.

Fed Chair Kevin Warsh Just Laid Out 3 Scenarios for "Super Intelligence." 2 of Them Are "Less Positive," Including 1 Scenario That Outright Disappoints

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The Federal Reserve has begun modeling the projected impacts of what the Trump administration calls "super intelligence" on the economy.

By Bram Berkowitz – Nov 23, 2026 at 5:00PM EST

As chair of the Federal Reserve's Board of Governors, Kevin Warsh has a powerful job. His words can move markets, and his view of the economy can play a big role in shaping the policy decisions that follow.

That's why his thinking on artificial intelligence, or what Warsh refers to as “super intelligence,” matters. Super intelligence (SI) is the term adopted by President Donald Trump and his administration to describe AI, and has become a driving force of both corporate spending and stock market gains. Whether it delivers on its promise could impact growth, jobs, and inflation for years. These are the very concerns the Fed is charged with managing, so it has to try to figure out what's coming before it arrives.

On Nov. 16, at a conference for the Fed's Productivity and Jobs Task Force hosted by the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, Warsh said the Fed is modeling three possible paths for the economy as AI continues to develop.

Two of these scenarios are "less positive," including one where artificial intelligence outright disappoints.

Image source: Official Federal Reserve Photo.

During his opening remarks, Warsh said Fed staff had built three illustrative scenarios over the next several years, stressing that they are not forecasts and that he was not there to offer predictions.

The first is the one that every investor hopes for. Productivity rises, and the gains are shared evenly between workers and those who manage and own capital. In this scenario, growth remains above its long-run average, and the economy remains near full employment.

The second scenario is interesting because it doesn't require artificial intelligence to fail. It requires it to work.

"An SI-driven expansion could bring substantial labor market disruption as some jobs are displaced, rather than complemented, by technology," Warsh said. "GDP growth would still rise above its long-term average. But widespread automation, combined with labor market frictions, could push the jobless rate above levels consistent with full employment."

The third is a letdown, where "SI could disappoint." After heavy investment and the strong market rally we've seen, returns on capital are lower than expected, a concern some investors already share. This leads to a decline in valuations, and the economy slows before eventually recovering without the productivity gains.

Again, these aren't predictions from Warsh, who has tried to avoid any kind of forward guidance since becoming chair. Warsh also noted, "many other outcomes -- probably including one that will prove most accurate -- could be drawn."

Still, if the Fed is beginning to model these three scenarios, it's likely at least somewhat on the minds of Warsh and the rest of the Fed's board. Interestingly, the first and third scenarios are easier to understand because they are more black-and-white. Either AI works, benefiting the economy, or it doesn't, and the economy and likely the stock market struggle.

But it's the second that I find most interesting, and likely the one most investors are not factoring in.

A successful AI boom that leaves workers behind is harder to see coming and likely harder to model. It's also one that could make it difficult for the Fed to manage its two goals of stable prices and maximum employment, because this scenario could push them in different directions.

If AI delivers on its promise, companies will use it to do more with fewer people. This would lift margins and productivity in the near term, which is exactly what bulls are counting on, and may already be happening this year.

But the people who lose their jobs or are forced to switch to lower-paying jobs are also consumers. Consumer spending accounts for more than two-thirds of the U.S. economy.

Fewer or lower paychecks, or less confidence in keeping them, means less spending power. Over time, that could hit the revenue of the same companies that saved money by cutting costs.

AI investment is already a major source of growth. Business equipment spending jumped nearly 21% in the third quarter, helping lift GDP growth to 3.4%, according to Reuters. But this doesn't replace the spending of millions of households.

Nobody knows what will happen. Everything may work out, but investors should remember that forecasts rarely pan out as expected. The best case for AI isn't necessarily the best case for the consumers who power the economy.

Long-term investors should remain invested, but it's important to consider these scenarios and make sure your portfolio is prepared.

When humans live on the moon, we'll need to grow food on the moon, too.

It's no secret that NASA is working towards a permanent presence on the moon. The space agency even plans to build a moon base for its astronauts to call home. But humans living inside lunar habitats around the clock will need three square meals a day, so researchers have been working on how to grow food on the moon.

To that end, a team from the College of Agricultural Sciences at Oregon State University have been raising potatoes in simulated moon dust with promising results. The researchers published their findings Nov. 20 in the Microgravity science journal.

"We found that potatoes can complete their entire life cycle in simulated lunar soil when supplemented with organic matter," Aymeric Goyer, associate professor at OSU's Department of Botany and Plant Pathology, said in a statement. "That's an important step toward developing reliable food production systems that could support long-term human exploration of the moon."

To grow the spuds, the scientists used five different materials that are similar to regolith, the dust and rocks that cover the moon's surface. The moon contains various regions with unique surface makeups, so the materials they used simulated regolith from both lunar mare and lunar highland regions.

The secret ingredient was vermicompost, a soil that's rich in nutrients. The team added different levels of vermicompost to the simulated regolith to grow the potatoes. They figured out that fake regolith mixed with 30 percent vermicompost worked about as well as growing potatoes with regular soil.

"We believe the compost helps balance some of the toxicity we see in lunar regolith by reducing plant exposure to harmful compounds," David Handy, a postdoctoral researcher in Goyer's lab, said in the same statement.

The idea is that astronauts in the future could bring a little nutrient-rich soil with them and mix it with regolith to grow potatoes. After all, it'd cost a ton to ship all that food from Earth.

"Ultimate Fantastic Four" also reimagined the FF's nemesis, Doctor Doom. "Ultimate Fantastic Four" #7 (by Warren Ellis and Stuart Immonen) depicts Victor's childhood. Victor von Doom acts like an aristocrat, but unlike Victor Van Damme, he came from nothing.

12 Things to Do on Christmas If You Don’t Celebrate

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

This series will help you pick the perfect presents, decor, and activities to make this Christmas the Best Christmas Ever.

In a way, the Christmas season is more fun if you don’t celebrate it. You get to enjoy all the brightly colored lights your neighbors put up, but you don’t have to put up your own. You don’t have to do any duty-shopping. There's no pressure. But Christmas Day itself leaves you in a mostly shuttered world that feels like there’s nothing going on. Fortunately, there are options to make Dec. 25 a fun day off, regardless of your observances.

You can’t go wrong with some moo shu pork and a trip to the multiplex. Chinese restaurants and movie theaters traditionally stay open on Christmas Day for those who don't celebrate, and they’re often filled with fellow Christmas refugees. Check Google for the hours of your local Chinese restaurant and give them a call to make a reservation. If you’re more adventurous and you’re in a city large enough to have a Chinatown (or a whatever-town), just head straight there on Christmas and see what happens—that’s how the best days usually start.

If Chinese food isn’t your thing, look at restaurants serving any non-European cuisine—Greek places, Japanese places, and Mediterranean places are usually open, as are plenty of Italian restaurants and steakhouses. Just make sure you check first. Lots of chain restaurants are open on Christmas Day too, including Applebee's, McDonald’s, Benihana, and more. You can even get Christmas breakfast at your local IHOP. But not every location of every outlet follows the same hours.

As for movies, most chain multiplex theaters are open on Dec. 25, and theaters are usually pretty full. The movies attract both people who don't celebrate Christmas and people who do, but want to get away from their families for a couple hours, so make sure and reserve a seat beforehand. Consider hitting up an out-of-the-way spot or that theater with the table service and cushy recliners to make it feel like more of an occasion.

Most people going to the movies on Christmas Day aren’t there for Christmas movies, so interesting “secular” movies are often slated for release on the 25th. This year's Christmas is unusually packed with interesting new movies, including family flick Jumani: Open World, a Robert Eggers horror movie called Werwulf, and the crowd pleasing football story Mr. Irrelevant, so you have serious options.

Chinese restaurants and movie theaters aren’t the only establishments open on Christmas. Here are some other options:

Go skiing or snowboarding: Most mountains are open on Christmas, and the lift lines are blissfully empty. There's no better way of celebrating a winter-based holiday than on a wide-open mountain.

Visit a skating rink: Speaking of winter sports, ice skating rinks are generally open on Christmas too.

Pretend to be a tourist: Tourist attractions are often open on Christmas, especially in larger cities. This includes places like Madame Tussaud’s and Ripley’s Believe It or Not, as well as ziplines and Segway tours—so do that touristy thing you’ve never done before.

Get a room: If you’ve got some spare cash and have nothing better to do, get a room at a hotel. Hotels don’t usually close on Christmas, and they typically offer plenty of amenities, so take advantage of the pool at the Holiday Inn or the spa at the Four Seasons, depending on your lifestyle.

Hiking and biking: Hiking trails and parks are open on Christmas if you want to get outdoors. If you’re into biking, hit the trails or hit the streets: Traffic is minimal on Christmas, so it’s a great time to put in some serious roadwork.

Volunteer: Not having anything to do on Christmas isn’t the biggest life problem, especially compared to people who have nowhere to go and nothing to eat. So use Christmas to help people and actually deliver on that whole “good will towards men” thing. Check your local Craigslist for volunteering opportunities.

Use Christmas as a catch-up day: This is strictly for type-A people, but if everyone is out of town and no one is going to call you, why not spend the day catching up on neglected chores or personal projects? You can spend at least part of your day knocking a few things off your to-do list and the rest of the time relaxing. That way you’ll get a break and still feel accomplished.

Just sleep all day: Type-B personalities like me see a day free of obligations and other people as a golden opportunity to do as little as is humanly possible, a day to fully commit to sloth.

Spend time with friends and family at home: Not celebrating Christmas doesn’t mean you can’t spend the day with family and/or friends. Get together with your loved ones, play games, watch a movie, cook together, talk, or whatever else you all enjoy. You don’t have to go out to have fun, so don’t rule out staying in with people you care about.

Shopping: This is at the bottom of the list because it’s iffy. Drug stores and convenience stores like 711 are usually open on the 25th. Grocery chains like Kroger’s and Trader Joe’s usually aren’t. Your local shopping mall or retail district might be open, but probably won't be, so check ahead.

Because so many places are closed on Christmas Day, you should plan ahead (unless you like spontaneity and are OK with the possibility of disappointment) by:

Making a list of the places you want to go.

Calling nearby locations to confirm they’ll be open, as not every chain follows corporate holiday hour recommendations.

Making reservations at any restaurant(s) you plan to visit.

Putting together a basic timeline of the day, accounting for travel time, so you don’t lose any reservations, miss a movie, or arrive after the store closes.

The most important advice here is to do something intentional on Christmas, even if it's sleeping in, so the day doesn't feel depressing or empty. So hit the slopes, suck down some dim sum, or keep your couch from floating away, but be sure you make Christmas your own.

SINGAPORE, Nov. 23, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Only 20% of employees in Asia Pacific have strong strategic clarity about AI, according to a new report from Boston Consulting Group (BCG). Employees with strong strategic clarity know that AI is a priority, understand the strategy and their role in it, and have clear guidance on what to do with the time AI saves. Communication also shapes trust. Where employees say leadership communicates very clearly about AI, 61% fully trust leadership, compared with 3% where communication is not clear.

Respondents in Australia, India, and Japan were surveyed for the global AI at Work 2026 study of 11,749 employees, published in June, and the rest in September and October.

AI use is now near-universal across Asia Pacific. Eighty-five percent of employees use it regularly, up from 78% in 2025 and in line with 84% globally. Japan saw the sharpest increase over the past year, from 51% to 79%, while India leads the region at 96%.

Far fewer employees use AI deeply. Only 26% use it regularly and for at least half of their daily tasks. Those employees save an average of 14.3 hours a week, compared with 6.2 hours for people who use AI less than a few times a week.

Many companies are going beyond deploying AI tools. The report also finds a "joy paradox," with work becoming both more demanding and more enjoyable. Among employees with strong strategic clarity, 76% enjoy their work more and 47% report a higher cognitive load, versus 30% and 21% with no clarity. Only 7% of them are anxious about AI, against 19% without clarity.

"Each market in Asia Pacific needs its own playbook, but three priorities hold everywhere," said Romain de Laubier, Asia-Pacific regional chair at BCG X and a coauthor of the report. "Make strategic clarity the number one priority, measure the value AI creates rather than how much it is used, and put the guardrails in place to use it responsibly."

Van Cleef &amp; Arpels Explores the Secrets and Mysteries of Ancient Egypt

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The maison’s latest high jewelry collection taps into the mystique of the Valley of the Kings

After archaeologist Howard Carter and his team discovered King Tutankhamun’s tomb in 1922, regal diadems, golden collars, and bold cuffs brought a new wave of Egyptian Revival into the Art Deco movement. Van Cleef & Arpels took note and added Egyptian-inspired calligraphy, hieroglyphs, and semiprecious stones into its lexicon of deco bracelets, sautoirs, and brooches, bringing a historic feel to the frostbitten glamour that defined the roaring ’20s. Now, over a century later, the maison’s new Fascinating Egypt high jewelry collection explores more avenues of the region’s mystical splendor.

From opulent collars to sculptural rings, each design harkens back to the arts, values, and beliefs that defined the New Kingdom. But just as Egyptologists explored hidden messages beneath the grandeur of the 18th dynasty, Van Cleef conveys the mystique beneath its history. Shape-shifting necklaces function like secret passageways to new realms of expression, and Mysterioux brooches reincarnate sacred deities in gold and gemstones. Just take the Bennou, a terrestrial vessel for the sun god Ra, whose feathers of emeralds, marine-blue sapphires, lapis, cerulean slips of turquoise, mother of pearl, and drops of diamonds represent the colors of the earth. A blood-orange spessartite garnet with rays of diamonds hangs from a red spinel, like a drop of sunshine.

“There are a lot of real animals that were fashioned into amulets and small statues, likely to imbue the owners with some of their traits, but also to revere the creature and the benefits it provided,” archaeologist Randall Hixenbaugh tells Bazaar. His namesake gallery in Manhattan’s Design District is home to artifacts sourced in accordance with the ethical standards set by the International Association of Dealers in Ancient Arts.

Talismans were worn like supplements to enhance the human experience—cats to spark joy, mongooses to offer protection from snakes, and frogs for fertility. Stones of certain colors were valued for their connotations with abundance and immortality. “The Egyptians prized bright blue and green materials, which were associated with the Nile, the sky, and life itself. Lapis lazuli was rare and valuable and therefore associated with the pharaohs. Its extensive use on King Tut’s gold mask shows how highly regarded it was,” Hixenbaugh explains.

The cobalt stone finds its way into all kinds of Van Cleef’s latest creations. It’s used generously in the “Ornement de Saphir” necklace, whose central totem resembles an evil eye, with an oval-cut sapphire pupil plopped cleverly in an iris of pavé diamonds. Suspended from a panel of lapis lazuli interspersed with slivers of gold and baguette-cut rubies, the pendant is detachable and can be worn like a brooch, while its Corde chain incorporates clasps concealed beneath colorful rondelles that allow it to transform into a pair of bracelets. A concept conceived in the 1940s, the versatile signature speaks to the ongoing conversation between the house’s heritage and Egypt’s mystique.

Some of Van Cleef’s most striking interpretations of the ancient kingdom come through in Art Deco bracelets. The articulated cuff style was a staple of the 1920s, with ample real estate for planes of illustrious motifs to flourish. Instead of a painter’s palette, Van Cleef brought diamonds, emeralds, and rubies together like dots on a pointillist painting through the “mystery set” technique (a manner of setting gemstones sans prongs).

The house patented the method in the 1930s and has continued to enlist it to craft seamless gemscapes, extending into a series of “Paysage” bracelets. Clad in fields of gemstones, the deco-style pieces bring Egyptian royals, celestial skies, and monuments to life. Openwork, a technique that minimizes the use of solid metals, enables light to pass through each bejeweled mural, amplifying the high shine of each cuff. Other inconspicuous elements, like golden hieroglyphic nuggets, rest beneath articulated columns, like artifacts awaiting discovery.

The house’s bold fonts of design can be equally understood through the juxtaposition of lolly-like cabochons and sugar-white diamonds. Amid the sea of platinum and flashy Asscher cuts that defined Gatsby-era decadence, Van Cleef & Arpels brought teardrop emeralds into the mix. Suspended from pillars of diamonds set in platinum, the plump stones hung like enticing lozenges throughout a collaret necklace, circa 1929. The spellbinding masterpiece drew the eye of Egyptian royalty, Her Royal Highness Princess Faiza, who purchased the necklace in 1937. It was a convergence of brilliant facets and vivid, dome-shaped gems that bridged the gradual shift into midcentury modern shapes and matte stones. The provenance of the design influenced the maison’s updated rendition of the crown jewel, featuring luscious emeralds and slender pearls suspended from elongated pendants of white gold and diamonds.

By the 1950s, Van Cleef’s interpretations of Egyptomania embraced organic forms, space-age ellipses, and plots of semiprecious stones. Smooth, matte surfaces of turquoise, malachite, and coral enhanced the realism of biomorphic designs, like a scarab clip with wings of turquoise, circa 1957. (The house would lend the brooch to the Petit Palais for the debut of “Tutankhamun and His Time” nearly a decade later.) Such vibrant stones extended into contemporary aesthetics of the 1980s, such as a collar necklace slathered in slices of jade, stripes of onyx, and birds carved out of coral.

This collection revisited the era through the language of Ettore Sottsass and the Memphis Group, a 1980s decorative arts firm, for spherical shapes and elements of cubism to take flight. Some of Sottsass’s most recognizable pieces include a sterling silver fruit stand with zigzag pillars and a colorblocked bookstand named “The Carlton.” Van Cleef translates this friction between elements smooth and sharp, glossy and matte into pieces like a saucer-shaped Mythe Celeste clip, coated in sheets of lapis and malachite with buttons of coral—elements beheld by the Egyptians as modalities of abundance and immortality. By contrast, a Frise Divine ring juxtaposes an oval-shaped Sri Lankan sapphire with a rigid tile of coral, and rows of diamonds set in zigzag streams of gold.

All creations in the Fascinating Egypt œuvre are united by tradition, a preservation of heritage echoed in the foundations of other art forms, like ballet. In fact, the house’s seamless dances of emeralds, rubies, and diamonds compelled George Balanchine to choreograph Jewels for the New York City Ballet in 1931—a moment that came full circle when the jewelers debuted a collection of ballerina clips and brooches a couple of years later. The nature of their anatomically sound arms, gilded in gold and ribbons of gemstones, extends into the collection’s Muse Éternelle brooches. Modeled after Cleopatra, the palm-sized figures are enrobed in a variety of dazzling threads of gold, strewn with diamonds, sapphires, rubies, and emeralds.

Fireclay Tile Turns Traditional Textiles Into Hand-Painted Tile

Plaids and florals might seem more at home on upholstery or wallpaper than ceramic tile, but Fireclay Tile’s latest collection makes a compelling case for bringing those familiar patterns to harder surfaces. Called Idyllwild, the new hand-painted collection takes inspiration from traditional textiles, translating everything from delicate botanical illustrations to richly layered plaids into ceramic designs that feel both nostalgic and contemporary.

Interestingly, the collection didn’t begin with flowers at all. “We were seeing designers use a lot of intricate textiles, colors, and patterns together,” explains Jamie Chappell, VP of Brand and Product at Fireclay Tile, adding that early explorations focused on plaids and stripes. “As a true lover of ’90s alt-rock, I actually went down a rabbit hole early on, wondering if it should be a ‘grunge’ collection.”

Fireclay Tile’s Idyllwild collection in its Winter palette, featuring deep blues, earthy greens, and warm neutrals across floral, striped, and plaid patterns.

That direction eventually gave way to something softer. Recognizing the longstanding relationship between florals and plaids in interiors, the team introduced botanical patterns to balance the collection’s more structured designs. The name Idyllwild followed, referencing the California mountain town.

The resulting collection includes 16 patterns organized into four categories: Specimen Flowers, Idyllwild Florals, Idyllwild Stripes, and Idyllwild Plaids.

The Specimen Flowers series features four individual botanical motifs—Allium, Carnation, Lily, and Bluebell—hand-painted on ceramic tile. Photography courtesy of Fireclay Tile.

Specimen Flowers features four individual botanical designs—Allium, Carnation, Lily, and Bluebell—that can be arranged across backsplashes, niches, and accent walls. The Idyllwild Florals take a more expansive approach, with repeating patterns named Pomegranate, Tulip, Morning Glory, and Meadow. Their overlapping stems, leaves, and blossoms create the dense, layered effect of a garden growing beyond its boundaries.

The Stripes and Plaids explore a different kind of ornamentation. Four striped patterns—Stuga, Mokki, Blockhaus, and Thatch—introduce rhythm through repeated lines and geometric details. The Plaids, meanwhile, borrow the visual language of woven fabrics, with Chalet, Highlands, Minka, and Torp creating intersecting grids that can read as anything from understated texture to a bold, room-enveloping pattern.

The Idyllwild Plaids series features four geometric patterns inspired by traditional woven textiles, shown here in warm shades of burgundy, pink, and brown. Photography courtesy of Fireclay Tile.

While the designs reference traditional textiles, translating them into hand-painted ceramic required a different way of thinking about pattern. “We had to simplify and edit the artwork so our painting team could actually execute it by hand,” Jamie says.

The process uses resist lines to define individual areas of color, making the scale and spacing of each detail particularly important. “When we translate intricate woven textiles into screen art, we have to pay very close attention to the thickness of the lines, how small the fields are to fill in, and making sure the painters have enough natural ‘starts and stops’ so they can work efficiently,” Jamie says.

A closer look at Fireclay Tile’s hand-painting process, with glaze applied to the Allium motif from the Idyllwild Specimen Flowers series. Photography courtesy of Fireclay Tile.

Fireclay’s painting team was closely involved in prototyping, providing feedback on details that were too small or areas where the glaze couldn’t be applied smoothly. The company also developed a new glaze base specifically for the hand-application process.

The relationship between design and production extends to color. Each of the 16 patterns is available in four curated palettes inspired by the seasons, but developing those options required another significant undertaking.

The Meadow pattern from Fireclay Tile’s Idyllwild Florals series features an assortment of botanical motifs in the Spring color palette. Photography courtesy of Fireclay Tile.

Fireclay’s previous hand-painted palette had become disconnected from its broader ceramic offering, limiting customization opportunities. To address that, the production team spent months matching the company’s 50 core ceramic colors in both gloss and matte finishes. The resulting 100 finishes can be combined across the collection.

A selection of floral, plaid, and striped patterns from Fireclay Tile’s Idyllwild collection, shown in the Winter palette of deep blues, muted greens, and warm neutrals. Photography courtesy of Fireclay Tile.

To develop Idyllwild’s seasonal palettes, the team worked with physical tile samples alongside 4K renderings, testing combinations before translating them into paint sheets for production. The color stories also reflect shifts the company has been observing in interiors, including an interest in deeper reds and earthy blues.

This allows patterns to be introduced selectively or carried across larger surfaces, with stripes used as borders or accents and more intricate designs covering entire walls.

Although Idyllwild draws from traditional patterns, the finished designs are shaped as much by the practical demands of hand-painting as by their textile references.

Posted November 23, 2026 | Reviewed by Monica Vilhauer Ph.D.

Fans of the X-Men film franchise, which premiered in the long-ago year of 2000, were treated to the original version of Hugh Jackman’s now-longstanding portrayal of the character Wolverine. Appearing frequently shirtless in the movie, at the time Jackman seemed muscular enough to be super-heroic. He has played Wolverine nine more times in the decades since then, but rather than growing older and more relaxed, Jackman’s physique has seemed to develop in the opposite direction. Bigger, leaner, and more defined, Jackman could stand for an increasingly familiar male ideal.

Like him, many men spend hours in the gym trying to bulk up. They may carefully monitor their diets, timing their protein intake for maximum effect. They may indeed grow larger and more muscular; family, friends, and partners will notice. They may share images of weight-lifting transformations on social media, or even consider anabolic steroids or testosterone injections. Despite it all, some of these men will continue to see themselves as too small, too ill-defined, and not muscular enough.

This condition is known as muscle dysmorphia, a form of body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) characterized by an unrelenting preoccupation with the idea that one is not muscular or lean enough. Even when a man is objectively well built, he may perceive himself as physically inadequate. It’s not common, but it’s real: a 2025 study of nearly fifteen hundred boys and men between the ages of 15 and 35 (Ganson et al., 2025a) concluded that the disorder likely existed in approximately 2.8% of those surveyed — across demographic groups, not only among weightlifters or athletes. (Indeed, a 2024 study in the Journal of Eating Disorders found higher muscle dysmorphia symptom scores among men who wanted to increase their body weight than among those who wanted to reduce it.)

As I’ve suggested, while physical fitness is usually worth aspiring to, there’s a significant difference between ordinary, healthy fitness and muscle dysmorphia. Even when a man is objectively strong, “cut,” and full of muscles, he may look at himself in the mirror and see only weakness. This preoccupation can then lead to repeated, muscle-seeking behavior, such as checking himself in the mirror, researching new exercise routines, measuring body parts, or seeking new nutritional information. It can also involve excessive weightlifting, spending too much time at the gym, or taking on rigid dietary pursuits that can even become unhealthy or dangerous.

But as all that exercise produces results, it never seems like enough: getting bigger just feeds back into the perception that they still aren’t big enough. And when men with the disorder can’t soothe their anxiety by working out, they can feel real emotional distress, as well. The central characteristic of muscle dysmorphia is being absorbed by the perception that one is not muscular enough; it describes men whose daily routine and sense of self are dependent on the pursuit of muscularity, not those who just don’t like missing a workout (Cooper et al., 2020).

These days, muscle dysmorphia is particularly associated with male body-image concerns, although women can experience it as well. As we’ve seen in Hugh Jackman’s transformation, men in popular culture are expected to be physically strong and imposing, with muscles sharply defined and standing out through their clothing. (So many superheroes!) Women face body image concerns as well, of course, but the pressure on women often focuses on losing weight and appearing thin rather than strong. For men, being muscular can stand in for physical attractiveness, competence, or even dominance.

It’s easy to find examples of these masculine norms on social media nowadays; just as in the past, we’ve been treated to male movie stars whose bodies are designed to show off a great deal of time in the gym; adherence to these norms has been associated with the symptoms of muscle dysmorphia (Grunewald et al., 2022). Research by Ganson and colleagues (2025b) supported this, finding that greater exposure to muscle-oriented social media content was associated with higher levels of probable muscle dysmorphia.

Filters, strategic lighting, and increasingly sophisticated photo-editing tools can offer further enhancement to these already extreme physiques, and sometimes the men in these social-media posts use performance-enhancing drugs, which pushes them still further out of the realm of the realistic. Overall, the fitness content on social media can complicate the distinction between healthy exercise and unhealthy physical enhancement. Psychological vulnerabilities may also play a role: in a 2024 study of men attending gyms, Kandemir et al. found that perfectionism helped explain the relationship between vulnerable narcissism and the risk of muscle dysmorphia.

Muscle dysmorphia can be harmful in a number of ways. Training too hard, even through pain, can lead to injuries — some of which can be lasting. Restricting one’s diet can also have serious consequences for one’s health, such as hormonal disruption (meaning reduced production of testosterone, among other things), reduced bone density, and an increase in the risk of fractures or hypotension (Mountjoy et al., 2023).

The obsession with being muscular can take its toll on one’s social life, too, when men seem to prioritize their nutrition or their workout schedules over their partners’ needs. Daily work obligations can be affected when muscle dysmorphia affects one’s ability to concentrate (Cooper et al., 2020).

And of course there are the serious emotional consequences of feeling ashamed of oneself — like depression, anxiety, or self-hatred — because of body-image-related beliefs. Even when a man’s friends seem to admire their discipline or dedication, or comment in positive ways about the change in his physical condition, these would-be compliments can have a paradoxically damaging effect if they spur a man to try even harder to achieve his unrealistic muscular aspirations, or if they make his beliefs seem more reasonable than they are.

As I’ve noted before, men may be comparatively less likely to seek help for emotion-related problems than women; when the topic is body-image concerns, they may be less likely still. Some may not even believe they have a problem, as the effort to become (and look) strong seems to be valued by their peers and by the culture overall (Brown et al., 2023).

In therapy, however, it is possible to draw a subtle but meaningful distinction between a realistic, healthy body image and a source of compulsive striving. Irrational or distorted cognitions may be examined; other sources of self-worth can be identified. In other words, the effort to become “big” may also have an idiosyncratic meaning to each individual man, and this meaning stands a chance of being explored and identified in the context of the therapeutic relationship. (Medical intervention may become necessary as well, in cases involving potentially harmful medications or nutritional restriction.)

In the end, muscle dysmorphia creates a slippery slope: trying to be big seems to promise better self-esteem, but the effort only results in further, more extreme beliefs about how big one needs to be to achieve those benefits. The only real way out is to grow in a different direction — to learn that feeling good means accepting yourself as you are, and trying to live in a healthy way without wondering what you still need to change.

This Is Why Your Mashed Potatoes Are Gluey (and How to Fix It)

Consider this your mashed potato primer, complete with tips on how to fix gluey mashed potatoes and how to prevent them from the start.

My mom’s potato masher is one of my favorite kitchen tools, from its retro design to the thick, grid-patterned bottom that hasn’t bent or warped over the years, despite heavy use. I attributed her consistently perfect fluffy mashed potatoes to this simple tool, even when she was experimenting with new mashed potato ideas. And it turns out there is some truth to it because it created smooth mashed potatoes quickly yet thoroughly, preventing her from pummeling the spuds into a sticky, gummy mess.

You see, overworked potatoes are the biggest reason for gluey mashed potatoes, so using the right tool is the best way to prevent them in the first place. And if you came here because your potatoes are already gummy, we have some helpful tips for how to fix gluey mashed potatoes after the fact.

The biggest culprit for gluey mashed potatoes is overworked potatoes. Potatoes are naturally starchy, and a little bit of starch is a good thing. Just as aquafaba (starchy cooked chickpea liquid) can be whipped into a rich vegan mayonnaise, potato starch thickens mashed potatoes and contributes to a creamy texture. But there is a limit.

As you mash chunks of potatoes into a mostly smooth mixture, the spud’s cell walls collapse, freeing the starch within those cells. The more you mash, the more cells are broken down, and the thicker, starchier and gummier the potatoes become. This can happen when you mash potatoes too long by hand, but it happens more quickly when using electric tools.

Food processors and immersion blenders have incredibly fast, sharp blades. As they whip around, they cut through the potatoes’ cells so rapidly that the once silken mash becomes gluey in a flash. I can’t urge you strongly enough to pass on these tools when mashing potatoes.

There are three components to avoiding gluey mashed potatoes: Choosing the right potato, using the right tool and considering temperature.

For starters, choose a starchy or all-purpose potato, like a russet or Yukon Gold. These potatoes are relatively low in moisture, helping the potato break down into a creamy mixture. They’ll give you more wiggle room as you mash than waxy potatoes, so the potatoes will turn out delicious even if you lose yourself in exuberant mashing.

Next, use the right kitchen tool for the job and reach for a potato masher, food mill or ricer. These handheld tools give you more control over speed and power as you mash. I prefer a potato masher with a crisscross grid pattern over those with a wavy design because there’s less open space, making each mash more productive.

Finally, warm your butter and milk before adding them to the potatoes. This step may sound finicky, but it prevents the warm potato starches from seizing against cold ingredients and becoming dense. Switch to a spatula and stir them in to keep a gentle touch and retain a fluffy texture.

You’ll never be able to totally remove the excess starch and get rid of the gluey texture without starting over, but you may be able to improve it. Adding more fat or liquid can improve the mouthfeel, and stirring in other ingredients can alter the overall texture.

Knowing what types of potatoes are best for which recipes can save you a lot of grief—and this knowledge will go a long way to preventing gluey potatoes. For the creamiest, smoothest, lightest-as-air mashed potatoes, go with a starchy russet potato. Some people like using all-purpose Yukon Gold potatoes for their naturally buttery flavor, but I prefer to use all russets (or a blend of russets and Yukon Golds for flavor).

It’s best to avoid waxy potatoes, like red or fingerling potatoes, for mashed potato recipes. They keep their shape when they’re cooked, which is why they’re better for potato salads and soups than creamy mashes. They do make an excellent smashed potato, though, if you’re looking to do something a little bit different with your side dish.

As with all cooking rules, this one is made to be broken. If you only have red potatoes in the pantry, you can still use them to make mashed red potatoes. The price per pound on a shelf tag misses how long each pound lasts. A pound of a calorie-dense food feeds your pet for more days than a pound of a less dense one.

The 10 Best War Books of All Time, Ranked

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

There’s a story below that’s older than books as they're currently understood, which speaks to how storytelling has always been a thing culturally, sure, but it also speaks to how war has always been a thing. Throughout the history of literature, stories about war have been significant, important, and dramatically compelling, and then there’s at least one great story about warfare technically from even earlier.

As best as possible, there are books about a range of wars below, and to keep things more interesting, there are a couple of fantasy novels that also count as war books included below, too. What you won’t see here are great non-fiction books about war, nor memoirs or strictly biographical novels. This ranking of the best war books of all time will include some works of historical fiction, though, and there are only a couple of inclusions that are about conflicts of an entirely fictional nature.

Like another book from the 1960s concerned with World War II that'll be mentioned shortly, Catch-22 is famously about how weird, despair-filled, and almost comically absurd war can be, but without the sci-fi elements of that other novel and also with a bit more of a focus on humor. It’s a very dark comedy, though, and the humor is of a surreal nature, too, all of it getting quite exhausting at a point.

Then again, that exhaustion does feel like the point of Catch-22, and at least it’s got a great premise, since it explores the notion that anyone who wants to exit the war must be sensible, and so they can’t request a check for insanity as a way to get out, since doing that demonstrates their sanity. There’s a lot more chaos on top of all that, and the novel is fairly episodic, at a point, albeit in a way that mostly works (and, again, feels deliberate).

All Quiet on the Western Front was written and published, as it just so happens, between the end of World War I and the beginning of World War II. So back then, it wasn’t called World War I, and you'd have to assume people were pretty hopeful there wouldn’t be any more world wars. Plenty of books post-World War II ended up being about that conflict, for fairly obvious reasons.

So, All Quiet on the Western Front, despite being nearly a century old, might well be the ultimate anti-war book about the First World War. The narrative here is what you get in many stories that take an anti-war approach to things, with young people being swept up in a conflict they don’t fully understand until it’s tragically too late, but when it’s done well, it has to be appreciated. And, to All Quiet on the Western Front’s credit, it did this kind of anti-war narrative before a good many other novels and/or movies with similar approaches and stories.

The Aubrey-Maturin series overall concerned the Napoleonic Wars, but the first of 20 (completed) books, Master and Commander, takes place shortly before the outbreak of the conflict proper. Still, like the rest of the series, Master and Commander does concern naval warfare, and then it’s set during the War of the Second Coalition, which was part of the French Revolutionary Wars, and those wars were the prelude to the Napoleonic Wars.

Sorry if that’s simplifying things. It’s not in the spirit of Aubrey-Maturin to simplify things, since Patrick O'Brian’s attention to detail is legendary, and you get a sense of authenticity almost right from the start of the first book, Master and Commander. It’s clearly the best place to start, and you're obviously in luck if you like what you read, seeing as the rest of the series is acclaimed and very readable as well.

The elephant in the room that’s always there when you're talking about A Song of Ice and Fire is that it’s a series that has infamously not finished, and it feels increasingly unlikely that it will. To paraphrase that now-dead meme, we really did get GTA 6 before A Song of Ice and Fire #6. Still, what does exist of the series is impressive, and war is a pretty big part of all the books.

Of those, A Game of Thrones (book #1) is where things happen that really set off the main war of the series, and so book #2, A Clash of Kings, feels the worthiest of being included here. Those potential kings really do clash, with there being a whole War of Five Kings that gets progressively messier and truly feels like the focus of the bulk of this book. It’s not where that conflict ends, by any means, but it feels most accurate to call A Clash of Kings a “war book,” out of all the entries in the series to date.

This is the one that pairs fairly well with Catch-22, since Slaughterhouse-Five is also bold, about World War II, and sometimes darkly funny. It’s also got some unusual sci-fi elements, in case the idea of a sometimes-funny, sometimes-sad, sometimes semi-autobiographical, and sometimes mind-bending book about World War II didn’t sound interesting enough on its own.

Here, a good deal of the book concerns the bombing of Dresden, which is something Kurt Vonnegut himself experienced, so he was a good authority to write on the subject, of course. The time travel and other parts are where it strays away from feeling autobiographical, yet the reliving of war and trauma is authentic, and made more literal with it being genuinely revisited and more than “just” flashed back to. Oh, and Slaughterhouse-Five does all this within a very short duration, all up. It’s wild stuff, and very little else out there is quite like it.

The Winds of War is a massive novel, and yet it’s hard to talk about it without also acknowledging that it’s just one half of an overall story that continued with War and Remembrance. If you want, consider them both worthy of taking up this spot, since they function as one grand family saga that spans World War II quite comprehensively, including the lead-up to it, at least from the U.S. point of view; like, the lead-up to the U.S. joining the conflict.

That’s what book #1 builds to, at least, with War and Remembrance perhaps being more about the war and being in the thick of it. Anyway, both stand out as great pieces of historical fiction, since the broad events here really happened, but many (albeit not all) of the characters are fictional. You do need to devote a lot of time to reading something like The Winds of War, and then even more time to (the even longer) second book, but it’s an ultimately worthwhile endeavor.

There was a lot of talk about the 2026 film adaptation of Homer's The Odyssey changing a good deal of the original text, but the changes there have nothing on all the changes made to Homer's The Iliad with 2004’s Troy. The name was changed there, so maybe that dulled the impact a bit. Also, The Iliad feels like a more difficult story to get right, if you're adapting something, since it’s got more moving parts and a higher number of truly important characters featured throughout.

It’s technically about part of the Trojan War, but not with all the events you might expect, and with fantastical elements (though not as many as you get with The Odyssey). Clearly, The Iliad is important as far as stories about warfare go, and that importance and the overall history/legacy here make it an essential read, even with it being quite complicated and overwhelming (to greater or lesser extents depending on which translation you tackle).

Oh, Gravity’s Rainbow. This is a fairly well-known book for something that’s also remarkably impenetrable and overwhelmingly dense, not to mention incredibly confronting and even disgusting in parts. Those factors could contribute to why it’s well-known, so maybe calling it notorious is more accurate, but maybe all that works in its favor as well. It’s a difficult one to summarize, beyond saying that it’s largely set during the final stages of World War II, and also, there’s a lot of talk about V-2 rockets.

Some people are searching for one particular V-2 rocket, seemingly, and then others aren’t really as tied to what seems to be going on, sort of just dropping in and out of what ends up being a very sprawling and complex novel. Parts of Gravity’s Rainbow are thrilling in an unusual sort of way, yet much of it’s also overwhelming, which is something that can be said about a great many Thomas Pynchon novels. But for capturing the madness of war, and for also being unforgettable (for better or worse), Gravity’s Rainbow does feel like a deservedly controversial classic of sorts.

There isn't much else that can be said about The Lord of the Rings, since if there is a reason for it to be ranked high on any kind of ranking, you do more or less have to put it there. And high. But here, it might be worth admitting that it’s more of a fantasy epic, and as far as war books go, it’s not conventional, seeing as the conflict here is a fantastical one, and the war’s entirely fictional and all (not like in Slaughterhouse-Five, where the war was real, but a sci-fi/fictional spin was put on things).

And then it also differs from A Clash of Kings, because the warfare in that book felt a bit more authentic, with less by way of magic and non-human creatures and all. You Know Animal-Print Rugs, But Do You Know Animal Pictorials? (2026)

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The oldest known pile rug—pulled from a frozen tomb in Siberia in 1949—features a procession of 24 fallow deer and a parade of men on horseback. Some 2,500 years after its creation, animal illustrations are making their way back onto the floor. A serpent coils through the Snake and Pomegranate rug by Christopher Farr and Studio Shamshiri in Lucy Hale’s Los Angeles home, and a Tibetan tiger stretches across Demi Lovato’s modern farmhouse and Ethan Cook’s SoHo loft. These aren’t spots or stripes standing in for a neutral—they’re meant to be noticed and discussed. “Animal print is a texture; an animal motif is a story,” says AD100 designer Pavitra Rajaram, who recently designed an animal-themed collection for Jaipur Living called Majnun. “I think people are tired of decoration that says nothing and are looking for objects that carry meaning.”

Rajaram is describing a literal animal rug, also known as an animal pictorial. These playful floor coverings often feature a bucolic scene of several animals or a single animal form woven into the textile, and they come in styles ranging from avant-garde and surrealist to Art Deco and folk-inspired.

“What we’re seeing now is a shift toward rugs where the whole animal is part of the design and has a role in the composition,” says Kerri Anne Besse, design director at rug brand Tumble. “The oversized, blown-up animal print feels dated.”

As for styling, Sophie Salata, head of brand at Vinterior, recommends that designers let the pictorials stand on their own. “I’d be inclined to treat the rug almost as a piece of art, allowing it to become a focal point rather than trying to make everything around it match,” she says. Pamela Marshall, creative director at artisanal rug brand Patterson Flynn, agrees with this approach: These designs are more than backdrops.

Reformation's nine-piece rug collection with Les Éditions de Tapis includes depictions of cats, swans, and tigers.

Recently, brands have been launching their own interpretations of animal pictorials. Last week, Reformation launched its first-ever home collection, a collaboration with Les Éditions de Tapis that features hand-drawn swans and cats. At Milan Design Week this year, Fornasetti launched a surrealist-inspired line of rugs with CC-Tapis depicting slithering serpents from the luxury designer’s archive. A few months earlier, Ruggable released its collaboration with Scalamandré to bring the upscale design house’s iconic zebras to more homes. And in October 2025, Nordic Knots launched its Swedish Grace collection, including the Grace 03 Fauna rug, which shows a wildlife scene. Looking further back, Beni’s serpent rugs have been a designer favorite since 2020.

One of the earlier examples of literal animal rugs is a collection between longstanding collaborators Patterson Flynn and AD100 designers Charlap Hyman & Herrero. It grew from a one-off rug into a full collection of graphic natural-and-black silhouettes that launched in 2018 and continues to expand. “Areas of inspiration for these come from Calder’s simplified animal forms, the surrealists’ play on scale, and 17th-century cabinets of curiosity, to name a few,” says Marshall, who credits Hyman’s “encyclopedic” design references for the originality. We would be remiss not to mention Diane von Furstenberg’s long-running collaboration with the Rug Company, which has been producing the iconic Climbing Leopard rug since 2005.

These rugs’ rich history might be part of their renewed appeal. Animals have been a subject for as long as people have made pictures. The oldest known figurative cave painting—found in Indonesia and dated to at least 51,200 years ago—shows a wild pig with three human figures.

One of the world’s rarest fish lives in a single cave in Death Valley National Park, and earthquakes have left it on the verge of extinction

📅 Nov 23, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Devils Hole pupfish live in just one cavern of Death Valley National Park. Earthquakes are rapidly pushing this fish towards extinction.

One of the rarest fish on Earth lives in just one place: a water-filled cavern in the Nevada desert called Devils Hole, part of Death Valley National Park. In the spring of 2025, after two earthquakes shook its tiny home, biologists counted only 38 of them.

The National Park Service announced the new figure in November. Divers and surface counters found the 137 Devils Hole pupfish during a survey on October 10, nearly double the 77 seen in the spring of 2026 and about double the 69 recorded in the fall of 2025.

Devils Hole is a cave about 12 feet wide and more than 500 feet deep. The bottom has never been mapped. The water stays at a constant 92 degrees Fahrenheit (33 degrees Celsius).

Pupfish have been found as deep as 66 feet, but they feed and spawn only on a shallow rock shelf near the surface, where algae grows.

The species has been isolated there for 10,000 to 20,000 years, longer than any other pupfish in the Death Valley system. The region’s caves keep other long records too, like a Nevada cave that reveals 580,000 years of climate swings.

It is the only naturally occurring population of the species, which was listed as endangered in 1967. Biologists from the National Park Service and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service have counted the fish almost every spring and fall since 1972, with scuba divers in the cavern and counters at the surface.

One of the fish’s main threats can come from thousands of miles away. Large earthquakes as far away as Japan, Indonesia and Chile have made the water slosh in Devils Hole like water in a bathtub, according to the park.

Waves can splash as high as two meters (about 6.5 feet) up the walls and sweep the shelf clean. The park calls it a tsunami in the desert, although it is tiny next to the ocean tsunamis that big earthquakes can unleash.

That is what happened in the winter of 2024 to 2025. Two earthquakes, one in December and one in February, set off waves that moved algae, organic material and fish eggs off the shelf.

The spring count in 2025 found 38 fish. One year earlier, the official spring count had recorded 191.

On July 29, 2025, just 21 minutes after a magnitude 8.8 earthquake struck Russia’s Kamchatka Peninsula, waves rippled through Devils Hole, nearly 4,000 miles away. Those waves were only about 10 inches high and removed just some material from the shelf.

Then, after an earthquake in Panama on October 9, 2026, nearly 3,000 miles away, biologists at first saw signs of stress in the fish. On a later visit, they found them in healthier condition.

The team had prepared for a crash like this for years. Pupfish eggs from Devils Hole had been collected regularly to build a backup population at the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service’s Ash Meadows Fish Conservation Facility.

In 2025, biologists added captive-raised fish to the wild population for the first time. Between March 2025 and February 2026, scientists released 72 of them into Devils Hole. They also gave the fish extra food while the algae grew back.

The fish live an average of only 10 to 14 months. Because of that, biologists believe most of the 137 fish counted this fall hatched and grew up in Devils Hole itself.

“It’s exciting to see Devils Hole pupfish numbers increase so significantly over the past year,” said Death Valley National Park Superintendent Mike Reynolds.

“These results reflect the dedication of our scientists and conservation partners working to protect this extraordinary species. While challenges remain, we’re encouraged by this progress and committed to its long-term survival.”

The pupfish has survived human threats before. In the late 1960s, a farming operation drilled large wells in the Ash Meadows area, and the water level in Devils Hole began to drop toward the shelf the fish need.

Biologists even hung a floating, artificially lit shelf in the cave as a substitute. The fish never used it.

A federal court halted further pumping in 1971, and the fight reached the U.S. Supreme Court. Its 1976 decision in Cappaert v. United States recognized the water right of Devils Hole as part of a national monument and limited pumping so the natural shelf would stay underwater.

Until the mid-1990s, spring surveys counted more than 200 fish. In the early 2000s, spring counts averaged about 90, and in 2013 the number fell to 35. The cause of that long decline has not been determined.

The new count is an encouraging step, but the park says the population naturally fluctuates and remains highly vulnerable.

It calls continued monitoring, research and habitat management essential if one of the world’s rarest fish is to keep its foothold in a single desert cave.
