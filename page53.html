The European Commission's new Digital Fairness Act could change monetisation in games forever - and not just in Europe

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

"Well, we'll just do this for the rest of the world."

The European Commission has published its proposed Digital Fairness Act, which has the potential to change how video games are monetised - worldwide - forever. A draft of the Act was leaked yesterday and what's been published today appears to be, to my eye, the same, though there's now also a mini-site accompanying it that explores the Act in more detail and uses more understandable language.

And note that while the Digital Fairness Act isn't about video games exclusively - it's much broader than that - it does mention video games specifically, highlighting both their increased importance to society now, and that policy-shaping bodies like the European Commission are prepared to take them seriously.

The Digital Fairness Act is set to become European law, probably sometime between 2028 and 2029, once everyone's discussed it and made whatever amendments they're going to make to it. And at that point, breaching the regulations will come with potentially steep fines (up to six percent of a company's worldwide annual turnover, or up to €2m where an annual turnover isn't available).

The biggest things in the Digital Fairness Act relating to video games are regulations around loot boxes (sometimes referred to as "variable reward systems" or systems with "uncertainty-based rewards") and virtual currencies, particularly with regard to "minors" - anyone under 18 years old.

The Act stipulates paid-for virtual currencies must be disabled in a game by default for minors, then activated by parental and guardian approval, and that paid loot boxes "shall not" be offered to minors at all. Moreover, games must provide a way for everyone to disable paid loot boxes completely, and clearly show the probability of receiving items from them.

It's these changes that have the potential to overhaul how loot boxes work everywhere going forwards, and whether companies use them at all. To understand why, I talked to games business expert George Osborn, who runs the excellent Video Games Industry Memo newsletter, and who's been following the Act closely.

Osborn sees this as a "really big" regulatory change and believes that companies like EA, Xbox, Activision Blizzard, PlayStation, and other big gaming companies, both in console gaming and mobile gaming, will be "really concerned" by this. That's partly because a lot of these things being outlined are not necessarily easy to implement.

For starters, a company now needs a way to work out who's a minor playing its game and who isn't, and it needs to provide "a master switch", as Osborn calls it, for people to switch paid loot boxes off with. These things require not just design changes but perhaps entire alterations to a game's approach; it means theoretically being able to turn paid card packs off in EA Sports FC Ultimate Team, example, and their not being able to be sold to minors at all.

"EA might be looking at it and going, 'This isn't great news for [EA Sports FC] Ultimate Team because people can switch it off,'" Osborn added, "but Ultimate Team is at least hived off in a box." EA could potentially age-gate Ultimate team as a mode and deal with it that way. But what if you're a card game where random paid rewards are central to your premise? "What that now means is you're possibly going to have to find a way to just completely turn that off for users, and it is very difficult to do that when they're embedded into the design of the game."

The virtual currency issue is potentially easier to tackle because existing parental controls can control spending, so those tools could expand to cover it. But another proposal brought forward in the Digital Fairness Act states that a game must clearly show, in a region's local currency, how much an item costs when bought with virtual currency, which adds another layer of complication when considering different currencies and people moving between countries with their devices.

These changes require investment and thought, and they sit alongside broader changes that affect things like subscription services, such as Xbox Live and PlayStation Plus and Nintendo Online, to make them easier to cancel and less able to obfuscate prices and hidden extras. There's even a stipulation that people be able to talk to a human in customer service rather than a chat bot. There are more technicalities for game-making companies to consider.

But why should people outside of the European Union care? These rules are only enforceable there. Because: if a company is selling a game into the European Union, which I expect every game company will want to do - "you don't just want to rule out a market of a few hundred million consumers; it's not a light thing to cut that out", Osborn says - then it will have to comply by these rules. And if it does, how many different versions of a game will it be prepared to make?

Think about the recent global move towards all devices using USB-C chargers: that was a movement started by a European Commission ruling. Think of the new Switch 2s with removable battery packs: that was another change instigated by the European Commission. "Because that market is big enough, you design for it," Obsorn says, "and then your logic becomes, 'Well, we'll just do this for the rest of the world.' You're not going to produce one with the battery sealed in and the battery without. You're just going to find one way to produce things.

"That's likely to be the biggest impact in the UK and the rest of the world," he adds, "as people adjust the designs of their games to follow these rules. There's going to be a strong likelihood that some of the design features will remain within the games because it's easiest to work off a single universal version."

The other thing, which Osborn thinks is more important, is that policy makers around the world are "desperate" for solutions to problems and don't know where to look for them. "So if someone comes up with an answer to a question that's been annoying everybody, like 'what do we do about loot boxes?'," he says, "and it provides a particularly strong - or perceived to be effective - solution, it'll be copied. And it'll be copied because a big regulatory bloc like the European Union, which is known for protecting consumers particularly well, has decided this is a good thing. People trust it."

Then, because companies have already developed a way to comply with those rules, it becomes difficult for them to argue that they can't do it elsewhere. "Because those regulators go, 'Well why not?'" Osborn says. "Because if you build the solution in one place, you can build it elsewhere."

Whether or not this will be the end of loot boxes as we know them, Osborn isn't sure. "My guess would be this will continue to push loot boxes into the fringes of video game monetisation. It was already starting to happen," he says. "I know some really high-profile games have them very prominently, which makes people feel like is there really any change here? But I know a lot of companies behind the scenes started to quietly move away from them in the last few years when they were a reputational problem. If you move to a world in which you're meant to have a massive off-switch in your game for them, that's a world in which fewer companies will go in that direction."

Some will stick with it. "If you're an EA, it's going to be very difficult to move away from the model that has supported your business for years," Osborn says. And card-based deck-building games will struggle to avoid it. "But overall, we're going to see a shift away from it," he adds. "And more broadly what this marks is a much wider crackdown on the way that video games monetise their players. As you start to see that crackdown come into force, game developers are going to have to think much more carefully about how the design decisions they make don't just interact with ethical practices, but actually with legal practices too."

Scientists have proposed a new tabletop device to detect the subtle way Earth's daily spin on its axis twists the space-time around it ‪—‬ an effect predicted by Einstein's general relativity.

If built, the sensor could measure the quantum effect known as frame dragging with the same precision as the best satellite experiments, but in minutes instead of years, the researchers proposed in a new study.

The research, published Sept. 17 in the journal Physical Review Letters, aimed to address a classic mismatch in physics. Picture a ring of clocks bolted to a spinning merry-go-round. You synchronize each clock with its neighbor around the ring. Then, you compare the last clock with the first. They don't match.

"It is impossible to synchronize that collection of clocks when you're in a rotating frame," Kai-Isaak Ellers, a physics graduate student at the University of California, Berkeley (UC Berkeley) and first author of the study, told Live Science.

A photo of scientists working on NASA's Gravity Probe B in 2003. This device measured Earth's frame-dragging from space, confirming Einstein's predictions with an 18% margin of error.

Einstein's theory of general relativity predicts that a massive, spinning object drags the fabric of space-time along with it, just like a spoon twirling in honey. The effect is clearly visible around supermassive objects, like the Milky Way's central black hole. But around Earth, the effect is very faint.

So far, scientists have measured Earth's frame dragging only from orbit. They tracked how the twisting of space-time slowly shifts spinning gyroscopes or the paths of satellites. In 2011, NASA's Gravity Probe B satellite, which carried four gyroscopes, confirmed Einstein's prediction with a margin of error of about 18%. Satellites tracked with lasers have since done even better. In July, researchers reported in the journal Nature that the LARES-2 satellite had shrunk that margin of error to just 0.2%.

However, that took about three years of laser tracking. Researchers also had to account for the fact that Earth bulges at the equator and its mass is spread unevenly, which gives the planet’s gravity small irregularities that tug on satellites far more strongly than frame dragging does. To subtract those tugs, the team relied on detailed maps of Earth's gravity made by other satellites.

Measuring frame dragging more precisely is a way to stress-test general relativity. Several alternative theories of gravity predict a slightly different amount of twisting, and a lab-based measurement would also be free of the modeling assumptions that satellite experiments depend on, Ellers said.

The new device is a loop of superfluid helium-4 — helium chilled until it flows without friction and behaves like a single, giant quantum object. The loop is interrupted by a thin barrier riddled with tiny holes, called a Josephson junction. Superfluid helium can slip through the holes, but ordinary liquid helium cannot.

"That's where the quantum behavior comes from," said K. Birgitta Whaley, a quantum physicist at UC Berkeley and a co-author of the study.

As Earth spins and drags space-time, it nudges the quantum state of the helium flowing through the junction. That nudge shifts the frequency at which the helium sloshes back and forth in the loop, which the researchers could detect.

A visualization of Earth's uneven gravity field taken by NASA's Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment in 2009. The lumpiness of our planet's gravitational field makes it harder to precisely measure relativistic effects.

Superfluid devices, many of them built at UC Berkeley, have detected Earth's rotation since the late 1990s. But the helium-4 versions built around a Josephson junction, like the new design, have worked within about a hundredth of a degree of 2.17 K (minus 455.76 Fahrenheit, or minus 270.98 Celsius), the temperature at which helium-4 becomes a superfluid. At that temperature, a lingering fraction of "normal" helium mixed in with the superfluid adds noise.

Instead, the team calculated how the device would behave at a few thousandths of a degree above absolute zero. Below 0.7 K, the noise changed character, and the team found it drops sharply as the helium gets colder. One key finding in the new paper is the result of the team's thermal noise analysis, which showed how sensitive these devices could be at millikelvin temperatures, Elles said.

The catch is the barrier. Earlier helium-4 devices used holes about 90 nanometers wide. Working at these frigid temperatures demands much smaller holes, about 1 nanometer across ‪—‬ roughly the size of a helium atom. Keith Schwab, a physicist at Caltech and co-author of the study, and his collaborators are developing such materials, but they haven't been built into a working sensor yet.

If they can be, the researchers calculate that the device could measure frame dragging to 0.2% precision in about 35 minutes. If its vibrating membrane were made from the lowest-loss crystals, such as quartz or sapphire, that could drop to about one second, they said.

"This seems to be a very serendipitous discovery that at millikelvin, we can get this high sensitivity," Whaley told Live Science.

The signal corresponds to a time difference of about 10-35 seconds between helium flowing one way around the loop and the other — the same mismatch as the clocks on the metaphorical merry-go-round.

"The interesting thing about it is just how small it is," Whaley said. "It's not so far off from the Planck time," about 10-44 seconds, the shortest slice of time before the known laws of physics are expected to break down. This experiment is still a billion times too large to reach that limit, Whaley said. But future helium experiments could help explore whether time flows smoothly, like a ramp, or ticks forward in tiny, indivisible jumps, like the frames of a movie.

This year, Khouri appeared on ARTnews’s Top 200 Collectors list.

He started collecting in the late 2000s by acquiring a work by the Lebanese painter Nabil Nahas. Since then, he has been adding 30 to 35 works every year to his collection. Another day, another cybersecurity update. This time, it's the turn of immensely popular NAS provider Synology to advise its users about some now-fixed gaping holes. The company has updated its DiskStation Manager (DSM) operating system, patching out two security vulnerabilities in various product versions.

Just another patch? Not quite. The first of these vulnerabilities has a CVSS3 base score of 9.8 out of 10, which makes it of "critical" severity (via International Cyber Digest on X). According to the company:

"An improper neutralization of special elements used in an SQL command vulnerability in the Auth API in Synology DiskStation Manager (DSM)... allows remote attackers to read or write arbitrary files and conduct denial-of-service attacks."

That's a pretty big security hole if I've ever seen one, particularly for those storing extremely important data on their home NAS setup. The second one gets a lower severity rating of 8.6, but also manages to allow attackers to skip the login process.

Synology describes the second vulnerability as "An authentication bypass using an alternate path or channel vulnerability in the rsync in Synology DiskStation Manager," which can again lead to unwanted reads, writes, and DDOS shenanigans.

Nvidia board’s idolatry of Jensen Huang could open the door to shareholder lawsuits in Delaware Chancery Court

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There is no doubt that Nvidia founder and CEO Jensen Huang is a visionary genius, whose vision as the ‘Godfather of AI’ has improved billions of lives around the world and contributed greatly to American prosperity and innovation. He has attended our Yale CEO forums and has been revered by CEOs across sectors and around the world for his bold, entrepreneurial vision and his revolutionary technological triumphs, plus he is a showcased contributor to our new, upcoming book, When Machines Act: The Promise and Perils of our Agentic AI Future, published by MIT Press and written with Two Sigma Co-Founder David Siegel.

Yet, amid calls for Huang’s immediate resignation from top GOP voices, including Sen. Kevin Cramer of the Senate Banking Committee and others, and demands that he answer to Congress, there were some blatant red flags in Huang’s recent leadership that were apparently missed by the Nvidia board.

In October 2025, the New York Times reported that Megaspeed, a little-known Singapore firm, had bought billions of dollars of Nvidia chips amid suspicion that it was helping China evade U.S. restrictions by laundering banned chips on the U.S. export restriction list to China via neutral countries. Nvidia said a review found no evidence its chips were diverted, but that reporting was followed by an explosive report by a short seller, Culper Research, alleging “uncomfortably close ties” between Alice Huang, the head of Megaspeed, and Jensen Huang, in May 2026. Jensen described the Megaspeed dealings as normal, and apparently the Nvidia board was content to leave it at that.

Late last month, the Times reported that U.S. intelligence believed that Alice Huang was a Chinese asset. A company spokesperson said the diversion claims had been debunked and described the relationship as nothing unusual. Ten days later, Jensen admitted he had been unfaithful to his wife, in a message that referred to the national security concerns in the press – a troubling sequence of events after all the repeated denials.

The Caremark doctrine, established in 1996 in Delaware Chancery Court, establishes that corporate directors have a fiduciary duty of oversight, and that directors breach their duty of loyalty when they make no good-faith effort to monitor the risks central to the business, or when they consciously ignore red flags.

Under this doctrine, and derivative variants of it, several boards have run into trouble in Delaware Chancery Court for similar sins of omission when it comes to oversight. In 2019, Delaware’s Supreme Court revived a suit against the directors of Blue Bell Creameries, an ice cream maker whose board had no system for overseeing food safety before a deadly listeria outbreak. In 2021, the Court of Chancery let claims proceed against Boeing’s directors, who had no board committee responsible for airplane safety before the 737 MAX crashes. That case settled for $237.5 million. The lesson of both: on risks that can sink the company, the board must watch for itself: the board cannot simply trust management, even when those risks were unforeseeable or unanticipated.

So plaintiffs’ lawyers will ask the questions directors should have asked many months ago. Who conducted the review that cleared Megaspeed? Did it report to independent directors or to management? Did any director know the CEO had a personal relationship with an executive of the customer under review? Did the board ever discuss Megaspeed at all, or Jensen Huang’s relationship with Alice Huang? Did any board members even care to ask any questions about these topics?

The answers are in the board meeting minutes, and alarmingly for Nvidia’s board, shareholders can now get those minutes with nearly complete transparency. Nvidia knows this better than most companies for a very ironic reason. In 2022, it fought a books-and-records demand all the way to Delaware’s Supreme Court. Even after Delaware narrowed those inspection rights last year, board minutes and board materials remain within reach for plaintiffs litigation. That is how the Boeing plaintiffs built their case.

All of the major risks that have now landed on Nvidia in such dramatic fashion were exactly the risks directors should have been most alert to.

First, and most importantly, every board entrusted with protecting minority shareholders should be alert to management conflicts of interest. A Megaspeed affiliate bought about $4.8 billion of Nvidia-powered equipment in under two years. A chief executive in a personal relationship with an executive of a customer that size has an apparent conflict that Nvidia’s own code of conduct would have likely required him to disclose. And Megaspeed was no ordinary customer: by October 2025 both Washington and Singapore were investigating it.

That conflict poisons the compliance record. When the questions first surfaced, Nvidia said it had visited Megaspeed’s sites and had no reason to believe chips were diverted. That assurance came from people who report to a CEO with a personal tie to the customer being cleared, a tie no investor had been told about. Bloomberg reports that Nvidia’s general counsel went so far as to contact a senior Commerce Department official about the government’s Megaspeed probe. Did the board authorize that call? Did it even know?

Then comes national security and export-control evasion, as “mission critical” a risk as food safety at an ice cream maker or flight safety at Boeing. Nvidia makes the most strategically sensitive product on earth; and The Information reported today that companies tied to Alice Huang helped Chinese buyers obtain $7.5 billion of Nvidia chips, including banned ones that were laundered through neutral parties. That report is unconfirmed, and Megaspeed denies wrongdoing. But the penalties for export violations run from fines to criminal prosecution to the loss of export privileges, and a board should not have been waiting for verdicts before it starts asking questions. According to Nvidia’s own proxy statement, the board committee responsible for trade compliance met three times last fiscal year, and the company has no chief compliance officer. How much did they know, and when?

The national security risk is larger still. According to the Times, U.S. officials concluded that Alice Huang was recruited by China’s Ministry of State Security to get close to Jensen and to shape his views on chip sales to China – which of course, Jensen Huang has been very vocal on in Washington and in the media, arguing that the U.S. should continue to sell chips to China so that China is not building their own chips. No one has alleged that Jensen knew Huang was a spy, and Beijing calls the claims a smear. But the president was reportedly briefed in late 2025, and a secret memo circulated among policymakers this spring, the same year Jensen was appointed to the President’s Council of Advisors on Science and Technology. Now three House committee chairmen want him in a closed-door briefing, and the president says the FBI is reviewing the matter. Yet not one Nvidia director, as Semafor noted, has a national security background; what questions were asked and what prudent diligence steps were taken by the board?

Then there is the risk every board dreads: that management did not tell it the truth. On Oct. 29, a company spokesman described Jensen’s interactions with Alice Huang as “not unusual or unexpected,” the routine customer contact of any CEO. The same week, Jensen called the Megaspeed dealings normal. Nine days after that he told employees he had been unfaithful to his wife, and tied the admission to the national security concerns in the press. If directors approved those October statements knowing what Jensen knew, they could be in trouble. If they never asked him, that is the Caremark problem.

Shareholders have already paid. Nvidia closed today at $168.89, down almost 10% in the two sessions since the admission, a loss of more than $400 billion in market value, and about 30% below its early-October record. Nobody can attribute this recent fall merely to just the AI sell-off, as the evidence shows it was an idiosyncratic move. On Monday, as Nvidia fell 4.3%, the semiconductor index jumped nearly 7%, and direct competitors all rose, including AMD.

The last risk is one the board manufactured itself. Jensen has more than 50 direct reports and no identified successor, and one prominent analyst estimates Nvidia would be worth 25% to 30% less without him. That reflects poorly on the board. Succession is a director’s first duty, and these directors let one man become the company with no obvious succession plan until it is too late.

Even now, the reflex appears to be deference. The same statement that launched the board’s investigation declared, before a single interview, that the board had “no reason to believe that either national security or Nvidia secrets were compromised,” and that it had confidence in Jensen. A board that announces its conclusion on the day it opens the inquiry, makes one wonder whether the board has already predetermined what their preferred outcome would be rather than following the trail of wherever facts lead them in a way that protects all shareholders.

Readers of Fortune will know that we have been critics of Vice Chancellor Travis Laster on the Delaware Court of Chancery, who wrongly attacked us in his Cloudflare opinion as “advocates for dual class shares” and for founder control more broadly. Vice Chancellor Laster did not apparently understand that we are not advocates for anything; rather, we call it as we see it as fact-based academics with five decades’ experience as a widely recognized scholar of corporate governance.

We have published extensively on dual-class shares, on when they work, and when they don’t, with extensive academic analysis and quantitative research into performance of companies with dual class share structures.

In our extensive careful research, published widely in academic outlets such as the Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance as well as FORTUNE, Chief Executive Magazine and many other outlets, we have studied instances when dual class shares work well, such as when a founder or family with real money at stake uses the protection to invest through cycles and outlast short-term activists, as at Berkshire Hathaway, Alphabet and The New York Times.

Similarly, we have studied instances when dual class shares should be retired, such as when they shield controllers with ulterior motives from consequence, as at WeWork under Adam Neumann or Paramount under the Redstones.

Critics of founder control tend to treat dual-class shares as the bogeyman behind every entrenched founder, and one share, one vote as the cure. Nvidia is the case study that breaks that outdated, prosaic theory. Nvidia has a single class of common stock, not dual class shares. Jensen owns about 3.5% of it and casts 3.5% of the votes. The board is declassified, every director stands for election every year, and a simple majority of directors could replace him as CEO at any meeting. On paper he is as removable as any hired manager. In practice, no CEO in America has been more immune from proper scrutiny and oversight.

What entrenched Jensen was not the capital structure of the company; rather, it was the apparent idolatry of a board which was in thrall socially and interpersonally to Jensen Huang. He earned the adulation honestly, building a company from a booth at Denny’s into, for a time, the most valuable on earth. But the directors whose job it was to provide effective oversight to guard shareholders’ interests, apparently effectively joined the congregation. No charter provision did that, and no proxy adviser would have caught it. Nvidia has the independent majority, the lead director, the annual elections and the single share class that the checklists of ISS and Glass Lewis reward.

Nor has one share, one vote ever been a vaccine against scandal. Enron, Tyco, Wells Fargo and Boeing all had it. One of us argued in the Harvard Business Review in 2002, after Enron, that failed boards routinely passed every structural test, and that what set great boards apart was a culture of open dissent, in which directors challenge the chief executive and each other. That was true then and Nvidia shows it is true now.

When it comes to providing effective oversight, what matters is the oversight culture of the board and the quality of the board, not the ‘check the boxes’ yardsticks so often misleading propagated by governistas, such as ‘one share one vote’.

Delaware needs no new invented legal theory for measuring whether Nvidia’s board did its duty as a board in providing oversight. If shareholders bring Caremark claims against Nvidia’s directors, the court will ask what the board knew and what it did to find out. We have called out the Delaware Court of Chancery when it overreaches, and we will be again. But failing to question a celebrated CEO about a customer under federal investigation is no business judgment at all. Nvidia’s directors were paid to provide oversight over Jensen Huang, however brilliant. And in that duty, by all outside appearances, they apparently whiffed.

Aristotle famously warned, “There is no great genius without a touch of madness.” Psychiatrists call shared disorders “folie à deux.” It is up to the board to ensure a company’s owners benefit from the genius of founders but are protected by the madness.

Scarlet Witchs return is everything thats wrong with the MCU

The biggest story out of VisionQuest's finale wasn't the long-awaited reunion between twins Tommy and Billy Maximoff (Ruaridh Mollica and Joe Locke). Instead, it was the resurrection of Wanda Maximoff (Elizabeth Olsen) at the hands of Dr. Doom (Robert Downey Jr.), which occurs during the second of VisionQuest's two mid-credits scenes.

In theory, the return of Scarlet Witch is an exciting one, as she's one of the most powerful heroes in the MCU. Now that she's apparently under Dr. Doom's thrall, that will spell major trouble for the Avengers in Avengers: Doomsday. Powers aside, Wanda is also one of the MCU's most compelling characters, with Olsen's grief-stricken turn in WandaVision earning her an Emmy nomination.

Yet the reality of Wanda's return is that it, like much of the MCU post-Avengers: Endgame, is downright exhausting.

Wanda is the latest MCU mainstay to return from the dead. In addition to the return of everyone who got blipped by Thanos, characters like Loki (Tom Hiddleston) and Gamora (Zoe Saldaña) both stuck around after their deaths in Avengers: Infinity War thanks to some timeline meddling. Vision's complicated return also resulted in three MCU spin-off shows.

For Doomsday, Marvel is also bringing back characters and performers who received fitting send-offs in Avengers: Endgame. Chris Evans is returning as Steve Rogers, again likely due to timeline meddling, while Robert Downey Jr. is back, this time as Dr. Doom.

While I'm often excited to see these characters, Wanda included, the trend of constantly bringing everyone back is a tiring one. It's proof that the MCU has lost all sense of stakes: No one can be killed. Anyone can come back to be smashed together in a massive crossover event like Doomsday.

These constant returns also retroactively cheapen the most impressive feat in the MCU: Tony Stark's (Downey Jr.) mass resurrection of everyone who's been blipped. When half the universe, including some of Marvel's most beloved heroes, turned to ash at the end of Infinity War, it marked Marvel's heaviest moment to date. To reverse it would require a similarly heavy sacrifice, leading to Tony's death. Now, though, Marvel is bringing everyone back with the ease of a Dr. Doom spell, or some timey-wimey hand-waving. Next thing you know, Tony will be back. At this point, why not?

Keeping up with all the intricacies of the MCU has always been a daunting task, but it truly became a chore after Endgame. Now, instead of just watching Marvel's films, viewers also had to keep up with each TV show in order to understand what the hell was going on.

VisionQuest was already a fairly egregious example of this, as audiences had to have a solid understanding of WandaVision and Agatha All Along prior to watching, not to mention 2015's Avengers: Age of Ultron. It exacerbates the problem with its mid-credits scene, which treats Wanda's resurrection — one of the biggest MCU moments in recent years — as a simple teaser, instead of a pivotal story beat that deserves more fleshing out. Will Doomsday dive deeper into Dr. Doom's journey to resurrect Wanda, or will it just skip over it entirely and carry on with her being alive as if nothing's happened?

If so, that will be extra confusing for the casual Doomsday viewer who might not be aware of Wanda's return. Then again, they might have forgotten about her death, because there have been 10 other movies to keep track of in the four years between Doctor Strange in the Multiverse of Madness and Doomsday.

Doctor Strange in the Multiverse of Madness certainly did Wanda dirty with her villain arc and choice to sacrifice herself, so a part of me is ready to embrace what could be a new, more fulfilling arc for her. Yet a larger part of me is simply ambivalent, as I have been for much of Marvel's Phase Four and beyond. If you can't trust your own dramatic choices to stick, how can you expect me to care?

Four ideal signings for Mauricio Pochettino’s Man Utd in January

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Mauricio Pochettino is set to take the reins at Manchester United and they’ll be hoping for an immediate boost in their fortunes – but they might have to add to their squad at the next opportunity to give themselves the best chance.

United’s season so far has been a disaster. They’ve gone into the international break sitting in the Premier League’s relegation zone. It’s cost Michael Carrick his job as manager, but quite rightly, the blame is more widespread.

United’s owners, directors and players have all come in for criticism, but if anything’s going to change, it’s probably the latter.

It’d be wrong to expect a mass overhaul in January, a notoriously difficult transfer window to do big business in, but it’d be equally daft to expect United to just sit around and do nothing.

A new manager coming in gives them a chance to bring in new ideas and players to suit those ideas.

So, keeping it semi-realistic, who’d be a good signing for them under Pochettino?

It’s tempting whenever a manager gets a new job to look at who’s at his former clubs that he might like to reunite with.

Ironically in this case, the one familiar player that might most appeal from Tottenham isn’t someone Pochettino worked with at Tottenham – it’s been a while, after all – but at Chelsea instead.

Gallagher was Pochettino’s most-used player during his sole season at Chelsea, playing in 48 of his 51 games in charge.

Sure, United already did a lot of work on their midfield over the summer, but they still seem to be missing runners there. And if there’s one thing Gallagher can do, it’s run. And if there’s one thing Pochettino wants his midfielders to do… you get the picture.

On average, Gallagher has covered 12.2km per 90 minutes when playing in the Premier League this season. It’s the highest average in the squad of anyone with three or more starts.

Pochettino used to rave about Gallagher at Chelsea. Exhibit A, this ringing endorsement in February 2024:

“He is a player with a great commitment to the team. He is always trying to compensate in every situation, in offensive and defensive situations. It’s priceless to have a player like him.

“‘He is in the starting XI nearly every game and one of the captains. For me, he is the type of player that the club need to have.

“He runs, he plays, he fights. He does everything to win. He is a really important player that the coaching staff want to have on the team.”

Gallagher hasn’t exactly been a regular starter for Spurs this season, but he only joined them in January, so he might be tricky to prise away.

But hypothetically, he’d get more of a look in from Pochettino in a midfield where Kobbie Mainoo is a top prospect, but Carlos Baleba has had injury issues, Youri Tielemans hasn’t convinced with his energy and Andrey Santos remains an unknown quantity.

Santos spent the 2023-24 season out on loan away from Chelsea rather than as part of Pochettino’s squad.

Gallagher would add to United’s homegrown quota too, but for a Pochettino team, he wouldn’t just be making up the numbers.

Pochettino got the best out of Luke Shaw when they worked together at Southampton, but Luke Shaw then was different to Luke Shaw now.

Back in those days he was a talented teenage prospect. Nowadays, he’s United’s tired only natural option at left-back. Quite simply, they need to freshen it up.

Pochettino will probably be happy to keep Shaw around as a senior, trustworthy figure. But he’ll be wanting more energy from the full-back positions and that means fresh legs.

Some of Pochettino’s best teams thrived when their full-backs bombed on to offer width while the wingers cut inside. Shaw can’t really do that anymore.

One player who can get up and down the flank is Palace’s Mitchell, who might not be the most inspiring choice but could be the best of what’s available.

Palace’s precarious position, coupled with the fact that Mitchell is out of contract at the end of the season, could make January his ideal time to escape.

His attacking output hasn’t always been cited as a strong point, but he can cover plenty of ground up and down the channel. A Pochettino team don’t necessarily need their full-backs to be the ones getting goals and assists, just to offer support and threatening runs.

Mitchell also has a good record in duels and could offer United more stability in defence, as they desperately need. There might be a signing with a higher ceiling to make, but that’s the kind of thing that might have to wait until the summer.

It’s not just at left-back United need to upgrade. The battle between Diogo Dalot and Noussair Mazraoui on the other side is hardly awe-inspiring, but unless they can shift one or the other, finding an upgrade might be a summer job.

At centre-back, though, they might not be able to afford to wait. Getting Harry Maguire back from injury will be a boost when the time comes, but with Lisandro Martinez suffering a sharp downturn in form, a brand new centre-back would help.

We reckon Nottingham Forest’s Murillo would be a pretty smart signing for United. He’s strong and imposing but also good on the ball, which is perfect for Pochettino.

Is he gettable in January, though? Probably not. He signed a new contract in the summer and we know what Forest are like when it comes to selling players. Or stopping them from being sold.

File this one under the dream signing category. Murillo has Premier League experience, plenty of good years still ahead of him and the attributes that Pochettino values highly in his defenders.

Look, we can safely say Pochettino would love to work with Harry Kane again. We know there have been numerous times when United would have loved to sign Harry Kane. It’s just not happening, though, is it?

Ink dry on his new Bayern Munich contract, Kane is off the menu. But a striker is still high on United’s wish list.

By January, Benjamin Sesko will be just about ready to return from injury, but who knows in what state. In his absence, United don’t have great alternatives. Joshua Zirkzee has had enough chances now and not taken them, while Matheus Cunha and Bryan Mbeumo are probably better out wide.

The summer transfer window is usually the more likely option for big striker moves. United could still do with someone to put them on, though.

The right kind of middle-ground between luxury signing and mere stop-gap could be someone like Borussia Dortmund’s Guirassy.

He’s the same height as Kane at 6’2 and boasts some similar strengths, such as his link-up play and chance conversion.

With eight goals from 14 games this season, including four from four in the Champions League, he’s in solid form. It’s no anomaly, looking at his last few seasons: 22 goals last time out, 38 the season before, 30 in the one before that.

At 30 years old, he’s not necessarily the long-term solution. But Kane is 33 and you’d sign him, wouldn’t you?

Guirassy might be one of the best options out there in terms of value for money.

The audit tool I mention below counted 1,565 of these events in 24 hours on that node. On the registry side, the server log had 1,068 requests for that manifest digest since 2026-11-09 23:58, every one answered 404 manifest unknown.

The usual causes of manifest unknown did not fit. The tag existed in the registry's filesystem. The same node had pulled other images from the same registry that afternoon. The Deployment had not changed since March.

I found the outage on 2026-11-11 at 05:08, from a read-only audit of the cluster (kubegap) that I had run for another reason. It listed the Deployment with zero available replicas and the pull failures next to it. The investigation then went through the disk, the registry, and the node cache, in that order.

The node's root filesystem is 106.7 GiB. On 2026-11-08 free space went from 19.4 GiB at 12:45 to 2.6 GiB at 13:48. The node reported DiskPressure from 13:47 to 14:10 and again from 16:10 to 16:20.

I caused this. That afternoon I pushed 25 deploy commits for a new service, and one of them shipped a 4.5 GiB image with the service's ML models inside. The Deployment picked it up at 13:37 and the pod started at 13:40. The kubelet's evictionHard is imagefs.available<5% and nodefs.available<5%; on k3s with a single root filesystem those are the same disk. When a node crosses that threshold the kubelet first reclaims node-level resources, which for an image filesystem means deleting every image no container is using, and then evicts pods if it is still under. The jump from 2.6 GiB to 27.3 GiB free between 13:48 and 14:05 is consistent with that. By 16:15 the node's eviction counters read 23 for imagefs and 27 for nodefs, and 16 pods across the cluster carried the Evicted reason.

The signaling pod is not among those 16. It ended at 13:46:42, one minute before the DiskPressure condition was recorded, with exit code 0. That is not what an eviction looks like, and I could not read the kubelet journal on that node that day, so I do not know what stopped it. The sequence I can show is: the container stopped inside the disk-pressure window; the first replacement died during the second window; the second replacement needed an image that was no longer in the node's store.

The models-in-image problem got the obvious fix the same evening: models on a volume, 1.6 GiB image.

I turned it off after another image broke, and did not go back to check what else the flag had removed.

This was reported as distribution/distribution#3004 in 2019 and again as #3178 in 2020. The fix, #4285, was merged in April 2024 and is only in the 3.x releases. 2.8.3, the last 2.x release, predates it.

containerd on the node had the image, and with the default imagePullPolicy: IfNotPresent for a non-latest tag nothing asks the registry for an image that is already present. The pod restarted five times between March and November, most recently on 2026-10-28, each time from the local store. The first client to ask the registry for the platform manifest after March was the replacement pod on 2026-11-08, after the cached copy had been removed.

The save button is bg-blue-500, but your brand color is teal. One card has 18px of padding while every other card has 16. A grey shows up that isn't in any file you wrote.

What actually fixes it is boring: put your design values in code, give the agent a short rules file it reads every time, and check the output with two searches. Here's the whole setup.

The rules file needs something to point at, so tokens come first. Plain CSS works fine:

Two small things here save a lot of cleanup later. Hover and focus get their own tokens, because if they don't, the agent invents a "slightly darker" shade every time it needs one. And dark mode changes the values behind the names, not the names themselves, so components never need a dark-mode color of their own.

Spacing you can leave alone. Tailwind's spacing already runs on a 4px step, so you only need to say which steps are allowed. If you haven't settled on a scale, I wrote up a ready-made 8pt grid scale here.

The 10 Bleakest Christmas Movies, Ranked

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Christmas may be the holliest, jolliest, brightest time of the year, but it can also be the darkest, coldest, and most bleak as well. It's that wonderful dichotomy that makes it such a fertile time to set movies in. That has inspired a great many Christmas stories that are uplifting, even where they're also surprisingly dark, like It's a Wonderful Life or A Christmas Carol. It's also inspired some movies that only use the holiday as a backdrop for all sorts of dark, dreadful content.

The bleakest Christmas movies offer a bitter antidote to all the saccharine sweetness that overloads the most wonderful time of the year. They make us feel bad for a few hours so that we can then put our own lives into perspective. Even though 2026 has been one mean mother of a year so far for many, and the holiday season doesn't yet seem to have anything to offer as respite, we can at least take comfort in being miserable together. And we can do it while watching the ten bleakest Christmas movies.

At this point, there are more bleak movies featuring Silent Night in the title than there are festive ones. There's an entire horror franchise that started with a killer in a Santa Claus suit, a violent John Woo action revenge thriller, and this apocalyptic dark comedy where a Christmas party is pretense for mass suicide. Christmas parties have served as ironic settings for all kinds of dark occurrences in alternative holiday movies, and while Silent Night isn't quite as sharp as it wants to be, it's still the bleakest party you can attend this season.

Nell (Keira Knightley) and Simon (Matthew Goode) are hosting their annual Christmas party at their country home. It's also their final Christmas party since an ecological disaster is sweeping across the globe, killing everyone in its path. That's why all the attendees have taken a page out of Jim Jones' book and plan on committing suicide before the bell tolls. As the directorial debut for Camille Griffin, Silent Night uses its premise to poke fun at polite society in a bleakly funny way. It's a twisted holiday party that's likely more bearable than another political debate with your far-right relatives.

If this isn't the bleakest Christmas movie ever made, it's definitely the most brutal. A horror film which is often categorized as part of the New French Extremity, Inside was directed by Alexandre Bustillo and Julien Maury. Its story is centered around a birth on Christmas Eve, but instead of some hopeful Nativity-inspired narrative, it's a relentless, ultraviolent survival story. Like many other films of the same cinematic movement, Inside is a nasty, cruel piece of work that is definitively not meant for everyone. It takes a strong stomach to make it through this film, but it will definitely put your Christmas woes into perspective.

Sarah (Allyson Paradis), is a recently widowed woman who is set to give birth on Christmas Day. She plans to spend the eve before alone, having become withdrawn due to her grief. That's when a strange Woman (Béatrice Dalle) comes knocking at her door, demanding Sarah give her the unborn baby. What follows is a very red Christmas that even the most seasoned gorehounds may have trouble with. Inside is a truly bleak slasher movie that turns the miracle of birth into a holiday nightmare.

Spending time with your significant other's family is a part of the holidays that we all have to accept, and while for some that might mean enduring a bit of casual racism peppered into a long-winded story, it's got nothing on the tension that comes with being the new stepmom to a couple of kids who resent you. Add in a remote cabin in the woods and there's a recipe for a very bleak Christmas. That's the set-up for The Lodge, a psychological horror film from directors Veronika Franz and Severin Fiala, best known for their debut film Goodnight Mommy.

Grace (Riley Keough) is a former cult member and soon-to-be stepmother of two children who she's spending the Christmas holiday with in the titular location. When her fiancée leaves for work, Grace begins to experience strange occurrences, which she can't be sure whether are supernatural in nature, or the work of the children. The Lodge takes a couple of twists down its snowy path toward a ending that is as bleak as the frigid weather in the film. It's an atmospheric horror film anchored by Keough's bravura performance.

Elaine (Eva Birthistle) and Jonah (Stephen Campbell Moore) are spending the holidays with family along with their two young children and teenage daughter. Things take a swift turn towards violence as a virus infects the adolescents and they begin attacking with ferocity. The Children knows exactly which buttons it's pushing with its sickening premise, and it pushes harder and harder as the movie goes on. It's a twisted horror movie that proves that family can be murder around the holidays.

Family drives during the holidays can be hellish, but Dead End takes it to a more literal level. This underrated supernatural horror movie has a Twilight Zone-esque premise that heads toward an inevitably macabre ending. Directed by Jean-Baptise Andrea and Fabrice Canepa, and starring genre stalwarts Ray Wise and Lin Shaye, it's a bleak but darkly entertaining horror movie for the holidays.

While on a drive on Christmas Eve, a family led by Frank (Wise) finds themselves lost in the woods on a seemingly endless road. Their path is beset further by a mysterious woman in white and a hearse that's in pursuit of them. Dead End doesn't do anything spectacular or particularly original with its genre elements, but it's simple and effective, and definitely the bleakest holiday road trip.

Director Álex de la Iglesia makes films that often defy easy summation or categorization. Similarly, their tones can be just as equally indefinable. Most of his films have some degree of comedy to them, but they are also often grotesque, violent and unremittingly bleak. The Day of the Beast is no different. It's a satanic horror comedy that's absurd and outrageous in its approach to Christmas, with a sardonic mood that belies its bleak narrative.

Father Ángel (Álex Angulo) is a priest who heads to Madrid to commit as many sins as he can manage, in the hopes that it will allow him to gain access to the pending birth of the Antichrist so he can commit satanic infanticide. He's eventually aided in his quest by a heavy metal music fan and an occult television host. If you're looking to put Christ back into Christmas, The Day of the Beast isn't the right movie for you, but if you're looking for an irreverently bleak vision of faith, it's a gift from Hell.

You may not remember Christmas playing a big part in David Fincher's adaptation of Stieg Larsson's The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo, but the holiday actually bookends this bleak thriller. The film was also infamously marketed as the "Feel Bad Movie of Christmas," since it also released during the holiday season, and it fully lives up to the promise of that tagline. Filled with horrific acts of violence perpetrated against women, this chilly thriller is completely uninterested in making you feel excited or enthralled by its bloody action. It wants to rub your nose in it and then send you back out into the cold.

Lisbeth Salander (Rooney Mara) is the titular character, a skilled hacker who's endured a lifetime of abuse who finds a way to enact cathartic justice when she's recruited by disgraced newspaperman Mikael Blomkvist (Daniel Craig) to help him find a killer of women on a secluded island. Fincher's meticulous and cold direction meshes perfectly with Larsson's unflinching narrative, making this the definitive film version of the novel. It was supposed to be followed by sequels, but it turns out American audiences weren't ready to embrace such a bleak Christmas blockbuster.

No genre does bleak like noir, and when it comes to Christmas noirs, there's none bleaker, or better, than Blast of Silence. This low-budget hitman thriller, directed by and starring Allen Baron, is set in New York City during the holiday season, and utilizes both its mean streets and its bright lights to great discordant effect. Between its bleak visuals and blunt violence, the movie means to leave a bad taste in your mouth in the most noir way possible.

Frankie Bono (Baron) is a hitman who has come home to the Big Apple at Christmastime not to see family, but to kill a stranger. Between plotting his kill, Bono reconnects with some past acquaintances and deals with some less than savory underworld types. There's an authentic grittiness to the film, and Baron makes for a suitably scabrous lead. He's an anti-social man of violence, with a second-person narration that runs through his head. Blast of Silence is rough around its edges, doesn't pull its punches and ends on a suitably dark note.

Slashers are nothing new under the Christmas tree. There have been plenty of psycho Santas, killer snowmen and masked maniacs over the years. Even the seminal Black Christmas has three separate editions. While the 2006 remake is sleazy as hell, and the 2019 version gave it a Me Too makeover, there's nothing that can compete with Bob Clark's bleak original. It's a totemic slasher film which had a huge influence on John Carpenter's Halloween, and it's just as twisted.

Set at a sorority house during a Christmas Party, the film follows the house sisters as they are increasingly harassed by an anonymous caller, who, unbeknownst to them, has also taken up residence in their attic. As the night goes on, more and more of the women are stalked and killed, while the police ineffectually try to help. There's a strong feminist undercurrent to Black Christmas, which is more subversive and intelligent than many of the slashers that would follow in its wake. That the women it portrays are such well-rounded and independent characters only makes their demises that much more effective. Between this film and A Christmas Story, Clark covered the entire spectrum of Christmas movies, from happy and bright to hopeless and bleak.

Things are always bleak in a dystopia, even when it's Christmas, and even when that dystopia comes with a heavy helping of satire. Terry Gilliam's magnum opus Brazil is set around the Christmas holiday, which highlights the consumerist society which is run by inefficient corporate bureaucrats. It also meshes well with the whimsical nature of the movie, which features several fantasy sequences and absurdist depictions of its industrial future world. Despite, or perhaps because of, the humor and whimsy, the film is still unremittingly bleak, depicting a world where the government-sponsored torture and death of an innocent man comes down to a typo.

Sam Lowry (Jonathan Pryce) is a sad little cog in the massive machinery of the totalitarian government who dreams of being a hero with wings who saves a distressed damsel. When he sees a woman who resembles the damsel of his dreams, his pursuit of her turns him into a fugitive of the state. Filled with the kind of outlandish imagery Gilliam is known for, Brazil influenced a decade's worth of dystopian cities on film, and its sobering vision of the future has only become more disconcerting for many viewers who fear we're edging toward it as a reality. Executives at Universal Pictures at one time wanted to cut the film's infamously sad ending, but it remains intact as the perfect punctuation for one of the bleakest Christmas movies ever made.

Warren Buffett's Successor, Greg Abel, Just Bought $9.6 Billion of the Oracle of Omaha's Favorite Stock (No, Not Alphabet!)

These Will Be the Snowiest Adventure Towns This Winter, According to a Meteorologist

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Colorado’s San Juan Mountains are heavily favored for atmospheric rivers to create stellar snow totals. (Photo: Getty)

Every year, America’s snowiest towns are typically located in the mountains or on the banks of the Great Lakes. With a few ski resorts already open, if you’re a powder chaser you are likely chomping at the bit for ski season to get into full swing—especially with projections of Super El Niño turbocharging snowfall.

I’m a television meteorologist with over 30 years of forecasting experience, as well as an avid skier and snowshoer. Growing up in Greenville, South Carolina, I prayed for snow days. Now, as an adventurer, I chase the powder.

A prime sign of Super El Niño’s influence is what just happened in Chile. In late August, a resort called Ski Portillo got multiple snow dumps tallying over 30 inches in a few days. Its record-breaking season hit almost 500 inches, double the  typical average.

As Super El Niño continues to strengthen, the NOAA Climate Prediction Center is still forecasting a greater-than-83-percent  chance of a strong-to-very strong El Niño, likely from January through March. But not everywhere is going to get a stellar snow season.

With the strengthened subtropical jet stream, Super El Niño means an active southern storm track and wetter-than-normal conditions for the southern tier of the United States. That favors the Southwest, the Southern Rockies, and California’s Sierra Nevada.

The kicker to the season—one that’s not getting a lot of buzz—is the Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO), a fluctuation of ocean temperature in the Pacific, entering a cooling phase. That was a big factor in Juneau, Alaska, getting its record-setting snowfall of 201.2 inches during the 2006-7 winter. A strong El Niño and a cool PDO are a highly unusual pairing we haven’t seen since 1972-73, a winter with a significant snowstorm that buried parts of the Southeastern U.S.

Then there is the recent weird behavior of the polar vortex, a rotating mass of cold air and low pressure. Its unusual weakening would normally send a significant Arctic blast into the United States, but there’s not enough cold air across Canada yet for this to happen.

Simply put, it looks like we’re in for supersized and wild weather this winter.

These are my picks for the adventure towns most likely to receive the snowiest winter, including a couple of spots that don’t normally top the “most snow” list.

Based on historical records during very strong or Super El Niños, and its maritime air pipeline at full throttle, Mammoth Mountain Ski Area is pretty much a lock for a big snow year. While the 2015-2016 very strong El Niño recorded a near-average season of 393 inches, the 1982-1983 Super El Niño produced a whopping 546.3 inches. In 1983, January picked up 118 inches of snow and March had 112 inches, extending Mammoth’s ski season through late July.

Off-Slopes Snow Fun: Take a thrilling ride on the mountain coaster or go snow tubing at Woolly’s Adventure Summit. You can zipline year-round too, or zoom through scenic terrain with Snowmobile Adventures. For a surreal, scenic stop, head to Mono Lake, just 30 minutes away, to see its pogonip ice fog. Because of the lake’s high salinity, it doesn’t freeze. The low-lying ice fog against the limestone tufa towers is otherworldly. Plus, it’s a prime place for wintering waterfowl, such as bald eagles.

Basecamp: The Village Lodge Mammoth puts you in the heart of The Village, whereas the historic Mammoth Mountain Inn is best for ski-in/ski-out access.

Where to après: For a next-level après, book the VIP Après Experience at Lincoln Bar at Canyon Lodge. For late-night shenanigans, join the “Night Shift” party at Outbound’s Bar Sierra. The best place to chill on a slopeside sundeck is Yodler Restaurant & Bar. For local sips, stop by Shelter Distilling, Mammoth Brewing Company, or Distant Brewing.

Yes, there’s skiing in Arizona, and it’s not far from the Grand Canyon. For a high-desert ski experience, the historic settlement of Fort Valley makes the perfect playground for the Arizona Snowbowl. This tiny unincorporated community is just northwest of Flagstaff, which averages more snow annually than Denver or Salt Lake City.

With a front-row seat to atmospheric river impulses riding the subtropical jet, Fort Valley and Arizona Snowbowl should have well-above-normal snow totals, with the heaviest snow likely in February.

Located in the San Francisco Peaks, Snowbowl historically gets spikes in snowfall during strong El Niño years. During the 2023-24 El Niño, Arizona Snowbowl recorded 281 inches of snow. Its six inches of snow October 24-25, 2026, is a good sign for Arizona’s only ski resort, and for Fort Valley. It is also interesting to note that during the 1972-73 winter of strong El Niño and cool PDO, nearby Flagstaff got a record-setting 210 inches of snow.

Off-Slopes Snow Fun: After a morning on the slopes, make a stop at the Museum of Northern Arizona. Don’t miss the “Native Peoples of the Colorado Plateau” and “Southwestern Jewelry” exhibits. Go cross-country skiing or snowshoeing at Arizona Nordic Village or the Peak View Winter Recreation and Parking Area in the Coconino National Forest. Just 20 minutes away, Flagstaff Snow Park is perfect for snow tubing and sledding fun.

Take a day trip to Grand Canyon National Park. The South Rim is just over an hour away. The bonus of a winter visit is the lack of crowds. You can easily visit three national monuments in a day: see Ancestral Puebloan dwellings in Walnut Canyon and Wupatki, and experience a moon-like landscape at Sunset Crater Volcano, where every astronaut who has  stepped foot on the moon has trained. Bundle up for stargazing at nearby Lowell Observatory in Flagstaff for a truly out-of-this-world evening.

Basecamp:  Book a mountain cabin for a cozy stay. Basecamp at Snowbowl is a good budget-friendly and pet-friendly option. Plus, you get two complimentary lift tickets per night.

Where to après: Basecamp Restaurant is the go-to spot, as it’s less than 5 minutes from Fort Valley and on-site at Basecamp at Snowbowl, located 7 miles from the ski area. You’re also close enough to Flagstaff to devour delicious eats at favorites like Diablo Burger, The Toasted Owl Cafe, or MartAnne’s Burrito Palace.

Sitting at 9,800 feet in Dixie National Forest, Brian Head is the highest incorporated town in Utah. The rustic mountain town is named for nearby Brian Head Peak. Dubbed the “highest resort town in America,” it’s home to the small ski area of Brian Head Resort, which has the highest base elevation in Utah.

Brian Head is in a sweet spot for Super El Niño’s elevated snow amounts. Plus, its high elevation helps sustain snow quality. Multiple rounds of significant snow in October were the prelude to what should be above-average snowfall and cooler temperatures this winter.

Off-Slopes Snow Fun: Soak in the scenery of red rock hoodoos dusted with snow at nearby Cedar Breaks National Monument, where you can go cross-country skiing and snowshoeing. Explore the backcountry on a snowmobile tour with Thunder Mountain Motorsports.

Zoom down the hill while snow tubing at Giant Steps Snow Tubing Park. Bundle up to admire the night sky while enjoying a some high-altitude stargazing. Brian Head also makes a great high-elevation base camp for day trips to Bryce Canyon National Park and Zion National Park.

Basecamp: Book two nights at Brian Head Lodge and get two free lift tickets with promo code FreedomToSki. There are also numerous cabins and condominiums for rent.

Where to après: Last Chair Grill and Brews on the third floor of Giant Steps Lodge is the premier slopeside spot. They boast the largest beer selection in southern Utah and have live music on Fridays and Saturdays. For more low-key options in town, head to 1st Chair Thai and Ramen or Pizanos Pizza.

Telluride is my number-one pick for where I want to go this winter. I love the Victorian architecture in the historic mining town paired with the ski resort experience in Mountain Village, connected by the free Telluride Gondola.

Off-Slopes Snow Fun: Whether you go to Bridal Veil Falls—Colorado’s tallest free-falling waterfall—or hike along locals’ favorite, the Jud Wiebe Trail, winter hiking and snowshoeing around Telluride are breathtakingly beautiful. Experience some of the best ice climbing in the world on a guided climb with San Juan Outdoor Adventures. For the ultimate backcountry experience, try heli-skiing or riding with Telluride Helitrax. Go with Telluride Outfitters on a snowmobile tour to Alta Ghost Town—a  unique way to experience Telluride’s mining history.

Basecamp: I prefer staying in town with the European chalet vibes of The Hotel Telluride being my personal favorite. If your preference is closer to the slopes, the Madeline Hotel and Residences in Mountain Village is excellent.

Where to après: Bellying up to the Historic Bar at the New Sheridan Hotel is an absolute must in Telluride. Dating back to 1895, it’s considered one of the most famous bars in the West. For the best sunset, take the gondola to Allred’s Restaurant.

Historically Wolf Creek gets the most snow in Colorado, with multi-day storms that drop feet of snow, such as the February 17-21, 2026, such as storm cycles that dumped almost five feet of snow (59 inches). During the El Niño winter of 1978-79, Wolf Creek got 807 inches of snow, which is Colorado’s record for a single-season total at a ski area or resort.

There are three reasons Wolf Creek gets so much snow. First is its high elevation. The base sits at 10,300 feet. Second is the funneling effect of its local topography. Located just south of the summit of Wolf Creek Pass, it sits in a unique L-shaped bowl that acts like a catchall basin for snow. Third are the storm tracks pushing up against the San Juans, which should bode well for a high snow total this season.

Off-Slopes Snow Fun: As one of the premier destinations on Colorado’s 800-mile Historic Hot Springs Loop, Pagosa Springs on the west side of Wolf Creek Pass is perfect for a ski-and-soak getaway. Hike to frozen Treasure Falls. The 105-foot plunge waterfall between Wolf Creek Ski Area and Pagosa Springs freezes into massive ice columns, attracting technical ice climbers.

Take the scenic drive, then hike the Piedra Ice Fissures Trail to see ice caves. Famous for its historic adobe architecture and vibrant art scene, Taos doesn’t normally rank high on the list of America’s snowiest adventure towns. But this year it could, since it’s in the favorable storm track of the active subtropical jet stream.

Less than 20 miles north of town, Taos Ski Valley has over 300 inches of snow annually. In town, snow typically peaks in December and January but continues to fall well into March at the ski resort. Angel Fire Resort is also about 45 minutes away.

Off-Slopes Snow Fun: Taos Pueblo is a UNESCO World Heritage Site that’s been inhabited for over 1,000 years. With the chill in the air and fireplaces glowing, I love visiting the pueblo in December. Grab a latte in Dawn Butterfly then stroll through the artisan shops, such as Pueblo Runner. Make sure to sample the fry bread while visiting. Taos Pueblo does close annually for six to eight weeks in late winter and early spring for traditional ceremonies.

Beyond the nearby slopes, you can also go snowshoeing, cross-country skiing, or winter hiking in the Carson National Forest. Soak in the therapeutic waters at Ojo Caliente, about an hour away from Taos. There are several museums and historic homes to tour in town, or you can simply stroll around Taos Plaza. Chocolate + Cashmere is my favorite spot to find something cozy, and the Sage Fine Art Gallery is filled with works by award-winning local artists.

Basecamp: If you want to stay where you ski or ride, The Blake is a ski-in/ski-out property at Taos Ski Valley. For something completely off-grid, book a stay at Earthship Biotecture.

In town, the Adobe Bar is a classic après haunt next to historic Doc Martin’s Restaurant. My favorite place in town to chow down is Orlando’s New Mexican Café. Go hungry, and order the blue corn enchiladas. New Mexican enchiladas are layered, rather than rolled.

Located on the cusp of the transition zone between wet and dry, historical data shows mixed results for the northern Sierra. Strong El Niños trend toward below-normal seasons, while very strong El Niños historically produce above-normal snow averages. It’s a roll of the dice for Tahoe.

FAMU football QB Isaiah Knowles turns 'what-if' into lasting impact

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

Isaiah Knowles' daily routine looks quite different nowadays.

Instead of suiting up as Florida A&M's starting quarterback, the graduate student is now holding a practice itinerary, teaching, and observing the offense from the sidelines during games.

"Man, I wish I was able to play," Knowles told the Tallahassee Democrat. "But given the cards have been dealt, I'm just happy that I got an opportunity to help in any way that I can."

After winning back-to-back Southern Intercollegiate Athletic Conference Offensive Player of the Year awards and an NCAA Division II Black College Football National Championship at Albany State, Knowles made the no-brainer decision to transfer to FAMU to follow head coach Quinn Fordham Gray Sr.

The Tampa native entered as the presumed starter for Gray's first season at FAMU following their successful tenure together in the Peach State.

But his time under center was cut short to two games.

On Sept. 6, Knowles broke his right throwing-hand thumb in the Rattlers' Orange Blossom Classic football game against South Carolina State. While attempting a pass, his hand hit off of a teammate's helmet.

The quarterback attempted to pop his thumb into place himself. FAMU trainers tried also to no avail.

Knowles ran to the sideline, and then X-rays later revealed the severity of the injury that ended his season and ultimately his college football career.

"They told me there were too many bones moving," Knowles recalled the injury, "So we covered it up, walked back to the X-rays, and that's when they told me what happened."

Knowles' injury was the first domino to fall in a disastrous FAMU season as the Rattlers (2-8, 1-5 in SWAC) are on track for their fewest wins since 2015.

"You know, I look at that kid every day and just think, 'What if?' But you can't harp on things that happened in the past that aren't in your favor," Gray said of Knowles. "Losing a guy like that was tremendous for us in terms of our development this season."

After two starts in relief of Knowles, backup Dustin Fletcher suffered a similar throwing-hand thumb injury and has been out since mid-September.

So, the Rattlers have turned to true freshman quarterback Armond Parker, who's started six games this year fresh out of high school.

Parker was an early FAMU enrollee, arriving on campus this past January for organized team activities and spring football training camp, wanting to learn everything from Knowles, who's spent four years in Gray's offensive system. They've "been attached at the hip" ever since, and Knowles has "been there every step of the way."

"It's a testament to his character," said FAMU running back Jamal Hailey. "Still waking up at five in the morning, showing up so he can be there so our freshman quarterback can be prepared week to week."

Knowles began watching games from the coach's box for an aerial view of the offense.

That stopped once Parker asked him to be on the sidelines to be a mentor, explain situations and playcalling rationale, and to bring a calming voice during the madness of a college football game.

"He keeps me confident, making sure I know what I'm doing, and takes me through progressions with my decision-making," Parker explained Knowles' impact. "With him being on the sideline, that's a big help for me — keeping my head up, keeping me confident."

Parker has experienced freshman highs and lows.

He's fifth in the Southwestern Athletic Conference with 1,790 passing yards and 14 touchdown throws in eight appearances out of 10 possible games.

However, the 18-year-old is still in his developmental phase, completing 56% of his passes and also tossing 11 interceptions, many of which turned into pick-sixes.

"Helping him battle through adversity, especially as a freshman quarterback," Knowles said of Parker. "See him grow each and every game; I think FAMU's going to be in good hands."

After his arm was in a sling for a few weeks after the injury, Knowles has recovered from surgery and began treatment last week. His range of motion and strength are beginning to return, and he should be able to throw in December.

Knowles is hoping to get an invite to February's sixth-annual HBCU Legacy Bowl, an all-star showcase game for draft-eligible players from Black Colleges. Knowles shared that the Legacy Bowl invited him the last two years, but he wasn't leaving college yet. FAMU coaches have been in constant contact with the Legacy Bowl, hoping Knowles gets the nod to make the trip to Atlanta.

Knowles will then perform before scouts at FAMU's Pro Day, typically held in March.

The National Football League, Canadian Football League, United Football League — the opportunities are endless for aspiring pro players.

Knowles is encouraged by scouts telling him that they have enough film on him from Albany State that the season-ending injury may not hinder his professional football dreams as much as one might expect.

"Lord willing, I go get an opportunity somewhere," Knowles said. "If not, I'll jump right into coaching."

Knowles time in college is coming to an end.

He began at the United States Naval Academy before transferring to Gray's inaugural Albany State recruiting class ahead of the 2023 season.

This Saturday, Knowles will be one of many Rattlers as part of the pregame Senior Day ceremony before FAMU battles Mississippi Valley State with a 3 p.m. kickoff on Ken Riley Field at Bragg Memorial Stadium. He has a bachelor's degree in exercise science from Albany State and is in the process of a second bachelor's in interdisciplinary studies from FAMU.

"He's going to be missed as he's missed now," Gray said. "That's a part of the game. You got guys that come through your program and are there for a certain period of time. And ultimately, they leave you to become those great citizens that you hope to develop them into, to become the great husbands and fathers that they are truly meant to be."

On Saturday, Knowles will be joined by his fiancée, Jayda, and parents Chuck and Linda. And even though his time donning the Orange and Green on the field was brief, his impact on the program in a lone year could be everlasting.

"It's going to be a surreal moment," Knowles said. "Definitely looking forward to walking there with my family, dapping up Coach Gray on the field, and getting loved on."
