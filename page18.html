TPU Model Design Considerations and Optimization Guide

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Google designed TPUs from the ground up to be exceptionally efficient for machine learning workloads, delivering top-tier performance per total cost of ownership (TCO). Because TPUs trade general-purpose hardware scheduling for wide systolic arrays, dedicated SparseCore engines, and custom interconnect fabrics, adhering to specific architectural best practices is essential to achieve maximum performance. To help developers get the most out of TPUs, we are launching the open-source AI-Hypercomputer/tpu-codesign guide. This post highlights its key principles—spanning shape alignment, architectural choices like LatentMoE, and parallelism strategies—to accelerate LLMs, Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, and other hardware-intensive workloads on TPUs.

Performance tuning starts with model definition. Architectural choices made before the first training step set permanent ceilings on MFU (Model FLOP Utilization), cost, and serving latency. This guide gives concrete, hardware-grounded design rules for teams building or adapting models for TPUs, organized into three groups:

How to use this guide. The shape rules apply to anyone designing a new model. If you are adapting an existing model rather than designing one, the Expressing Parallelism section applies without touching the checkpoint. The general rules hold for any large-matmul architecture (dense LLMs, multimodal, and world models), and the companion AI-Hypercomputer/tpu-codesign repository grows model- and workload-specific sections over time.

Eighth-generation TPUs. These principles carry forward to TPU 8t and TPU 8i and to the newly announced TPU 8tx and TPU 8i Max. The reasoning stays the same and the thresholds move with the hardware. TPU 8t and TPU 8i both add native FP4. For training, TPU 8t also brings more balanced Vector Processing Unit (VPU) scaling. For serving, TPU 8i adds the Boardfly topology, a Collectives Acceleration Engine (CAE), and 3x more on-chip SRAM. We note the effects inline in Principles 5–8, and follow-up posts will cover eighth-generation designs in depth.

Measured evidence. Alongside the published sources, notes marked Measured carry results from our own Ironwood profiling program: open 20–30B MoEs (a Qwen3-30B-derived LatentMoE variant; GPT-OSS-20B) plus dense controls, single slice (except for the 512-chip TPU v7x and 1,024-chip TPU v4 ablations in Principle 4), BF16, training shapes up to 32K sequence, all against tuned-systems baselines. Treat the trends and rankings as transferable, the exact percentages as config-specific.

TPUs are purpose-built custom ML ASICs designed from the ground up to deliver state-of-the-art performance per total cost of ownership (TCO). While their architectural choices—such as wide 256×256 systolic arrays, software-managed VMEM, dedicated SparseCore engines, and direct multi-dimensional ICI fabrics—enable exceptional efficiency, they inherently create specific structural constraints and programming patterns that must be carefully followed to unlock maximal performance. Reaching peak efficiency requires aligning model shapes, routing, and parallelism directly with these underlying hardware characteristics:

Make d_model, FFN and expert intermediate widths (d_ff), latent widths, attention projections, and padded vocabulary sizes (used as contracting or output dimensions) multiples of 256 to match the 256×256 MXU systolic array. Token/batch axes and counts such as heads, layers, and experts are exempt. The most frequently missed dimension is head_dim: at 64 or 128, flash-attention QK products leave the MXU ≥ 50% idle. Reshape to head_dim = 256 with proportionally fewer Q and KV heads, preserving total projection widths and logical KV cache bytes (iso-quality in our ablations).

Measured: Reshaping Qwen3-30B MoE attention from 32 × 128 to 16 × 256 (identical parameters and FLOPs) sped up end-to-end training, with gains widening at longer context:

These rules apply for non-quadratic attention as well, notably GDN ablations from Yang et al. 2025 also noted at par or better quality for head dimension 256 for their Gated DeltaNet Attention.

XLA compiles each program for exact tensor shapes, so every buffer has a fixed address and every loop a known trip count, and a new shape means a recompilation and a host stall. What about dynamic shapes? Dynamic workloads fit this model: the trick is to separate the shape of a buffer from the extent of the data inside it.

Instead of padding to the largest group or the longest sequence, we keep the buffers at fixed shapes (tokens sorted by expert and padded only at the tail, the KV cache as a fixed pool of pages) and describe the ragged structure with a few small integer arrays (group sizes and offsets; KV lengths, the page table, cumulative query lengths). Pallas copies these into the TensorCore's scalar memory before the grid starts (PrefetchScalarGridSpec), and the kernel reads them to choose the next tile and bound its loops, with a mask covering a tile that straddles a group boundary or a partly filled last page. The data dependence becomes an address and a trip count instead of a shape, so the compiled program is the same for every routing and every batch composition and the kernel runs at the unpadded FLOP count.

Standard EP dispatches every routed token at full d_model width, making EP + FSDP slower than plain FSDP in our Ironwood benchmarks. LatentMoE projects routed tokens from d_model down to latent width ℓ (d/ℓ ≈ 2–4×) before dispatch, executes experts in latent space, and projects back up after combine (introduced by Nemotron 3 and adopted by 962B MAI-Base-1 and 2.8T Kimi K3):

Measured: On a 30B / 128-expert MoE, an ℓ = 768 (2.67×) LatentMoE cut on-device permute time 2.8×, lifted end-to-end training throughput +14% tokens/s (322 vs 270 TFLOP/s/chip, or +19%, since the latent projections add ~5% FLOPs per token; tokens stay ℓ-wide past the down-projection, so on-device permute and combine shrink alongside EP all-to-all), and freed ~25 GB/device of activation HBM when remat checkpoints were anchored on the compressed latent. While current MoE recipes in tpu-recipes run FSDP (+ TP), LatentMoE + FP8 dispatch makes EP roofline-viable when per-device batch is too small to hide FSDP weight all-gathers. Under both FSDP and EP, avoid over-fragmenting experts: small tokens × K / N group sizes starve MXU tiles.

When candidate MoE layouts match on quality, pick the one with higher compute intensity at a similar active-parameter budget. A larger shared expert (wider d_shared) shifts work into dense, communication-free GEMMs that overlap EP dispatch/combine in training and amortize weight reads across the decode batch. Fewer, wider routed experts (halving N and K while doubling d_ff at fixed sparsity K/N) cut dispatch traffic in half and double per-expert GEMM fill. Optimal (N, K) grows with model size, and over-coarsening hurts routing diversity. Ablate expert granularity and the shared-to-routed split at your target scale rather than copying GPU co-designed models, since the TPU optimum is more shared-heavy.

Measured: On a 63B LatentMoE (ℓ = 768, K/N = 1/32, 8K context, 512 TPU v7x chips at EP = 128), halving (N, K) from (512, 16) to (256, 8) while doubling d_ff and the shared expert lifted training throughput +57% tok/s and held lower loss across 120.8B tokens:

Low-concurrency decode streams model weights every token (~300 concurrent tokens per weight shard are needed in dense FP8 to reach compute roofline). Combine two architectural levers with explicit concurrency sizing:

Norms, activations, masking, routing math and quantization scale applies run on the Vector Processing Unit (VPU), which is roughly two orders of magnitude below the MXU in throughput and executes in sequence with the matmuls unless XLA fuses it into an adjacent GEMM or we move it off the TensorCore. Therefore we drop reshapes and transposes that carry no quality, standardize on fusable RMSNorm and SwiGLU so the elementwise work folds into the neighbouring GEMM, and offload the MoE sort, gather and scatter to the SparseCore.

Note that fused VPU work is free only while its cycle count fits inside the MXU window it is fused into. A quick illustration of this concept is block-scaled fp8 GEMM.

With a scale per block of B elements along the contraction axis, the MXU emits one partial sum per output element per block and the VPU rescales each one (about three vector ops), so per output element the MXU does 2K flops and the VPU does 3·ceil(K/B) ops. The rescale hides when B ≥ 1.5 × r_mxu / r_vpu, which on v7x (fp8 peak 4,614 TFLOPS against a 19.2 TFLOPS vector peak, a ratio near 240) puts the threshold near 340. In simulation on a DeepSeek-V3 expert shape, block 512 runs at the per-tensor time, block 256 is VPU-bound at 1.2x and block 128 at 2.4x. Achieved MXU efficiency is lower than peak, which lowers the effective ratio and can make the smaller blocks workable; the way to decide is to measure the GEMM at each block instead of using peak ratio. This threshold is specific to Ironwood. TPU 8t is designed with specialized support for scaling so quantization for MXFP4 and MXFP8 formats has no overhead. Re-measure the block-size threshold there instead of reusing 340. The rule itself carries forward: keep vector work hidden inside a matmul window.

For workloads dominated by BF16-sensitive matmuls (most notably long-context quadratic attention where direct FP8 degrades accuracy), native single-pass BF16 leaves MXU capacity unused whenever hardware low-precision MXU throughput exceeds native BF16 throughput by more than the pass count. Use the multi-pass BF16 algorithm to execute BF16-accurate matmuls via multiple passes over the higher-throughput lower-precision MXU pipeline without losing numerical fidelity. This matters more as low-precision throughput pulls ahead of BF16. TPU 8t adds native FP4, which doubles MXU throughput. On Ironwood the FP8 to BF16 ratio is exactly 2:1, so two or more passes cannot beat native BF16.

Assign communication-heavy parallel axes to the fastest physical links and keep logical mesh factors aligned with torus dimensions:

Minimum-degree CP matched to kernel trade-offs: All-gather CP is simplest (though its all-gathers can sit exposed) when paired with zigzag load balancing (context_parallel_load_balance in MaxText) so causal masking does not idle tail ranks; Ring CP overlaps collectives but trades off SplashAttention chunk efficiency; Ulysses A2A CP avoids causal skew but exposes all-to-alls and caps at head count.

Start every project with the quality-safe foundation: 256-aligned dimensions (1), static shapes with dropless routing (2), one parallelism plan mapped to physical links (8), wall-clock ablations on a tuned stack, and published reference targets (both in Measuring what matters below). For MoEs, add LatentMoE (3) after an ablation to cut routing/dispatch traffic and decode weight reads. Then prioritize by bottleneck regime:

Google engineered TPUs from the ground up as custom ML ASICs to deliver top-tier performance per total cost of ownership (TCO). Unlocking that potential requires bridging the gap between hardware architecture and model design. By standardizing on 256-aligned shapes, leveraging LatentMoE, and mapping parallel axes directly to physical links, engineering teams can maximize TPU efficiency across both training and serving workloads.

A reference blueprint is only as good as the loop used to validate it. Always evaluate architectural changes in wall-clock time on a tuned software stack rather than paper FLOPs: in our Ironwood benchmarks, pure systems tuning (SparseCore offload, MXU-aligned tiles, and remat tuning) lifted an open 30B MoE from 12% to 23% MFU before any model change, and published Ironwood baselines show 256-aligned MoEs (DeepSeek-V3, Qwen3-235B at 608–630 BF16 TFLOP/s/chip) running at roughly 2× the throughput of unaligned GPT-OSS-120B (330 TFLOP/s/chip). Now 40, Alex Honnold Must Juggle Climbing with Fatherhood and Fame. It Isn’t Always Easy.

Alex Honnold, holding a bowl of Cheerios in one hand and a spoon of peanut butter in the other, stands at a kitchen island between his two giggling daughters and scowls at the clock. Here it is, barely 8 A.M., and he is already running late. Honnold quickly calculates the jam-packed agenda for what will be his family’s final summer day at their vacation cabin in South Lake Tahoe. Valuable seconds tick by.

After breakfast, Honnold must drop the girls off at preschool and then link up with a team of photographers and videographers at Pie Shop, a climbing crag on the other side of town. There, he will pose for a magazine cover shoot while clinging to rotund granite boulders, before speeding back to the cabin to have lunch with his aunt, who is visiting from Idaho. After that, Honnold will pack his luggage for an upcoming family trip to Italy. Finally, the Honnolds will host a few friends and neighbors for a goodbye barbecue.

The breakneck schedule is made more challenging by lingering jet lag from a recent trip to London, where Honnold delivered a motivational speech at an event hosted by his longtime sponsor the North Face.

“These days I feel like a ping-pong ball,” Honnold says. More seconds tick by.

Honnold will have to complete much of today’s agenda while answering the prying questions of a pesky journalist—me. Back in January, Honnold’s Netflix special Skyscraper Live thrust him into millions of living rooms across the planet, elevating him to a loftier stratosphere of fame. At Outside, we named Honnold an Outsider of the Year, in part, for bringing the grandeur of climbing to such a massive audience. Now, I have traveled to the Honnold family cabin to examine how a sudden spike in stardom is impacting outdoor recreation’s biggest celebrity.

How’s he coping? Well enough, I’d say, but boy does he seem exhausted. A coffee might help, but he still doesn’t touch the stuff. Honnold rubs his forehead. I notice more than a few streaks of gray dotting the sides of his close-cropped hair. Creases run across his cheeks, and deep crow’s-feet flare from both eyes. He yawns as he presents the bowl of cereal to June, who frowns. Sanni McCandless Honnold, his wife of six years, reaches over the sink to deliver Cheerios to Alice, who everyone calls Summer. There’s more giggling. McCandless Honnold presents each child with outfit options for the day. Red dress, yellow, or green? Definitely green.

Remembering his own wardrobe choices for the upcoming photo shoot, Honnold lumbers down the hallway toward the garage, which houses indoor climbing walls, free weights, and exercise equipment. He grabs several North Face shirts. Red or green? Definitely red. Back in the kitchen, a spoon clatters to the floor. The pitter-patter of approaching feet echoes from the hallway, and in a flash, the kiddos burst into the gym. Breakfast is over.

June, the elder of the two, grabs a gymnastics ring dangling from the ceiling and begins to twirl. Summer crouches on the padded floor and watches her sister.

“Yesterday was classic,” Honnold tells me later. “I woke up really early so I could do my gym workout before everyone got up. Somehow the babies heard me and woke up too and came in right when I was in the middle of everything.”

Honnold pauses before delivering a playful imitation of his wife. “Sanni is like, ‘You’re finally home from your London trip and all you want to do is climb?’’’

All you want to do is climb? may seem like an odd question for the world’s most famous rock climber. But these days, scaling rock is just one of the many tasks on Honnold’s daily agenda. In the wake of Skyscraper Live, Honnold’s life has become a dizzying array of paid speaking engagements, sponsor meet-and-greets, recording segments for his two podcasts Climbing Gold and Planet Visionaries, video shoots for his Outside TV show Out There with Alex Honnold, and other media hits. He graciously agrees to a high percentage of these interviews—yes, including Outside—which leads to an impossibly tight schedule.

He also has family commitments: dropping his daughters off at school, feeding them, and the usual weekend outings to playdates and playgrounds. In casual conversation, Honnold affectionately refers to his daughters as “the babies,” even though June is 4 and Summer is 2.

“The babies were harder this summer than last year,” Honnold says. He recounts a recent backpacking trip that sounds familiar to anyone who has attempted to adventure with small children. There was crying, smiles, ups, and downs. In total, the family covered just a few miles over the course of two days.

“You get those moments where it feels wholesome and fun and everything is cute,” Honnold says. “And other times when it feels like a tremendous amount of labor. I suspect that, for me, the best times are still ahead.”

So, back to rock climbing. Alas, it’s a somewhat touchy subject. On many days, climbing is an activity that Honnold squeezes in, pounds out, or cuts short. Daylong ascents of gleaming granite are often replaced by two-hour sweat sessions in his home gym. Honnold admits it is not what he predicted he’d be doing at age 40.

“This year I can count on one hand the number of times I’ve really been proud of something I’ve climbed on rock,” Honnold says. “The frustration of sucking is the essence of middle age.”

Honnold pauses before laughing. “Middle age is the ratio of how often you fall below your expectations, compared to how often you surpass them,” he adds. “Unfortunately, my standards for my climbing haven’t shrunk. I think it would be easier if they had.”

It’s well past go-time, and Honnold leads the charge toward his green Rivian SUV waiting out front. I snatch the middle seat in the back, flanked on each side by the girls in their car seats. Today, breakfast will be finished on the way to school. McCandless Honnold hands out fruit pouches. The girls squeal with glee.

There are hugs, kisses, and see-you-soons, and Honnold drives out onto the main road, toward preschool, Pie Shop, and what will be a serene afternoon in the Sierra Nevada. I process the whirlwind morning: the chaos, crunched schedule, spilled cereal, and Honnold’s occasionally futile pursuit of outdoor time. It’s all so much to take in, and yet, it feels so incredibly familiar. It’s the grind that millions of outdoors-loving moms and dads, myself included, live every day. We squeeze in that hike, that bike ride, that jog in between Zoom meetings and school pickups. We do so knowing that we’ll never quite be as fast or as strong as we were in our twenties, when our carefree lives afforded us the freedom to play outdoors all day, day after day, with zero consequences.

Honnold is one of us now, I conclude. Ever since Honnold burst into the mainstream consciousness with his death-defying free solo ascents, journalists—including yours truly—have sought to explain why he is so different from the average person. Now, at age 40, he is a Netflix star, a millionaire, and at the absolute zenith of his popularity. And yet, Alex Honnold is potentially more like us than at any point in his life.

Honnold has now been part of our lives for the better part of two decades. The iconic image of him tiptoeing across Thank God Ledge on the face of Half Dome graced the cover of National Geographic way back in September of 2011. The 60 Minutes profile of Honnold, with footage of him free soloing the Sentinel in Yosemite, beamed to TV audiences a month later. His memoir, Alone on the Wall, came out in 2015. And, of course, 2018’s Free Solo (and its Academy Award) made him outdoor recreation’s official crossover star of the 2020s.

Since Free Solo, the mainstream has sought to pinpoint the roots of Honnold’s fearlessness. Over the years, these explanations have ranged from the absurd (his amygdala is damaged!) to the offensive (he doesn’t have feelings!) to the highly speculative (he’s on the autism spectrum).

“That explanation has always felt off to me,” Honnold says when I present it to him. “I know I might be an unusual person, but I’ve taken a lot of tests, and I’m pretty sure I’m not on the spectrum.”

In a 2016 story for Nautilus, Honnold subjected himself to a bizarre psychological test, in which neuroscientists showed him photographs of gruesome or scary images and then placed him in an MRI machine to study his amygdala, the part of the brain that supposedly triggers a fear response. The scans seemed to confirm that Honnold’s brain responds less to fear than the average person. But a decade after the test, Honnold isn’t convinced that his ability to free solo is just the byproduct of some biological quirk.

“People always want simple explanations for anything that seems out of the ordinary. If someone sees an exceptional athletic performance, or something that seems different, they want to know that the person doing it is different,” Honnold says. “And, you know, the obvious explanation that it’s the product of lots of hard work and years of practice is just unsatisfactory. Because who the heck wants to dedicate their entire lives to doing that?”

“It’s just so much easier to say, ‘That guy is weird,’” he adds.

Honnold is unwavering in explaining the root of his superpower. For 25 or so years, climbing rock consumed every waking hour of his life. Unencumbered by preschool drop-offs or honey-do lists, he threw himself at big walls in Yosemite and abroad, climbing hundreds of days each year, year after year. His physical gifts helped him surpass his peers on the toughest ascents.

Was he ever scared? You bet—almost every day, he says. But, while clinging to the side of cliffs thousands of times over, Honnold found ways to silence his fear.

So, which explanation is more accurate: cerebral oddity or hard work? Longtime climber and film producer Cedar Wright, who has climbed with Honnold for two decades, says it’s closer to the latter—with an important caveat. Honnold chose to pursue free soloing, while most people—heck, most climbers—think it’s insane.

“He and myself and the small group of free soloists out there were of the mindset that soloing really matters, and is worth the risk,” Wright said. “A lot of really good climbers, like Tommy Caldwell or Adam Ondra, want nothing to do with it. But to us, the most badass thing you can do is climb without a rope. You kind of have to believe that if you want to keep going with it.”

This singular focus created, shall we call it, a relatability gap between Honnold and John Q. Public. Most stories about Honnold from his twenties and thirties mentioned that he ate and slept and filled Gatorade bottles with his own urine inside a 2002 Ford Econoline van. And in interviews from this era, Honnold comes across as, at best, robotic. At worst, an alien from another planet.

But the oddball lifestyle is ultimately what propelled him to National Geographic, the Academy Awards, and into our consciousness. Wright climbed with Honnold during the mid-aughts, when they were both dirtbags living in Yosemite. Even amongst his peers, Honnold stood out because of his utter love of climbing.

“There was a point when literally nobody on the planet was climbing more than Honnold, in terms of sheer volume and days in a row on rock,” Wright said. “The guy was on a whole different level of dedication and focus.”

Alas, those days are now long in the rearview. While Honnold’s motivation and passion and talent for climbing remain, the free time to climb every single day, day after day, has vanished, squashed by his many commitments. This tension is at the heart of Alex Honnold’s life in 2026.

The Rivian zips across South Lake Tahoe as Honnold points out landmarks. We pass a road that leads to Lover’s Leap, the local 600-foot wall where Honnold made some of his first free solo climbs in his early twenties. On the horizon sits Mount Tallac, where Honnold was saved by backcountry rescuers after he slipped and fell down a couloir when he was a teenager.

As kids, Honnold and his older sister, Stasia, spent summers at the vacation cabin in South Lake Tahoe. There were camping trips, baseball games, and afternoons spent at the nearby beach—usually with no parents in sight. The cabin also represents an eternal connection to Honnold’s father, Charles, who died of a sudden heart attack in July 2004, when Honnold was 19. Charles’ father built the cabin in a gated community a few hundred yards from the California/Nevada state line. Now, Honnold says, going to the cabin brings up memories of his dad.

“In some ways I feel his loss more now than when I was 19,” he says. “I realize that my kids never got to meet their grandfather. And he would have been fucking awesome with them. He’d probably be retired by now and would want to spend all summer here with the two of them.”

The Honnold family cabin is important for other reasons, too. On days when the Zoom calls are few and the indoor gym has grown stale, Honnold can bolt from the cabin to breathe fresh air, climb on rock, or push his body on a trail, while still making it back for dinnertime. Honnold’s handful of 2026 outings have included more than a few local hot spots: Space Invaders, Eagle Lake Buttress, and the Tahoe Triple Crown, a punishing 18-mile out-and-back up three high peaks.

“You have to do hundreds of climbs like these to feel like you’ve done something epic,” Honnold says. “That’s what it takes for me to feel normal these days. You can’t be a real climber if you don’t climb rock.”

Of course, in true Honnold fashion, these short outings are often high-octane sufferfests that would cripple even a seasoned climber. And Honnold will push himself past the limits that most of us require for sleep and recovery in order to squeeze in an epic day. In the summer of 2025 in South Lake Tahoe, Honnold made regular outings to The Incredible Hulk, a 2,000-foot vertical wall in California’s Sawtooth Ridge, with routes rated up to 5.13. The trailhead is a two-hour drive from the family cabin, so Honnold would wake up at 2 A.M. in order to complete the round trip and be home for dinner. Longtime friend Chris Weidner would sometimes call Honnold on the phone during the drives to and from the spire for a friendly chat.

“It was such a crazy schedule—for most of us, doing that climb fresh would be extremely challenging, and here he is doing it after hard days of climbing in his home gym,” Weidner said. “Yes, he is very committed to his family, but his intense drive and relentless need for climbing volume is still the same.”

And climbing still provides Honnold with an emotional salve, much as it did when he was in his twenties. In the 2015 book, Alone on the Wall, Honnold described soloing the 5.12b Rainbow Wall in Nevada’s Red Rock Canyon, and how the ascent helped him work through a relationship that was not going well.

I ask if he still seeks out tough climbs to help process the frustration of middle-aged life. Honnold shrugs.

“I actually have a healthier approach to that type of thing now than when I was younger, because I would basically channel my inner angst to do climbs I was proud of,” he says. “It almost became a process, because I’d have to harness those moments of peak feeling to do a climb I cared about. I eventually realized I can just go out and climb the same way, without the angst.”

Honnold adds, “Being in a committed and healthy relationship for ten years also helps.”

But there are still times when Honnold’s emotions and his climbing intertwine. He recounts the story of a recent free solo gone wrong near the family’s full-time home in Las Vegas.

“For whatever reason, I was upset about something. Home life was hard, whatever, kids and stress,” Honnold says. “So, in the middle of the afternoon, I’m like, ‘Fuck it, I’m going soloing’ as a reason to get out of the house.”

But alas, there was a high-wind advisory in Vegas. The smart thing to do was to climb indoors, but Honnold was undeterred. He drove to Black Velvet Canyon trailhead in Red Rock Canyon and pointed himself up a towering route. He began free soloing a wall rated 5.10d—impossibly dangerous for most climbers without a rope—and soon realized that conditions were less than optimal.

“I’m up there just getting hammered by the wind. It’s insane,” Honnold says. “And I turn the corner and all of a sudden this huge gust, this updraft, blows chalk in my face, and the gusts are even harder, and I’m on a prow in the center of this 2,000-foot rock face.”

A gust billowed up Honnold’s shorts and knocked his iPhone out of his pocket. The device tumbled hundreds of feet to the valley floor, breaking on impact. The phone is the only car key for Honnold’s Rivian SUV, which was also parked at the foot of the cliff. He didn’t have enough daylight to summit the climb and then hike back down. Instead, he downclimbed the wall’s 16 pitches without a rope—a sketchy undertaking. He eventually found the shattered device in the sand. Miraculously, it still turned the car on.

“The next day I’m at the Apple Store buying a new phone and it hits me, what an idiot I was,’’ Honnold says. “I was like, ‘I should have just gone on a jog or something.’”

The whole ordeal sounds horrifying. But Honnold laughs and grins as he recounts the memory.

Gym time, dad adventures, and the occasional death-defying free solo simply hold Honnold over through the busiest part of the year: the spring and summer. Every fall, he carves out two weeks or so for a hard-charging adventure on rock. In 2025, he spent two weeks in Yosemite, where he and Tommy Caldwell completed a free ascent of the Platinum Wall on El Capitan, among other hard climbs. In August 2026, Honnold will spend two weeks in Europe, completing ascents on the Eiger, Matterhorn, and tough rock walls in the Dolomites.

I ask Honnold if his relationship with climbing has changed at all, now that it’s something he has to schedule into his complicated lifestyle.

“In some ways, climbing is like my only opportunity to feel like I’m good at something,” he says. Being a celebrity, a paid speaker, and even a parent, Honnold admits, doesn’t always come easily. “With parenting, especially, it’s like you always feel like you’re bad at it. You know what I mean?” he adds.

It’s something I know all too well. I then ask Honnold if, at age 40, he’s just as good at rock climbing as at any point in his twenties or thirties.

“I’m probably a better climber today, in some ways, than I’ve ever been,” Honnold says. “I’m probably stronger than I was at age 30. I suspect I could do most of the climbs that I’ve ever done, if I had the time to do them again.”

Every 40-year-old weekend warrior can relate. If I only had the time. Honnold shrugs and takes it all in. He’s philosophical about the push and pull of middle age. And he tells me that, in so many words, this phase of life, with the kids, the commitments, the schedule, was, to a degree, planned. It’s the life that he and McCandless Honnold sought for themselves when they became a couple a decade ago.

“Yeah, I’ve probably given up some of my best years of climbing,” he says. “Some doors open, others close. But it was always part of our plan.”

Honnold didn’t develop this adult mindset overnight, of course. The journey from van-dwelling dirtbag to committed father capable of prioritizing family and career over recreation took time and effort. And it hasn’t always been easy, Sanni McCandless Honnold tells me.

“I think, like anyone, he misses the freedom of having all the time in the world and no responsibilities, and not having to juggle the complexities of his life,” she says. “And at the same time, I don’t think he’d go back to that life.”

McCandless Honnold, 34, met Honnold at a book signing in 2016 in Seattle, and the two quickly became a couple, traveling to climbing trips in Europe and California together. When directors began filming Free Solo six months later, they chronicled the early stages of their relationship, and captured the tension of how Honnold’s lifestyle—you know, the van and all of that climbing—collided with the requirements of partnership.

Some outdoor athletes never grow out of a lifestyle that prioritizes daily doses of adrenaline and adventure. But Honnold has, she says, even if he’s had to work at it. “Alex doesn’t suffer from the Peter Pan syndrome that I’ve seen a lot of adventurous people have,” she says. “Even back then, he had the ability to see his life longterm, like, ‘Yeah, I’ll probably get married and have kids. I want to provide for my family.’”

The first turning point in this evolution, McCandless Honnold says, occurred immediately after Free Solo. Honnold had spent years scouting El Capitan for the ascent, and then months meticulously preparing for the climb. When it was over, she says, he faced a big question: What’s next? The answer included marriage, buying a house, and traveling around the world to speak about the film to audiences.

Another major turn occurred in 2022 after the birth of June. McCandless Honnold underwent an emergency cesarean section, and June spent time hooked up to a ventilator in the intensive care unit. Perhaps the biggest turning point, she says, happened in 2024, when Summer was born. McCandless Honnold suffered a painful infection just after delivery and did two stints in intensive care. Then, several weeks after returning home, she fell and broke her elbows and spent more time in the hospital.

“I handed him a two-week-old and a two-year-old, and he went home without Mom,” she says. “It was one of those periods in life where you have to learn ruthless prioritization.”

Over the course of several months, Honnold molded his life around the needs of the kids, staying up late to mix formula, waking up early to change diapers, and sacrificing workouts to keep his wife off her feet. Months turned into years, and as McCandless Honnold explains, Honnold learned how to juggle the many demands in his life in a way that didn’t put the entire burden of child rearing on her shoulders.

He also underwent a period of emotional growth, she says, progressing from a left-brained rationalist into a person capable of acknowledging and understanding the feelings of others. The antithesis of a robot, she assures me.

“He was never emotionless; he was never a robot,” she says. “He’s now more willing to communicate his feelings and to engage with the feelings of others.”

When I present this question to Honnold (Are you more empathetic now than you were at age 20?), he shrugs and delivers a hard no. “I’ve always understood what people are thinking, or why they’re thinking it, even if I don’t always care all that much,” he says.

But others in his life disagree. Weidner, who has climbed with Honnold for more than two decades, said he often suffered the wrath of Honnold’s impatience early in his career. Honnold was a better climber, and would chide Weidner when he took too long to ascend a pitch.

In the fall of 2025, the two reconnected for an ascent of the Middle Cathedral Rock in Yosemite, and Weidner noticed a major shift in Honnold’s vibe.

“He used to be like, ‘C’mon dude, it’s only 5.12, what’s your problem?’” Weidner says. “Now he is a lot more willing to be a kind and patient climbing partner. Instead of scolding me, it’s more like, ‘C’mon Chrissy-Poo, I’m ready to climb again.’”

Maybe getting older isn’t that bad. Still, Honnold’s balancing act between climbing and middle-aged life is ever-present, and every few months brings a new wrinkle for the Honnold family to navigate. McCandless Honnold spent much of 2026 writing her first book, a self-help title called Mosaic Theory: How to Craft a Life of Many Summits. She says that, as her own ambitions and career have grown, Honnold has had to take on more family responsibilities.

“On any given day or week, things may not appear that balanced between us, but over months and years it gets balanced out,” she says. “And I trust my partner is going to understand when I’m like, ‘OK, it’s my turn for free time.’”

Thus, in some ways, they were prepared for the dramatic increase in non-climbing opportunities that came Honnold’s way after Skyscraper Live beamed across Netflix. The streaming giant aired the ascent live on January 24, and over the course of the next week, it generated 11.4 million views. In the weeks afterward, requests for interviews and engagement poured in from all corners of the globe.

But in other ways, Skyscraper Live changed Honnold’s life in ways he was not prepared for—something I witness as he pulls up to the girls’ school on the western corner of South Lake Tahoe. Honnold helps his daughters walk up to the cute green building, and then briskly strolls back to the Rivian. Just as he’s about to reach the car, an enthusiastic passerby stops him, extends his hand, and begins talking. Honnold smiles and nods as he casts a few nervous glances at his watch.

“Going out in public has gotten harder,” Honnold says after getting back to the car. “Fan sightings happen all the time now. I’m still not used to it.”

It used to be that only other climbers or outdoor rec aficionados would stop him for a handshake or a photo. After Skyscraper Live, it’s everyone. Several days earlier, he tells me, while cycling on the local bike path with June, Honnold was stopped by a series of tourists, all of whom wanted his photograph. The encounter ended the bike ride prematurely. Losing the ability to blend into a crowd, Honnold says, has been a struggle.

“Every once in a while, you’re like, ‘Did I make the wrong choice? Did I botch this whole thing? Should I still be living alone in my van?’” Honnold says. He laughs. “And the obvious answer is no, because I don’t want to be a 65-year-old guy living in my van alone.”

Honnold did not foresee the changes to his life when he summited the metal spire atop Taipei 101 and waved to the tens of thousands of awestruck onlookers below. Like free soloing El Capitan, ascending Taipei 101 was the finish line of a years-long process. Honnold first had the idea to climb a building live on television in 2013. The following year, he worked with National Geographic on the idea, eventually homing in on Taipei 101, which at the time was the world’s fourth-tallest building. But the project fell apart when the media empire learned the cost of staging the stunt.

But Honnold never forgot about the crazy idea. After the pandemic, he began working with UK production company Plimsoll Studios, eventually filming two successful adventure series: Arctic Ascent and The Devil’s Climb. The company’s head of adventure filmmaking, James Smith, told me that Honnold frequently brought up the skyscraper idea during pitch sessions.

“Alex and I end up spending a lot of time together in these remote places,” Smith told me. “And ever since I’ve known him, he has mentioned the idea of climbing a skyscraper on TV.”

Honnold and Smith connected for a preparatory meeting about the project in 2023, and the following year Smith traveled to Los Angeles to present the ideas to producers at Netflix. Netflix bought the idea, but Smith’s work wasn’t done—they had to choose a building. By 2023, Taipei 101 was no longer one of the world’s top-ten tallest skyscrapers. Once they winnowed the group down to four, Smith reached out to see if they could have access.

After months of outreach, Taipei 101 emerged as the final choice. Then, Smith had to figure out how to film the climb in a way that would make it compelling to the masses. His plan involved a small army of cameramen, riggers, ropes professionals, and a safety crew.

“We put together one of the most ambitious camera rigs in TV history,” Smith said. “The camera is 1,700 feet high, and we had cameras covering every foot of it.”

The crew suffered a minor catastrophe when, on the January 23 shoot date, they awoke to see the building shrouded in fog and buffeted by steady rain. After pushing it back a day, they enjoyed perfect conditions for Honnold’s climb. The cameramen strapped into their harnesses and zipped up ropes on the building’s facade. Honnold and McCandless Honnold stepped out of a white van at street level and embraced, and then Honnold began climbing the building’s metal frame toward the top, and toward a new future.

“You could tell he was having a lot of fun on a worthwhile endeavor,” Smith says. “The success of Skyscraper Live at some point comes down to Alex’s authenticity.”

The first inkling I got that Skyscraper Live was about to boost Honnold into a new level of fame came a few days before the stunt when I watched a 30-minute interview with him on CNN. Then, in the days after the climb, Honnold was everywhere: Jimmy Kimmel Live, the pages of Hollywood trade magazine Variety, and even on the popular podcast hosted by comedian Hasan Minhaj. The media crush kept rolling, and in fast succession Honnold did lengthy one-on-one interviews with Rich Roll, British celebrity Jay Shetty, entrepreneur Steven Bartlett, and others.

In addition to interview requests, requests for paid public speaking engagements also rolled in, many of which Honnold agreed to. After a few months, Honnold and his team decided to raise his speaking rates. In 2019, Honnold charged a low five-figure fee to speak. For 2026, Big Speak, a website that allows you to book a motivational speaker, lists Honnold’s fee at above $50,000. Another site, WSB, estimates his fee at $70,000.

Prior to Skyscraper Live, the New York Times reported that his fee for the ascent would be $750,000—noticeably low for a professional athlete. But Honnold emphasized that he would have happily climbed the building for free. The comparatively low upfront pay, he says, has also generated a substantial uptick in revenue afterward.

“Every segment of earning has grown a bit each year: sponsorship, corporate speaking, and then TV and film stuff,” Honnold says. “Taipei 101 just turned everything up quite a bit.”

In many ways, Honnold is charting his own path as a crossover outdoor athlete, who earns paychecks from doing jobs that are only tangentially related to his sport. In a famous scene in Free Solo, Honnold tells a class of schoolchildren in 2016 that his earnings from rock climbing are comparable to what a successful dentist would earn. Now, Honnold says, that’s no longer the case.

“I think I’ve exceeded that of any normie jobs,” he says. “Maybe if you owned a dental franchise.”

Honnold’s long-term financial plan has been to earn enough from climbing to never have to work a normal job in his life. Honnold created that goal long before he had three other mouths to feed and a personal charity to back. The Honnold Foundation supports solar power programs across the globe, and in 2026 the foundation committed $11 million in donations, its largest amount ever.

Now, Honnold says, the lifetime earning goal is within reach.

“I’ll hit that lifetime savings goal in the next year or two,” he says.

I ask Honnold if the flying, the showing up, and the stress are worth it.

“The pro climbing thing is going to wind down at some point—I’ve always known it,” he says.

For three whole hours Honnold is a remarkably good model for the camera lens. He clings to the side of massive boulders, tilting his head this way and that, as a photographer belts out curious orders. The monotone, mechanical personality parodied by Instagram memes is nowhere to be seen. Honnold cracks jokes and tells stories as though he’s addressing a room full of software salesmen in Orlando.

I ask Honnold if the strategic dad adventures, scheduled between work obligations, are a permanent part of his life as an adventurer. Honnold shakes his head: this phase in his life doesn’t last forever, he reminds me. The babies will get older, the corporate speaking engagements may evaporate, his free time to climb will someday return.

“The things I’m stressed about, I know that they are temporary,” Honnold says. “At some point I’ll be able to slow things down a bit.”

Whether or not that’s true is, of course, up for debate. Those who know him best are dubious that Honnold will ever back away from the breakneck balancing act. In many ways, the demands of family and work, when added to climbing, give Honnold the level of commitment he needs to thrive.

“With Alex it’s gonna be nonstop—not just with climbing but also with work and family,” Weidner says. “He has always seemed really uncomfortable with any downtime. To him, it’s just a waste.”

Wright puts it more bluntly: “Alex would be miserable with an easy life.”

“Alex wants to be charging headlong at all times at everything he’s doing,” Wright adds. “He’s just taken that ethic for climbing and is now applying it to the crazy opportunities that are open to him. I think it’s impossible for him to say no.”

Thus, there are likely to be more flights, more hurried ascents, and more gray hairs in Honnold’s future—in addition to another media project that showcases his skill and bravery to the widest audience possible. As we drive back to the family cabin, I ask Honnold if he’s done soloing buildings, to which he replies with an emphatic no. The next chapter of Skyscraper Live hinges on finding the right skyscraper. The world’s highest building, the 2,717-foot Burj Khalifa in Dubai, is the obvious choice. But the war with Iran has made that structure abnormally complex.

“There are five or six others in China that are also a bit of a risk,” he says. “Not off the table, but not preferred.”

The energy is high at the family cabin when we arrive: neighbors buzzing by to say their goodbyes, a cadre of relatives in town to share the afternoon, and McCandless Honnold preparing afternoon snacks for the kids. Honnold and I leave the chaos for the garage climbing gym to continue our conversation. As we bounce from climbing to money to fame, I can tell that the pull of his life is feeling more like a strong tug. The babies will be home soon. Then, houseguests will arrive. Then, packing for Europe. His phone buzzes with texts and missed calls.

We wrap up the interview, and Honnold gives me a firm handshake. He’s been more than generous with his time. And now it’s off to the next family outing, corporate speaking event, or climbing crag, to do what he has always done best, which is being Alex Honnold.

Jimmy Chin on His New Mount Everest Film and Latest Book

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Jimmy Chin, pictured in Joshua Tree National Park, described writing his new book as both a marathon and a sprint. (Photo: Beau Grealy/Trunk Archive)

In spring 2026, Jimmy Chin flew to Nepal. It was his 15th trip to the Greater Ranges over 25 years spent on expeditions to climb and photograph its snow-covered giants. The trip was a departure from the high-octane sufferfests he’s often done, and it also marked a different type of first: the first time he brought his kids, Marina (12) and James (10), along. Showing them the mountains that have inspired him for decades, and often pulled him away from them for months at a time, was memorable for Chin.

Together, they made their way to Everest Base Camp, stopping in the tiny villages that populate the roadless Khumbu Valley. When they crawled into their tents at 17,598 feet, they were proud of themselves.

Outside Phortse, a small village in Sagarmatha National Park, a helicopter dropped off a special guest who’d come to say hi: Jim Morrison. Morrison hadn’t wanted to miss the kids’ first trip to Nepal, and they were shocked to see him walking toward them on the trail. In town, they went to the Khumbu Climbing Center, where Nepali climbers and high-altitude workers train to operate safely in the big mountains, to hang with Sherpa friends and their families, and to pick up their guide, Pemba Sherpa (Sharwa).

Twenty years earlier, Chin had encountered a then 15-year-old Pemba for the first time in that exact spot. It was just a few years after the center had opened, and he and the legendary mountaineer Conrad Anker were on the way to climb Cholatse. “I really encouraged his photography and filmmaking,” said Chin.

It turned out to be kismet: Pemba worked with Chin as a cinematographer on his film, Everest: The Other Side. Pemba was there in October 2025 documenting Jim Morrison as he made his first turn off the 29,032-foot summit. Morrison made history that day after completing a mind-boggling ski descent: an unforgiving 9,000-foot line of the North Face of Everest via the Hornbein and Japanese Couloirs.

Chin’s team of climbers, filmmakers, and support crew added 12 new names to the short list of people who had ever summited Everest via the North Face. Ascending the route alone would’ve been enough, but Chin was there as Morrison’s climbing partner while simultaneously directing a feature film. The 53-year-old has made a career of combining high-angle endeavors with filmmaking. In 2011, he climbed the Shark’s Fin, a 4,500-foot route, alongside Anker and Renan Ozturk to film what would become the 2015 movie Meru. When Alex Honnold climbed the 3,000-foot granite prow of El Capitan in Yosemite National Park without a rope, Chin was there to capture that too. Free Solo went on to win an Academy Award.

His newest film, which was co-directed with Elizabeth Chai Vasarhelyi and made its theatrical debut in IMAX on October 30, was built on every single one of the skills Chin has developed over his career climbing and documenting expeditions on some of the world’s most challenging peaks.

“This is kind of like the culmination of all my collective experiences as a climber and a photographer and a filmmaker and a producer and a director and all the things,” said Chin of making the film.

When Chin took the last few steps to the summit of Mount Everest, his third time standing up there, he was surprised to find himself overcome with emotion. During the climb, he’d been so focused: He was thinking about breathing, consumed by the sheer physicality of climbing at altitude, busy worrying about Morrison and the team’s safety, and planning out shot lists and contingency plans. He’d thought about every worst-case scenario, but he’d failed to consider what it would feel like to succeed. The years of hard work flashed in front of his eyes. He collapsed into Morrison’s arms, in tears.

“I looked over into Nepal and saw Makalu and Lhotse, and I mean the whole Himalaya spread out in front of me,” said Chin. It had taken seven years to get the team up there. “Everything hit me at the same time and I totally fell apart.”

While he felt a big weight lift from his shoulders after getting everyone up and down Everest safely, he still felt the enormous weight of having two of his closest friends, Morrison and Morrison’s late partner Hilaree Nelson, entrust him with their story. He’d met Nelson 20 years earlier when they both joined the athlete team at The North Face at the same time. He knew he had to get it right.

After six years of working on the film, Chin and producer Shannon Dill traveled to Tahoe City last June to show Morrison a rough cut. When it was over, there was a lot of emotion in the room, and people were crying. Morrison got up and walked to the back of the theater to wrap Chin in a big hug.

During the final phases of editing the film, Chin was also working on a deadline to finish his new book, Rules to Live By. “I had like three months of 16-hour days, seven days a week,” he said. “When people say it’s a marathon, not a sprint, I was like, ‘This is both.’”

For the book, Chin interviewed ten people he looks up to, from Lynsey Addario to Simone Biles to Yo-Yo Ma, all of whom have helped shape the way he thinks about what really matters in life.

Both Bill King and Ridley Scott talked to him about honesty, and it’s stuck with him. “You have to tell the truth,” he said. “It’s hard to do, but you have to do it, and you have to do it early because it only becomes harder.” Telling the truth is like training a muscle, he said, and exercising it becomes easier with practice and, with enough time, just becomes how you naturally operate. We’ve all had an experience that would’ve been easier if we’d just been honest up front, said Chin.

He draws inspiration from actress Annette Bening and how she leads: quietly, in a way that embodies the Daoist idea that the greatest leaders are the ones that no one knows are leading. “She makes people feel seen and heard, and when she walks on set, people will do anything for this woman because they want to,” he said. “I was really paying attention to how she moved through the world. It’s about listening to people and making people feel heard.”

Years after Pemba met Chin, he started working on climbing expeditions, following in his father’s footsteps. His dad bought him a Lumix digital camera to take with him on his first expedition to Mount Everest. On the summit, he snapped a few photos.

When Meru came out, Pemba remembers feeling deeply inspired by Chin’s work. He could not have imagined that a decade later, after years spent working in the mountains and honing his craft, he would find himself filming for Chin’s production, with his camera aimed at Chin during the emotional moments that transpired on the summit before Morrison’s ski.

Chin has spent much of his life forging his own path in the mountains, making daring first ascents and ski descents, but has since shifted his focus toward giving opportunities to the next generation coming up behind him. When a big objective is on the horizon, he makes sure to bring new faces along.

“Jimmy is a big influence on me, and a big mentor,” said Pemba. “Experiencing a climb with the man himself and working for the man himself? Yeah, it was really an honor.”

I built a tool to find people asking for my product. Most of the work was throwing results away.

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

For my last product I sent about 2,000 cold emails. Almost nobody replied. What bothered me more was what I found afterwards: the people who needed it had been asking for it in public the whole time. In forums, in review sites, in "what tool do you use for X" threads.

So I started building Crossroad (https://crossroadhq.com/?utm_source=devto). The idea is simple: find people and companies who are publicly asking for what you sell, show you exactly what they said, and help you write a reply you send yourself.

The idea is simple. Doing it honestly is not. Here is what I learned building the first version.

1. Searching is easy. Deciding is the product.

Step 2 returns a lot. Step 3 is where almost everything gets thrown away.

In a real test week for a small CRM for independent insurance agencies, the tool read 48 results and kept 2. Both were business news: an agency opening a new office, and an agency acquiring another one. Those are moments when an agency suddenly needs better tooling. The other 46 were set aside, each with a reason.

2. Most of the open web is vendors talking

For broad products (a helpdesk, a developer tool, a generic AI assistant) the open web is mostly vendor content: comparison pages, "top 10 tools" lists, launch posts. They contain all the right keywords and zero buyers.

Keyword matching can't tell the difference. "Looking for a CRM for my agency" and "We built the best CRM for agencies" share almost every word. So every result gets a question: is this a person or company with the need, or someone selling to them? Vendor content gets dropped, however well it matches.

The honest consequence: the tool works best for products with a clear type of buyer. For very broad products I'd rather show nothing than show noise.

If any of that is missing, it isn't shown. A summary of "why they fit" without the original text is just a guess you can't check.

4. Finding isn't the bottleneck. Replying is.

This week another founder building something similar shared his numbers on Reddit: his users contacted about 2% of the people the tool found. Finding people wasn't the problem. "I don't know what to say to a stranger, so I'll do it tomorrow" was.

So the reply matters more than the list. A good reply helps with the actual question first, and only mentions the product if it's genuinely the answer. And the tool never posts for you. Communities are right to ban bots, and an automated reply is spam no matter how well it's targeted.

The site went live two days ago and early access is open. The first three matches for each product are free, checked by hand while I learn what makes people actually reply.

DHCP Client Identifier vs MAC Address: What a Router Is Showing

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 17 • Originally published at deviceshelf.app

A router's DHCP page may show a local IP address, a hardware or MAC address, a hostname, and sometimes a field called “client ID” or “DHCP client identifier.” When two entries look similar, it's easy to read client ID as another name for the MAC address. That isn't always the case.

The difference matters when you're checking a changed router entry or deciding whether to create a DHCP reservation. A router can associate a lease with information supplied by the client, but router interfaces don't all display that information the same way. It also can't reliably tell you which person or physical device is behind an entry. The task is more specific: work out which current lease belongs to a device you manage before changing anything.

DHCP gives a client configuration details such as an IP address, subnet mask, router address, and lease time. A client request can include a client identifier option. DHCP messages also carry a hardware-address field. RFC 2131 defines these parts of the exchange.

Not every router shows both values, though, and the labels aren't always accurate. One interface may show only “MAC address.” Another may put “client ID” next to a separate MAC address. A third may get its device label from a remembered lease. Before comparing two rows, write down the exact field labels and local IP address, along with the displayed lease time and whether the row is active or historical. Only do this on a network you own or are authorized to manage.

Don't guess at missing details. A field that looks like a MAC address could be a hardware address, an identifier built from one, or a vendor-specific way of displaying the information. Check the documentation for that router model to find out what a particular screen means. The DHCP specifications explain the protocol's terms; they don't determine what a consumer router calls a column.

A MAC address is a link-layer address used to deliver traffic on a local network. On an IPv4 segment, Address Resolution Protocol associates an IP address with a link-layer address for local delivery. That's the limited role described in RFC 826. It doesn't make a MAC address a permanent, universal identity for an entire device.

A DHCP client identifier is an option carried in DHCP messages that lets a DHCP server recognize a client for lease management. Some clients use an identifier related to a hardware address. Others can use a different identifier. RFC 4361 specifies a DHCPv4 client-identifier format that combines an identity association identifier (IAID) with a DHCP unique identifier (DUID), also used by DHCPv6. So a client identifier and a displayed Wi-Fi MAC address can be related without being identical text.

Devices with more than one interface make this distinction particularly useful. A laptop may have both Ethernet and Wi-Fi interfaces, while a phone can use a network-specific private Wi-Fi address. A virtual machine, dock, or USB adapter can introduce another interface. If the router records a lease for each interface, several entries may belong to equipment you recognize. But similar names alone don't establish that two records belong to the same device.

Changing a reservation or reconnecting can interrupt services and your remote session. Make these changes with local access or an alternative management connection available.

If you have physical or administrative access to the device you think an entry belongs to, compare its current settings instead of relying on an old list. Connect it to the relevant network and open its network settings. Note the local IPv4 address and the current MAC address reported for that interface. Then find the router's active lease with the same local IP and compare its time and connection type, where available.

Don't assign a duplicate IP to create an address conflict as a test. It can interrupt another device and make the original evidence harder to interpret. A normal renewal, reconnect, or documented router refresh is a safer way to see which current lease changes.

DHCP data is meant for configuration, not proof of ownership. A router may keep an expired or remembered entry after a device disconnects. When that device reconnects, it may use another interface or receive a different address. It may also supply a different identifier after an operating-system change. A Wi-Fi privacy setting can cause a familiar device to present a network-specific address, too.

Privacy is one reason not to require a stable, human-readable identifier from every DHCP client. RFC 7844 discusses DHCP identifiers and options that can make a client easier to correlate. It won't explain an individual router entry. It does explain why a changing or limited identifier can be deliberate protocol behavior rather than a sign of a network fault.

Only draw conclusions the evidence supports. Example observation: “This active lease matched the laptop's current Wi-Fi address at 14:20” is a useful observation. “This identifier proves who owns the device” is not. If you still can't explain a record, mark it as pending. Before changing access rules or passwords, check nearby facts such as a guest network, a recently added adapter, or a device that's asleep.

A DHCP reservation is useful when a managed service needs a predictable local address, such as a NAS share, printer queue, or home-automation endpoint. Use whichever identifier the router explicitly asks for. That means a MAC address on some routers; on others, it may mean a documented client-ID value. Before copying an identifier from an old historical row, confirm that the current device still uses it.

Once you've saved a reservation, renew the lease only on the device you manage and check that it receives the expected address. Test the intended local service from another authorized device. A reservation doesn't guarantee a fixed hostname or continuous availability. You still need a short inventory note explaining why it exists.

For another local observation, an inventory tool can help organize the IP address, interface address, time, and service clues seen on a network you administer. DeviceShelf is one option for that comparison. As with the router record, compare its result with the device's own settings. It isn't a guarantee of identity or security.

Pause if you can't match a current active lease to any authorized device after checking the network name, connection type, timing, and known adapters. Review who has access to the Wi-Fi password and whether a guest network is separate. Check for pending router firmware updates as well. Avoid publishing a complete DHCP table or screenshot, since it can expose local addresses, hardware addresses, and device names.

Changing a Wi-Fi password can be appropriate when you have a grounded reason to suspect unauthorized access. It's disruptive, though, and a client-identifier mismatch alone usually isn't enough. First, keep a record of what the router shows and compare the current device settings you can access. Then make the smallest safe change that answers the next question.

The Shenyang Aircraft Design and Research Institute, the developers of China’s J-35 fighter jet platform, used metal 3D printing to create large primary load-bearing structures for military aircraft applications. Global Times reports the structure is over 16.4 feet (five meters) in length, using segmented printing and fusion techniques.

Shenyang Aircraft Design and Research Institute, a research organization under the Aviation Industry Corporation of China (AVIC), has been working on 3D-printed aircraft components since 2003. The impressively large 3D-printed components are the culmination of decades of research and development of various metal additive manufacturing techniques.

The engineers are no strangers to large 3D-printed components: in 2005, they achieved secondary load-bearing structures; then, in 2012, large primary load-bearing titanium alloy structures, earning additive manufacturing expert Wang Huaming and his team China’s National Technological Invention Award.

Recently, however, the team created a primary load-bearing structure even larger, at over 16.4 feet. The massive component is not printed in one piece, as printing such a large structure faces warping and cracking issues, as stated in Progress and Challenges of Laser Direct Manufacturing of Large Titanium Structural Components, published in the Chinese Journal of Lasers. To avoid these problems, two techniques were employed: Segmented Additive Manufacturing (SAM) and additive fusion joining. It sounds like fancy words to say it was printed separately and welded, and it technically wouldn’t be wrong — but the key distinction is that it is part of the whole workflow; basically, metal 3D printing into the intentionally designed gaps between the components and creating one whole printed piece.

Additive manufacturing eliminates the need for expensive molds and multi-part construction. Titanium alloys are notoriously difficult to machine, cast components are usually weak, and joining parts using bolts and screws is additional labor — metal 3D printing solves all of this by creating a strong printed part in the desired shape in one large piece, minimizing the need for additional fasteners. By eliminating molds, the team can iterate much faster and create more complex structures that would otherwise not be possible with traditional manufacturing techniques.

Billboard also publishes genre charts alongside its two flagship lists. These cover areas such as country, Latin, rock and R&B/hip-hop. The Billboard Music Awards have included genre categories, such as top country and top Latin honors. Those categories give the narrower charts a role of their own.

The way Billboard counts a stream or a sale has been adjusted over the years. Streaming data, for example, was not part of the charts' earliest formulas. Because of those changes, a chart position from one era does not measure exactly the same thing as a position from another. The exact formula behind any particular year's award finalists depends on the rules in force for that year.

Grammy winners receive a gilded gramophone, and MTV Video Music Awards winners receive the Moon Person, a statue of an astronaut. One trophy depicts a machine for playing sound. The other depicts a person.

A gramophone is an early device for playing recorded sound from a flat disc. A needle followed grooves cut into the spinning disc. A large horn projected the sound into the room. The Grammy statue takes that shape, horn included.

Gilding is a thin layer of gold applied to a surface. On the Grammy, it gives the statue its gold finish.

The gramophone form ties the trophy to the kind of work the Grammys judge. The awards cover recorded music. The statue shows a machine built to play recordings back.

The Moon Person does not depict audio equipment. Its subject is an astronaut, a person trained for space travel. The VMA trophy draws on space travel rather than on the tools used to make or play music.

Neither statue reflects how its winner was chosen. The gramophone points to the medium the Grammys honor, not to the peer vote behind each result. The astronaut points to neither the video craft the VMAs judge nor the mix of fan and professional ballots that decides them.

The difference in shape makes each trophy identifiable on sight. A winner holding a gold gramophone has won a Grammy. A winner holding an astronaut figure has won a VMA.

The Grammys, VMAs and Billboard Music Awards each answer a different question about music. The Grammys turn to music professionals, the VMAs add fans to the vote, and the Billboard awards have leaned on chart data.

The first difference is who holds the decision. At the Grammys, it sits with members of the Recording Academy, the same community that makes the records under review. A Grammy result does not hinge on how many copies a recording sold or how often it streamed. A commercially modest record can win if enough voting members rate it highly. A best-selling one can go home empty-handed.

At the VMAs, the decision is shared. MTV has described its process as a mix of professional and fan input. Fans vote online in many categories. Part of a result can rest on how many members of an artist's audience turn out to cast ballots. The professional side keeps fan turnout from being the only factor.

At the Billboard Music Awards, chart-driven years moved the decision away from ballots and toward measurement. Winners in those editions were based on sales, streaming, radio airplay and touring. Billboard's Hot 100 and Billboard 200 charts serve as the data foundation for these chart-based honors. Fan voting has still appeared in some Billboard categories in some years. The line between measured and voted outcomes has not always been clean.

The competing work differs as well. The Grammys and the Billboard awards center on recordings and the artists behind them. The VMAs center on music videos. That brings visual production into what voters weigh, alongside the song itself.

Each method also leaves something out. Peer voting captures the judgment of insiders, not the size of an audience. Fan voting reflects the people who choose to vote, not everyone who listens. Chart data records commercial activity, not an assessment of craft.

Comparing winners across years runs into the same issue at all three shows. Each has changed its rules over time. A win in one edition was not necessarily decided the same way as a win in another. Each result reflects the rules in force that year. A Grammy from an earlier year may have been decided before a nomination change. A VMA may come from a year with a different fan-vote split. A Billboard award may come from an edition with revised criteria.

Taken together, the three shows can judge one song by separate standards. Its recording can compete for a Grammy. Its video can compete for a VMA. In a chart-driven year, its chart performance can count toward a Billboard award. Each result describes a different part of how that song was made and received.

Some of the country's largest companies have spent the past few years ordering people back to their desks. At the end of 2024, Amazon $AMZN's chief executive, Andy Jassy, told corporate staff to come in five days a week. In the throes of the COVID-19 pandemic, Goldman Sachs $GS CEO David Solomon said working from home was "an aberration that we're going to correct as soon as possible." Many workers protested. But both men were determined to have employees working the same way they had before the pandemic.

Millions of office workers spent more than a year working from home. Many of them stopped working at the same hour every day and stopped answering messages late at night. Many more found meaning in their jobs. Once they were ordered back to the office, though, they had to decide whether they could maintain the same schedule without looking like they were slacking off.

Employees put up with return-to-office mandates if their manager accepts the schedule they built at home, according to Harvard Business School's Grace Mele-Cormier and colleagues. The problem is less about the time they have to come in and more about settling what time people can leave.

Mele-Cormier and his team found that employees tend to quit less often and earn slightly higher performance reviews when they're allowed to maintain the same working schedules as before. But many employees still fear that their boss will think they care less about the job if they go home at the same hour every day. They become less tense once they know their manager is on board, according to HBR's researchers.

The problem is that many managers never tell employees which hours they're expected to work. Their silence on the topic forces staff to assume they have to continue answering emails and messages at all hours of the day and night, just as they had done before the pandemic. Some employees end up leaving their roles for ones that have a clearer schedule.

Executives see the resignations, but they rarely learn that employees quit over their working hours. To executives, some teams seem to lose people for no reason while other teams don't.

Many managers never ask employees what time they need to leave each day. They assume people coming back to the office want one fixed schedule from their boss, with the same start and end time for everyone. If a manager asks each person what hours they need, the team ends up with different schedules for different people, and many managers worry they'll lose control of the team's day.

They also fear their staff will think the boss has no plan for how the team should work. Asking, though, actually leaves a manager with a better plan, according to Dan Cable, a London Business School professor. By hearing employees out, managers can spot the people most likely to quit over their working hours while there is still time to change their schedules. But Cable warns against giving employees decision-making powers. "You get a voice, not a vote," Cable told an audience at the school. "Listening shapes better decisions. It does not remove the leader's responsibility to decide."

Acting on what employees say doesn't always mean giving them what they asked for. That applies in almost every situation. After Jennifer Dulski and her leadership team laid off employees at Change.org, Dulski, then its president and chief operating officer, met with the remaining employees in several countries, according to the Society for Human Resource Management. She couldn't bring back the jobs the company cut. So her team started showing employees the company's finances instead. Once employees saw the numbers, Dulski said, they understood why their leaders had cut staff.

Other leaders change a plan or cover a cost after employees speak up. Geno Cutolo spent his first 90 days as president of staffing firm Adecco North America meeting employees across the business. He then redesigned a reorganization Adecco had already planned, to try to remove many of the frustrations and obstacles employees described to him. "It's important to act on what you hear," Cutolo said.

At Safeguard Global, the HR team heard that European employees were worried about winter heating bills, so the company paid them a monthly stipend through the winter, chief people officer Katherine Loranger said. In each case, employees could point to something that changed because they spoke up.

Employees expect the same proof after a manager asks about their hours. A manager can approve working hours, move team meetings to times everyone can make, or explain why a schedule can't change. If a manager asks and then changes and explains nothing, employees conclude the manager never had any intention to listen in the first place. Employees then stop telling managers what they need to know, and managers can't possibly tell which employees are thinking about leaving until they hand in their notice.

Can America Build More Housing Without Gutting Historic Protections? Inside the Senate’s Permitting Battle

A sweeping permitting reform package moved closer to a final Senate vote Monday, when lawmakers voted 70–27 to limit debate on legislation that could weaken a federal safeguard for historic places.

At issue is Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act, which requires federal agencies to consider how projects they fund, approve, or permit might affect historic properties—and consult with those affected before proceeding.

The proposed changes would exempt most projects whose only federal connection is financial assistance, while imposing tighter deadlines and restricting legal challenges.

As the nation confronts a shortage of 4.03 million homes and an aging energy grid, supporters argue that reducing regulatory hurdles will accelerate infrastructure development. But preservation advocates warn the changes could leave communities with less influence over what gets built—and what gets lost.

"Preservation is not a hindrance to progress," Heather Veneziano, director of Tulane University's Historic Preservation Program, told Realtor.com® during a recent walking interview through Bayou St. John, one of New Orleans' oldest neighborhoods.

Rather than keeping neighborhoods frozen in time, she argued, preservation can help communities determine how they evolve.

"Keeping things static is impossible," Veneziano said.

New Orleans provides a particularly salient standpoint from which to understand Veneziano’s perspective.

The city is home to 26 nationally recognized historic districts encompassing more than 45,000 properties and roughly 40% of its urbanized area, according to the city. Indeed, its architectural heritage is so extensive that it became the only entire city included on the World Monuments Fund's Irreplaceable America list.

Veneziano helped lead the nomination, informed in part by the aftermath of Hurricane Katrina and the connections to place that could not be rebuilt elsewhere.

"You lose all your really strong ties and connections to place, but also to people," she said of the communities uprooted after the devastating 2005 superstorm. "And places don't matter without people."

But Veneziano sees the buildings themselves as part of what makes those communities viable.

"It's an economic driver for the community, it's housing at a basic level for the community," she said. "Those are the containers for all of the services that happen in your community."

Along Bayou St. John, Veneziano pointed to the Pitot House, built in 1799, as an example of another kind of value older buildings retain.

Its elevated living quarters reflect a time when residents built knowing water would eventually come, while its surviving materials offer lessons in constructing homes suited to Louisiana's flood risks.

Yet, those lessons might have been erased had preservationists not found a way to accommodate another pressing community need.

In 1964, the religious order that owned the property needed land for a girls' school. Rather than allow its demolition, preservationists raised money to move the house 220 feet down the street on flatbed trucks.

The compromise allowed both the school and the house to continue serving the community. And today, the deal serves as a reminder of how preservation can accommodate development rather than prevent it.

Not every historic property can be relocated, however, and the expense of finding alternatives can test even the most committed to protecting a neighborhood's housing resources.

In March, Jane Place Neighborhood Sustainability Initiative, a community land trust dedicated to permanently affordable housing, sought permission to demolish a century-old shotgun house in Mid-City.

The nonprofit argued that rehabilitation would be prohibitively expensive and higher-density construction would better serve the property.

Preservation authorities disagreed, finding insufficient evidence that the house was beyond repair. Jane Place eventually withdrew its appeal without a finalized replacement development plan.

The spat concerned local demolition rules, not Section 106. But it exposed competing interpretations of how best to protect a neighborhood's housing resources: Preserve an existing home or make room for more?

Veneziano acknowledged that unclear local requirements can frustrate homeowners trying to maintain or renovate their properties.

"If your municipality doesn't have their act together and isn't clear about what their guidelines are," she said, "it can be very challenging to homeowners."

In a city facing an estimated shortage of 44,000 affordable rental units, the case for preserving old buildings can be difficult to reconcile with the urgent need for new homes. But elsewhere in the Big Easy, a long-vacant public building offers another approach.

In September, a century-old firehouse reopened after decades of vacancy, transformed into seven affordable apartments above an early-childhood education center.

The mixed-use redevelopment cost approximately $8 million, including $1.35 million in state historic tax credits and $725,000 in federal disaster recovery funds.

"The Louisiana Avenue Firehouse shows us that demolition of historic buildings is not always the answer," Louisiana Housing Corporation Executive Director Kevin Delahoussaye said.

But the very public investment that helped save the building also triggered Section 106 review—and that additional requirement is what housing advocates argue can make federally assisted developments more expensive and difficult to finance.

Their argument is simple: More reviews take more time. More time costs more money. And combined, that can make a project not worth it.

YIMBY Action, which supports the proposed Section 106 exemption, estimates that at least $10 billion annually in federal housing and community development funding could move faster under the broader permitting package, citing delays that can jeopardize financing or discourage developers from accepting public money altogether.

Veneziano emphasized that preservationists aren't simply trying to "save this fancy house." Their work, she explained, is "really community engagement"—helping residents recognize and protect histories that might otherwise be overlooked.

That concern brought hundreds of preservationists, civil rights advocates, and tribal representatives to the U.S. Capitol on Nov. 9 to oppose changes to Section 106.

"Our sacred places are living places of prayer," Harry Antonio Jr., governor of the Pueblo of Laguna in New Mexico, told the crowd.

The Senate proposal would narrow consideration of certain effects on historic places, including noise and visual impacts, raising concerns about the protection of sacred landscapes, burial grounds, and other culturally significant sites.

Veneziano pointed to a conflict farther upriver in Wallace, LA, where federal scrutiny exposed problems with a developer's assessment of a proposed grain terminal.

There, descendants of enslaved residents opposed the project, which would have been built near historic plantations, burial grounds, and their predominantly Black community.

A ProPublica investigation revealed that an archaeological consultant's original findings about potential harm to historic sites had been stripped from a report prepared for the developer. The Army Corps of Engineers subsequently rejected the assessment as insufficient and demanded further investigation.

In April 2024, the Corps determined that noise, light pollution, and the terminal's industrial appearance would adversely affect Wallace's historic character—precisely the kinds of impacts the Senate proposal would subject to a narrower standard of review.

The company behind the terminal abandoned the project later that year, blaming prolonged permitting delays. Residents celebrated the outcome as a victory.

Such lengthy reviews, however, may be the exception rather than the rule.

An October analysis by Virginia's Department of Historic Resources found that 85% of nearly 9,900 projects completed Section 106 review within 30 days. Just 6% took longer than 90 days, primarily because they required additional surveys or involved potential harm to historic properties.

Those figures aren't necessarily representative nationwide, nor do they establish whether reviews actually delayed construction.

"If you're trying to get a project through, yeah, it's annoying," Veneziano acknowledged.

Still, Section 106 doesn't prohibit development. It requires agencies to consider the consequences and potential alternatives before proceeding.

And as Congress weighs how to accelerate construction, lawmakers will also confront a more fundamental question: Who gets to determine what a community can afford to lose?

Veneziano posed a question of her own: "How do we take the best of what we have, and build upon it into the future?"

A short pre-launch check can catch problems before they affect campaign data or customer experience.

Read the ad and landing page together. The page should make it easy for visitors to confirm that they have arrived in the right place.

A mismatch can increase abandonment even if the page itself is technically functional.

Do not rely only on a desktop preview. Open the page on a mobile device and complete the main action from start to finish.

The U.S. Department of Education opened a string of investigations into teacher preparation programs on Nov. 13, alleging that they discriminate on the basis of race under Title VI or sex under Title IX.

The investigations mark one of the first times the department's Office for Civil Rights has used civil rights protections to challenge diversification efforts in teacher scholarships, hiring, training and recruitment practices.

The programs at the center of the investigations are aimed at recruiting historically underrepresented groups in the teaching profession, such as men and teachers of color.

“Race, national origin, or sex should not decide who will shape America’s next generation, yet many states and universities seem to prioritize recruiting and training ‘minority’ candidates rather than evaluating candidates based solely on merit,” said Assistant Secretary for Civil Rights Kimberly Richey in a Nov. 13 statement.

“Education agencies and institutions that consider race, national origin, or sex as a means to withhold opportunities in selecting, supporting, and preparing teaching candidates violate federal law and compromise education excellence."

Richey concluded that students should be "judged on their hard work and merit, not prejudged by the color of their skin or their sex.”

EdTrust, a nonprofit that advocates for racial and economic equity in American education, said in a statement to K-12 Dive that the Trump administration is using civil rights protections meant to "dismantle discrimination and expand opportunity" to instead "undermine pathways into teaching for people historically excluded from the profession betrays that purpose."

“The administration’s false divide between ‘merit’ and diversity perpetuates the damaging suggestion that teachers of color are less qualified or less deserving," said Eric Duncan, director of Pre-K-12 policy of EdTrust, in the Nov. 16 statement. "It discounts their skills, expertise, and contributions while ignoring the systemic barriers that have denied talented people opportunities for generations. Scholarships that address those barriers help ensure that family wealth does not determine who can become a teacher."

The Education Department's investigations, Duncan said, "risk turning civil rights enforcement into a tool for preserving the very inequities it should confront.”

CUNY, which houses the NYC Men Teach Program being investigated by the department, said in a statement to K-12 Dive that it was "proud of its role educating the teachers of tomorrow and our programs are open to all students regardless of race, gender or national origin."

According to the city's website, the program was created around 2015 in collaboration with the federal Education Department and then-Mayor Bill de Blasio with the goal of putting an additional 1,000 men of color into the city’s teacher pipeline.

A March 2026 evaluation of the program conducted for the NYC mayor's office by Westat, which provides research services for government agencies, shows that the program contributed over 3,100 new male teachers of color to New York City Public Schools between school years 2015–16 and 2023–24.

The American Association of Colleges for Teacher Education said in a Nov. 16 email to K-12 Dive, “Strengthening and diversifying the educator pipeline is essential to addressing persistent teacher shortages and ensuring every PK-12 student has access to well-prepared, effective teachers. Decades of research confirm that a diverse educator workforce benefits all students academically, socially, and emotionally." AACTE added that it does not comment on active federal inquiries involving specific institutions or state agencies.

While the investigations are some of the first of their kind by OCR, they build on previous efforts by the Trump administration to weed out DEI in teacher preparation programs — especially in the wake of the U.S. Supreme Court’s 2023 ruling in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard.

Some of the earliest federal grants terminated in 2025 by the administration were under the Supporting Effective Educator Development Grant Program, the Teacher Quality Partnership Program and the Teacher and School Leader Incentive Program, due to what it said was the promotion of "divisive ideologies". The department admitted in a March court filing that it had cancelled 104 out of 109 active TQP and SEED grants.

The loss of over 100 federal teacher training grants last year caused at least 127 teacher candidates to drop out of their programs and another 259 accepted candidates to not enroll after all, according to survey findings released in October.

Those cuts were preceded by an anti-DEI directive that has since been heavily litigated and was most recently blocked in court in September.

That anti-DEI letter specifically called for agency personnel to review all new grant awards and issued grants to ensure those federal dollars “do not fund discriminatory practices — including in the form of DEI.” The directive added that “Illegal DEI policies and practices can violate both the letter and purpose of Federal civil rights law and conflict with the Department’s policy of prioritizing merit, fairness, and excellence in education.”

The Justice Department in August 2025 also warned districts they could be exposed to legal liability by asking job applicants how their “cultural background informs their teaching,” using recruitment strategies targeting candidates from specific geographic areas or racial backgrounds, and asking job candidates to describe how they overcame obstacles.

Since the Supreme Court’s decision in Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard, civil rights groups have raised concerns that it would set back educational equity efforts even outside of race-conscious admissions. That decision undid race-conscious policies in college admissions, and the Education Department under Trump has used it to justify scaling back other diversity, equity and inclusion initiatives in education.

Scholarship availability, teacher pipelines and student affinity groups were among the top areas beyond college access that advocates were concerned could be impacted in the wake of the ruling.

World of Warcraft: Forever has suffered its first economic nightmare, as a dungeon farm exploit has left some players with thousands of gold in their bags while the vast majority of players are barely above their first hundred.

In basic terms, the new Blackmaw Hold dungeon includes several packs of non-elite, easy to kill enemies right at the start of the dungeon. Prior to the hotfix, the loot drop tables for these enemies were incorrect, and worth vastly more in terms of gold than they should have. Keen-eyed players spotted this but kept it quiet, running into the dungeon and quickly killing off these early mobs and resetting the dungeon every hour.

This was possible on every class, and according to Twitch streamer Ziqo, allowed players to farm out hundreds of gold in a few hours. He is seen streaming with over 4,000 gold - and claims other streamers made out with more than that.

For an idea of how much money that is, one of the most expensive things a player will likely buy on their character throughout the original Classic World of Warcraft is the expert riding skill. It takes a long time to save up for, and stands as a major accomplishment in the life of a character. It costs around 900 gold. These players, in just a few days of dedicated grinding, will have been able to pay this price several times over.

Looking at community hubs for the game (Reddit or the official World of Warcraft forums), regular players are demanding Blizzard take action - going further than just the already-deployed hotfix. Maya on the WoW forum wants the ill-gotten gold to be removed and for players to be banned who made use of the Blackmaw Hold exploit, writing: "Remove the gold or I and many others will just quit. Flnrodi on Reddit, referring to streamers who took advantage of this exploit, wrote: "Not sure what to say. The fact that he did this all offstream says enough that [Ziqo] (and others) knew that this is not intended and 100 percent considered an exploit. Personally, I think there should be some kind of punishment and definitely all the gold [should be] revoked."

You may be wondering why this is such a big deal? So what if a few players can get fancy mounts faster, or snatch up some really good gear on the player auction house? Well, exorbitant sums of money in the hands of a few players can have a massive impact on a server's economy. You could, for example, use your newfound wealth to flip every listing of a certain rare resource on the auction house and mark the price up significantly for profit, which obviously inflates the price for regular players. Likewise, you can out-bid other players for rare and powerful BOE items, which in turn raises the prices of those as sellers may find they can make more cash than they could off such drops prior to the exploit.

This all has an inflationary effect, and could push a server's economy to a more expensive level faster than it otherwise would. Even if this effect isn't so drastic, these players can essentially gear out multiple characters in fantastic gear without putting in the hours, or out-bid the rest of the community on the Black Market Auction House for rare and largely unparalleled special loot.

The counter argument against punitive measures is that these players didn't cheat. They didn't buy gold from real-money-traders, nor did they plug in some external tool to give them an unfair advantage over others. They simply put in the time to get to around level 55, ran a dungeon, and killed mobs repeatedly for valuable drops. Would it have been a good thing to flag the exploit the moment they found out about it? Sure. But once other players at your level take advantage of it, and no strict ruling around whether doing so is punishable, it's arguably in a player's best interest to join in while the going is good.

As of writing, there's been no official word from Blizzard on what it intends to do beyond fixing the exploit. Given the required level to take on Blackmaw Hold, the number of exploiters cannot be too large, and the sheer amount of gold they gained in a small amount of time should make them easy to flag. Rolling back their accounts and reverting the gold they gained back to a pre-exploit amount certainly appears to be a popular option. Whether Blizzard actually does so, or enforces other punishments, is the big question.

Meta has changed how Instagram picks posts to show users in India. The aim is to show less sexually suggestive and violent content. The company made the change public on Tuesday, Reuters reported.

Meta now uses better AI models to check posts against its rules, Bloomberg reported. TNW has not seen Meta’s own statement and relies on both reports.

The change covers posts from accounts that users do not follow, according to Meta. It limits what Instagram pushes to users. It does not ban the posts.

Meta said users in India now flag suggested posts about half as often as a year ago. It did not share the raw figures, Reuters noted.

Users can also ask for fewer political posts and less sensitive content. In India, the most mature posts are not suggested by default, according to Bloomberg.

The update follows an October 10 notice from India’s tech ministry. It asked Meta to review its recommendation systems, so false or misleading posts do not spread too far.

Meta has faced months of pressure in India, its biggest market by users. In July, the ministry summoned Meta executives after the BBC found Instagram ads that pushed child abuse material. It then ordered the ads removed.

The Homeownership Gap Between Young and Old Americans Has Doubled—These Cities Reveal Why

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The homeownership gap between younger and older Americans has more than doubled over the past four decades, with some of the nation's most expensive cities seeing the sharpest increases, according to a new report.

The analysis, from the Urban Institute, finds that nationally just 43% of households headed by adults aged 25 to 35 owned their homes in 2024, compared with 79% of those headed by adults 65 and older. The 35.8-percentage-point difference is more than twice the 17.5-point gap recorded in 1980.

But that divide pales in comparison to the gaping chasm in some of America's most expensive metropolitan areas. Between 1980 and 2024, the homeownership gap grew by 29 percentage points in Denver, 23 points in New York City, and 22 points in San Francisco, compared with 18.3 points nationally.

But those widening gaps also expose a deeper economic trade-off: The cities offering young Americans the greatest opportunities to earn more may be the very places preventing them from building wealth through homeownership

"The cities with the strongest labor markets tend to be the least affordable, so some will take the better job and rent indefinitely, giving up equity accumulation," explains Hannah Jones, senior economist at Realtor.com®. "Others will pass up higher-paying roles or move to cheaper, lower-opportunity markets in order to buy."

In 1980, younger households in Denver were nearly as likely as their older counterparts to own a home.

At the time, 55% of households headed by adults aged 25 to 35 owned their homes, compared with 67% of households headed by adults 65 and older.

By 2024, the younger group's homeownership rate had fallen to just 37%, while the older group's rate climbed to 78%, expanding the divide from 12 to 41 percentage points.

But the decline in young homeownership may reflect something besides worsening affordability: Denver's success in attracting young workers.

"Two things are likely at work. One is the affordability decline itself. The other is Denver’s growth as an economic hub that appeals to young workers, many of whom arrive as renters," says Jones. "That adds renters to the mix and dilutes the young homeownership rate, even for people who never intended to buy."

There, younger households were already far less likely to own homes in 1980, when their homeownership rate stood at 28%. By 2024, it had slipped only slightly, to 26%.

Older New Yorkers, meanwhile, saw their homeownership rate climb from approximately 43% to 63% over the same period.

"In New York, homeownership has always been expensive and out of reach for most young households, so there was little room to fall," Jones explains. "Young homeownership slipped only slightly, which suggests the current rate is close to a floor."

She suggests low COVID-19 pandemic-era mortgage rates may have helped some older households that had been waiting to purchase finally enter the market.

And regardless of how the divide developed, entering homeownership later can carry lasting financial consequences.

Separate research from Realtor.com, co-authored by Jones, found that purchasing a home by age 30 was associated with 22.5% higher net worth—approximately $119,000 more—by age 50, compared with buying in one's 40s, even after accounting for income, education, and marital status.

Those advantages can extend across generations, too. Children raised in homeowner households were 18.4 percentage points more likely to become homeowners themselves by age 35, the same research found.

One of the Urban Institute's most striking findings concerns a milestone that comes before homeownership: establishing an independent household.

In 1980, young adults in high-cost metropolitan areas were slightly more likely than their counterparts nationally to head their own households. By 2024, that relationship had reversed.

Just 41% of young adults in expensive markets were household heads, the lowest share among the study's housing cost categories.

Instead, more are living with parents, other relatives, or roommates. Research from Realtor.com backs this up, finding that a third of young adults lived with their parents across the country in 2025.

Yet expensive cities haven't lost their appeal. The share of young households living in high-cost markets actually edged up from 28% in 1980 to 31% in 2024, partly reflecting a broader shift toward urban areas.

What's changed is their housing tenure. In those markets, the homeownership rate among younger households fell from 40% to 32% over the same period.

So young adults are continuing to pursue opportunities in expensive cities, but fewer are establishing households of their own once they get there. And another finding suggests that rapidly rising prices may be changing the age composition of metropolitan areas, regardless of how expensive those places were to begin with.

Between 2014 and 2024, metropolitan areas experiencing faster home price appreciation generally saw declines in young adults' share of the population, the Urban Institute found.

The relationship held across high-, moderate-, and low-cost markets. But Jones cautions that the findings don't necessarily mean young people are being priced out.

"That can come from young people leaving, older people moving in, or both," she says. "Florida metros on the included chart may be an example. Their older populations are large and grew significantly during the pandemic, which could erode the young share on its own."

Indeed, the relationship between home prices and demographic changes could run in either direction.

"Young people may be fleeing places where costs are climbing, or older, more financially equipped buyers may be moving in and competing for homes," Jones says. "That pushes prices up, and the newcomers then make up a larger share of the population."

Determining whether younger residents are actually leaving would require examining changes in the number of young adults, not just their share of the population.

Still, other economic research suggests that housing costs can constrain where workers pursue employment.

A May 2025 analysis from the Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond found that geographic mobility has declined across all age groups since the 1990s. Among the contributing factors, researchers identified restrictions on housing supply in economically productive cities, where high costs can discourage workers from relocating to pursue better opportunities.

The researchers also identified another potential obstacle: Older homeowners have become less responsive to rising local housing costs, making them more likely to stay put even as prices climb. In supply-constrained markets, that can make it harder for newcomers to move in.

Meanwhile, a February 2026 analysis from the St. Louis Fed found that home prices had risen approximately 207% from 2000 to 2024, outpacing the roughly 155% increase in per-capita incomes.

Together, the findings suggest that the costs of entering high-opportunity housing markets could affect younger Americans' earnings prospects as well as their ability to build wealth.

There may be a way around that trade-off for some.

"Remote work may be loosening the link between where people live and where the jobs are, which could enable more households to achieve homeownership without giving up job opportunities," Jones says.

But that flexibility is not available to everyone. Workers whose jobs require them to be physically present may face a more difficult choice between buying a home where they can afford one and pursuing careers in the cities where their skills command the highest wages.

And unlike the wealth accumulated by longtime homeowners, the career opportunities they pass up may be difficult to recover later.

Plants mix different breeding strategies to survive in a world with changing climates and fewer bees

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Simulations show plants that reject their own pollen and plants that self-fertilize can coexist for ages, swapping dominance without settling.

Apple trees have a strict biological rule for reproduction. They reject their own pollen. That means they need a completely different type of apple tree nearby to produce fruit.

While many wild plants rely on this exact same partner system, plenty of other plant species have evolved to drop the rule entirely, allowing them to successfully fertilize themselves.

A population holding both kinds was supposed to be passing through on its way to choosing one rule or the other.

In computer simulations of evolving plants, the mixed populations didn’t get there. With nothing in the environment changing, the two kinds kept trading dominance for as long as the simulations ran. Neither kind took over.

The researchers call it a volatile mixed mode. A mixed population in the wild that looks unstable, they wrote, may be in a lasting state of its own.

Tamar Friedlander, a senior lecturer at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem’s Robert H. Smith Institute of Plant Sciences and Genetics in Agriculture, led the work with Amit Jangid.

Ohad Noy Feldheim of the University’s Einstein Institute of Mathematics is the third author. Everything here happened inside a model.

In an interview with Earth.com, Friedlander said the result broke a habit of thought. Biologists expect that when a population has several options, some are better than others, she said.

Over the long run, the population is supposed to reach the better one and stay there. Earlier models had the mix fading away or holding steady at fixed proportions.

“Thus, it was very surprising that we didn’t get either of these under a large parameter regime,” she said.

The populations, in her words, “not only did not settle to either of the ‘pure’ strategies (only self-fertilization or only outcrossing) but also did not settle at fixed proportions of these strategies.”

The rejection system works like a lock and a set of keys. The female side of a flower makes a toxin that stops a pollen tube from growing. Each pollen grain carries proteins that disarm the toxins of other mating types but not its own.

About 40 percent of plant families use some version of this, including wild tomatoes, potatoes, apples, pears, citrus, and coffee. Washington State University‘s tree fruit site has a primer on why orchards need two varieties.

The system can break. A mutation that silences the toxin, or adds a key that fits the plant’s own lock, produces a plant that can fertilize itself.

That plant gains a sure route to seeds when pollen is scarce, but it passes on two sets of genes to each seed instead of one.

It pays for that with inbreeding. Seeds from self-fertilization carry more harmful mutations, and more of them fail.

The plants that reject their own pollen pay a different price. Their pollen is useless on every plant of their own mating type, so a type that grows common finds fewer partners.

Friedlander’s team simulated populations of 500 plants for 100,000 generations, discarding the first 50,000 as a warm-up.

Each plant carried two copies of the gene set, and each copy held one toxin and nine keys. Every protein was written as a string of 18 amino acids that could mutate.

Two dials set the outcome. One was how badly self-fertilized seeds suffer from inbreeding. The other was how loosely the keys fit the locks, which decides how many mating types a population can hold.

With heavy inbreeding costs and loose-fitting keys, every plant ended up rejecting its own pollen. With light costs, every plant ended up self-fertilizing.

In between lay the third state, where the share of self-rejecting plants rose and fell for the rest of the run and the number of mating types swung with it.

Types tended to vanish several at a time, in what the authors describe as avalanches. What triggers those collapses, they wrote, is still obscure.

A shortage of pollen was expected to push populations toward self-fertilization, since a plant that can pollinate itself doesn’t need a visitor. The model found the opposite.

When cross-pollen was scarce, the range of settings in which self-rejection won grew at the expense of the mixed state. Friedlander said the explanation lies in how the two sexes compete.

When pollen is plentiful, every female is sure of a partner and only the males compete, so female proteins evolve mainly to avoid matching their own pollen.

When pollen is scarce, females must compete too, which makes them easier to match and raises the number of mating types a population can support.

Researchers often treat self-fertilization as a plant’s backup plan for when cross-pollination fails, she told Earth.com. Self-fertilizing plants do turn up at the edges of a species’ range, where partners are few.

The move from rejecting self-pollen to accepting it has long been treated as a one-way street. A plant that gives up the rule usually sheds the flower traits that attract insects and purges some of its harmful mutations.

Across much of the model’s settings, though, the end state didn’t depend on the start, and a population that began self-fertilizing could evolve the rejection system from scratch.

The exception was the loosest-fitting keys, where the mutation needed for the reverse trip was so rare that the wait became impossibly long.

The model also held the cost of inbreeding fixed, so it describes the short term, before prolonged self-fertilization purges enough mutations to change the odds.

Mixed populations exist in the wild. Long-term studies that track one population’s proportions over time are lacking, the authors wrote, and those are what would test the prediction.

The model also predicts that mixed populations carry fewer and shorter-lived mating types than purely self-rejecting ones, and more inbreeding written into their genomes. Both could be checked in plant genomes.

Asked which population she would most like to watch, Friedlander pointed to wild tomato species growing in South America.

She also said the model doesn’t apply directly to orchards, where trees are genetically identical and do not evolve. Self-rejecting crops do depend on pollinators, she added, and a decline in them could cut yields.

A warming climate and fewer pollinating insects both change the pressures on how a plant reproduces, the authors wrote.

Whether real populations respond by picking a side, or by swinging between the two for generations, is what a long field study of those wild tomatoes could begin to answer.

The full study was published in the journal PRX Life.

Which launch providers in particular? So far, AST has flown its satellites with India's ISRO, Blue Origin, and SpaceX itself. That last one is obviously suboptimal, though -- it's not a great corporate strategy to make yourself dependent on your biggest competitor for launch services!

And there are other options. The list of private companies that have successfully flown a rocket to orbit may not be a long one, but it's getting longer:

A couple of other names might deserve to be on this list, having flown once or twice but not continually. But for the most part, this is it. And one of these launch names jumps out especially: Firefly.

Last week, you see, Firefly offered to help AST out of its jam ... and collect a big payday for the service. Viewed from the perspective of a Firefly investor, the only quibble I have with this deal is the form of payment. Firefly says AST will pay the $1.3 billion in "cash and shares of AST Class A Common Stock." The cash part is fine. When all's said and done, as much as $900 million of the $1.3 billion may be paid in hard currency. james bridle and the power of solar, zooplankton, and non-human entities

ancient symbols and microscopic organisms meet contemporary solar technology in an artistic reflection on energy, ecology, and coexistence.

annalise kamegawa November 17, 2026 7 min

Back in 2022, James Bridle—artist, technologist, and author of the book New Dark Age (2018)—created a work called Solar Panels (Radiolaria Series). It was a series of five functional 100W photovoltaic generators, the faces of which were etched with tiny, microscopic zooplankton called Radiolaria. Looking at images of their silica skeletons, enlarged hundreds of times so they are visible to the human eye, reveals a universe of spiky arms, swirling bodies, intricate lattices, and geometric patterns. Juxtaposed with each other, and containing their own advanced modes of capturing energy, Bridle creates a compelling parallel between non-human and human intelligence, and what that could mean for the future of the earth.

Across their writing, installations, and lectures, James Bridle has become known for thinking about the ways that technology, the natural world, and humanity influence each other, with a strong emphasis on understanding ‘intelligence’. They look beyond the popular, mainstream ideas associated with the word, often linked to IQ, large language models, and other forms of reconstructing human thought patterns. In their piece, AI was built on human intelligence. Nature has more to teach, they express the urgency of going beyond this definition, and to learn from the other creatures we share the Earth with.

It is this line of thinking that has underwritten their works with solar panels, water collection systems, and vernacular architecture. Looking back to their 2019 exhibition, Through Other Eyes, which they curated with NeMe Art Centre in Cyprus, an emphasis on more-than-human forms of thinking and creating is evident. Here, they invoked the 1922 writing of Dziga Vertov, in which he describes his fascination with documentation through the lens of a camera. Bridle parallels this with the technologies of the twenty-first century – all the mania, fascination, and consequences – saying that, if we apply these new or different ‘eyes’, those of machines and animals, ‘They might also, if we are open to them, offer us visions of unexpected insight, and startling beauty.’ It is within this vision – trans-species and trans-machine – that Bridle’s work takes form.

Putting an image of radiolaria on a solar panel conjures ideas about the ability to sustain life and other modalities of using energy generated by natural processes. It calls into question the human need to consume power. Radiolaria, microscopic yet possessing highly advanced skeletal structures, can exist symbiotically with algae that provide them with energy. There is an ancient logic to their existence that can be traced back to the Cambrian. Bridle, in their description of the project, highlights how they were ‘famously illustrated, by the German naturalist Ernst Haeckel, who coined the term “ecology” in 1866.’ The complex structures of these organisms continue to inspire wonder, demonstrating how something so small can be so powerful and complex.

Expanding this logic to the scale of solar panels, a technologically intensive mode of capturing and converting energy from the sun in a way that can power human inventions calls into question the effectiveness of human ‘technology’ when compared to that of the non-human. Here is an incredible invention that can create sustainable energy, a promising alternative to fossil fuels, yet it takes advanced electronics and logistics to achieve what a single-celled organism has been doing for thousands of years. Here, Bridle once again asks us to consider what innovation means, not when we compare it to the span of human history.

In 2023, Bridle expanded on the concept in Things Said, Things Seen, and Things Done, an installation on the archaeological site of Elefsina in Greece, Mystery 19 Visual Arts Initiator III – But Don’t Tell Anyone, just north of Athens. Instead of radiolaria, the images engraved on three 100W solar photovoltaic panels are ‘the wheatsheaf, the poppy, and the calathus,’ which ‘honor the ancient cult of Demeter, and the veneration of the living Earth which she represents.’ Here, the comparison of contemporary power generation with beliefs in ancient deities asks us to question the ways in which energy is created, used, and distributed. Bridle used solar panels to power other works in the installation and the site itself, going on to remark that ‘The technology to do these things, is available to us, and the knowledge of how to do them is not a mystery, nor a secret, but it might require new rituals, and new initiations, to distribute it more widely. This is one of those rituals.’

Ultimately, James Bridle’s solar panel creations reflect a broader vision of posthuman utopias that runs throughout their artistic practice: one in which technology, humanity, and the natural world exist in a more reciprocal relationship. By bringing together ancient natural intelligence and contemporary technologies, Bridle challenges human exceptionalism and conventional ideas of progress.
