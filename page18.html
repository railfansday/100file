Cologne Auction House Moves Forward with Sale of Gothic Paintings as Jewish Dealer’s Heirs Claim They Were ‘Stolen’

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Cologne-based Lempertz auction house said it still planned to sell two works by Gothic painter Johann Koerbecke after the heirs of a German Jewish art dealer who was persecuted by the Nazis claimed the pieces were “stolen.”

Dealer Max Stern held the paintings in Düsseldorf between 1936 and 1937, the year the Nazis shut down his gallery. Per a provenance supplied by Lempertz, both paintings were then held by Düsseldorf’s Galerie Hans Bammann, which appears to have then sold them. On Monday, the Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation issued a release in which it called on Lempertz to stop the sale of the two works planned for Saturday. According to the foundation, such a sale should not occur “until two distinct ownership claims of different victims are justly resolved according to the Washington Principles on Nazi-Confiscated Art.”

Both paintings—The Annunciation and The Vision of Saint Bernard—are likely from Marienfeld high altarpiece, which was completed in 1457. They are being auctioned in a sale of Old Masters art. They carry high estimates of around 300,000 euros each, or about $329,000 apiece, making them the auction’s most expensive works. “For art historical reasons,” the Lempertz lot entries read, “the two important panels (Lots 1005 and 1006) will first be offered individually and then together.”

The Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation pointed out that both works appear in several databases of missing artworks, including the Interpol Database of Stolen Art since 2021. On the German Lost Art Database, both are reported as “Nazi-confiscated property”; the foundation registered that claim with the organization in 2018.

The foundation has demanded the return of multiple artworks belonging to Stern over the past decade, and in some cases, it has been successful. In 2017, for example, the FBI returned a painting by Jan Franse Verzijl that had been on view at an US art fair two years earlier.

Potentially complicating the matter is the fact that, prior to Stern’s possession of the Koerbecke paintings, they were owned by Ellen Funke, who, during the Nazi era, was classified as a Mischling, or “mongrel,” because she had Jewish ancestry through her mother. She sold her collection in 1936 to fund her emigration from Germany, and works she once owned have also been the subject of restitution claims—two of which happen to involve the Koerbecke works.

In 2025, Bavaria’s state museums restituted Koerbecke’s Vision of Saint Bernard to Funke’s heirs. At the time, the Bayerische Staatsgemäldesammlungen noted that The Vision of Saint Bernard and The Annunciation were at one point part of a single work, with both images appearing on either side of the same panel, and that Stern had taken them apart with the hope of selling them both. Funke’s heirs are listed as the sellers of The Vision of Saint Bernard on the auction house’s website.

Similarly, Funke’s heirs are also listed as the sellers of the The Annunciation, which entered the Kolumba Art Museum of the Archdiocese of Cologne as a donation in 1996. The museum restituted the work in 2025.

The Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation’s release notably does not mention Funke by name. But it does state that the foundation “maintains that the rights and histories of both Jewish victims must be taken into account, and neither claim should be privileged at the expense of the other.” In an email to ARTnews, a spokesperson for the foundation said “the museums restituted the paintings to Ellen Funke’s heirs in accordance with the priority principle set forth in the guidelines established in Germany to apply the Washington Principles,” adding that that restitution ”did not result in a ‘just and fair solution’ in accordance with the Washington Principles.”

In a statement to ARTnews, Lempertz said it would not cancel its planned sale of the paintings by Koerbecke, who is widely considered one of the great painters of the Gothic era in Germany.

“We adhere to current restitution practices and follow the recommendations of the Federal Government,” a representative for the auction house said, claiming that the Stern “had the panels sawn in two” and went on to sell one of them for “double the price.”

“These magnificent paintings were not taken away from Max Stern,” the Lempertz representative said, adding, “Max Stern received compensation after the war; the heirs of the Funke family did not.”

The Stern Foundation spokesperson said the “view that the paintings were not ‘seized’ from Max Stern has long been refuted,” citing a 2019 statement from the now-defunct German Advisory Commission that the persecution of Jewish art dealers began at latest by August 1933, “so that from that point on at the latest, it can in no way be assumed that ‘proper, customary business transactions’ took place.” The spokesperson added that the claim that Max Stern was compensated for the paintings is “incorrect“ and has “no legal basis.”

Lempertz did not dispute the listings for both paintings on the Art Loss Register, but the representative also noted that the listings contain details of the works’ respective restitutions.

Hougan’s optimistic views signal significant market shifts. His comments show that as institutional capital enters the on-chain space, the complete transparency of public blockchains may pose a major obstacle for serious players.

How Does The REO Runabout Stack Up Against Slate?

Like the idea of a Slate but don't want an EV? Well, an upstart from Dallas, Texas has just the thing for you. That is, if it ever becomes a reality. Startup company REO recently unveiled renderings of its new truck, dubbed the Runabout. It's a similar idea to Slate, with just one basic frame, three different body variants to choose from, no frills, a bunch of customization, and a sub-$30,000 price tag. The main difference is that that Runabout uses a four-cylinder gas engine, rather than electric motors. So how does it compare to Slate?

First, let's look at price. The most basic Slate you can get—cheekily called "The Blank Slate"—costs $24,950, and it's a two-door pickup with nothing more than some seats, a steering wheel, a battery, and a single rear electric motor to get you around. There's no radio, power windows, or screens to speak of.

The cheapest REO you can get will be the Runabout T4X, which costs $21,500*. Note the asterisk, as REO says the current listed price is an estimate, and the company has no factory or clear plans for getting it to market. For that shockingly low price, it's estimated that you'll get a four-cylinder engine, a choice of either a six-speed manual or automatic transmission, and mechanical four-wheel drive. It's a two-door, two-seat pickup with drop-down sides for the bed, like a Kei truck.

What four-cylinder engine will the Runabout have and which manufacturer will supply it? REO isn't saying. There are far more questions than answers so far. REO does say that it has a "targeted powertrain life of 500,000 miles." To quote myself reading that in the press release: "lol."

While Slate uses a hybrid body-on-frame style chassis, the REO has an old-school ladder frame, like a classic pickup. It's similar to slate in that it offers modular body styles, but with key differences. There's a two-door pickup and an SUV version, like Slate, but there's also a four-door pickup, which Slate doesn't have. REO claims its truck has solid rear axles, too, to give it more off-road credibility.

The design isn't bad, even if it looks like a Grand Theft Auto version of an Australian-market Land Cruiser, but that's to be expected. REO hired longtime automotive designer Frank Stephenson, whose previous work includes the first BMW-owned Mini Cooper, the Maserati MC12, and the McLaren P1. Overall, it looks handsome enough and utilitarian, even if all the photos are just renders at this point.

Inside, REO's truck is said to be pretty barebones (it hasn't been revealed yet), but not as barebones as Slate's. There's a digital screen for gauges and one for navigation, but everything else has a physical control. You're probably wondering if it has power or hand-crank windows. REO used its press release to throw a little shade: " Each model will feature power windows for convenience: hand-cranks are best left to others."

The entire idea behind REO is to offer the sort of basic pickups customers loved in the '80s and '90s, trucks that were simple utilitarian workhorses and nothing else. It's an admirable goal, especially given how expensive pickups have become. But do customers want to go backward in powertrain technology, especially with affordable mainstream electric trucks like the Ford Fathom coming to market soon?

So for less money, you can get a potentially more capable truck, in a similar configuration as the Slate, with a bit of extra interior comfort. But you lose the electric powertrain, which reduces maintenance and fuel costs. Instead, you'll have to make due with a generic four-cylinder of some sort. There is one big "if," though. You can only buy one if REO ever actually makes it. Sure, having a design by Frank Stephenson helps it feel more real, but just because they found the money to pay him to design something doesn't mean they'll actually get around to building it.

Slate is already here, it's ready to launch production vehicles soon. We've seen them in person, touched them, and tested them. Until REO can actually muster a viable product, it should probably avoid throwing anymore shade in its press releases.

The provenance in the Sotheby’s catalogue entry notes, as it has done since last month, that the painting was acquired by the Berlin publisher Samuel Fischer and his wife Hedwig in 1909. No details have been released on the nature of the agreement and whether the Fischer family will receive financial compensation.

What impact, if any, the short-lived dispute will have on the sale will only become apparent with the bidding.

Bitcoin Lightning developers needed two patches to stop a potentially costly attack

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The initial July update blocked one crash method but missed a persistent vulnerability that could leave affected nodes unable to restart.

Bitcoin's Core Lightning software required two security patches to eliminate a vulnerability that could repeatedly crash payment nodes and potentially expose funds held in other channels.

A Nov. 13 security disclosure revealed that an initial repair released in July failed to address a second attack method that could keep affected nodes offline even after they restarted.

The vulnerability, tracked as LNF-2026-0004, affected Core Lightning, one of the software implementations used to operate nodes on Bitcoin's Lightning Network.

Security researcher Nishant Bansal discovered that attackers could exploit how Core Lightning assigned identifiers to payment channels, creating duplicate records that caused the software to crash.

The weakness originated in the network's channel-opening process, where each payment channel receives an identifier derived from the Bitcoin transaction output that funds it. Core Lightning could accept multiple channels referencing the same funding output, producing identical identifiers that conflicted with assumptions in its internal software.

In the first attack, a malicious peer could create duplicate pending channels and crash a victim's node simply by reconnecting. The attack required no Bitcoin transaction fees because the fraudulent channels did not need on-chain funding. Each subsequent reconnection could trigger another crash, allowing attackers to repeatedly interrupt payment processing.

However, researchers identified a more persistent variation involving previously closed channels.

By reusing an identifier associated with a closed channel and subsequently closing another channel with the same identifier, attackers could trigger a software failure that persisted across restarts.

Unlike the initial attack, this method required on-chain transaction fees but could leave the node unable to recover without a software upgrade.

Core Lightning developers addressed the original vulnerability in version 26.06.6, released on July 22, after researchers privately reported the reconnection attack earlier that month.

The update prevented new channels from reusing funding outputs associated with existing live channels. It also modified connection handling to prevent duplicate records already stored from triggering the original crash.

However, the protection did not extend to identifiers associated with closed channels, leaving the second attack route available.

Researcher Crypt-iQ independently identified the remaining weakness, prompting developers to introduce broader safeguards in version 26.06.8, released Sept. 22.

The second update expanded duplicate-identifier checks across active and closed channels belonging to different peers. It also allowed nodes containing previously stored duplicate records to recover without repeatedly crashing.

Does It Matter That We’ve Taken the “Mormon” Out of the Mormon Wives?

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There is something about hearing “come ye saints, come ye sinners” that sets off a Pavlovian response in my brain; a physical trigger to sling the (dirty) Diet Coke back and turn the volume up. It means The Secret Lives of Mormon Wives is back, this time with its OC-set spinoff: The Secret Lives of Mormon Wives: Orange County.

In the show’s marketing, Hulu leans into the California setting as the clear divide between SLOMW: OC and its Utah predecessor. SLOMW: OC isn’t as ensconced in the shadow of the Mormon church as the Utah show is. Instead, it offers a more laid-back version of Mormonism (they drink! they get tattoos! they’re in throuples!). But in taking the “Mormon” out of the “Mormon Wives,” are we missing out on what makes the show so interesting?

It’s a bold choice to greenlight a spinoff about Mormons without actually casting any Mormons. In fact, of the eight new cast members (Jen Affleck is the only familiar face), only four identify as practicing Mormons at all: Mayci Neely’s identical-looking sister McCall DaPron, McCall’s bestie Jordan McClanahan, YouTuber Madison Bontempo, and Ashleigh Pease.

Meanwhile, the other half of the cast have either left the church altogether, as is the case with influencers Aspyn Ovard and Salome Andea, or were never religious to begin with like podcasters Avery Woods and Bobbi Althoff. It’s the “Molly Mormons,” or traditional Mormons, vs. the “Black Cats,” the rebels of the group. What connects these women is not so much a shared religion as a shared career path: influencing and podcasting.

SLOMW Utah’s strength was its portrayal of the contradictory rules of Mormonism. For example, what do you mean it goes against the church to drink caffeine but it’s totally fine to get high off laughing gas at a Botox appointment?? The rules only got more interesting as the show progressed and the women went from housewives to reality TV megastars overnight. Suddenly they were grappling with bigger religious conundrums: how to be both subordinate in the church and a breadwinner at home.

SLOMW: OC takes a different approach by focusing more on influencer mom drama, shifting away from the central tension of the show: women striving for independence and equality while navigating deeply patriarchal relationships. Take Mayci in season 5 when she fends off a fourth pregnancy request from her mother, sister, and husband (keep in mind, she just popped out baby #3!). Or Jessi, whose husband Jordan felt emasculated by the number of zeros in her paycheck.

In the Orange County-set spinoff, the men are curiously absent. Madison, Salome, and Aspyn are the only women to feature their romantic partners on screen—and even then it’s only a few moments of the show’s eight-episode run. The absence of the men feels like a strategic response to the DadTok hate that plagued the Utah show. Cursor Traces Malicious Claude Code Extension to Third-Party Breach

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A compromised key issued to AI infrastructure provider Fireworks was used by threat actors to access Cursor data and credentials.

Cursor has traced the origins of a security incident involving a malicious version of Anthropic's Claude Code extension in its marketplace to a breach at AI infrastructure provider Fireworks.

As many as 5,000 users may have downloaded the tampered extension during the roughly 11 hours it was live on Oct. 29. It was a reminder of the risk organizations face when they rely on third-party marketplaces for software applications and extensions. It also illustrates how one security lapse can lead to a cascade of subsequent breaches and supply chain attacks.

When Cursor first disclosed the incident, the company said attackers had placed malicious versions of Anthropic's Claude Code extension in a cache serving its Visual Studio (VS) Code extension marketplace. Users who requested either of two specific cached versions of the extension (2.1.312 and 2.1.313) during the exposure window may have downloaded the malicious builds, the company said. The malicious extensions attempted to collect information about infected systems and establish persistent remote access, including by installing an attacker-controlled SSH key.

Related:Grok Bots Attempt to Access Corporate Data Without User Approval

Cursor quickly removed the malicious extensions, temporarily shut down its marketplace, and audited the store for signs of further tampering. Cursor also rotated its keys, rebuilt the marketplace, and worked with the cloud provider hosting the attacker's server to take it offline. The company's initial advisory, however, did not explain how the attacker might have gained access to its environment and planted the malicious extension in its extensions marketplace.

In an update on Nov. 13, Cursor filled in that blank, with a detailed description of what its investigation had unearthed in the days following the Oct. 29 incident.

The intrusion, according to Cursor, began when an attacker obtained an access key the company had issued to Fireworks, an AI startup based in San Mateo, Calif. Cursor said "the key was exposed by Fireworks," and the attacker used it to copy data from a read-only storage bucket containing historical coding-agent session records belonging to Cursor users. Most of the records were from 2025 and involved sessions that were not in Privacy Mode and that Cursor was permitted to use for model training purposes.

Some of the stored session records contained credentials that had not been properly redacted. The attacker used those credentials to access what Cursor described as select SaaS tools for accessing non-production source code repositories, customer support data, emails, usage metrics, and other data.

On Oct. 29, the attacker subsequently used one of the compromised credentials to tamper with the cache that Cursor uses to deliver extensions through its VS Code marketplace. The attacker inserted unauthorized copies of Anthropic's Claude Code extension in the marketplace where they remained available to users for about 11 hours.

"Anthropic did not publish this copy, and this was not a vulnerability in Anthropic's product," Cursor stressed in its update. "The marketplace source, Open VSX, never had the malware. At most 5,000 users who requested specific cached versions of the extension on October 29 potentially received the malicious build."

Cursor estimated that API credentials belonging to fewer than 0.01% of its customers were exposed in the incident. Historical coding-agent session records, or traces, belonging to fewer than 0.05% of users were accessed, the company said.

However, the supply chain attack goes back even further than Fireworks, which itself was a victim of a third-party breach. In a Nov. 9 update, Fireworks said a cloud provider hosting the AI company's R&D systems "experienced a security incident on Oct. 23." The attackers used the access to the R&D systems to obtain sensitive data, including API keys and access tokens.

According to Fireworks, the available evidence linked the breach of its environment to a financially motivated cybercriminal group seeking to monetize unauthorized access to large language models. It's a practice known as LLMjacking, which Google and others have described as a rapidly emerging threat for organizations across multiple sectors in North America, Asia-Pacific, and other regions.

Following the incident, Cursor said it has implemented a broad security overhaul to reduce the risk of similar compromises. Immediate steps the company has taken include cutting off Firework's access to all its shared data sets, revoking and replacing potentially exposed credentials, and rotating internal credentials. The company has also tightened restrictions on how its systems and third-party service providers can communicate with one another and expanded automated scanning for secrets across its code repositories and internal data.

The Cursor incident is another indication of how the rapid adoption of AI-powered development tools and increasingly interconnected software ecosystems has exacerbated software supply chain risks. Recently, for instance, researchers uncovered more than 550 exposed secrets in Microsoft VS Code and Open VSX extension packages, including over 100 publishing tokens that could have allowed attackers to distribute malicious updates to legitimate extensions. Similarly, earlier this year researchers identified dozens of malicious GlassWorm VS Code extensions seeded in the Open VSX marketplace to steal developer credentials that attackers could use to spread poisoned software packages.

The Hidden Financial Stress of Thru-Hiking

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

For better or worse, I was born with the fiscal sensibilities of a Depression-era grandmother. This can be both an asset and a liability for thru-hiking. Back in the day, my frugal spending habits made it easier to save enough money to comfortably thru-hike the Appalachian Trail, but they were also an unexpected handicap once I was actually on trail.

People rarely talk about how psychologically challenging it can be to live with the stress of spending without earning for the six-ish months that a long thru-hike usually takes, and I definitely wasn’t prepared for it. Money stress comes in many forms, but most people experience it one way or another — and that struggle can follow you out onto the trail.

In today’s challenging economy, it’s more important than ever for aspiring thru-hikers to consider not just whether they can afford to hike, but how they’ll cope with the psychological stress of watching their savings dwindle with each passing mile. It’s easy to take fiscal responsibility too far and get caught up in an unhelpful scarcity mindset. Don’t let that be you.

Hiking is hard. Don’t make it harder by beating yourself up for literally no reason.

Thru-hikers often talk about how easy it is to spend way too much money on zero days. For me, spending money in town was one of the hardest things about the trail. I’m not saying I didn’t spend — the lure of a soft bed and a hiker-sized restaurant meal was usually enough to overpower my frugality — but I agonized over every purchase in every single town. There were times when I even felt so guilty about the spending that I considered ending my hike early.

It wasn’t just that I was spending money without replacing it; it was that I wasn’t doing anything about it. I wasn’t applying for jobs or upskilling or preparing myself to reenter the job market. I was just existing in the woods day in and day out, being thoroughly unproductive, with no particular plan for after the trail. This was apparently too much for my little capitalist brain to handle.

This mindset would have been more reasonable if lack of funds was a serious threat to my thru-hiking goals. But I had been fortunate enough to save enough money that I didn’t need to worry about running out mid-hike. I spent half my time worrying about running out of money for literally no reason.

Me mentally berating myself for overspending on name-brand peanut butter, probably.

What is the solution? Should I have saved even more money ahead of time? Of course that wouldn’t hurt, but such is the nature of anxiety that I doubt it would have stopped me from stressing. Should I have taken out a Coursera subscription and spent my evenings in camp learning how to code, or treated every impromptu hiker gathering like a networking event?

Obviously not. In reality, there’s usually no easy solution to the assorted neuroses that play out in our heads. In my case, it was just a matter of learning to live with the uncertainty of not having an income or a guaranteed path back into paid work after the hike. New hypothesis explains why the right side of the human brain controls the left side of the body

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Automotive  |  Read Source →

The brain's crossed wiring runs through every vertebrate group, and two neuroscientists trace it to the eyes of the first fishes.

A stroke on the right side of the brain weakens the left arm and leg. The brain is cross-wired that way, and for more than a century scientists have assumed there was an evolutionary reason for it.

Two neuroscientists have traced the crossing through every major group of living vertebrates. They now propose that evolution may never have selected it for the jobs it does.

On their reading, the crossed wiring may simply be inherited from the way the first fishes’ eyes were wired to their brains, some 500 million years ago.

Martin Wessendorf, an associate professor emeritus of neuroscience at the University of Minnesota, and Daniel Wang, a researcher in the University’s Department of Neuroscience, wrote the review.

It is a hypothesis built from more than a century of published anatomy rather than a new experiment. However, the authors wrote that it may be testable in fossils.

In an interview with Earth.com, Wang said the question reaches beyond his own field.

“This’ll be relevant to anyone who wants to know more about why brain injury on one side of the head often leads to loss of function on the opposite side of the body,” he said.

The crossing begins at the eye. The lens flips the image, so whatever sits to your right lands on the left side of each retina. The nerve fibers carrying the right half of the view then sort themselves at a junction behind the eyes and run to the left side of the brain.

One place that information goes is a structure in the midbrain called the optic tectum. It holds a map of the opposite half of visual space, and it fires fast turning and escape movements.

Every vertebrate examined appears to have one, lampreys and hagfish included, along with the crossing junction behind the eyes.

Living jawless fish carry both, so the authors conclude that the last common ancestor of all vertebrates probably did too. That puts the first crossed wiring in the Cambrian period, among jawless fishes that lived roughly 538 to 486 million years ago.

Anatomists call crossed wiring contralateral organization, and its age is what struck Wang.

“What surprised me the most was likely just how old the contralateral organization of the optic tectum is for vertebrates – spanning even to the Cambrian,” he told Earth.com.

Body senses crossed next. Two pathways carry touch, pain, temperature and body position from the spinal cord up to the brain, and both cross the midline in amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.

The one for light touch and body position is missing in fishes, so the authors trace it to the last common ancestor of the four-limbed animals.

Movement pathways cross too. One runs from the optic tectum down to the spinal cord and turns the body toward whatever just appeared at the edge of vision.

Lampreys and bony fish appear to have no such pathway. Sharks have one that mostly stays on its own side, and rays and lungfish have a crossed one.

Reptiles appear to have lost that pathway. A second crossed pathway, from a cluster of midbrain cells called the red nucleus, is thought to help control limbs.

True mammals appeared about 165 million years ago. By then, those crossed senses and movement pathways had already existed for almost 200 million years.

The pathway that lets you move one finger at a time is the special case. It runs from the cerebral cortex, the six-layered outer sheet found only in mammals, down to the spinal cord, and it crosses in the lower brainstem.

In most mammals, it ends where touch signals enter the cord and adjusts those signals. Few mammals besides primates, raccoons and kinkajous have a version that reaches the nerve cells that drive muscles.

So the circuit that gives humans fine control of their hands appears to be a sensory circuit put to a new use. Wang said that was the finding that surprised his co-author most.

“This demonstrates that circuitry that’s involved in one function can, with just a change or two, become a function that’s entirely different.”

The crossing isn’t universal in this pathway. In goats and elephants, it runs down both sides of the spinal cord, and in hedgehogs and moles it mostly stays on its own side. Even in humans, an estimated 10 to 25 percent of its fibers don’t cross.

Wang pointed to the goats and elephants, “so there’s a decent amount of diversity there,” he said. The authors read that variety as a sign that natural selection wasn’t strongly favoring the crossing in this pathway.

Isaac Newton proposed in 1704 that the junction behind the eyes sorts nerve fibers by visual field.

Ramón y Cajal, who later won a Nobel Prize for his work on the structure of the nervous system, went further in an 1898 paper. Once the visual map crossed, he proposed, it would pay for the maps of hearing, touch and movement to match it.

In computer models published in 2014, an animal controlled by the opposite side of its brain was likelier to survive a blow to one side of the head or body.

At least two other research groups have blamed a twist in the body plan, dating to the split between vertebrates and their invertebrate ancestors. That argument goes back to an 1822 dissection of a crayfish.

The authors see a problem with the twist. The visual system was probably crossed in the first vertebrates, but the pathways for touch, limb control and finger control didn’t exist yet.

So the authors doubt that a single ancient twist can explain them.

The authors’ own answer starts with the optic tectum. Its map of the opposite half of visual space sits on top. Beneath it, in register, sit maps of touch and hearing from the same half, and at the bottom sit the cells that fire movements.

Wessendorf and Wang propose that this stack biased the sensory and motor pathways that evolved later toward crossing.

When the cortex evolved, most of the pathways carrying signals into it were already crossed. A cortex wired to its own side would have needed extra crossings to collect vision, touch and hearing from the far side, or a rewiring of every older circuit.

The authors argue that either change would have carried a “wiring cost,” leaving mammals that were less energy-efficient, less intelligent, or both.

Not everything crosses. Smell and taste mostly stay on the same side, and the authors note that neither sense maps space the way vision, hearing and touch do.

The cerebellum, which coordinates movement, works the same side of the body it sits on.

“We propose that contralateral organization didn’t necessarily evolve as an adaptation, but because evolution is an engineer that builds with the foundation and materials it has at hand,” Wang and Wessendorf wrote.

The authors list what would test the idea. Their first prediction is that the last common ancestor of vertebrates had both the crossing junction and an optic tectum.

Cambrian fossils of jawless fish exist, and a fossilized brain cast from one could show whether both were there.

Wang said the finding that would complicate the idea is an early fossil vertebrate whose optic tectum handled vision on the same side rather than the opposite one.

The authors also predict that crossed pathways from the forebrain to the spinal cord will prove widespread in birds. Unfortunately, relatively few bird species have been examined.

Crocodilians appear not to have been checked, and the authors predict they have one too.

Hearing is the one sense the authors treat with caution. It appears to have evolved partly on its own in different lines.

When Amazon CEO Andy Jassy became a parent for the first time 26 years ago, he didn’t want to choose between being a good worker and being a good father.

Jassy was used to working grueling hours—often staying at the office late into the night—and he wasn’t sure if he could do his job effectively on a tighter schedule.

“I didn’t know how I would do my job without [working all the time],” he said. “But like everything else, if you think about it and you rework it and you find ways to be more efficient and you make trade-offs, you can do it.”

One step was to ensure that, whenever he was at the office, he was using his time efficiently.

“I did spend a lot of time in the hallway talking to people, and I wasn’t always prioritizing the things that mattered most,” Jassy said. “It forced me to get more efficient in what I did.”

He also established rules to help him prioritize family: being home for dinner every day, having weekly breakfasts with each of his children, and maintaining a standing Tuesday date night with his wife.

The challenge Jassy faced will likely be familiar to any parent struggling to balance career ambitions with responsibilities at home. And it is increasingly common among men, a growing number of whom say they are interested in being parents or caregivers. 

In one recent survey, over two-thirds of men said they consider caregiving to be as important as paid work and that both parents should contribute equally—yet only 40% of men who are caregivers said they shared the responsibility equally with their partners.

For fathers who want to keep their career ambitions and still be equal parents, Jassy said there may be no one-size-fits-all solution.

“There are times you have to be flexible with what’s happening in your life and at work,” he said. “But it’s doable if you want to do it.”

“musubi” has been generating a huge response. When did you start working on it?

Around two months ago, in mid-August. I finished the first verse and chorus at the end of August and posted it on TikTok maybe two days after that.

The title is symbolic. “musubi” is a love song about the bonds that tie people together. Where did the idea come from?

In May​,​ I released CLASSIC, my first album in four years, and most of it was new songs I co-wrote with overseas producers. I put so much into making it that I felt pretty empty in a way afterward. I was gradually taking things in and building my energy back up while touring, and as I considered what to make next, I vaguely thought it’d be nice to do something about bringing people together, because I feel that’s the biggest purpose of making music. I started making it with that as the big goal.

Bringing people together through music — is that one of the motivations you’ve always held onto?

Maybe not always, but with the way the times are going… right now I feel like everyone’s own worlds of the things they like are scattered all over the place. It’s becoming harder for everyone to get excited about a single piece of music or to share the same feelings. That’s why I wanted to make a song like “musubi.”

You announced your partnership with BMSG this February, and in April you also shared that you’d gotten married. Do you feel those changes in your working environment and life stage have influenced you?

I wasn’t thinking about those things in particular. More than anything, I really wanted to make something that carried on from the album. I put out CLASSIC after forming the partnership with BMSG, wanting to show the fullest extent of myself all over again, like, “This is Ayumu Imazu.” So doing the same thing didn’t feel right. My next focus naturally turned to what’s at the core of my music, the theme of connecting people.

CLASSIC was an album built around powerful, polished dance numbers that were quintessential Ayumu Imazu. At the same time, vocal-driven songs that showcase the beauty of the Japanese language, like “Anata To Itai” and “musubi,” are essential to any discussion of your discography. How do you think about balancing or blending the two?

When I was first starting out and trying to show people who Ayumu Imazu is as an artist, I was really conflicted between going with dance tracks and the fact that I also love ballads. But my strongest feeling was that I didn’t want to choose (just one), so after putting out all kinds of songs, I now feel the dance tracks are very Ayumu Imazu, and I also really feel that slow-tempo songs like “musubi” are me, too. The range of musical styles is broad, but I don’t really feel like I’m moving away from the foundation of my own feelings and personality.

Does your actual production process change depending on the style of the song?

It does. With dance tracks​,​ I usually start with the beat, and with ballad-type songs like this one, I usually work out the chords on acoustic guitar or piano, write the melody to a certain point, and then program it.

What did you focus on most in terms of your vocals?

The demo was already out there and everyone had gotten used to hearing it, so when I recorded the final take, I had to either make it sound similar or surpass it. I’d gotten used to hearing it myself, especially from just before the chorus through the first half of the chorus, so that was where I struggled most, and probably the part I was most particular about.

Where do you feel you managed to “surpass” it?

If anything, I think it has a different kind of appeal. The demo had its own appeal as a demo, that relaxed quality of casually singing at home. The released version has more feeling put into it, I guess… it really comes across as emotional. Personally, I really feel that difference.

When you shared the demo, you posted on TikTok that you were “still deciding on a title.”

I read through lots of comments and ideas. A lot of people said they “want to use it at their wedding,” so when I was finishing the full version, I thought, “Maybe I could make it a bit more like a wedding song.” It was a great reference and really helped me out.

Tell us about the changes in your environment, too. BMSG, the company you partnered with, brings together artists brimming with individuality under the slogan “​​Never extinguish talent.” What kind of inspiration and influence have you gotten in that environment?

At first​,​ I was completely wrapped up in my own stuff, but I recently took part in BMSG FES for the first time and got to see the other BMSG artists perform, and I was really inspired. Personally, it was my first time riding a trolley float, so that was a great experience too. [Laughs] Also, it reminded me that it truly is a music-first company, and since what everyone expresses is pretty different, I thought it was really great that every group and every artist puts their own colors front and center without being bound by any set format.

BMSG also has a vision called “TYOISM,” about reaching the world from Tokyo. With you joining — someone who moved to the U.S. at 14 and is still based there and in Japan — it seems likely to spark some new chemistry.

I think I have a pretty good feel for how things are done abroad, so when it comes to opening doors to global challenges, I definitely want to do that together with BMSG. Mr. Hidaka (SKY-HI) and I had talked about that kind of thing from the start.

Now that five years have passed since your 2021 major-label debut, how do you see the phase you’re in as an artist?

Right now might be a phase where I just make music really freely, without overthinking it. Last year or so, I was thinking a lot about things like, “I want to release this kind of song and become this kind of artist,” but lately I feel like I’ve thought it all through, so it’s more like I’m going with the flow.

What artists have you been listening to a lot lately?

I’ve had Remi Wolf’s album (Mud) on heavy rotation lately. I really love indie rock-type stuff, too.

You have a headlining show set at Nippon Budokan in January 2027. Tell us how you’re feeling going in.

The Budokan is truly a stage I’ve set as a goal, so I think it’s going to be a really flashy show. [Laughs] More people than ever are listening to my music, and I’ve got a concert booked at my biggest venue yet, so I’m super happy and feel like all I have to do is keep doing what I’ve been doing. I want to keep creating spaces where everyone can come together as one and enjoy music at bigger and bigger venues.

AI has made finding potential vulnerabilities fast and cheap. Fixing them still takes human judgment, and in open source, that work falls to maintainers. Many are already flooded with AI-generated security findings, including duplicates and reports that aren’t vulnerabilities at all. Another raw scan doesn’t help them. Anyone pointing a new model at a project should ask its maintainers first and come with fixes, not just findings.

Maintainers are under intense pressure from this flood of reports. As Christian Grobmeier, a maintainer of Log4j, has put it, the AI defending open source has to be better than the AI attacking it.

AI shouldn’t add to maintainers’ workload. So, with maintainers, GitHub and Microsoft ran a pilot together: could AI help maintainers ship fixes instead of handing them more findings?

GitHub and Microsoft are using Codename MDASH, Microsoft’s multi-model agentic scanning harness, to find and fix security vulnerabilities in widely used open source software, strengthening the foundation that much of the world’s code is built on.

Our pilot had two goals: send only findings that matter, and never send a maintainer an unverified finding. Here’s how it worked:

Reports and fixes stayed on GitHub, where maintainers already work.

The result: Codename MDASH, using Microsoft’s MAI Cyber model, flagged thousands of possible issues across the six projects. After AI triage and hand review by seven Microsoft FORGE Lab researchers, maintainers received 99 private reports. As of November 15, they had fixed 55, and 14 of those fixes were already in a release.

Six projects took part, all alumni of the GitHub Secure Open Source Fund. Their maintainers agreed to the scan, received every report privately, decided what to fix, and shared what worked:

These lessons come from what the six projects fixed and what their maintainers chose to share. Six projects is a small sample, so read them as early lessons.

Fixing is where maintainers spend their time. So every report arrived already checked, with a suggested fix to review, adapt, and merge. Maintainers started from a well-scoped patch, not a raw finding.

The first fixes shipped about a week after the initial scan. Caddy’s maintainers fixed all 22 of the reports they received in a week or less. Maintainers used the suggested fixes, some as written and some with changes.

AutoGPT’s maintainers had recently shipped a new check, and it was broken. The report landed three days later and pointed at the right line. They took the fix further than the suggested patch, wrote it within a day, and had it in production less than a week later.

Finding vulnerabilities is no longer our bottleneck. Fixing them is the hard part, and that depends on maintainers.

Maintainers got a short list, not a flood. Express maintainers said the reports cut through the noise. Caddy maintainers said they surfaced obscure bugs and filled gaps that earlier reports had missed. For FastMCP, the scan went beyond what AI scanners had given them before. ImageMagick, maintained by a two-person team, received 22 reports.

AI did the finding, researchers did the checking, and maintainers stayed in charge.

One ImageMagick report looked like a kind of issue the maintainers had seen before. Its summary showed it wasn’t and pointed to the exact lines of code. They were able to push a fix in 15 minutes.

Normally, a security advisory would take us around 30 minutes to 1 hour on average to read, understand, and make a fix. It is a huge difference.

Codename MDASH was able to find unknown, obscure security bugs in our project with succinct reports, and it filled in gaps from some prior reports that hadn’t been noticed.

The pilot worked because people did careful work at every step, and that work doesn’t scale yet. Seven researchers spent about two days per project checking findings by hand. Meanwhile, low-quality reports still reach maintainers every day.

For open source, fixes need to be delivered via private vulnerability reporting (PVR). Maintainers need to run CI/CD tests on private forks to verify those fixes. And to close the gap between defenders and attackers, maintainers need access to cyber-capable models to verify a proof of concept, before and after the fix.

America's hyperscale bit barns draw as much power as entire states

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Harvard study claims emissions attributable to datacenters are two to three times higher than in 2018

Just 403 hyperscale datacenters consumed an estimated 1.8 percent of US electricity in 2024-25, and their carbon dioxide emissions were roughly two to three times an earlier forecast for 2018.

Research led by the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health and the Harvard Data Science Initiative looked specifically at hyperscale facilities, the largest class of server farms that typically support cloud computing and AI processing.

The published study, "Assessing the carbon emissions and energy consumption of US hyperscale data centres," estimates their combined electricity use approached that of a single state such as Indiana, Michigan, or Tennessee under the highest-load scenario.

The researchers identified 403 hyperscale facilities by using a larger datacenter dataset, satellite imagery, and building footprints on OpenStreetMap.

Rather than reading electricity meters, the researchers worked out consumption from each site's electrical capacity, the hours in a year, and assumptions about how heavily the facility was running.

Across four operating scenarios, ranging from low-intensity use to AI-heavy workloads, electricity draw was believed to total 68-99 terawatt-hours (TWh). Each of those states consumed approximately 100 TWh during the study period.

The researchers also calculated carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions using eGRID2023 plant-level generation and emissions data from the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). Emissions ranged from 21 million to 31 million metric tons of CO2 across the scenarios, with a central estimate of 25.7 million. That compares with 10.5 million for 2018.

The study covered May 2024 to April 2025, so electricity demand and carbon emissions are likely to be even higher now, the researchers say.

"What we're seeing is already striking, but it's only a snapshot of a rapidly expanding industry," said Gianluca Guidi, lead author on the paper and postdoctoral fellow in the Department of Biostatistics at Harvard.

"The datacenter boom hasn't slowed down since our study period ended in April 2025. New facilities have been built, existing campuses have expanded, and demand for AI computing continues to grow. That means the electricity use and carbon footprint we're reporting could already be substantially larger now in 2026."

Harvard's report also considers the mix of fuels being used to generate the electricity that powers all those hyperscale data houses. The mix varies considerably between regional grids, so where a hyperscale facility is built makes a substantial difference to its attributed emissions.

Across the sites studied, roughly 54 percent of the electricity attributed to their consumption came from fossil fuels, mainly natural gas and coal. The electricity attributed to these facilities nevertheless had a lower average carbon intensity than the US grid overall.

The researchers acknowledge limitations, including that there wasn't enough available data to study all hyperscale sites in the US, and that the data did not allow them to differentiate between power used for general-purpose compute versus AI-specialized operations at individual campuses. ®

Declan Rice’s new contract with Arsenal recognises a transformation built over three seasons. When he arrived at the Emirates Stadium in July 2023, the midfielder was a high-impact signing: he cost £105m, a record for a British player at the time, and carried the expectation of lifting a team that had just lost the Premier League title to Manchester City.

Today, at 27, Rice is an English champion, one of the leaders of the dressing room and one of the team’s technical reference points. The new five-year deal shows Arsenal’s intention to keep him at the centre of the project through his physical and technical peak. Rice was not just an expensive signing who lived up to expectations. He became a more complete player, widened his influence across different phases of play and took on responsibilities beyond his role in midfield. The promise of 2023 has become one of the faces of a championship team and a leader for the cycle that starts now.

Rice’s arrival came at a decisive moment for Mikel Arteta’s project. In 2022-23, Arsenal spent much of the season at the top but lost steam in the closing stretch and finished five points behind Manchester City. The team had shown that they could compete for the title, but they needed more quality, depth and experience to sustain a challenge to the end.

Rice had characteristics that answered several of those needs. At 24, he was already West Ham United’s captain, had experience with England and had just won the Conference League. Arsenal beat the competition and completed the signing for £100m fixed plus £5m in bonuses.

The fee raised expectations. It was not enough for Rice to be a good holding midfielder: he had to justify a historic investment and help Arsenal take the step needed to compete with City. His first season showed the scale of the signing quickly.

The number of goal contributions was a considerable improvement on his West Ham record, but his impact was not limited to attacking output. Arsenal again finished as runners-up, but Rice had already become one of the team’s most important players. Early in his time at Arsenal, the Englishman often played as the deepest midfielder, responsible for protecting the defence and offering a reference point for build-up. It was a familiar role, close to the one he had at West Ham, but it did not use his full repertoire.

The change let Rice use one of his main qualities: carrying the ball at speed, breaking lines and arriving in the opposition area while still contributing to defensive recovery.

The Englishman gained more freedom to attack space, appear at the edge of the box and join attacking combinations. His physical power became a weapon for taking Arsenal into the opposition half, while his ability to get back helped keep balance when the team lost the ball. Instead of just protecting team-mates, Rice also began to drive the team.

Set pieces were another important tool. Rice began taking corners and free-kicks, using the accuracy of his delivery and his ability to put the ball in dangerous areas. Since his arrival, he has provided ten assists from set pieces in the Premier League. His two free-kick goals against Real Madrid in the 2024-25 Champions League quarter-finals were the most emblematic moment of that evolution.

Versatility does not mean Rice has no fixed position or needs to take part in every attacking action. The most recent development lies in choosing moments. With Martin Zubimendi, Myles Lewis-Skelly and Martin Odegaard offering different partnership options, Arteta has alternated the height at which the Englishman receives the ball and the freedom he has to advance.

The ability to perform different roles is one of the reasons Rice has become so important to Arteta. He is not just a player who adapts to different positions: he lets the manager change the structure of the team without necessarily changing personnel.

Rice’s growth was not only tactical. Over the seasons, he also became one of the squad’s main voices. His direct personality, willingness to demand more from team-mates and ability to take responsibility in difficult moments made him a reference point for the group.

One example came in April, when Odegaard was out injured and Rice was given the captain’s armband. He had worn it on other occasions, but the choice reinforced a perception that had been building: his influence does not depend on being the official captain.

More than his statistical contribution, his ability to maintain intensity in different roles was fundamental.

‘Both utopians and the doomers are going to be disappointed’: Fed advisor Marc Andreessen on AI going from boom to bust

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Marc Andreessen got the last word at the Federal Reserve’s big AI conference in San Francisco on Monday, and he used it to tell the central bank that nearly everyone in the room was going to be let down.

“My line has been both utopians and the doomers are going to be disappointed,” the venture capitalist said at the San Francisco Fed, “specifically disappointed by the scope and magnitude of the real-world impact, and specifically the economic impact.”

Andreessen co-leads the Productivity and Jobs Task Force, which Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh set up to tell the central bank what super intelligence—“SI,” in the White House’s preferred term—will do to growth, prices and jobs. On Monday, Andreessen previewed what he’ll contribute to the group’s report. Without using the name, he described one of the oldest problems in economics: William Baumol’s “cost disease,” the reason flat-screen TVs get cheaper every year while hospital bills, tuition and rent go the other way. His case is that AI won’t cure it, and could even make it worse.

The Fed hasn’t quite laid out that outcome.

The task force is advisory, and Andreessen has no vote on interest rates. He co-leads it with Stanford economist Charles Jones and Microsoft executive Asha Sharma, one of five outside panels the Fed named in June to rethink how the central bank works. Its charge, per the Fed, is to assess the economic impact of “new general-purpose technologies, including super intelligence, to inform the Federal Reserve’s policy judgments.” The panels report to the rate-setting committee with recommendations due by year-end, though Warsh said in October that “some, but not all” would turn their homework in on time. The Fed’s task force page lists no ethics or recusal terms for the panels. Andreessen’s firm, Andreessen Horowitz, raised more than $15 billion in new funds in January, as venture firms raced to keep up with the AI boom.

Andreessen is also Silicon Valley’s best-known accelerationist. His 2023 “Techno-Optimist Manifesto” declared, “We believe in accelerationism,” which he defined as “the conscious and deliberate propulsion of technological development.” It listed @BasedBeffJezos, the pseudonymous account behind the “effective accelerationism,” or e/acc, movement, first among its “patron saints,” and it named the enemy: “Our enemy is stagnation.” When OpenAI’s board fired Sam Altman a month later, Andreessen posted simply “E/acc!” This is the man who told the Fed that stagnation might win out over an AI-powered industrial revolution on Monday.

Warsh opened the day by laying out three “illustrative scenarios” Fed board staff have begun to evaluate. In one, AI lifts productivity, the gains are broadly shared and the economy stays close to full employment. In another, growth still runs above its long-term average, but automation pushes unemployment higher. In the third, AI “could disappoint after significant capital investment and stock market gains,” stocks fall, the economy slows and it eventually recovers “without the productivity gains.”

All three turn on whether AI arrives in force. Andreessen’s version is duller: most of the economy just doesn’t let it in.

He splits the economy in two. The “fast sectors,” like consumer electronics, media and commodity goods, take up new technology quickly, and “what those sectors all have in common is they have rapidly falling prices.” The “slow sectors”—health care, education, housing, and law and government—“are not legally allowed to have rapid change, like literally not.”

The machine “cannot get an MD, cannot prescribe medication,” he said. “The machine cannot get admitted to the bar.” The slow sectors, in his telling, pair restricted supply with subsidized demand: you can’t easily build new housing or open new hospitals, and when voters revolt over the cost, Washington steps in with student loans and housing subsidies. “So, of course, prices can only go up.”

Then he followed it to its end. As prices crash in the fast sectors, they shrink as a share of GDP, and the slow sectors take over. Health care, he said, “is already a fifth of US GDP and I don’t see why it doesn’t go to a fourth, a third and then half and then ultimately 80%.” (The government’s count is 18% in 2024, or $5.3 trillion, per CMS actuaries.)

“You might interpret this as good news because it means the AI revolution is not going to, you know, destroy all the jobs or kill everybody,” he said. “You could also view this as very negative, which is productivity growth just simply may not grow very much. Economic growth just simply may not grow very much. We may just continue to stagnate.”

Andreessen called himself “extremely optimistic,” saying that AI gives the country “a magic formula” and comparing it to being “handed this elixir to boost productivity growth.” But policy is a problem, he suggested. The decisions about what to do with AI, he said, “are going to matter actually way more than what the technology is capable of,” because without them “the technology will simply not get rolled out.”

That landed in a room where, three hours earlier, Warsh had brought up “secular stagnation,” the 1930s worry that economists returned to after the financial crisis, and asked whether superintelligence “dramatically” changes that picture.

Baumol, the late New York University and Princeton economist, worked this out in the 1960s, starting with the economics of the performing arts. A string quartet needs the same four musicians and the same playing time it did in Mozart’s day, but those musicians have to be paid wages set by an economy where factory workers keep getting more productive. So live music, and later education and medicine, keep getting more expensive relative to everything else, and keep taking a bigger share of spending.

In his 2012 book, The Cost Disease, Baumol projected that if health costs kept growing at their recent pace, they would rise from 15% of the average person’s income in 2005 to 62% by 2105. He was an optimist about it. “No matter how painful rising medical and educational bills may be, society can afford them,” he wrote, because productivity elsewhere makes everyone richer. The real threat, he added, was “foolish public policy.”

For Baumol, the disease was built into the work: you can’t speed up a string quartet. For Andreessen, it’s built into the law. That matters for the Fed, because an agency that sets interest rates can’t fix zoning boards or medical licensing.

The economist who has done the most to bring Baumol into the AI debate was in the audience. Jones, who is on leave at Anthropic, stayed quiet because the company is preparing to go public, as Andreessen remarked to the crowd. In a 2017 paper for the National Bureau of Economic Research, Jones, Philippe Aghion and Benjamin Jones argued that “growth may be constrained not by what we are good at but rather by what is essential and yet hard to improve,” crediting the idea to Baumol.

Earlier on the same panel, though, Wexler complicated Andreessen’s picture. AI tools that listen in on appointments and write up the notes are saving some physicians “two to four hours of time a day,” he said, and doctors have called it “life-changing.” In 35 years in the industry, he said, he’d never heard them say that about anything else. So the hours are being saved inside a slow sector. The open question is whether they ever show up in the price of a hospital visit.

Andreessen’s forecast cuts against the case Warsh made before he got the job. In a Wall Street Journal op-ed late last year, Warsh wrote that AI “will be a significant disinflationary force, increasing productivity and bolstering American competitiveness,” as Axios reported. In speeches and writing, he argued AI-driven productivity could justify lower interest rates, an echo of Alan Greenspan’s Fed holding off on hikes in the late 1990s.

As chair, he has hedged. “My judgment is we have seen an improvement in productivity; we can’t measure it precisely,” he told reporters on Oct. 28, adding that “most of that change in productivity precedes everything we’re talking about—super intelligence.” The Fed held rates at 3.75% to 4% that day, and a reporter told him most of his colleagues “seem to be on board with raising rates in December.” Warsh declined to prejudge the meeting.

On Monday, Warsh didn’t push back on Andreessen. He restated the argument in Fed terms, as product prices versus services prices, and moved on. He closed the day “cautiously optimistic,” calling it “a hinge point for the US economy,” and said, “I’d rather have our problems than anyone else’s in the world.”

Andreessen has been making this case for years. In 2017 he warned that the slow sectors were “basically just going to eat the economy,” and in March 2023 he wrote that “AI is simply already illegal across most of the economy.” Back then the argument was a rebuttal to the job-loss doomers. On Monday, in front of the Fed chair, it came with the other half of the bargain: if the jobs are safe, the productivity boom may be stuck too.

For companies, the slow sectors are a line item. Workers now pay an average of $7,471 a year toward employer family health coverage, up 9% from 2025, according to KFF’s annual survey. That’s the part of the economy that, by Andreessen’s account, AI isn’t legally allowed to touch.

For the Fed, the question is what kind of disinflation AI brings, if any. Earlier in the day, Warsh asked a panel of economists whether AI might do to services what the China shock did to goods—“productizing services”—and push prices down. Stanford’s Nick Bloom said falling token costs made him “kind of bullish.” Stanford President Jonathan Levin gave Baumol’s answer without the name: health care and education are “the parts of the economy where the disinflationary pressures from automation have not hit,” where “the costs have continued to rise with wages.” Whether that changes depends on what people want, he said, “and there’s also regulation.”

If Andreessen is right, AI’s price cuts land mostly where prices were already falling, and services, where inflation has been hardest to bring down, stay out of reach.

At the other is a bet between two people Collison described as having “considerable influence” over AI, on whether global energy use doubles in a single month sometime in the next decade. Andreessen is betting on a third outcome: a decade where nothing that dramatic happens, and the prices Americans complain about most keep climbing.

Warsh has asked his task forces for recommendations by year-end, and the Fed’s next rate decision comes Dec. 9. Andreessen put the choice to the central bank as “a political economy” question: “what kind of pace of change do we want, how much productivity growth do we actually want?”
