Distributed LLM Training Across Metros: Streaming DiLoCo in MaxText

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Traditionally, training state-of-the-art Large Language Models (LLMs) relies on a monolithic cluster of accelerators, where all devices are collocated and tightly coupled via high-bandwidth to constantly synchronize gradients. However, physical and cost constraints make scaling a single slice increasingly difficult.

To scale further, we can connect multiple compute slices across geographically distributed datacenters. However, when training across multiple datacenters over wide-area networks (WAN), standard data-parallel training suffers from severe network stalls because slow WAN transfers cannot be fully overlapped with computation.

To address this, the Google DeepMind and Google Research teams developed DiLoCo (Distributed Low-Communication), a distributed optimization algorithm designed to make training resilient to these low-bandwidth, high-latency connections. In this blog post, we discuss the core concepts of DiLoCo, how it is implemented in MaxText (Google's open-source JAX-based LLM training framework), and the performance benefits it brings to large-scale LLM training on Google Cloud.

DiLoCo was introduced by Google DeepMind in the paper DiLoCo: Distributed Low-Communication Training of Language Models. Instead of synchronizing gradients across all devices at every single training step (as in standard Data Parallelism), DiLoCo partitions training across M independent compute groups, referred to as learners, and allows each learner to perform many local optimization steps independently before communicating across groups.

Despite communicating significantly less frequently (by a factor of H, e.g., 1-2 orders of magnitude less) than standard synchronous training, DiLoCo has been shown to achieve comparable convergence and model quality.

The Vanilla DiLoCo described above reduces total communication volume by a factor of H, however, all learners still transmit their weight deltas for the entire model all at once at every H-th step. On a thin network pipe, that full-model transfer creates a large periodic stall every H steps. This issue is alleviated by Streaming DiLoCo, which is described in the next section.

In multi-slice training, communication within a single slice is handled by high-speed Inter-Chip Interconnects (ICI). When scaling to multiple slices, synchronization across slices must traverse the Data Center Network (DCN) or, if the slices are geographically distributed, wide-area networks (WAN).

Within a single datacenter, DCN bandwidth is high enough that JAX and XLA can often hide standard Data Parallel all-reduce collectives by overlapping gradient communication with backward-pass computation. Across datacenters or congested links, however, bandwidth drops and latency rises sharply. Standard DP stalls at every step waiting for global gradient synchronization, collapsing Model FLOPS Utilization (MFU).

Streaming DiLoCo eliminates this synchronization stall by partitioning the model's parameters into P disjoint fragments. Over the course of H steps, every single fragment in the model is synchronized exactly once, achieving the exact same outer-loop cadence as vanilla DiLoCo without spiky bandwidth utilization.

Using a roofline model (similar to the JAX Scaling Book), we can calculate the network bandwidth required to prevent communication stalls for a model with ∣Θ∣ parameters (at b bytes per parameter, e.g., b=2 for bfloat16) and per-step compute time tstep=tfwd+tbwd. For example, the standard Data Parallel algorithm transfers 2∣Θ∣b bytes per sync via an all-reduce collective, which is a distributed reduce-scatter where each node reduces a different part, followed by an all-gather so all participants receive all parts. To achieve zero communication stalls, we overlap the reduce-scatter of the gradients with the backward pass, which produces them and takes tbwd time. Therefore, the peak required bandwidth is Bdp ≈ 2∣Θ∣b / tbwd.

Season Passes for a Venue With WooCommerce: Guest Passes and Cooldowns

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 20 • Originally published at pluginlab.dev

A season pass is not just a ticket with a longer expiry date. You need to decide who can use it, whether the holder can bring a guest, and what happens when someone presents the same code again at the entrance.

I built Tickets & Passes for WooCommerce with a separate Pass product type for admission across a period. It supports guest passes, cardholder photos and a cooldown between scans. Here is how I would plan a WooCommerce season pass for a venue without confusing admission with a broader membership system.

The plugin adds Ticket, Timeslot Ticket and Pass to WooCommerce's Product data dropdown. A Ticket is plain admission with a validity window and a set number of uses. A Timeslot Ticket is for a particular date and time, with capacity per slot. A Pass is valid across a period, which suits season passes and annual admission cards.

I would choose the product type from the admission rule, not the marketing name. If each visit must reserve a specific session, look at Timeslot Tickets instead. Do not assume that calling a product a membership makes it a reservation system.

The WordPress.org readme documents the setup. WooCommerce must already be active, and the phone scanner needs HTTPS for camera access.

Before selling publicly, I would make a test purchase and follow it through from checkout to check-in. Check the holder details, the validity you intended, and what staff actually see on their phone.

A pass can carry a cardholder photo collected at purchase. At check-in, staff see that photo beside the scan result and can compare it with the person at the door. The QR code identifies the pass; the photo gives staff another piece of information.

This is not a promise of automatic identity verification. I would write a simple entrance procedure explaining when staff should compare the photo and what to do if the person does not match. Test that procedure with the people who will use it, not just the administrator who configured the product.

A Pass can include guest passes that the holder gives away from their account page. That provides a guest route without treating the main holder's QR code as something everyone should share.

A cooldown does something different: it prevents the same pass code being scanned again immediately. The product also determines the maximum number of uses. I would decide those rules before advertising the pass, then test repeat entry against the intended policy.

Do not describe a cooldown as a complete sharing-prevention system. It is a rule about repeat scans. The cardholder photo, the guest allowance and the staff procedure address different parts of the admission process.

The accounting: take each top-level object's offset from qpdf --show-xref, size it as the distance to the next offset, and classify it from its dictionary with PyMuPDF 1.26.5. NDI (Network Device Interface) is a technology for sending and receiving live video and audio over IP networks. Your Prometheus server has been quiet for months, scraping a few hundred targets and evaluating alert rules that other teams depend on. Then Prometheus 3.16 lands, released on 2026-11-04 according to the official release page. Prometheus ships a new minor roughly every six weeks, so the question is not whether to upgrade but how to do it without surprises in alerting or storage.

This is why it matters now. Grafana 13.3 and a stream of exporter updates are landing in the same weeks, and a monitoring server that falls many minors behind makes every later upgrade larger. The release notes are the authority on what is in 3.16.0. This guide gives you a repeatable procedure to find the changes that touch your setup and prove the new binary behaves before it owns production.

Short answer: read the [CHANGE] entries for 3.16.0, run the new promtool against your real config and rules, then canary 3.16.0 beside the old server with its own data directory and no alerting or remote write. Promote it only if the metrics in the verify section stay clean. This applies if you already run Prometheus 3.x. On 2.x, do the 3.0 migration first.

When not to bother yet: if you run a long-term-support line for stability, you may deliberately skip non-LTS minors. Check the project's release schedule on prometheus.io to see which line is marked LTS. The cost of staying put is that you batch more changes into each later upgrade.

Prometheus keeps a CHANGELOG.md where every bullet is tagged [CHANGE], [FEATURE], [ENHANCEMENT] or [BUGFIX]. The [CHANGE] lines are the ones that can alter behavior you rely on. This pulls just those for one release:

Compare the two outputs line by line. Put anything that mentions a flag you pass, a config key you set, or a metric name your dashboards query on a checklist. I am deliberately not listing the 3.16 entries here. Read them from the source, because a summary written from memory is how people miss the one line that matters.

Watch out for: feature flags. A flag you enabled to get an experimental behavior may be promoted to default, renamed, or become a no-op. The changelog entry is where that is announced.

The cheapest safety net is running the target version's own promtool against your real files. It catches removed config keys and rule syntax problems before any process restarts. Adjust the paths to your layout:

A clean run reports SUCCESS for the config and for each rule file, with a count of rules found. Watch out for: a passing check config does not prove your scrapes still work. Scrape behavior, such as how content types are handled, only shows at runtime, which is what the canary is for.

Start 3.16.0 on a different port with a fresh data directory. Never point two servers at the same TSDB, since the data directory is locked by one process. Strip anything with external side effects from the canary's config copy: the alerting: block and any remote_write: entries. Otherwise you get duplicate pages and duplicate samples in long-term storage.

Mount the volume at the image's own /prometheus path. The image runs as an unprivileged user, and Docker copies that directory's ownership into a new named volume. A mount point at a path that does not exist in the image is created owned by root, and the TSDB fails to open with a permission error. If port 9091 is already taken on the host, for example by a Pushgateway, pick another port.

Let the canary run through at least one full cycle of your slowest scrape interval, plus a rule group evaluation or two. A day is better if you have daily batch jobs that expose metrics.

The cost is real but small: roughly the memory and CPU of a second server for the canary period. On a very large instance, that may mean scraping only a subset of jobs with a trimmed config.

Confirm that the config loaded with the prometheus_config_last_reload_successful metric in the verify section. Expect a restart to replay the write-ahead log, which takes longer as the head block grows.

Alerts with for: clauses restore their pending state from the ALERTS_FOR_STATE series stored in the TSDB. That restore is governed by --rules.alert.for-outage-tolerance and --rules.alert.for-grace-period. After a short restart, most pending alerts resume rather than reset, but a longer outage can still delay firing. Schedule the restart outside deploy windows and let the replay finish before judging health.

Rollback: keep the previous binary or image tag and the snapshot. Whether a given minor can safely read data written by a newer one is stated in the release notes, so check before relying on a downgrade. Restoring the snapshot is the fallback that does not depend on that.

What follows is a working translation of Google’s rules for multi-tenant portfolios, plus the monitoring boundary that keeps the retainer honest.

Google-Agent is the name Google uses for agents hosted on Google infrastructure that navigate the web and perform actions when a user asks. In Google’s user-triggered fetchers table, the User-Agent strings include compatible; Google-Agent with a documentation URL, for both mobile and desktop shapes. Traffic for this fetcher uses IP ranges published in user-triggered-agents.json.

That is a different job from Googlebot, which crawls for Search. It is also a different product conversation from Google Cloud or Gemini “agent builder” marketing pages that dominate results for the bare query google-agent. When a client asks you to “allow the Google agent,” it helps to confirm they mean this crawling User-Agent and IP list, not a Vertex or Workspace product toggle.

On one of those, in the middle of the night, I did something clever.  I picked up my poncho, which was just lying on the ground soaking wet, and I draped it over a pine tree, so it looked like a scarecrow.  Or maybe a scare-bear?

Above all, I worried about what I would be facing in the morning.

Indeed, the morning came.  I got up reasonably early, at 6:30, and had breakfast in my tent.  In my warm, now semi-dry tent.

The good thing was that the rain had stopped.  The poncho I stood up as a decoy in the night, was now wind dried.  So, I used it for staging.  I put it on the wet ground and pulled all of my stuff out of the tent and placed it on the dry poncho.  If things were dry, they stayed dry; if wet, at least they didn’t get any wetter.

Things looked a LOT better at my camp site in the morning!

This mattered, because I had a lot of wet stuff.  I had a wet hoodie.  I had wet shorts, wet socks, and wet shoes.

After evacuating the tent, I took it down.  Thanks to the wind, the fly – that had been soaking wet — was basically dry.  But the ground cloth and the bottom of the tent were very wet.

What to do?  I could either dry them and be done with it, or pack them wet, and dry them later.  While thinking about this, I put them on logs, and laid them out to dry, so that my indecision became a decision.  When the sun peeked out a little, I put them on rocks.  Yes, it ate up a lot of time, but when they were finally dry, I could stop worrying about that issue and just hike.

At 9:00 a.m. I set off.  I was wearing wet-cold shoes, wet-cold shorts, wet-cold gloves, a wet-cold hoodie, and a wet-cold hat.  Only one thing was not wet and cold:  I decided to go ahead and use my dry socks.  I hoped that my shoes were just moist and not soaking wet, and my dry socks would be a barrier to that moisture.  That plan worked out pretty well.

Because it was cold in the aftermath of the storm, I started with a lot of energy, to get my blood flowing.  I needed it too, for the trail sang one refrain:  up, up, up, and up.

Looking back at the hill I climbed — but at least I was warm, and getting dry!

By the time I reached the ridge, and looked down on Garnet Lake, I was mostly dry and all warmed up.  Then I got lost.

I could see Garnet Lake, I just couldn’t see how to GET there!

I knew I had to go east, and I spotted a little trail to the east, so I started down that trail until it kind of disappeared.  Then I spent about ten minutes wandering around up there, trying to find my way, unable to believe the east-going trail was a false trail.  Finally, I retraced my steps far enough to notice a separate west-going trail.  That was counterintuitive, but it was the correct trail, because it eventually looped around, lower down, and went east, as I knew it should.

I name this “One Pine Tree Island” in Garnet Lake

Walking along the shore of Garnet Lake I envisioned what might have happened if I had made it to this destination in the storm the day before.  I could imagine myself, struggling with my tent in the rain, and having a huge gust of wind lift the tent like a beach ball and deposit it in the middle of the lake!  It would have been bad!  Yes, hilarious, but bad.

To reinforce the power of the storm the day before, this was Ninja Queen’s  description of her experience, as she sent it to me via text, with almost no editing:

“…you can imagine how horribly wrong the day of the storm went for me.  I should have hunkered down early like most other hikers had, but I foolishly trusted my InReach weather forecast a little too much, went over Island Pass (a tiny pass but a pass nonetheless) when the storm hit full force two hours early.  It was bad.  Really, really bad.  The trail flooded very quickly, got washed out and started diverging in multiple directions from the torrential downpour.  They were ankle-deep and I kept losing the actual trail; it had become mini rivers at that point.  Needless to say, I got wetted out through and through, shivering intensely, my hands no longer functioning, unable to take another step.  I ended up setting up camp on some random flat-ish spot over some vegetation that also started pooling water.  I was so afraid my tent was going to get flooded.  Almost everything inside the tent was wet and muddy as well, though I was able to change into dry sleep clothes and sleeping bag, thanks to my nylofume pack liners.  It took me a couple of hours, but I eventually warmed up in the bag, cooked and ate food inside (bears were the last of my worries at this point) and drank as little water as possible so I wouldn’t have to go out until the next morning.  Putting back on cold, dripping wet clothes the next morning felt torture-adjacent; something I hope I’ll never have to go through again.”

“It gave me a small chuckle seeing other hikers also emerge from non-established tent sites the next morning.  I quickly learned what I went through was not a typical rainstorm experience.  Almost everyone I talked to said it had been one of the worst storms they’ve seen on trail.  One guy, a firefighter, told me this was far worse than the hurricane weather he had caught on the AT a few years back.  Now I felt like a survivor, instead of feeling like a fair weather backpacker who crumbled at the slightest hint of bad weather.”

I filled up my water at the bridge where a stream exits the lake.  There were a lot of folks around there, laying out their tents to dry from the storm.  It was surprising, and a little disturbing, to see how much foam, and even spume-like foam, was on Garnet Lake, which looks otherwise pristine.  I didn’t ask anybody about it at the time, who would I ask?

But later I Googled AI and discovered:  “…foam usually forms naturally when decaying plants, algae, and organic debris release compounds that lower water surface tension, and wind or wave action whips air into bubbles.”  In other words, the wind from the storm had produced the foam, not some giant industrial complex just out of view!

I continued up, from Garnet to beautiful Ruby Lake, then to Emerald Lake, and finally the Thousand Islands Lake.  This was an exceptionally scenic stretch.

I think this was Ruby Lake… if the water were sparkling red, it would be easier to tell!

Of course this was Thousand Islands Lake — with a pretty terrific backdrop!

There was very little elevation gain from there to Island Pass.  Once the trail reached the ridge on the opposite side of the lake it seemed to stay at a mostly constant elevation for a very long time.  The longer it went on, the more I wondered, “Pass?  Is there really a pass up here somewhere?  Where?”

No pass, but a pretty cool-looking mushroom!

How can you tell when you have reached a pass?  When you can see the other side of the mountain.  It felt like that was never going to happen.

Finally, it was getting to be lunchtime.  I decided to sit next to a charming little pond up there, and to eat my lunch.

Basically, I ate half of the food that I had brought as lunch, saving the other half for “second lunch.”  But afterwards, I just didn’t feel like I had much energy.  Even finally reaching the edge did not inspire me.  I saw the edge of the pass and started heading down, but my legs felt heavy.  My legs felt like limp spaghetti.

When your legs don’t want to go, you need your brain to execute an override.  So, I forced myself keep going.  Finally, I stopped again and made myself a second lunch in that sort of halfway space between Island Pass and Donohue Pass.  About five miles separated them and Donohue represented the entrance to Yosemite National Park.

A dead tree by a stream; dead is how I felt before second lunch!

After lunch I had more energy, and I continued.

Throughout the JMT, whenever I approached a location where I knew there was a pass in the ridgeline, I always looked up to see if I could figure out where it might be.  “Is that it?  No, is that it?  Wait, is that it?”  I suspect most hikers do this.

With Donohue Pass it was hidden around the corner of an extending hump of rock, so of course I guessed wrong.  Just short of the summit, I saw a full backpack about 25 yards from the trail.  That seemed strange and I looked around.  Was someone off going to the bathroom?  There was nothing but silence.  I continued on, but with a nagging worry for whoever was not connected with their pack.

When I arrived on top, at 11,066 feet elevation, I was all alone.  In honor of my son Gabriel, who coined the pun, I bellowed, “YO!  SEMITE!!!”  I got no answer, not even an echo.  Even so, I was in Yosemite.  It felt strange to be just a few days from the end — barring injury, of course.

As I headed down from Donohue, I thought, “On the one hand, I don’t want to set up in the dark.  But there are not too many campsites up here and, of course, I am a couple of miles behind schedule because of the storm.”  I resolved to take a chance and push it a little bit.

As a result, the riverside campsites just below Donohue Pass did not tempt me; they were filling up in any case.

The first camp north of Donohue Pass was by this ugly little lake…

Instead, I descended rapidly and with determination.  As I did, I encountered a group heading southbound, up to the pass behind me.  One young man was not carrying a pack.  Apparently, he had come down to assist his family in making the ascent.  He told me, “I left my pack up near the Pass so that I would not be encumbered in helping my family.”  I breathed deeply; mystery solved.

The first stretch went through rocky scree.  I asked the mountain gods, “Why is there so much rocky scree?”  They rewarded me with forest.

See the meadow, far below there? My campsite was somewhere down there…

The descent continued sharply through the woods, and I passed several gorgeous camping sites near the steeply dropping steam.  Each time I told myself, “No!  Not yet!” and then each time I regretted it, and wondered if I would ever find another spot, after that perfect one I had just rejected.

At last, the steep downhill tapered off, and I reached the valley at the bottom.  The stream, now wider and larger, and the trail continued to parallel each other.  The going became easier for both the water and my legs.

Following the river there were a few more possible places.  As the sun slipped behind the mountains, but still with plenty of light, I saw a knoll overlooking a wide meadow.  On that knoll was a lovely camping spot.  I was home for the night.

The sun remains on the hillside, even as it gets dark in the valley

My dinner was rice and beans and chicken, Santa Fe style.  I let it soak as I pitched my tent and arranged my site.  I let it soak some more as I explored higher up, in the giant granite boulders above the site, to find a cat hole location.  It soaked still longer as I dug the hole in preparation for the next morning.  But then, with no chores left to do, I got impatient.  I ate.

The dish was very well-seasoned, very tasty.  This was certainly a meal I would recommend having again, on another hike, in another place.  But rice takes a long time to absorb water when cold-soaking, and I didn’t wait long enough.   The flavors were great, but the rice was… crunchy.   I didn’t care much.  It had been a strenuous day, coming the day after the intense storm.  I was tired and hungry and starved for calories.  I ate it all with gusto, crunch and all!

I was feeling good.  It was a long day, and I had crossed two passes.  I was finally in Yosemite.  I haven’t mentioned the aftermath of my fall too much in recent posts, but my ribs were finally hurting less.  My wrist was still a problem, to some degree, but it was functional.

The next day, I would be passing through Tuolumne Meadow.  There I would either get a permit for Half Dome, or I would not.  If I did, I probably had three days of hiking left.  If I did not, then it would likely be two days.  I had well under forty miles to go.

As the twilight deepened, I was loving my campsite facing the open meadow.  I was proud of the mileage I had achieved, after the shortened day in the storm.  My eyes closed and I welcomed blissful sleep.

Canva is one of those companies, and it's introduced a bunch of generative AI tools to its design platform. So the email I received yesterday made me do a double take. On the day that Anthropic completed the world's biggest IPO in history, it introduced an 'AI off switch'.

Canva has added two AI controls for both Free and Pro users. The generative AI switch can be used to hide all of its generative AI tools, including Magic Write, Magic Design, Magic Expand, Dream Lab and Canva Code. It can be switched on or off at any time.

Canva says AI-generated content remains a "small portion" of its library and that such content is identified in its metadata. Turning on the new function isn't guaranteed to remove it completely, but it should put human-made templates and design elements first in search results and recommendations.

The addition of a couple of toggles to the UI wouldn't normally be breaking news, so the fact that Canva feels this deserved its own announcement is telling. After heavily promoting AI tools, it's now presenting the ability to turn them off as a groundbreaking advance – and it is. This might be the first time that a major AI-forward creative software developer has recognised that a sizable portion of its user base just isn't into it.

An 'AI off button' is something that some creatives have been calling for. Part of the difficulty in implementing one is that it needs a definition of AI. Programs like Adobe's Photoshop have used machine learning for many years; it just wasn't branded as 'AI'. Many artists welcome tools that speed up their work, but not ones that do the creation part for them, especially if they're powered by models that were trained on unlicensed work.

Isaac Asimov’s Book Series Was Unfilmable — Now It's Apple TV's Greatest Space Opera

📅 Nov 20, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sci-fi fans would collectively agree that some stories of the genre are better off staying on the page. For long, it was also believed that Isaac Asimov's books could not be translated to the screen, and for good reason. Especially when it comes to the Foundation book series, it was hard not to believe it was largely "unfilmable."

Some of the greatest sci-fi books are written in a way that it is easy to directly adapt them for the screen. Isaac Asimov's Foundation books, in contrast, defy almost every rule of visual drama. It unfolds less as a fictional drama and more as a deep dive into everything from sociological mechanics to human psychology. While at it, it also spans across centuries, shifting its focus from one character to another.

The books' massive time jumps, anti-cinematic action, and prioritization of ideas over character beats made it impossible to adapt for the screen. This explains why many planned big-budget adaptations of the books also struggled to see the light of day in the past. Fortunately, however, Apple TV's Foundation took a massive risk and achieved the impossible by successfully adapting Isaac Asimov's books.

Impossible feats demand extreme risks. Owing to this, Apple TV's take on the Foundation books significantly changed the source material's approach. The biggest adaptation challenge came from the books' revolving characters. Every few chapters, the books experience time jumps, where old characters die of age and new ones emerge.

On the screen, viewers need rootable visual anchors who appear for longer periods.

Apple TV's Foundation carefully navigated this issue by introducing cloning, cyro-sleep, and digital consciousness. These became narrative devices that allowed the series to logically sustain the same characters and cast members across centuries and seasons. Even an immortal humanoid, named Lady Demerzel, emerges in Foundation from Asimov's wider universe.

Unlike the books, Apple TV's Foundation also visualizes key conflicts and collapses from the original story instead of solely focusing on their political implications. Even "psychohistory" from Isaac Asimov's Foundation gets a visual and more tangible representation in the series.

Armed with the Socratic method, Apple TV's Foundation understands that "The beginning of wisdom is the definition of terms." Instead of treating Asimov's story as a plot to be faithfully transcribed, the show first understands the true essence of its terms. It then turns into a more sprawling space-opera by wisely redefining its ideas.

Many readers have complained that Apple TV's Foundation is too distinct compared to its source material. They argue that it is unfair to even call it a worthy adaptation. It is hard not to agree with them. An adaptation cannot be considered great if it is a far cry from its source material.

Similar to Stanley Kubrick’s The Shining and Denis Villeneuve's Arrival, Foundation fails as a book-to-screen adaptation on many levels.

However, if purely judged as a modern television space opera, Foundation is easily one of the best sci-fi shows of our time. While Asimov's books seemingly argue that historical forces matter far more than individual stories of heroism, Apple TV's Foundation carefully bends the original narrative to focus on almost mythic heroes and solo characters.

It is also arguable that a direct page-to-page TV adaptation of Isaac Asimov's Foundation books would likely have failed. Whether Apple TV's adaptation approach is ideal is still debatable, but it at least gives us an epic sci-fi space opera.

Isaac Asimov's novel series is brought to life in Foundation, a television adaptation of the epic sci-fi saga created for Apple TV+. The show follows a group of exiled humans that find themselves as the last chance to save the Galactic Empire, primarily from itself.
