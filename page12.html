The Biggest Stock Winners and Losers From the Midterm Elections

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The market historically performs well after midterm elections, but some stocks appear to be clear winners and losers following the results.

The midterm elections shifted the political landscape in the U.S., and, regardless of anyone's politics, that will impact some industries and stocks in a meaningful way going forward. Stocks have historically performed well following the midterm elections, regardless of which party wins. The S&P 500 (^GSPC -4.36%) has climbed from November to November 95% of the time since 1938, so now should still be a good time to buy stocks in general.

However, let's look at some stocks set to benefit from the election results and others that could be hurt.

Tariffs have been a bane to the retail sector, and the election results give retailers hope that there could be a future shift in policy. This isn't likely to happen right away and may not take hold until after the 2028 elections. However, the market tends to be forward-looking and already anticipating better days for retailers. Here are three retail-oriented stocks that could benefit.

Amazon (AMZN -2.19%) is the largest e-commerce company in both the U.S. and the world. The company's logistics network gives it a significant advantage, and it has been seeing strong operating leverage from its investments in AI and robotics. Meanwhile, any removal of tariffs should be a big boost to its retail operations, as it would increase consumer demand and help expand margins on the goods it sells.

Interestingly, Amazon's AWS unit is also viewed as a winner. While the elections were seen as a backlash against the AI data center build-out, this is expected to hurt neoclouds and benefit the big cloud providers. The reason is that the big cloud computing companies are generally better positioned to navigate regulatory hurdles, given their established relationships with utilities, regulators, and local governments, as well as their better financial resources and ability to secure long-term power agreements.

Shopify (SHOP -6.06%) has become the e-commerce software company retailers are increasingly turning to in an effort to take on Amazon. The stronger its retail customers are and the better they are performing, the more it benefits Shopify, which gets a revenue cut through its Shopify Payments solution.

The company also appears to be one of the biggest potential AI winners. This is a top stock to own if tariff pressures ease and consumer spending strengthens.

e.l.f. Beauty (ELF -3.33%) was one of the hardest-hit brands from tariffs, given most of its products are manufactured in China. Meanwhile, a $1 price increase for its namesake brand impacted its sales growth. As such, a rollback in tariffs could be a huge benefit to the company.

Meanwhile, the company's acquisition of Hailey Bieber's Rhode brand is proving to be a huge winner. The company is expanding Rhode's product assortment and distribution, which is leading to strong growth. A tariff-reduction tailwind would only add to its growth story.

Space Exploration Technologies (SPCX -9.05%) has become one of the largest neoclouds, with aggressive goals to expand its computing power capacity. Tougher permitting could put a real dent in its ability to grow as quickly as it would like, with permitting delays leading to lost revenue. Texas governor-elect Gina Hinojosa, for instance, specifically ran on limiting data center growth.

Meanwhile, it could also hamper SpaceX's ability to launch another U.S. mobile provider and complicate a potential merger with Tesla.

On the upside, if SpaceX can overcome technical hurdles and costs come down, its orbital data center ambitions could become a real advantage in a tougher terrestrial data center permitting environment. Unfortunately, the same can't be said for other neoclouds like CoreWeave (CRWV -9.83%), Iren (IREN -16.95%), and Nebius (NBIS -14.95%).

Palantir Technologies (PLTR -9.73%) has become one of the most important AI software-as-a-service (SaaS) companies on the planet, with its solution akin to an AI operating system that makes AI more useful in the real world. Its growth has been outstanding, driven by U.S. commercial customers, but the U.S. government remains its largest client.

The company's U.S. government growth has been strong, but a potential shift in administration could slow it. Given the stock's hefty valuation, any slowdown in growth could hurt the stock. However, I'd likely view any significant dip as a buying opportunity.

Diamonds are forever - phoenix club seek FA Cup upset

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The notion of your football club ceasing to exist is unthinkable for many fans, but for supporters of Rushden & Diamonds it became a sad reality in 2011.

The Northamptonshire-based side, backed by the late Max Griggs, rocketed through non-league in the 1990s and in 2003 reached the third tier of English football before financial issues after he stepped away ultimately saw them fold.

A community lost its focal point, and within years their Nene Park stadium had been razed to the ground.

"It was heartbreaking - we knew we'd been robbed." reflected Rob Usher, the chairman of phoenix side AFC Rushden & Diamonds, who will make their FA Cup first-round debut this weekend.

"I think it was that anger that probably fuelled the fans saying 'no, we don't accept it. We're not gone, we're not done and we want our club back."

Some 2,000 travelling fans will make their way to see the now eighth-tier Diamonds take on League One side Burton Albion on Saturday - a bittersweet reminder of days the supporters of the original club could have once taken for granted.

Usher, like many of those involved with the Northern Premier League Division One Midlands side, has followed both incarnations of the club - firstly as a fan, but also as a groundsman and turnstile operator before becoming chairman in 2022.

"My first game was an FA Cup tie against Shrewsbury Town in 1998. It was the year we ended up playing Leeds United in the third round." he said.

"Rushden & Diamonds gave us some great times. Winning leagues, the Duane Darby's, the Onandi Lowe's of our past. But I tell everyone I've never traded for what we have with AFC Rushden & Diamonds.

"This is a community, this is an army of people who turn up and give their time and their money, their care, their devotion... not because they want the club to exist, but because they want the club to thrive. That's why it's so special".

Diamonds chairman Rob Usher said the club was at the heart of its community [James Burridge/BBC]

Diamonds, who now play their home games at Hayden Road, started their journey to the first round proper with a 5-0 win at Kimberley Miners Welfare in the extra preliminary round on 8 August.

They have also beaten Long Eaton United, Desborough Town, Peterborough Sports, Braintree Town and Morecambe to reach this stage.

Along with Stafford Rangers, who host Grimsby Town on Sunday, they are the only side left in the competition to have played from the earliest round and have so far earned £23,194 from the FA's prize fund.

The winners of the tie at Burton's Pirelli Stadium will collect £47,750, with the losers taking £15,800.

Joining AFC Rushden & Diamonds' attempt to make more history will be Billy Sharp, the Championship's all-time record goalscorer, who has reversed his recent decision to retire.

The 40-year-old, best known for his spells with Sheffield United and Doncaster Rovers, scored his first nine professional goals while on loan from the Blades to the original Diamonds in 2005.

"They [Rushden & Diamonds] made me as a player," said Sharp, who will be targeting a landmark 300th goal while filling in for forward James Clifton who is serving a suspension for accumulated yellow cards.

Diamonds' FA Cup story too hard to turn down - Sharp

Retired Sheff Utd legend Sharp signs for Rushden

"I've stayed in contact with the club over last few years, I should've been here for a legends game and I couldn't make it. But I always look out for their results.

"Elliot [Sandy, the club's manager] asked me if I'd consider playing, I said let's see what happens v Morecambe. Within 20 minutes of the result he text me an eyes emoji.

"I just smiled and said give me a day or two. It just seemed right. The opportunity to score another goal, to play another game in the FA Cup. It's special. This club means a lot to me, it gave me my chance when I was a young kid. Hopefully we can cause a shock, if we get through to the next round James [Clifton] can get his chance."

Sam Brown has played 350 games for AFC Rushden & Diamonds and supported the original club [James Burridge/BBC]

Defender Sam Brown, who grew up watching Rushden & Diamonds on the terraces at Nene Park, remembers chanting Sharp's name and expects it to feel "weird" to now play alongside him.

Brown was involved in the phoenix club's inaugural season and has made 350 appearances across two spells.

"It's been a rollercoaster," he said. "It's a big journey and I'm glad to have been a part of it.

"The club is owned by the fans so it needs all the fans to pull together to be able to grow. They turn up every Saturday no matter where we are and it's nice to have a chance for them to enjoy a different kind of environment on Saturday."

This is AFC Rushden & Diamonds' first appearance in the FA Cup proper. Their predecessors, Rushden & Diamonds, last featured in 2010-11, losing 3-0 to Yeovil Town in the first round [Shaun Frankham]

While the travelling supporters will hope to see an upset against Burton, for many, the experience of bringing their community together for such an occasion will provide the most lasting memories.

Diamonds say they have put on six coaches "at cost price" to help transport fans to the game in east Staffordshire.

Many are part owners and volunteers, including husband and wife Teresa and David Hales who help look after the club's kit.

"It's brilliant, absolutely brilliant." said Teresa. "For a little club like us it's absolutely amazing. I'm just so proud of everybody. Players, volunteers, everybody.

"There have been times over the years where I've sat with my head in my hands thinking, 'oh God, what's going to happen next?' But we're on the way up. We're really on the way up."

Teresa (left) and David (centre) Hales are part owners of the club and volunteer as kit managers [James Burridge/BBC]

"It would be the shock of shocks," said club chairman Usher. "I've seen this squad this year pull off unbelievable things when the odds are against us, so I don't count us out.

"I've seen this club come back from death quite literally in 2011 and once again four years ago [when the phoenix club faced financial troubles].

"Diamonds are never done. Diamonds are forever."

Nene Park, the home of the original Rushden & Diamonds, was demolished in 2017 [Shutterstock]

Rushden & Diamonds were formed in 1992 following the merger of Rushden Town and Irthlingborough Diamonds.

The merger was the brainchild of Max Griggs, the owner of Northamptonshire-based Dr. Martens, with the aim of getting the single, larger club into the Football League.

The club reached the Conference, now known as the National League, in 1996 and realised Griggs' dream of EFL football in 2001 under the management of Brian Talbot.

In 2003, Diamonds were promoted as champions to Division Two, now known as League One, but Griggs' retirement as chairman signalled the beginning of their ultimate demise.

By 2006 they had been relegated back to non-league and mounting financial pressures saw them kicked out the Conference in 2011 before ultimately folding.

Rivals Kettering Town moved into the club's Nene Park stadium at Irthlingborough for a brief and ill-fated period between 2011 and 2012, before the ground was demolished in 2017.

Jaguars' point differential of +108 this season is 50 points better than any other team

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

No NFL team is dominating opponents like the Jacksonville Jaguars.

After blowing out the Ravens 33-10 on Thursday night, the Jaguars have now outscored their opponents by a cumulative score of 229-121 this season. That’s a point differential of +108, by far the best in the NFL.

The team with the second-best point differential in the NFL is the 49ers, at +58. The Jaguars’ point differential is 50 points better than the next-best team.

At 6-2, the Jaguars do not have the best record in the league, as the Bills, 49ers and Vikings are all 6-1. The 49ers are favored to beat the Raiders and improve to 7-1, and the Bills and Vikings play each other on Monday night, so the winner will be 7-1.

But at the midway point of the season, point differential is a better indication of a team’s quality, and a better predictor of a team’s future success, than win-loss record. That’s because with as small a sample size as half a season, a few plays in a few close games can skew the won-loss record, but a few plays don’t skew point differential. Historically, the teams that prove to be the best over the course of a season are the teams that win some blowouts, even if they also lose some close games.

Winning blowouts and losing close games is exactly what the Jaguars do. Both of the Jaguars’ losses were one-possession games that were tied in the fourth quarter, while the Jaguars’ wins have included some massive blowouts: The Jaguars’ 35-point win over the Colts was the biggest blowout of this NFL season, their 29-point win over the Patriots was the fourth-biggest blowout in the NFL this season, their 24-point win over the Browns was the seventh-biggest blowout in the NFL this season, and Thursday night’s 23-point win over the Ravens was the 11th-biggest blowout in the NFL this season.

The Jaguars have won four games by more than 21 points this season; no other team has more than one win by more than 21 points.

Jacksonville has a 3.5-game lead in the AFC South, and six of the Jaguars’ nine remaining games are against opponents with a losing record. They’re poised to run away with their division, perhaps earn the No. 1 seed in the AFC, and maybe blow some teams out in the playoffs just like they have in the regular season.

Tanks or drones? Defense earnings reveal a more complicated rearmament boom

📅 2026-11-06  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Drone technology and artificial intelligence are rapidly reshaping modern warfare, but the world's biggest defense contractors are finding that demand for tanks, fighter jets and other traditional military equipment remains strong.

Recent earnings from U.S. and European defense companies show conventional weapons continue to drive year-on-year growth, even alongside surging demand for advanced sensors, autonomous systems and counter-drone technology.

Rather than displacing traditional weapons manufacturers, the rise of drones and autonomous warfare is creating new business for many of the same companies.

From counter-drone defenses to advanced sensors integrated into tanks and fighter jets, the technologies transforming the battlefield are also changing, or even expanding, the markets served by legacy defense giants.

Lockheed Martin reported a 24% jump in revenue from its missiles business in the third quarter, while RTX secured multibillion-dollar contracts for established missile systems. In Europe, German defense supplier Renk saw orders for its military vehicle business surge 46% in the first nine months of the year.

Yet, the same earnings also show how the boundaries between legacy weapons and emerging defense technology are increasingly blurred.

German defense electronics company Hensoldt reported a 52% jump in revenue from its optronics business, with its chief financial officer telling CNBC that much of the growth came from conventional military equipment, including tanks and armored vehicles.

Meanwhile, Rheinmetall's air defense business, which includes counter-drone systems, reported 63% revenue growth, even as the wider company cut its full-year outlook, citing project delays.

The rise of drones is transforming traditional military platforms rather than replacing them, with tanks and other weapons increasingly integrated into connected, data-driven battlefield networks, according to Morningstar.

Over the past five years and since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in early 2022, defense primes have seen their order books swell amid higher military spending among governments globally.

Yet, analysts mostly say that the rearmament boom is structural, and even an end to wars in Ukraine and Iran won't derail it. Crucially, Europe now has to play catch-up amid increasing uncertainty over U.S. security guarantees and Russia's next move, after decades of military underfunding.

However, concerns that valuations had run ahead of the industry's ability to deliver dented defense stocks this year amid several instances of project delays and contract cancellations.

Advancements in modern warfare and technology – as proven by Ukraine's use of drones on the front lines – further hit investor confidence in defense contractors amid worries that new technologies may displace demand for traditional military equipment.

Beyond the front line, NATO plans to invest more than $40 billion in counter-drone capabilities over the next five years, as Secretary General Mark Rutte warned that drones have fundamentally transformed modern warfare.

Even so, Hensoldt CFO Christian Ladurner told CNBC on Thursday that demand for traditional military equipment remains a key driver of growth.

Its fast-growing optronics business, which supplies optical and sensor technologies, is benefiting from European governments' efforts to replenish conventional military equipment, even as newer battlefield technologies attract increasing attention, Ladurner said.

He also highlighted growing demand for air defense systems, as militaries confront an expanding range of threats, from small drones to ballistic missiles, adding that the air defense market is "underinvested" despite growing by 20% a year.

Limits to Patriot interceptors produced by Lockheed Martin are being felt hard, not least in Ukraine, after the U.S. depleted much of its stockpile of the state-of-the-art system able to take down high-speed missiles in the Iran war.

More recently, defense stocks have come under pressure amid concerns that the Democrats' U.S. midterm victories could squeeze American military spending.

While the result has dampened expectations for defense spending, with Democratic control of Congress making a proposed $1.5 trillion budget unlikely and potentially weighing on military spending growth through 2029, nothing has materially changed the geopolitical calculations, TD Cowen analyst Roman Schweizer told CNBC's "Morning Call" on Thursday.

"This is a multi-year picture, and I think we've seen those changes both in the U.S. and also Europe," he said, referencing internal budgetary difficulties and questions around NATO's hiked spending targets.

Morningstar analyst Loredana Muharremi told CNBC Wednesday's weakness in European defense stocks was largely sentiment-driven.

"Some investors may now see a greater chance of stronger US backing for Europe, easing the pressure on European governments to spend more [but] even with stronger US backing, we expect Europe to stick to its spending plans," she said.

"It takes years of investment to close the gaps in Europe's own military capabilities and build the industrial capacity it needs to deter Russia."

Pope Leo sets off on 12-day South America tour

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The pontiff will hold meetings and celebrate Mass in Uruguay, Argentina and Peru, with the latter attracting particular interest. The trip will be the longest and farthest of his papacy so far.

The plane carrying Pope Leo XIV to South America for the beginning of his almost two-week tour set off from Rome on Friday.

Following a 13-and-a-half-hour flight, the pontiff is set to land in Uruguay, where he will spend three days before heading on to Argentina, and finally Peru.

The 71-year-old US-born pope is recovering from a lung cancer surgery that took place just three weeks ago.

Latin America is home to around 40% of the world's 1.4 billion Catholics, for whom the pope is their spiritual leader. However, fewer and fewer people on the continent identify as Catholic.

Pope Leo's first leg of the tour takes him to Uruguay, the most secular Latin American country.

Broadcom Stock Has Had Only 2 Losing Years Since 2009. 2026 Is on Pace to Be the 3rd.

After the last two, the stock rose 24% and then almost doubled. Can it rebound like that again?

2026 hasn't been good to Broadcom (AVGO -10.43%) stock. At roughly $300 as I write this, the chip designer's shares trade about 13% under their 2025 close of $346.10.

A losing year is rare for this stock. Since its August 2009 listing as Avago Technologies, the stock has ended just two calendar years lower. Shares dipped around 1% in 2018 and dropped 16% in 2022. But both times, a strong year followed, with the stock gaining 24% in 2019 and almost doubling in 2023.

So it's tempting to read this year's drop as the setup for another rebound. I'm not sure the pattern holds this time, though. The S&P 500 (^GSPC -4.36%) dropped around 6% in 2018 and 19% in 2022, so Broadcom beat the index both times. This year, the index is up about 9%.

If 2026 ended today, it'd be the first calendar year since its debut that Broadcom lagged the S&P 500 by over 5 percentage points.

Neither earlier drop was about the business. Broadcom's revenue climbed 18% in fiscal 2018 to almost $21 billion. Free cash flow grew even faster, up 50%. Fiscal 2022 revenue rose 21% to a record $33.2 billion.

Strangely, the rebound years were the slower ones. In June 2019, Broadcom cut its fiscal 2019 revenue forecast to $22.5 billion from $24.5 billion, as CEO Hock Tan pointed to "a broad-based slowdown in the demand environment." Revenue finished the year up 8%. And the stock's 24% rise in 2019 lagged the S&P 500's 29%, so that rebound mainly rode the market.

Fiscal 2023 revenue also rose just 8%. But Tan said that growth came from "investments in accelerators and network connectivity for AI by hyperscalers," and Broadcom forecast around $50 billion in fiscal 2024 revenue with VMware included, up 40%.

The starting price probably helped in both cases. Shares closed 2018 at around 12 times earnings and 2022 at about 15 times earnings, using non-GAAP (adjusted) earnings per share for the fiscal year just ended.

Broadcom's business is growing faster today than in any of those years. Revenue jumped 86% year over year to $29.6 billion in the fiscal third quarter of 2026 (the period ending Aug. 2, 2026), led by artificial intelligence (AI) semiconductor revenue of $16.7 billion, up 221%. Net income climbed 216%. And free cash flow has grown for three straight quarters, from $7.5 billion in fiscal Q4 2025 to $13.7 billion in fiscal Q3.

But shares have shed more than a third of their value since closing at a record $481.57 on June 2.

The stock also doesn't start at a cheap price. Shares trade at around 30 times adjusted earnings for the past four reported quarters, at least twice either earlier starting point. On fiscal 2027's expected earnings, though, the price-to-earnings ratio is about 16, which arguably looks fair for a business growing so fast.

Broadcom has said it plans to release its fiscal fourth-quarter results on Wednesday, Dec. 9.

Management guided for fiscal fourth-quarter revenue of about $34.8 billion, up 93% year over year, with $21.7 billion in AI semiconductor revenue.

The bigger test is fiscal 2027, when Broadcom expects AI semiconductor revenue to double to around $115 billion.

"This AI revenue guidance is being provided to you to give you the trajectory of our growth, and we do not intend to update it on a quarterly basis," CFO Amie Thuener said on the Sept. 2 earnings call.

So December's guidance for the fiscal first quarter of 2027 might be the closest check investors get. Hitting $115 billion takes an average of almost $29 billion in AI revenue a quarter, well over the $21.7 billion guided for the fiscal fourth quarter. The December report has also usually brought the annual dividend increase, which was 10% last year.

When I wrote in late September that Broadcom had climbed in 13 straight fourth quarters, shares were around $350. A gain of about 17% from here would continue that streak, and about 15% would keep 2026 out of the loss column.

Ultimately, Broadcom's business looks far stronger now than it did in 2018 or 2022. But this year's drop is Broadcom's own, not the market's, and shares don't start out cheap. Sure, a soft first-quarter outlook may send the stock down before the year ends.

I still think the stock is worth a look at this price. I'd just want to see that outlook before counting on another year like 2023.

The Football Association and WSL Football have teamed up with Imperial College London on a new study into ACL injuries, the knee problem that has cost the women’s game some of its biggest names in recent seasons. All clubs across the Barclays WSL and WSL2 are being asked to take part, from the players themselves to the medical, performance and coaching staff around them.

Announced on Thursday, the project is being run as a PhD programme funded by the FA. Imperial researcher Hanna Lehtovirta, based in the university’s Department of Mechanical Engineering, is leading the work, with Fortius Clinic knee surgeon Simon Ball also involved. According to the FA’s announcement, the aim is to find out why anterior cruciate ligament ruptures keep happening in the elite women’s game and to turn those findings into practical prevention advice.

The research is split into two stages. In the first, players complete surveys covering their injury record, how much they train and play, and hormonal, nutritional and psychological factors. Anyone who has ruptured an ACL since the 2018-19 campaign will have their answers set against those of players who have avoided the injury, in the hope of spotting patterns linked to risk.

The second stage goes back to the same group for interviews, and this time staff are brought in too. The FA says plenty of earlier research has looked at single factors, often in a lab, and that far less is known about how they combine in the daily routine of a professional squad. Lehtovirta said her team would be looking at everything “from minutes played and injury prevention practices at clubs to how players developed before they entered professional football.”

ESPN reports that data gathering began in June and that the study is set to run for three years.

The numbers explain the urgency. Research cited by ESPN suggests women are around twice as likely as men to suffer an ACL injury, and roughly two in every three cases happen with no contact from another player. England captain Leah Williamson and Beth Mead both missed the 2023 World Cup after rupturing the ligament, and Vivianne Miedema has also had to come back from the injury during her time in England.

Ritan Mehta, the FA’s Head of Women’s Medical, was clear about the scale of the problem. “ACL injuries remain one of the biggest challenges facing female footballers,” she said. “While significant progress has been made in understanding the factors that may contribute to these injuries, there is still much more to learn.”

WSL Football’s Medical Governance Lead, Dr Abosede Ajayi, pointed to the mix of football, healthcare and academia behind the project, saying the findings could “inform medical standards across the women’s game”. Ball put it more bluntly: “ACL injury is devastating for professional athletes,” he said, adding that “prevention is better than cure!”

Airbnb is planning to launch an AI travel agent next year. The company shared details of the project during its Q3 2026 earnings call on Thursday, reiterating its intention to "build an AI-native Airbnb."

"AI is the best thing to ever happen to Airbnb. It's changing both how we develop products and how we operate," Airbnb said in a letter to shareholders. "AI is helping our teams move faster, and in Q3 alone, we shipped more than 50 products and features — nearly 70 percent more than a year ago."

Now Airbnb intends to add an AI travel agent to that collection. While still under development, the company says its new AI agent is being designed to help users research, plan, and book trips on its platform.

"We’re developing a custom-designed, AI-native experience that builds the interface around your question," said Airbnb. "Rather than a chat window, our agent draws on a library of hundreds of travel-specific interface elements — maps, photos, comparisons, calendars, itineraries — and composes them in real time to match what you’re looking for."

CEO Brian Chesky elaborated during Airbnb's earnings call, stating that the AI agent will "feel a little bit like generative AI" due to its ability to respond to users' requests with relevant custom interfaces for specific travel needs. Chesky further stated that the agent will have a "post-chat-based interface," being less text-focused, though it will still retain a "chat element."

"If you want a car rental, the agent generates this little UI of a car, and it's [presented] beautifully in a custom-generated interface," said Chesky. "If you want to invite co-travellers, the agent helps you."

Airbnb's AI agent will also help resolve issues once a booking has already been made, including changing reservations or assisting with refunds and rebookings. Interestingly, Airbnb states that the AI will provide 24-hour safety support as well, though did not elaborate on exactly what type of assistance it will offer.

"Our agent will help guests explore options, make decisions with the people they’re travelling with, and take action directly in Airbnb," Airbnb wrote. "Over time, we want our agent to help guests plan their entire trip in one place, across homes, hotels, services, experiences, and more."

This won't be restricted to bookings that are available on Airbnb either. Chesky stated that the AI agent will be able to go "outside the walls of Airbnb," though noted it will stay within the realm of travel.

"Today's AI agents, like [Meta's AI agent] Muse and [invite-only AI assistant] Instinct, are good at simple routine tasks like making a restaurant reservation or scheduling an appointment," Chesky said. "But planning a trip is different. Travel is aspirational. Discovering where to go and where to stay is part of the experience… I don't think the general-purpose agents are the best to do that. That is why we are building our own AI agent."

Airbnb's new AI travel agent is expected to be released in the first half of 2027.

This upcoming AI travel agent isn't to be confused with Airbnb's AI customer support assistant, which is already in operation. Airbnb states that its AI assistant resolved around 40 percent of all customer support issues without any human agents in Q3, an increase from approximately 30 percent last quarter. In Q2, Airbnb's AI agent resolved nearly 45 percent of issues that specifically began in the tool.

Airbnb reports that its revenue grew 19 percent to $4.9 billion in Q3, bringing in a net income of $1.9 billion.

Lost audio recordings of Louisiana Creole give us a rare chance to hear the past voices of early New Orleans

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Recordings from 1935 reveal how Louisiana Creole speakers switched languages, capturing speech patterns that written accounts often missed.

To understand how Louisiana Creole sounded in the 1800s, scholars have had to rely on writing. But some writers changed what they heard.

One researcher wrote in 1933 that he had “purified the dialect” in his transcriptions.

Recordings made two years later, now analyzed for the first time, capture something that those written accounts often missed: people moving between Creole and French as they spoke.

Lorenzo Dow Turner, remembered as the first African American linguist, recorded those voices on 13 aluminum discs, way back in 1935.

Oliver Mayeux, a linguist at the University of Cambridge’s Faculty of Modern and Medieval Languages and Linguistics, tracked down the digitized recordings and worked out who was speaking.

The discs contain the earliest known recordings of spoken Louisiana Creole and Louisiana French. Both languages now have dwindling numbers of elderly native speakers.

“Turner’s lost recordings contain far more linguistic diversity than the texts we relied upon until now,” said Mayeux.

“This is the language of the levees and cane fields. The language that drifted out from French Quarter saloons along with early jazz music at the turn of the last century.”

Louisiana Creole shares almost all its words with Louisiana French, but the two languages use different grammar.

Creole developed on southern Louisiana plantations, where enslaved people, mostly from West Africa, came into contact with settlers who spoke regional varieties of French.

“Africans with little common language were forced onto plantations where French was the language of administration. Everybody is learning approximations of French and then approximations of approximations. Quite quickly, a new language is born,” said Mayeux.

By Turner’s arrival in July 1935, younger people and city residents were already moving toward English. Mayeux, who has family ties in Louisiana, knows that history through his own family.

“By the middle of the 20th century you see the end of intergenerational transmission. My grandfather wanted his children to speak English,” explained Mayeux.

“This was typical of many Louisianans who saw their future as American rather than Creole or Cajun,” he added.

Many children, therefore, grew up without learning the language their parents spoke.

Turner was known for studying Gullah, a creole language with English roots spoken by Black communities in Georgia and South Carolina.

He visited Louisiana to compare the two languages’ sounds, especially how speakers’ voices rose and fell.

“From casual observation I find that the intonation of Creole resembles very much that of Gullah,” Turner wrote to a funding body after the trip.

His recording machine weighed about 100 pounds (45 kilograms). He had to drive it to places with electricity, which limited where he could work.

“Turner relied on a network of Black doctors and intellectuals in New Orleans and out in the country. The physicians found him Creole-speaking interviewees and let him record in their offices,” said Mayeux.

“These were trusted spaces for the participants during the era of Jim Crow segregation and, crucially, were wired for electricity.”

In Lafayette and Opelousas, doctors Julius Blackburn Matthews and August C. Terrence hosted the sessions. One participant, Marie Raymond, said she had never known a doctor until she was 69.

A woman also helped Turner conduct many interviews. She seemed more comfortable speaking French than he did, and Mayeux thinks she was probably Turner’s wife, Geneva Calcier Townes Turner.

Turner never analyzed the Louisiana Creole recordings, and his applications for funding to continue the comparison were rejected. The Guggenheim Foundation turned down one application and awarded a fellowship to his former student Zora Neale Hurston instead.

Mayeux suspects Turner simply set the discs aside. Indiana University’s Archives of Traditional Music later digitized them, but their labels offered little information and sometimes got it wrong.

One clue came from a disc that a Turner biography described as two people singing. It actually contained solo piano music by Jessie Covington Dent, a New Orleans pianist and civil rights activist.

Dent’s family papers at the Amistad Research Center led Mayeux to Turner’s papers. An address book there named the doctors who had hosted him.

Another disc listed “Mme Emma Bronssard,” which Mayeux recognized as a misspelling of Broussard. The woman said she was 69, had been widowed for 20 years, and had raised 12 children of her own plus four orphans.

Census and death records matched those details to Emma Benoit, born Emma Broussard.

Her great-granddaughter, Valencia Pradier-Linton, later shared a magazine interview in which Benoit’s son said his mother “didn’t know how to talk English at all.”

Turner recorded three Creole speakers in New Orleans, where people shifted to English earlier than in the countryside. The city’s variety of Creole likely has no speakers left.

Mayeux identified two speakers: George Doyle, born in 1867, and Odelia Edmond, born in 1862. Doyle had worked as a police officer before the city segregated its police department.

Some of their speech matched the old texts closely. Doyle’s word for policemen, “lapolis,” came from the French “la police,” but the French word for “the” had become a permanent part of the noun.

Edmond used mo for “my” when she said “my mother.” French would use a feminine form of the word instead.

Other details challenged what scholars thought they knew. The speakers used rounded vowels, like those in the French words “peu” and “plus,” which researchers had treated as a later borrowing.

Edmond also moved between old and newer forms of the same word. While explaining that she was three days old when her mother died, she used a long form of “have” known mainly from 19th-century writing.

Just 15 seconds later, when talking about being nine days old, she used the short form. Hearing both from the same speaker suggested that New Orleans Creole was more varied than the written record made it seem.

Outside New Orleans, speakers often began interviews in Louisiana French, the more formal language. Others in the room then asked them to speak Creole.

Emma Benoit started with a polite French greeting. In Mayeux’s translation, a woman interrupted with “no no no! Creole!” and then “no no that’s French; that’s not Creole anymore.”

Benoit replied in Creole, “I forget that,” and said she would talk about her troubles. A few minutes later, she returned to French and received another reminder.

Anastasie Dumas switched languages too, answering in French when Turner addressed her in formal, European-sounding French. When his co-interviewer asked in somewhat stiff Creole how to make gumbo, Dumas returned to Creole.

Most participants could not read or write and did not own a radio. Speaking into a visiting professor’s recording machine would have been an unusual, formal experience.

The people in the room and the occasion helped shape how they spoke. Mayeux concluded that French signaled politeness and respectability, while Creole emerged in more relaxed moments of joking and storytelling.

For most speakers today, moving between the two languages no longer carries the same degree of social meaning.

Each recording lasts about two to five minutes, and static makes some speech hard to follow. The New Orleans speakers also came through one circle of doctors, educators, and musicians, so their voices cannot represent the whole city.

Mayeux describes his reconstruction as partial and interpretive, shaped by his position as a white researcher listening nearly a century later. He hopes others will offer their own readings.

He also cannot yet explain why the city recordings differ from the old texts. The language may have changed, writers may have removed variation, or both may have happened.

The next step is comparison with recordings that Turner’s former student Raleigh Morgan Jr. made in the 1950s. Researchers can study those voices alongside the 1935 discs and the Creole spoken today.

For some young learners, Creole offers a way to reconnect with their family history. Many are grandchildren of native speakers whose parents never learned the language, Mayeux said.

“Young people are learning Creole to connect with the richness of their heritage, helping them discover a distinct identity in an increasingly homogenized world,” said Mayeux.

The study is published in the journal Language Documentation and Description.

Most lithium comes from desert ponds or hard-rock mines, and a new lab method skips both

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Engineers pulled lithium from salty water in the lab using a solvent that reacts to heat, a step toward reaching brines that evaporation ponds can’t use.

The lithium in a battery usually comes from one of two places: a hard-rock mine, or a desert pond where the sun bakes salty water for up to two years.

Columbia University engineers have now pulled lithium out of salty water in the lab with neither one. Their solvent takes up lithium at room temperature and lets it go when the temperature rises.

Ngai Yin Yip, an associate professor of Earth and Environmental Engineering at Columbia, did the work with four co-authors. It’s a proof of concept. The team mixed salty water in the lab to copy the chemistry of the Salton Sea, a geothermal region in California.

About 40% of lithium production starts as brine, salty water that collects in large reservoirs under deserts. Nearly all of that lithium comes out through solar evaporation. Mining companies pump the brine into sprawling ponds and wait for the water to evaporate.

Evaporation ponds need a lot of dry, flat land, so they work in places like Chile’s Atacama Desert and parts of Nevada. They also use large volumes of water in places that can barely spare it. Hard-rock mining, the other main source, comes with steep tradeoffs of its own.

“There’s no way solar evaporation alone can match future demand,” Yip said. “And there are promising lithium-rich brines, like those in California’s Salton Sea, where this method simply can’t be used at all.”

The Salton Sea region holds enough lithium, by one estimate, for more than 370 million electric vehicle batteries.

Lithium-bearing brine turns up elsewhere in the U.S. too. In Arkansas, a U.S. Geological Survey-led study estimated 5 million to 19 million tons of lithium in brine beneath the southwestern part of the state.

In Pennsylvania, analysts who studied company data on wastewater from 515 gas wells found lithium in it as well.

The solvent is a liquid chemical that behaves differently depending on temperature. At room temperature, it attracts water. When it warms, it repels water.

The team mixed the solvent with brine at room temperature, and the solvent took up water and lithium together. Then the team warmed it a little.

The solvent now repelled water, so it released the lithium and the water as a purified stream. It returned to its starting state, ready to reuse.

The warming step needs only modest heat. “It can be powered by low-grade heat from waste sources or solar collectors,” Yip said.

In brine, lithium mixes with sodium and potassium, two metals that behave a lot like it, and separating the three is hard. The solvent still preferred lithium to sodium across versions with different chemical structures, by as much as about 10 to 1.

The solvent kept that preference even when lithium was 1,000 times scarcer than sodium or potassium in the water. In the simulated Salton Sea brine, the preference reached about 13 to 1 over sodium and 24 to 1 over potassium. So the solvent still separated lithium when lithium made up only a tiny share of the metals in the water.

Magnesium, another common contaminant in lithium brines, also dropped out as a solid, because the solvent is alkaline, the opposite of acidic. In the Salton Sea mixture, the team removed all of it.

The team reused one batch of solvent for four rounds. Together, the four rounds recovered nearly 40% of the lithium in the simulated brine. The solvent kept its preference for lithium throughout.

So about 60% of the lithium went unrecovered after four rounds. The team hasn’t yet optimized the system for yield or efficiency, so 40% reflects an early version of the method.

The team ran its tests in the lab, in batches, on brines it mixed itself. Real brine from the ground may hold more impurities than the lab mixes did, and the team still has to show how the solvent handles them. The same goes for its performance at the size of a working plant.

Whether recovery rises above 40% once the team tunes the system remains an open question. Columbia has invited companies that want to collaborate on the technology, or license it, to express their interest.

The full study was published in the journal Joule.

Zillennials are ‘loud working ’: They’re spending nearly 5 hours a week on self-promotion—and driving colleagues to quit

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

So you’ve heard of the trend quiet quitting, and its sister, loud quitting—but now workers are doing the complete opposite: “loud working.” Instead of doing the bare minimum to get by or turning their anger on their employers, they’re pouring all their energy into making their work more visible.

Think of the colleague who posts every win on LinkedIn or pings the whole team on Slack the second a task is done.

New research from Tide, a finance platform, found that the average U.K. worker spends 131 hours a year—more than 17 working days or 2.8 hours a week—making their work visible. That includes singing their own praises on social media, posting updates on Slack or Teams, speaking up in meetings, and vocalizing their wins to anyone who will listen.

And the loudest of all are younger workers: In fact, nearly 70% of 25- to 34-year-olds are “loud working.” They’re spending about 5 hours every week self-promoting. And it’s drawing attention from management, clients and even angry coworkers.

Perhaps unsurprisingly, it’s not always the most competent worker who gets rewarded—it’s the one who makes the most noise about it.

According to the research, 77% of “loud workers” have had at least one career opportunity in the past year alone, including new customers or clients, pay rises and promotions. For 15% of respondents, getting vocal about their wins has even gotten them awards.

And all the new noise around their success is attracting the attention of other employers; In fact, 17% say they’ve not only been poached, but they’ve actually landed a new job because they’ve been more vocal about their work.

But worryingly, it’s not just “loud workers” who are at risk of being snapped up and leaving the business.

Not everyone’s cheering. More than one in four (27%) workers have considered leaving a job because of a loud working culture, or because colleagues are too vocal about their own achievements.

Zillennials are both most likely to be guilty of “loud working,” as well as the ones walking off.  They’re posting about their success at more than three times the rate of over-55s. Yet nearly half of 25 to 34-year-olds have considered quitting a job because of it—and one in five already have.

Some industries are more forgiving of office braggers than others: Workers in IT, telecoms, and finance are the most likely to have had enough of performative culture. Meanwhile, men are more likely than women to have already quit over it.

“Loudworking has mixed reactions in the workplace and it’s easy to understand that a colleague who turns every small win into an announcement might be off-putting,” said George Schmidt, Tide’s CEO for the U.K. and Europe. “But our research shows there’s a difference between being performative and backing yourself.”

He said that for small business owners, sharing their work, wins and expertise is often the most effective marketing they have. And the same goes for anyone who has left their employer to go solo or who is eyeing up the exit.

Ultimately, though, many workers want to be at a business that rewards people for actually doing the hard work—not just shouting the most about it.
