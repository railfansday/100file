Exciting times are ahead for Manchester United, as the English giants are about to embark on a new era. Only time will tell if Mauricio Pochettino's spell at Old Trafford goes better than those who have held the job over the past 13 years.

The Argentine takes over from Michael Carrick, who took over from Ruben Amorim, who took over from Erik ten Hag, who took over from Ralf Rangnick, who took over from Ole Gunnar Solskjaer, who took over from Jose Mourinho, who took over from Louis van Gaal, who took over from David Moyes.

All of them failed to live up to the lofty standards set by the legendary Sir Alex Ferguson, who departed in 2013 with 13 Premier League titles under his belt. But Pochettino arrives with good pedigree. He knows the Premier League well, having previously coached Southampton, Tottenham Hotspur and Chelsea, and has dealt with other high-pressure jobs, taking charge of Paris Saint-Germain and, most recently, the 2026 World Cup hosts, the United States.

The 54-year-old will aim to change the culture at Old Trafford, and may have to make some unpopular decisions along the way. As a top-level coach, however, he's no stranger to clashing with senior stars.

The Special One made his comments while he was at Manchester United himself.

Upon Pochettino’s arrival at Spurs from Southampton in 2014, he allowed the players to vote on who should wear the armband following the departure of Michael Dawson. The squad picked Younes Kaboul.

Unfortunately, this didn't pan out well for player or coach. Indeed, the centre-back would be sold the following summer, having played just 15 times under Pochettino. His relationship with the Argentine had crumbled to the point where the pair were no longer talking to each other.

In 2016, he accused the then-Tottenham manager of 'disrespecting him.' The Frenchman angrily said:

"The end was not OK [at Tottenham], because the manager disrespected me, 100 percent. When Mauricio first came we had a very nice, human, man-to-man kind of respect. And then something happened and I don't know what it was."

He continued: "I got injured and then he didn't talk to me anymore. Then he was not putting me in the squad for no reason. One game, two games, three games, and that was strange for me because I am supposed to be his captain.

"I'm not saying because I'm his captain I need to play, no, no, no. If I am not good enough to play I am not playing. But you have to respect players, to talk to them and explain why they're not playing.

"After a few games I went to see him, to ask what was happening. I needed to understand because clearly he wasn't talking to me. But he said: 'There's nothing, I've got nothing to say to you.'"

He said after getting no clear explanation from the Spurs boss, they soon 'stopped talking to each other.'

The ex-Spurs captain carried a grudge as well. In 2018, Kaboul would reflect on their bad relationship once more. This time, he called Pochettino 'two-faced,' explaining:

"The one where things didn’t go well was Pochettino. On the field, nothing to say, very good manager, with a philosophy and a style of play that works.

"Personally, it’s the opposite. He’s two-faced. He would say things to you and did the complete opposite behind your back.”

“I didn’t like that, and I told him. That’s all."

It will be very interesting to see if Pochettino treats any of his new Man Utd stars in a similarly ruthless fashion. He will certainly need everyone pulling in the right direction if he wants to succeed at Old Trafford.

These Remote Atolls Are Among the Last Places on Earth Humans Haven’t Touched. Here’s What Scientists Found.

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Bikar and Bokak sit so far from people that they may show us what the ocean looked like before humans.

There are few havens on Earth as pristine as the Bikar and Bokak atolls. Formed from sunken volcanoes around which coral reefs grew, these atolls in the Ratak chain of the Marshall Islands teem with life and remain uninhabited by humans. Rainbows of fish swim among corals and sea anemones, while sharks glide silently through the water in pursuit of prey. Until recently, even marine biologists had rarely explored these habitats in depth.

That began to change when Alan Friedlander set out across an impossibly blue swath of ocean and led his team into paradise. Friedlander is a marine ecologist at the University of Hawai’i who served as chief scientist for National Geographic’s Pristine Seas program on the expedition, and he takes a special interest in remote and protected regions of the ocean. The people of the Marshall Islands have known Bikar and Bokak for generations, but the atolls had never been comprehensively surveyed when Friedlander and his team arrived. For the Marshallese, the ocean is home even more than the land is. Their survival depends on the health of the fragile reef ecosystems around the country’s 1,225 islands and islets, only some of which are inhabited.

The opportunity came in 2023, when Pristine Seas was invited to join the Marshall Islands Marine Resources Authority in surveying Bikar and Bokak, which are safeguarded by the Utirik Atoll community. Friedlander and his team received a traditional blessing and farewell on Utirik before sailing north to Bikar, which lies about 72 miles (116 km) from the nearest inhabited atoll. Bokak is even more isolated, about 239 miles (385 km) away. This remoteness is part of the reason the atolls have remained relatively untouched by human influence. Friedlander wanted to carry out an ecological assessment of everything from the land habitats to the reefs and the open sea. That meant studying the structure and biodiversity of these ecosystems, documenting how well they had been preserved and how the two atolls differed, and identifying the conservation measures they would need in the future.

“The remote northern atolls of Bikar and Bokak represent some of the least disturbed coral reef ecosystems remaining in the tropical Pacific,” Friedlander and his co-authors wrote in a study recently published in Frontiers in Marine Science. “Both atolls are uninhabited and geographically isolated from major population centers and industrial development. Previous expeditions documented portions of their terrestrial habitats, seabird colonies, sea turtles, and shallow coral reefs.”

The team’s first stop was Bikar, which curves across the water like a crescent moon. There, the researchers used a variety of sampling methods, including scuba surveys, photography, underwater video, deep-sea cameras, and a submarine, to record which species were present and in what numbers. Polynesian rats, which humans introduced, were sometimes seen scurrying through the island’s old-growth Pisonia forests. Even so, native species still thrive. Seabirds such as masked boobies, great frigatebirds, black noddies, and white terns filled the skies, while shorebirds like the migratory Pacific golden plover pecked at the sand in search of small invertebrates. Bikar’s beaches are also a nesting ground for green sea turtles; the team recorded 132 nest pits there. Its reefs are full of fish, including several species of snapper and grouper. Giant clams, which host photosynthetic algae, filter the water and are vital to the health of the reef.

Farther north and even more isolated, Bokak is an oasis that has seen very little human interference. Its drier, savanna-like landscape is covered in tufts of bunchgrass and tree heliotropes, which bear clusters of small white flowers. Many of the seabirds that soar over Bikar are also found on Bokak. Bokak’s waters are even richer than Bikar’s: its reefs are dominated by coral and hold more fish by weight. Friedlander dove in to investigate. He came across a school of humphead parrotfish, a threatened species once thought to have nearly vanished from the islands. He also saw an unusual reversal of the typical food pyramid. Reef sharks and black jacks outweigh their prey in total biomass. Because these top predators feed so often, they keep energy and nutrients moving quickly through the reef, which helps the ecosystem run efficiently.

After Bokak, Friedlander’s team had one more location to survey, and it offered a sharp contrast. From 1946 to 1958, Bikini Atoll was a center of U.S. nuclear testing, starting with Operation Crossroads. When the islanders were evacuated before the first tests, they took only a few changes of clothes, not knowing that they would never be able to resettle their home for good. The series of detonations devastated the atoll. From the ocean’s glassy surface, Bikini seems to be ringed by cities of coral. But divers who swam closer found that many of these formations, which had taken thousands of years to grow, were little more than skeletons. Still, Bikini Atoll is not without hope. After searching long enough, Friedlander found patches of live coral that had settled on the dead reef and were swarming with fish. It was a sign of how resilient life can be.

Even Bikar and Bokak can’t fully escape what is happening elsewhere. Their remoteness protects them from most direct human pressures, but climate change is slowly reaching them, too. When the water gets too hot, corals expel the symbiotic algae that feed them and give them their bright colors, a process known as coral bleaching. Leftover radiation from the nuclear-testing era is still a source of contamination in the region. Meanwhile, currents carry in trash from other countries, and plastics break down into microplastics. People are responding with conservation measures. In 2025, two years after the expedition, the government of the Marshall Islands designated the waters around Bikar and Bokak, about 18,500 square miles (48,000 square km), as the country’s first national marine sanctuary, as part of the community-led Reimaanlok conservation framework. Despite the threats that come from far away, Friedlander is optimistic.

“As relatively intact tropical marine ecosystems become increasingly rare worldwide,” he and his co-authors concluded, “remote atolls such as Bikar and Bokak serve not only as regional conservation priorities but also as globally important ecological reference systems for understanding the structure and future trajectories of tropical oceanic ecosystems in the Anthropocene.”

IEEE Celebrates 10 Years of Incorporating Ethics Into AI Systems

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Standards and certifications foster socially responsible products

Kathy Pretz is the editor in chief of The Institute, IEEE's member publication

Policymakers, technologists, and members of the public have raised concerns about the harm AI’s rapid advancement might cause to society. Their concerns include bias, data privacy violations, the spread of misinformation, and losing control of AI agents.

Those issues are relatively new to many people, but the IEEE Standards Association has been working for the past decade on ways to prioritize the responsible development of AI systems.

In 2016 IEEE SA launched the IEEE Global Initiative for Ethical Considerations in Autonomous and Intelligent Systems (A/IS) and published the Ethically Aligned Design report. EAD’s mission is to encourage everyone involved in designing autonomous and intelligent systems to prioritize human well-being by applying ethical considerations for the benefit of humanity.

Developed by a multidisciplinary community of experts from around the world, the report outlined eight foundational principles for A/IS. They include human rights, people’s well-being, and transparency. More than 100 leading AI ethics experts provided input. EAD’s Version II report was released in 2017. Both versions solicited public input and received hundreds of pages of feedback, according to John C. Havens, who was the global initiative’s founding executive director, and is now the global director for IEEE Planet Positive 2030. And by 2019, there were more than 400 contributors and the community continues to grow.

“Ethically Aligned Design was the first of its kind in the sense of a report that was more than a list of principles and from an organization with the credibility of IEEE,” Havens says. “At the time it was released, there wasn’t anything like it.”

Since its launch, a series of standards has been developed to provide practical guidelines on designing, building, and deploying technology that prioritizes human values. Several AI ethics certification programs have been introduced.

This year marks the 10th anniversary of EAD’s first draft. To celebrate, IEEE SA recently launched a website, a podcast series, and a blog that reflect on the report’s achievements and impact.

The word ethics is often used interchangeably with morality, but that is not the case in responsible AI systems design, according to EAD’s first version. The report includes insights from classical ethics traditions and value-based design methodologies to offer actionable recommendations for engineers developing AI tools.

The report established eight foundational general principles for designing autonomous systems: respecting human rights, prioritizing increased well-being as success criteria for development, empowering individuals with data agency to have control over their identity, effectiveness, transparency, accountability, awareness of misuse, and competence for the system creators.

The principles shift applied ethics to the design stage of AI systems, compared with being a concern after they’re released, the authors wrote.

The IEEE 7000 series of standards has been developed based on EAD’s framework. Currently the series consists of 29 projects, 11 of which have been published, and more are still being added. They cover topics such as ethical system design model process (IEEE 7000-2021), AI system transparency (IEEE 7001-2021), data privacy (IEEE 7002-2022), algorithmic bias considerations (IEEE 7003-2024), the impact on people’s well-being (IEEE 7010-2020), machine-readable personal privacy requirements (IEEE 7012-2025), and systems that mimic human empathy (IEEE 7014-2024).

IEEE SA offers a suite of ethics in AI programs including IEEE CertifAIEd, which includes two certifications: one for individuals and one for products.

The professional certification provides individuals with skills to assess autonomous and intelligent systems for adherence to IEEE’s methodology and ethics criteria.

“Ethically Aligned Design was the first of its kind in the sense of a report that was more than a list of principles and from an organization with the credibility of IEEE.” —John C. Havens, founding executive director of the global initiative

The product certification program assesses whether an organization’s AI tool or system conforms to the criteria in the IEEE Ontological Specifications. A CertifiAIEd assessor evaluates the product to ensure it meets the criteria. Upon completion of the assessment, the owner submits it to the IEEE Conformity Assessment Program, which can certify it and issue the certification mark.

IEEE SA also recently released its Responsible AI for Children certification program. This program certifies that AI products for children have been independently tested and verified to meet design and ethics requirements.

The IEEE Artificial Intelligence Systems (AIS) for Global Flourishing Collaborative Industry Collections group was formed to provide the resources, knowledge, and tools needed to prioritize people’s well-being to developers of AI systems. It has more than 200 members.

The collaborative describes its work as a practical, systems-level framework for responsible technology design that translates flourishing into applied methods, metrics, and tools that can be used throughout the AIS lifecycle.

The group is establishing guidelines for how to design, evaluate, and govern systems that can measurably improve human well-being and ecological sustainability.

Subcommittees are focused on cognition, education, faith, planetary care, spirituality, and other issues.

“The group is building directly off the well-being chapter of Ethically Aligned Design and the IEEE 7010 standard (Recommended Practice for Assessing the Impact of Autonomous and Intelligent Systems on Human Well-Being),” Havens says. “That’s on purpose because if human and planetary flourishing aren’t addressed first in design, then human well-being and planetary sustainability are afterthoughts.”

To mark the 10-year anniversary, IEEE SA created the five-part “Ethically Aligned Design: 10 Years of Impact” podcast series. It launched on 21 October. New episodes are being released every two weeks.

The episodes center around the vision for EAD, the process for selecting its foundational principles, and how they were translated into the IEEE 7000 standards.

The first podcast, “Origins of Ethically Aligned Design,” explored early efforts to make ethics a practical part of AI and autonomous system design. The experts discussed challenges, debates, global collaboration, and the real-world impact that helped move responsible technology from ethical principles toward actionable standards and practices.

The second podcast, “The Chapters and Committees” focuses on the multidisciplinary groups that worked on turning broad human values into practical guidance for AI and autonomous systems.

The podcast discussions serve as material for a thought leadership program that also includes blog articles about the IEEE 7000 series and educational resources to continue to raise awareness and support of this groundbreaking work.

The interesting part of the launch is what Google chose to publish alongside it. Hydrogen's page carries a ten-row comparison table against Claude Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6.1 Terra, with the rows where Hydrogen loses left visible. The price is $1.50 per million input tokens and $7.50 per million output tokens, and Google states that the price is flat across the whole one-million-token context window. Under Google's flat pricing, a 900,000-token repository dump carries the same unit rate as a 5,000-token question.

Hydrogen also pairs a one-million-token input with up to one million output tokens. Google describes the model synthesising dense PDFs, spreadsheets, charts, audio and recorded video into cited reports and structured deliverables. Consider a hypothetical French or Moroccan SME that scans supplier invoices and contracts: that combination sets the shape of the workflow as one long input, one long structured output and one flat bill.

Hydrogen ships with four thinking levels: low, medium, high as the default, and xhigh as the new top tier. Google's framing is that the levels scale in a predictable progression, so a team pays for reasoning depth only where a task needs it. On the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, the default high level scores 51 and xhigh reaches 54.

That ladder is the practical part of the launch. A slow overnight job can run at xhigh, a chat assistant answering a customer at 10 a.m. can run at low, and both run on one model with one price sheet. The score-versus-time chart Google published shows how the ladder behaves against two competing models: Hydrogen reaches its top score earlier in wall-clock time, and the competing lines sit further to the right.

The table inside the announcement is where a careful reader should slow down. Hydrogen wins SWE-sweep at 13.5% against 3.1% for Claude Sonnet 5.5 and 5.8% for GPT-6.1 Terra, and it wins AutomationBench-AA at 76.1% against 71.8% and 60.2%. A single aggregate index collapses those rows into one number, and that number currently places Hydrogen above Argon. The table says something narrower: Hydrogen is the stronger choice for repository-scale bug repair and for automation-style tool work, and the rival model remains stronger for computer-use tasks and for science-heavy terminal work. Any team that reads only the headline will route the wrong task class to the cheaper model and then blame the model.

Google also introduced Orchestrator in Google Antigravity, a component that switches between models such as Carbon and Hydrogen inside one session. On Terminal-Bench 4.0, Google reports that Orchestrator reached near-Carbon task success rates while consuming 24% less quota on average and finishing 19% faster. For parallel work, the company describes Carbon acting as the coordinator while Hydrogen powers worker and verifier agents across isolated workspaces.

Read that as the vendor building routing into its own product. Google's own engineering position is that a single model rarely fits a whole session: a hard architecture question wants the expensive reasoning model, and the code edits, test runs and verification that follow want the cheap fast one. A team that already pays for both tiers gains nothing from picking one by habit.

The pricing detail that carries this is quota accounting. Google measures Orchestrator on quota consumed, so the benefit shows up in a plan's allowance before it shows up in a dollar figure. A team on a flat seat price sees the difference as more work finished per seat.

Four measurements decide whether a cheaper tier belongs in production. The first is cost per finished task, which includes the retries and the failed tool calls that the per-token price hides. The second is latency at the thinking level the team would actually set, measured on the tasks the team actually runs. The third is failure behaviour when a tool call fails or a search returns empty, which Google lists as a specific improvement over Gemini 3.8 Flash. The fourth is where the model is offered, because a model missing from the editor developers use costs more in workflow friction than it saves in tokens.

GitHub's changelog states that administrators of Copilot Business and Enterprise manage access through the model policy in Copilot settings, and that under default model enablement a new model is enabled automatically unless an administrator has turned off the global default or disabled that model. An organisation that tracks AI spend per team should check that setting before the picker fills up.

For teams running agents against their own infrastructure, the model choice usually sits inside a larger control plane: the tool permissions, the egress policy and the approval steps around each call. One implementation is a self-hosted AI agent platform, and the model tier is one setting inside it.

Gravitational waves have revealed a population of black holes that may have been born when masssive stars failed to go supernova.

Most of us know the origin story of black holes, or we thought we did. Massive stars run out of the fuel for nuclear fusion and their cores collapse. As this happens, the stars' outer layers expand outwards. Shockwaves from the core collapse strike the outer layers, triggering a supernova explosion. The collapsed core creates a black hole.

However, new research based on gravitational wave data collected by LIGO (Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory) and its gravitational wave detector partners, Virgo and KAGRA (Kamioka Gravitational Wave Detector), suggests this might not be the complete picture of black hole birth. The team behind the research suggests some massive stars go out with a whimper rather than a bang and that these "failed supernovas" can also spawn black holes.

The hint of this comes from the over 300 black hole collisions spotted by LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA since the detectors first "heard" ripples in space from these distant events in 2015.

"A vast majority of the merging black holes in the universe are about 10 times the mass of our sun," LIGO team member Katerina Chatziioannou, of the California Institute of Technology (Caltech), said in a statement. "Our data are showing that this group of black holes may have a common origin — they may be a distinct subpopulation that failed to go supernova.

"These results represent a connection between the gravitational-wave data and underlying astrophysics we are trying to extract."

Currently, there is no hard evidence of failed supernovas, but their existence is well supported theoretically. In fact, scientists think there is a vast population of massive stars out there that imploded rather than exploded, directly collapsing into black holes. And these may be black holes with around ten times the mass of the sun (10 solar masses) found in merging pairs.

Chatziioannou led a team that examined gravitational wave data to confirm this concept. To do this, they looked at the distribution of the masses of 257 black hole mergers detected by the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA network over the past 10 years.

This revealed a peak of 10-solar-mass black holes that represented 75% of the entire sample. If this was expected, what the team found that wasn't predicted was a lack of black holes with masses 12 to 16 times the mass of the sun.

"We see 10 solar-mass black holes merging all the time," team member Isaac Legred of the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign said. "But why are, say, 14 solar-mass black holes so rare? The gap we see in the distribution of masses indicates there may be a special cocktail of internal physics that goes into forming the abundance of 10 solar-mass black holes."

The researchers also looked at the spin rates of black holes in mergers, a telltale signature of how they formed. This revealed that 10-solar-mass black holes seemed to have a distinct range of spin speeds. This indicates a common origin, perhaps the implosion of a massive star without a supernova explosion.

"We can look up at the sky and try to study historical records of massive stars that failed to explode, but it's challenging because they just vanish without exploding, leaving a dark patch of sky," Legred concluded. "With this study, we show the gravitational-wave data are consistent with most binary black holes forming from a very special kind of failed supernova, but more research is needed.

"Black holes are born in some of the most extreme environments in the universe, so the prospect of better understanding their formation is very exciting."

The team's research was published on Tuesday (Nov. 10) in the journal Physical Review D.

What exactly are Flock cameras and how can you spot one?

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Flock cameras are everywhere, and they’re easier to miss than you think.

Basic descriptions of Flock cameras fall woefully short of their true capabilities and potential uses. Most people are familiar with the automated license plate reader (ALPR) version, usually pole-mounted and paired with a solar panel. If that were all there was to it, this would be a very short article. But Flock Safety (Flock Group Inc.) has become deeply intertwined with the defining terminology of ALPR cameras.

In truth, there are more than a dozen manufacturers and vendors of ALPRs; their products just aren't called Flock cameras, and each has its own aesthetic characteristics. For all intents and purposes, however, they are identical. If you see Motorola on the side of one, it likely does much of what a Flock does: read plates and log vehicles.

Most are vertically oval, narrow in width and fairly small, roughly the size of your hand. They're designed to capture your license plate and the time and place you passed by. Most are also accompanied by a solar panel. Leonardo and Genetec make the most visually distinct versions. Leonardo's are blockier, rectangular in shape, with some elongated versions. Genetec's are horizontally oval, with the same narrow width as Flock's variations, only flipped. Motorola manufactures a mix of the two.

The problem is that some Flock cameras can easily be mistaken for a standard traffic camera and vice versa. Because of this, it's difficult to accept the absolute accuracy of a project like DeFlock, which uses volunteers to identify Flock cameras visually. There's no centralized or formal verification system to independently confirm the sightings.

Flock also sells more than ALPRs. Depending on what the customer wants, the system can log details like roof racks on cars and bumper stickers. Some setups have microphones and others can recognize things like clothes and beards.

Flock cameras usually turn up where traffic funnels. They're often mounted on poles at intersections and busy traffic areas. Since many homeowners associations (HOAs) are major Flock camera customers, it shouldn't be a surprise to find them at neighborhood entrances. Shopping centers aren't off the list of potential locations either. The aesthetics aren't irrelevant, but since several manufacturers make them, there are a few other telltale signs to look for as well.

Flock cameras are meant to capture license plates, so look at where they're aimed. Check for Flock's bird logo on the camera and a solar panel above it. Because most are self-powered, there's usually little to no additional equipment nearby. Red light and speed cameras are typically larger and wired to traffic signals or enforcement poles. White, elongated cameras are another lookalike, but they mostly help traffic signals adjust to traffic flow. Some states also mount signs next to speed cameras so they're easy to identify.

Keep in mind that there are other variations of Flock cameras out there. The Flock Condor is a mounted sphere that rotates 360 degrees and tracks people. Naturally, these are mounted near areas with heavy pedestrian traffic. Flock also makes a mobile security trailer, a gunshot detector and a drone-as-first-responder. Because companies are using video integration tools to convert standard IP cameras into plate readers, it will be increasingly difficult to identify Flock or Flock-type cameras.

As a rule of thumb, if it's pointed at you, it's potentially watching you. But for a broader view, check the Atlas of Surveillance, a searchable database from the Electronic Frontier Foundation that shows what technology law enforcement agencies use. Search your town or county to see whether police are using plate readers.

While some services like MapQuest can help you find Flock cameras in your neighborhood, be wary of crowdsourced maps. DeFlock uses OpenStreetMap data collected from volunteers, but there's no official public list, so a missing pin doesn't mean there isn't a Flock camera sitting there.

Pins can also be wrong, since it's easy to confuse a Flock camera with the countless security and traffic camera variations. A Flock camera doesn't have to belong to a public agency. Flock and Flock competitors also sell to HOAs and retailers.

If you want to find every Flock camera in your city, county or state, you have to put in the work. Submit a public records request, attend the next city council meeting or ask your HOA and local businesses.

Fenway Sports Group – born around the Boston Red Sox and Liverpool FC – have developed a holding company with interests in media, marketing, real estate, and entertainment. Adding branches to a live Supabase app came down to a small number of database rules, applied in an order where each step could be checked and undone. The SQL was the easy part. The practical takeaway: write down your row counts before you start, and treat any count that changes unexpectedly as a stop sign, because under RLS a mistake doesn't throw an error. It just shows less data.

Is the Supabase publishable (anon) key safe to put in the browser? Yes, it's designed to be public, but only because Row Level Security decides what it can reach. With RLS off or a policy missing, anyone holding the key can read that table through the Data API. The secret (service_role) key bypasses RLS and must stay on the server.

Is region or branch isolation the same as multi-tenancy? No. Multi-tenancy separates different organisations that must never see each other's data. Branch isolation separates parts of one company, often with super-users who see everything and some shared data. Both use the same tools: an ID column, a membership table and RLS policies.

Do foreign keys bypass RLS in Supabase? Yes. PostgreSQL runs foreign key checks without applying row security, so a row can reference another branch's row if its ID is known. Including the branch column in the foreign key, (region_id, parent_id) → parent(region_id, id), keeps references inside one branch.

How do I test Supabase RLS policies? Act as a real user by switching to the authenticated role with that user's JWT claims, never as postgres or service_role. Try to read, insert, update, delete and link another branch's rows, and assert on row counts, because RLS hides rows without raising errors. Also confirm each user still sees their own data.

Can I add a region_id column to a live Supabase table safely? Adding a nullable column with no default only changes PostgreSQL's catalogue and doesn't rewrite the table, though it briefly takes an exclusive lock. Backfill existing rows next, then add NOT NULL, the foreign key and an index, which all validate the data and fail if anything was missed. Rehearse on a copy first and compare row counts before and after.

It's 6:40 pm. Your pull request is green on your laptop. You push, CI goes red, and the error says Cannot find module '@acme/core'. You didn't touch that import. Nobody did. You re-run the pipeline anyway, because hope is a strategy, and it fails again.

If you've got two or three packages sharing code in one repo, you've probably met some version of this. NPM workspaces are the built-in way to keep those packages consistent: one install, one lockfile, shared dependency versions, and one command that runs a script everywhere. But the feature has sharp edges that most tutorials skip.

Those are symlinks. Your workspace packages show up in the root node_modules as links to the real folders, so import "@acme/core" resolves to your local source instead of the registry. Edit core, and the app sees it. No npm link dance.

Under the hood. --workspaces is a config switch that tells the command to run in the context of all configured workspaces. Product said the app should only be available in the United States and Canada. The first instinct was to drop a country database onto every origin and start returning 403s. Then we remembered the Worker already has a country code on the request, for free, with no extra lookup.

A country gate is a coarse filter: it looks at the IP’s country, not at the person. Behind a known VPN network, a Tor exit or a privacy relay, the country you see is the egress, not the user’s. location.country_code can also be null, so every example below fails open when the country is unknown or the lookup errors.

Use the ISO 3166-1 alpha-2 code (US, CA, …), not the city. City-level data is a best-effort estimate; save it for soft decisions like defaults or language, not a hard 403.

This post starts with request.cf.country, then shows when to look the IP up so Nginx can enforce the same rule. The longer version is the guide to blocking countries by IP in Workers and Nginx.

For the current visitor, Workers already give you a country code on request.cf.country. If all you need is “allow the United States and Canada, otherwise 403”, use it. That is the right default, and it does not spend API quota.

Reach for GET /v0/ip/{ip} when Nginx at the origin has to enforce the same policy (there is no request.cf there), when you want one source of truth for Workers and origin, or when you’re classifying IPs that are not the current visitor (logs, webhooks). Country is part of geolocation, which is on the Free plan: 1,000 requests per UTC calendar month, 1 request/second, for personal and non-commercial use. Cache per IP or you’ll burn the quota on repeat visitors. Commercial use needs a paid plan; paid plans start at $9/month.

The country code, name, continent and is_eu fields are documented on the IP to Country API page.

Store the key with npx wrangler secret put NDL_API_KEY, not in wrangler.toml. The visitor IP is in CF-Connecting-IP. Cache the JSON per IP in the Cache API, keep a tight timeout and fall back to request.cf.country (or allow) if the call fails:

The Cache API is per data center and costs nothing extra. If you want one shared cache across locations, store the JSON in Workers KV with expirationTtl instead. Use request.cf.country when you only need the current visitor’s country at the edge; use the API when origin must apply the same rule, or when you’re looking up IPs that aren’t this request. The two can occasionally disagree, so pick one source for the gate and stick to it.

Nginx doesn’t have the visitor country on its own unless you add a geo module or a local database. A small sidecar keeps the policy in one place: Nginx asks it on each request via auth_request, and the sidecar looks up GET /v0/ip/{ip}, caches the result and returns 200 (allow) or 403 (deny). The sidecar must fail open too: API errors return 200, and if the sidecar itself is down, Nginx maps 502/503/504 to 200.

Pass a single client IP in X-Client-IP. If Nginx is at the edge, that’s $remote_addr. If Cloudflare is the only proxy in front of Nginx, use $http_cf_connecting_ip. Don’t parse raw X-Forwarded-For — the client can put any address there.

auth_request_set copies X-IP-Country from the sidecar onto $ip_country, so the origin still sees the country after an allow. Skip the gate on health checks and static assets if you can, so those paths never spend a lookup (the in-process cache already covers repeat IPs).

Cache per IP for a few hours in the sidecar and in the Worker. Keep timeouts tight (500–800 ms) and always fail open on errors. Country is on the Free plan, but 1,000 requests/month is not enough to look up every request without a cache. For log backfill, POST /v0/bulk (paid plans) accepts up to 100 IPs per request; each IP counts toward the monthly quota.

If the user is on a VPN, you get the egress country. is_vpn (known VPN networks), is_tor and is_relay (privacy relay) are paid-plan flags on the same response if you want to treat that traffic differently. They are not required for a basic country gate.

GSK won accelerated approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration on Tuesday for neladalkib, a lung cancer drug sold as Nelkarmi.

OHSAA public-private playoff split headed for statewide vote

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

The Suburban League, a conference of 16 schools in Northeast Ohio, has officially gotten the ball rolling on a public-private divide for high school playoffs.

The league submitted a petition for a membership vote on separate tournaments for public and private schools to the Ohio High School Athletic Association on Nov. 16. The petition received 152 signatures, well exceeding the required 75-signature threshold.

Now, the question of whether public and private schools should compete in separate postseason tournaments will go to a vote for OHSAA member institutions. The OHSAA's next referendum period begins in May 2027, with each high school voting on all ballot proposals from May 1 to May 15. There are 691 public-school and 121 private-school OHSAA members, each of whose principals cast a vote in the referendum period.

If approved in May, separate state tournaments would likely be implemented for the 2028-2029 school year. According to the National Federation of State High School Associations (NFHS), only Alabama, Maryland, Texas, and Virginia currently hold separate public and private high school state tournaments.

This isn't the first time the question of public vs. private athletics has been brought to the OHSAA's members for a vote. Petition attempts in 1978 and 1993 both failed, but renewed discussion over disproportionate private-school titles led to the development of the OHSAA's competitive balance plan in 2014.

OHSAA member schools adopted competitive balance measures beginning in 2017, placing schools in appropriate postseason divisions for baseball, basketball, football, soccer, softball, and volleyball.

The competitive balance formula looks at a school's enrollment and assigns each rostered player a tier based on considerations like which school district the student lives in or whether they come from an assigned feeder school. The OHSAA then adjusts the enrollment count accordingly, with more local players lowering the enrollment number and more out-of-district players raising it. That adjusted enrollment figure then determines the school's tournament division.

Competitive balance has had some success at bringing state titles closer to a proportionate public-private split. In football, for example, private schools won over 50% of the state championships in the 16 years before the plan's implementation. Since 2017, private schools have won just 20% of the football titles.

While nearly every sport has seen more public schools win championships since competitive balance was adopted, some sports still seem to favor private schools. Girls volleyball has hardly been affected as private schools have won over 64% of state titles since 2017, with three Greater Cincinnati-based private schools winning this year alone. Both boys soccer (48%) and girls soccer (42%) also see high rates of private schools titles.

In OHSAA fall 2026 state finals so far, public schools won four of seven titles in volleyball, four of five titles in boys soccer and three of five titles in girls soccer.

9 Classics Where the Audiobook Version Is Better

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Audiobooks were technically born as far back as 1877, with Thomas Edison's invention of the phonograph. However, it wasn't until 1932 that the American Foundation for the Blind officially named them "Talking Books," introducing them as a medium for visually impaired people to enjoy literature as an alternative to Braille. Throughout the entire 20th century, and even well into the 21st, many classics have been turned into audiobooks so phenomenal that they're arguably even better than the original book.

Audiobooks and physical books are, of course, vastly different media that fulfill vastly different purposes, so comparing them can feel a bit like comparing apples to oranges. Still, sometimes, an audiobook is so good that it becomes entirely reasonable to say it provides a better experience than the original. These are audiobooks that anyone who enjoys classic literature should consider essential listening material. "Classic" is defined for the purposes of this list as any book that was originally published before the 21st century.

Folkloric vampires predate Bram Stoker's Dracula by hundreds of years. Inspired by such stories, as well as by Irish folklore and historical figures like Vlad the Impaler, Stoker wrote the 1897 classic that is nowadays widely remembered as the most foundational pillar of modern vampiric fiction. It's undoubtedly one of the best horror books of all time, which made the announcement of an original Audible audiobook-slash-audio-drama all the more worthy of skepticism.

In the end, however, Bram Stoker's Dracula ended up being one of the greatest horror audiobooks that readers have been treated to in years. With a stacked cast that includes the likes of Ella Purnell, Jonathan Bailey, and Aimee Lou Wood, the audiobook takes the classic epistolary novel and makes it feel even more immersive, cinematic, and well-paced. It's worth noting that it's an abridged audio drama rather than a full reading of the original novel, which is not ideal, but those interested in such a concept should love it.

Originally serialized between 1875 and 1877, Russian author Leo Tolstoy's Anna Karenina is widely regarded as one of the most foundational pieces of world literature in history, a must-read masterpiece whose daunting 1,000-page length shouldn't deter anyone from considering it essential reading. Since it was one of her favorite books of all time, Maggie Gyllenhaal agreed to narrate an Audible original audiobook that really does justice to one of the best epic books of all time.

Tolstoy's novel was such a monumental artistic achievement that the author was notoriously drained and exhausted by the whole experience by the time he was done, and reading such a vast book can itself also be a massive undertaking. With the raw immediacy and many emotional variations of Gyllenhaal's narration, listening to Anna Karenina can be a far more engaging and far less tedious experience than many may initially think. At over 30 hours long, it's definitely a time commitment, but oh so worth every minute.

Upon publication back in 1997, J. K. Rowling's Harry Potter and the Philosopher's Stone (published in the US as Harry Potter and the Sorcerer's Stone) was an immediate commercial and critical hit, kicking off one of the biggest multimedia franchises in history. The book's first audiobook version came out in 1999, and the performers varied by region. Whereas Jim Dale narrated the book in the US and Canada, it was Stephen Fry's voice that was heard in the British and international releases. If there is any one Harry Potter book that's better heard than read, it's Fry's performance of Philosopher's Stone.

As a whole, this is one of the most essential fantasy books to read before kicking the bucket, but there's nothing quite like hearing it through Fry's rich, velvety baritone voice. On the page, Philosopher's Stone is beautifully imaginative and cozy, but at times feels like an overly simple debut novel in terms of Rowling's prose. Fry elevates every word and makes the entire story feel like a wonderful fairy tale being told by the fire on a cold weekend night outdoors.

One of Stephen King's first novels was 1983's Pet Sematary, published before he had fully established and cemented himself as the true King of Horror. Fans had been begging for an audiobook for years, and once the project finally materialized, it was Michael C. Hall of Dexter fame who publisher Simon & Schuster picked as the right voice to narrate the story. The result is one of the best Stephen King audiobooks to date, and considering just how many great ones there are, that's saying a lot.

Pet Sematary was already absolutely legendary well before 2018, widely regarded by many—including King himself—as the author's most genuinely terrifying work. However, once the horror aspects really pick up steam after a relatively slow-burning first few pages, Hall makes it hit even harder. Getting an actor well-known for projects as death-related as Six Feet Under and Dexter to narrate a horror novel all about death and grief was not only brilliant meta-casting, but also hugely beneficial for the story as a whole.

Multiple books are almost always brought up when the endless debate of what "The Great American Novel" is gets brought up, but John Steinbeck's East of Eden is the most noteworthy. This family saga was likely key to Steinbeck being awarded the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1962, and it more than lives up to its reputation as one of the most perfect American books ever written. The first audiobook version of the classic ever recorded was narrated by respected character actor Richard Poe.

East of Eden was already widely recognized as one of the most perfectly written masterpieces of all time, and it's definitely a must-read, but there's something about the way Poe complements the narrative that makes his version arguably even more unforgettable. Steinbeck wrote East of Eden in such a sweeping, almost Biblical voice that it almost feels like the narration of a legend, and Poe's resonant yet deeply human voice allows that narration style to feel all the more intimate and personal.

The book that earned Harper Lee a Pulitzer Prize was 1960's To Kill a Mockingbird, one of the most essential and widely discussed American classics of the 20th century. Though certain elements of its narrative are undeniably dated by modern standards, its heart will forever be in the right place, with its critiques of racial prejudice and systemic injustice being as timely and hard-hitting today as they were back in the '60s. In 2014, legendary actress Sissy Spacek was tasked with narrating the audiobook version of the story.

The reasons why this is one of those great books that are even better as audiobooks are aplenty, and even though the Pulitzer-winning original remains absolutely foundational, Spacek brings a level of nuance and authenticity to the book that feels like a much-welcome sense of modernity. The actress' beautifully melodic Texan drawl makes the small-town Alabama setting feel all the more evocative and atmospheric, and the way she flawlessly captures Scout's personality as both a child and an adult is incredible.

Stephen King's 22nd book was 1986's It, still remembered by many as the author's best and most iconic work to date. At over 1,100 pages in length, it's an absolute brick of a horror novel, but its epic scope still works marvelously all these many years later. To narrate the 2016 audiobook, Simon & Schuster decided to go for an actor who already had a pre-existing tie to the Stephen King universe: Steven Weber, who played Jack Torrance in the 1997 three-part miniseries version of The Shining.

It was already one of those horror books where every chapter is perfect—something quite admirable for such a thick novel—but Weber took that literary doorstop and somehow turned it into an even bigger, more immersive 45-hour juggernaut of an audiobook. The actor gives each of the Losers a distinct voice both in their child and adult versions, and he goes all in on Pennywise without ever teetering too far into distracting camp. Perfectly paced and intense to the core, It is a must-listen-to audiobook through and through.

American poet, essayist, and civil rights activist Maya Angelou was a towering figure in modern American literature, completely reshaping how Black American identity was expressed in the written word. The first of her seven published memoirs, 1969's I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings, broke new ground and became one of the first major autobiographies by an African American woman to achieve global bestseller status. When an audiobook version was released in 2011, it was Angelou herself who narrated it.

Taking one of the best memoirs of all time and turning it into an audiobook worthy of its legacy was always going to be a tough task, so who better to achieve it than the author herself? By having Angelou narrate her own story with her unmistakably theatrical contralto voice, I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings feels even more honest and intimate than it already does on the page, balancing poignancy and humor to absolute perfection.

Well before he wrote what many still consider the greatest and most influential fantasy epic of the 20th century, J. R. R. The CFTC has stayed certification of some Kalshi mention contracts, citing possible manipulation, two months after a staff advisory

Kalshi halted trading on several contracts centered on President Donald Trump's remarks late last week after the Commodity Futures Trading Commission asked it to, according to CNBC.

The paused contracts invite traders to bet on Trump's use of nicknames ahead of 2027, his word choices in November, and the countries and companies he mentions before this month closes, according to CNBC.

Brooke Nethercott, a CFTC spokesperson, told CNBC the agency has put a hold on certifying several of the mention markets Kalshi has proposed. She pointed to a contract on which key words Trump would utter ahead of a set deadline, one that Kalshi self-certified in February 2025.

"The CFTC stayed the certification of these contracts because they may be susceptible to manipulation, which is consistent with the staff advisory the CFTC put out two months ago," Nethercott said, according to CNBC.

Staff of the CFTC's Division of Market Oversight issued the advisory on Sept. 22. It covers contracts that settle on whether an individual will say certain words, attend an event or interact with another person. The staff said they may view such contracts as presumptively readily susceptible to manipulation and expect exchanges to make a heightened showing to list them. The advisory states it represents only the views of staff and does not create new obligations.

The advisory says those closest to the outcome often have advance knowledge, such as access to scripts or prepared remarks, and can be targeted by others seeking to influence the result.

Kalshi and the White House did not respond to CNBC's requests for comment.

The CFTC was looking into mention markets internally in August, according to CNBC. The agency asked Kalshi to pull its mention markets tied to sports; CNBC describes the company as the sole U.S.-regulated exchange that lists this kind of contract. Kalshi complied.

A mention market tied to Trump came under scrutiny in July, when a White House teleprompter operator was found to have traded on the president's comments, according to CNBC. In a settlement, the CFTC ordered the operator to stop trading for three years and pay a penalty of more than $172,000.

A mention market on what Vice President JD Vance will say at the New York Times DealBook Summit on Wednesday was active as of Tuesday morning, according to CNBC.

The failure of a Vulcan Centaur rocket carrying a full load of internet satellites for Amazon was caused by an anomaly in its first-stage booster, rocket maker United Launch Alliance said today (Nov. 17).

That failure — which led to the loss of 40 Amazon Leo internet satellites — was recognized about 10 minutes after liftoff, but the problems began much earlier than that, ULA said in a statement.

"While we appear to have lost telemetry and the mission at approximately 10 minutes into flight, early indications are that just after liftoff the launch vehicle experienced low booster performance, resulting in stage separation occurring when the vehicle was low in altitude and below minimal velocity to reach orbit," ULA wrote in today's update.

The two-stage Vulcan Centaur is the successor to ULA's workhorse Atlas V rocket, which will retire after just six more flights. Vulcan debuted in January 2024 and now has a total of five launches under its belt.

The new rocket has experienced problems on three of those missions. On both its second and fourth flights — which launched in October 2024 and February of this year, respectively — the nozzle of one of Vulcan's strap-on solid rocket boosters (SRBs) fell off about 30 seconds after liftoff.

On both occasions, the rocket overcame the problem and successfully delivered its payloads to orbit. However, after the second occurrence, the U.S. Space Force paused national-security launches with Vulcan until ULA addressed the SRB nozzle issue. (The February 2026 flight was a Space Force mission.)

That work led to "design and process improvements to the nozzle," ULA said in an Oct. 28 update. And those tweaks apparently worked.

"All indications are that the solid rocket booster performance was nominal, following nozzle improvements implemented for this launch," the company wrote in today's update.

Vulcan's Centaur V upper stage, which features two RL-10C engines, did its job as well "but was unable to recover from the [booster] performance loss, resulting in loss of mission," ULA added.

Vulcan's first-stage booster is powered by two BE-4 engines, which are built by Amazon founder Jeff Bezos' Blue Origin. Today's update did not identify the exact problem with the booster; doing so will require more time, as the investigation has just begun.

If that investigation finds a serious issue with the BE-4, there could be broad ripple effects across the American space program. After all, that engine also powers the first stage of Blue Origin's New Glenn rocket, which is a key piece of hardware for NASA's Artemis program of moon exploration. (And New Glenn is currently recovering from a failure of its own — a massive explosion on the test stand that occurred on May 28.)

NASA plans to integrate the Centaur V upper stage into its Space Launch System moon rocket, the vehicle that launches Artemis astronauts to space. So the fact that the Centaur performed nominally on Sunday is a bit of good news for the space agency.

Vulcan's grounding is a big deal, no matter what the anomaly investigation ends up finding. The U.S. doesn't have a surfeit of powerful rockets, after all, and two of them — Vulcan and New Glenn — are out of commission at the moment.

There's considerable pressure on both Vulcan and New Glenn to bounce back strong, for the nation faces an impending launch crunch in the next few years. SpaceX plans to retire its Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy rockets and shift its launch load over to the fully reusable Starship megarocket. SpaceX is expected to prioritize its own needs in Starship's early days, using the vehicle primarily to build out its next-gen Starlink broadband constellation and Starmind AI data center network.

Amazon has booked about 100 launches on a variety of rockets to build its constellation, which will eventually consist of more than 3,200 satellites. Thirty-eight of those bookings are for Vulcan; Sunday's launch was the first of those.

The other rockets Amazon has booked are the Atlas V, New Glenn, Falcon 9 and Arianespace's Ariane 6. About 400 operational Amazon Leo satellites have reached orbit to date across 14 launches (not including Sunday's failure).

Cologne Auction House Moves Forward with Sale of Gothic Paintings as Jewish Dealer’s Heirs Claim They Were ‘Stolen’

📅 Nov 17, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

The Cologne-based Lempertz auction house said it still planned to sell two works by Gothic painter Johann Koerbecke after the heirs of a German Jewish art dealer who was persecuted by the Nazis claimed the pieces were “stolen.”

Dealer Max Stern held the paintings in Düsseldorf between 1936 and 1937, the year the Nazis shut down his gallery. Per a provenance supplied by Lempertz, both paintings were then held by Düsseldorf’s Galerie Hans Bammann, which appears to have then sold them. On Monday, the Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation issued a release in which it called on Lempertz to stop the sale of the two works planned for Saturday. According to the foundation, such a sale should not occur “until two distinct ownership claims of different victims are justly resolved according to the Washington Principles on Nazi-Confiscated Art.”

Both paintings—The Annunciation and The Vision of Saint Bernard—are likely from Marienfeld high altarpiece, which was completed in 1457. They are being auctioned in a sale of Old Masters art. They carry high estimates of around 300,000 euros each, or about $329,000 apiece, making them the auction’s most expensive works. “For art historical reasons,” the Lempertz lot entries read, “the two important panels (Lots 1005 and 1006) will first be offered individually and then together.”

The Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation pointed out that both works appear in several databases of missing artworks, including the Interpol Database of Stolen Art since 2021. On the German Lost Art Database, both are reported as “Nazi-confiscated property”; the foundation registered that claim with the organization in 2018.

The foundation has demanded the return of multiple artworks belonging to Stern over the past decade, and in some cases, it has been successful. In 2017, for example, the FBI returned a painting by Jan Franse Verzijl that had been on view at an US art fair two years earlier.

Potentially complicating the matter is the fact that, prior to Stern’s possession of the Koerbecke paintings, they were owned by Ellen Funke, who, during the Nazi era, was classified as a Mischling, or “mongrel,” because she had Jewish ancestry through her mother. She sold her collection in 1936 to fund her emigration from Germany, and works she once owned have also been the subject of restitution claims—two of which happen to involve the Koerbecke works.

In 2025, Bavaria’s state museums restituted Koerbecke’s Vision of Saint Bernard to Funke’s heirs. At the time, the Bayerische Staatsgemäldesammlungen noted that The Vision of Saint Bernard and The Annunciation were at one point part of a single work, with both images appearing on either side of the same panel, and that Stern had taken them apart with the hope of selling them both. Funke’s heirs are listed as the sellers of The Vision of Saint Bernard on the auction house’s website.

Similarly, Funke’s heirs are also listed as the sellers of the The Annunciation, which entered the Kolumba Art Museum of the Archdiocese of Cologne as a donation in 1996. The museum restituted the work in 2025.

The Dr. Max and Iris Stern Foundation’s release notably does not mention Funke by name. But it does state that the foundation “maintains that the rights and histories of both Jewish victims must be taken into account, and neither claim should be privileged at the expense of the other.” In an email to ARTnews, a spokesperson for the foundation said “the museums restituted the paintings to Ellen Funke’s heirs in accordance with the priority principle set forth in the guidelines established in Germany to apply the Washington Principles,” adding that that restitution ”did not result in a ‘just and fair solution’ in accordance with the Washington Principles.”

In a statement to ARTnews, Lempertz said it would not cancel its planned sale of the paintings by Koerbecke, who is widely considered one of the great painters of the Gothic era in Germany.

“We adhere to current restitution practices and follow the recommendations of the Federal Government,” a representative for the auction house said, claiming that the Stern “had the panels sawn in two” and went on to sell one of them for “double the price.”

“These magnificent paintings were not taken away from Max Stern,” the Lempertz representative said, adding, “Max Stern received compensation after the war; the heirs of the Funke family did not.”

The Stern Foundation spokesperson said the “view that the paintings were not ‘seized’ from Max Stern has long been refuted,” citing a 2019 statement from the now-defunct German Advisory Commission that the persecution of Jewish art dealers began at latest by August 1933, “so that from that point on at the latest, it can in no way be assumed that ‘proper, customary business transactions’ took place.” The spokesperson added that the claim that Max Stern was compensated for the paintings is “incorrect“ and has “no legal basis.”

Lempertz did not dispute the listings for both paintings on the Art Loss Register, but the representative also noted that the listings contain details of the works’ respective restitutions.
