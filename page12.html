COP31: Climate deal 'not adopted' as EU, others reject draft declaration over fossil fuels omission

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

The summit was extended as nations worked to reach a deal, but they couldn't agree on including fossil fuels. European nations, small island states and activists have criticized oil-producing countries for the impasse.

Diplomats and delegates from nearly 200 countries are meeting in Antalya, Turkey

The summit's president acknowledges that a deal was 'not adopted'

The final declaration omits any mention of fossil fuels

The EU says it cannot accept a deal that 'greenwashes fossil fuels'

Fossil fuels are a major flashpoint at this year's summit, which comes amid soaring energy prices and tensions over the US-Israeli war with Iran

These are the major developments from the closing phase of the UN's COP31 Climate Summit in Antalya, Turkey, on Friday, November 20 through Sunday, November 22:

The COP31 climate summit has failed to agree on a final joint declaration.

During a plenary session, the summit's Turkish president acknowledged there would be no climate deal.

"I understand that there is no agreement on this text. For that reason, these decisions have not been adopted," Murat Kurum, who is also Turkey's environment minister, told a closing plenary in the Mediterranean city of Antalya.

The rift centered on whether to include the phaseout of fossil fuels in the final declaration. Scientists say the burning of fossil fuels such as coal, oil and gas is the main cause of global warming.

European nations, smaller island states and climate activists have criticized oil-producing countries for the omission.

Several nations continued to raise serious objections to the draft.

Antwi-Boasiako Amoah, chair of the African Group of Negotiators, took a shot at the presidency for how it has handled the negotiations.

"With all due respect Mr. President, you have not shown respect to a group of 54 countries (from Africa) and my country (Ghana) for that matter," Amoah said.

Panama's representative said the Central American nation was amongst those who suffer most from the consequences and issue of climate justice.

Panamanian Environment Minister Juan Carlos Navarro said the talks were being held hostage by a "petroleum mafia" and need to be reformed.

He said science must be the basis for the cover decision, adding that those who contributed to this crisis must shoulder the responsibility to deal with it.

"We are running out of time," Navarro told attendees. "Panama cannot support this decision. The planet cannot continue to wait."

Ireland's Climate Minister Darragh O'Brien has said the European Union is "profoundly disappointed" with the current draft deal on the table at COP31 because it "fails to meet the moment" and that the bloc will not agree to it.

"We will not endorse a cover decision that does not respond to the science, that disregards the implementation gap, and risks deepening the chasm between global commitments and the action required to correct the overshoot and bring 1.5°C within reach," O'Brien said.

"We will not endorse a cover decision that does not even mention fossil fuels, the driver of climate change. And we will not accept negotiating backward in time. And we will not accept a COP that greenwashes fossil fuels," he added.

Former Greenpeace executive Jennifer Morgan said it would be better to end the COP31 summit with no deal than a watered-down declaration.

Her remarks come amid deep divisions over the omission of fossil fuels from the draft declaration.

Morgan rejected the notion that it would be better to accept the current draft than have no resolution at all, as the Turkish COP president has suggested.

"I think this is the day for the first time in my 31 COPs that I thought it was better not to have a decision that sets us back, that goes against what small islands and vulnerable people are saying," Morgan added.

She praised Germany for standing up to pressures to accept the current deal.

"Of course it would have been better to have a strong outcome here with everyone moving forward, but I agree with the German minister who just said if the text stays the way that it is, then it should not be accepted," Morgan said.

The US climate activist blamed oil-producing nations for the impasse.

"Saudi Arabia's fingerprints are all over" the current draft proposal, she said.

The omission has created a deadlock at COP31, which was due to conclude on Friday but has been extended as delegates seek consensus.

Morgan also blamed this year's Turkish presidency for failing to bring everybody together and instead letting countries that rely on fossil fuels dictate the terms of the agreement.

"Last year we had Brazil with a very strong presidency. They worked all year to bring countries together and they managed. It was not easy last year," Morgan said.

"This year we had presidencies that, in my view, were not well prepared. They did not bring countries together  (...) it's been shuttle diplomacy with no text for four days. That's not the way you get an agreement here," she added.

German Environment Minister Carsten Schneider has said the latest version of the draft agreement is "not acceptable."

Schneider said that the draft decision "not only fails to provide an adequate response but actually takes a step backward. A step backward from previous decisions on climate protection is the last thing Germany can support."

Schneider said that delegates had been working hard for days to reach a unanimous decision on "the world's greatest challenge: human-induced climate change," noting that this summer had "shown that the nature of this warming not only costs lives — as in Nepal — but also changes our world for the worse."

"It is up to us, as humanity, to do something about it. That is why a conference dedicated to climate protection — whose sole purpose is to protect the climate — must send a clear signal that we are taking action against this threat."

Schneider said he still believed most nations are willing to work to enact climate protection, but singled out those who still rely on fossil fuels to power their economies.

"The overwhelming majority of nations are committed to climate protection. Some, however, are still making huge profits from oil and gas and are unwilling to scale back their production or to pioneer a clear roadmap for reducing greenhouse gas emissions in this area," he added.

France has called a new draft deal proposed at COP31 a "backward" step.

French Ecological Transition Minister Monique Barbut criticized the deal for failing to mention fossil fuels, casting fresh uncertainty over whether nations will be able to reach an agreement.

"Time is ticking and we are not there yet," French Ecological Transition Minister Monique Barbut said in a statement.

COP31 President Murat Kurum has expressed his frustration over the deadlock at the summit.

The latest version of the draft deal again fails to mention fossil fuels, the main cause of global warming.

Over 40 hours after its scheduled conclusion and with no compromise in sight, the summit risks losing the numbers required to make any decision as delegates leave for home.

"If it is not adopted, I believe those who push the COP process into a deadlock will need to reflect on their position," Kurum said.

"It is our wish that this resolution be passed. We shall shortly be expressing our determination to ensure that this resolution is passed at the general meeting," he said.

"It is up to the parties involved. If there is sincerity here, and if there is a genuine desire to get things done, I believe that this can be achieved in this chamber. We shall see about that in a moment," Kurum added.

The third draft makes no mention of the words  "fossil," "oil," "coal," or "phase out."

However, as in the second draft, the latest version retains a goal to boost electricity's share of global energy use to 35% by 2035, as well as efforts to expand the use of "clean energy."

The draft text also does not stipulate that the "35-by-35" electrification target should be met using renewable energy sources.

It also urges countries to "accelerate" carbon capture and storage — a controversial technology backed by the fossil fuel industry.

The latest, and potentially final, version of the deal still makes no explicit mention of fossil fuels.

It does refer to a 2023 agreement that committed the world to transitioning away from fossil fuels — but stops short of naming them directly.

The third and presumably final draft text to emerge from negotiations at COP31, which lacks any mention of fossil fuels or how to transition away from them, has been rejected by NGOs.

In a statement, Teresa Anderson, Climate Justice Lead at ActionAid International, said the people who will benefit most from the new "35-by-35" electrification targets and carbon capture "are the fossil fuel, nuclear, data and CCUS (carbon capture, use and storage) industries.

"This text will help elites to profit while leaving people in the dark," Anderson said.

Brandon Wu, AAUSA's Director of Policy and Campaigns, said: "Rich countries came to the COP31 talks with nothing to offer other than red lines.

"They put forward meaningless fossil fuel transition roadmaps that were nothing more than restatements of inadequate existing policy, then hypocritically blamed developing countries for failing to take action on phasing out fossil fuels.

“This is the behaviour of an arrogant bully who cares about nothing but himself and it is no way to have a proper negotiation. COP31 was one of the worst COPs ever, and it’s because the EU and other developed countries came with zero spirit of solidarity, cooperation or compromise."

In the early hours of Sunday, COP31 President and Turkish Environment Minister Murat Kurum informed delegates at a plenary that a third and "final" text would be sent to them for review.

He added that negotiators would have until 6:00 am (0400 GMT) "to come to us with agreement on the text" and that a closing plenary would then be convened at 9:00 am local time.

The shift to green tech is already in full swing, including electric cars, electric buses, solar panels and heat pumps. But what is so electrifying about the latest wave of electrification?

Harjeet Singh, the global convener of the Fill the Fund campaign and strategic advisor to the Fossil Fuel Treaty Initiative, said that COP31 could be a failure due to a lack of funding.

"COP31 is stuck for one simple reason: money. Europe and other rich countries came to Antalya with empty pockets," he wrote in a statement shared with multiple reporters.

"They've spent two weeks pushing electrification targets and demanding more ambition from developing countries, while refusing to pay for any of it," he added.

Singh accused wealthy countries of blocking a just transition mechanism and of ignoring calls to increase funding for the Adaptation Fund and the Loss and Damage Fund.

"You can't ask developing countries to transform their economies while they're drowning in disasters and debt," he wrote.

The climate activist stressed that the reason this COP failed wasn't a lack of solutions. It was due to "a lack of money and a lack of will from those who caused this crisis," he said.

The 8 Best Miniseries of the Streaming Era, Ranked

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

We are living in the streaming era of television. Though Netflix launched streaming video-on-demand as far back as 2007, they didn't produce their first original series, Lilyhammer, until 2012; and it wouldn't be until 2013, with the release of shows like House of Cards and Organge Is the New Black, that streaming would truly gain cultural hegemony over the entire televisual medium. Gone was the era of network television, and in came the era of streaming.

Miniseries in particular find their roots in the British serials of the 1950s, but it was when American television adapted the format in the 1970s that it really became a cultural phenomenon. From classics like Roots to 21st-century masterpieces like Band of Brothers, the era of network TV delivered many a great miniseries. The streaming era of television has definitely not fallen behind, though, giving us in the likes of Adolescence and xxx some of the greatest miniseries of all time. Only streaming originals can qualify for this list.

After actor Stephen Graham read a news article about a young boy stabbing a girl to death, he proposed telling a story inspired by this wave of violent attacks by young boys against girls in Great Britain through the miniseries format. He and Jack Thorne then co-created Adolescence, a scathing critique of incel culture, male rage, toxic masculinity, and the "manosphere." It's an awfully timely yet sensitive topic, but the series treats it with precisely the care it deserved.

The result is one of the best miniseries of the last five years, composed of four episodes all shot in a single take, which lends the series an air of urgency and raw authenticity that elevates both its emotional and intellectual impacts tremendously. Bolstered by its exceptional cast (including Graham himself and a revelatory Emmy-winning turn by young Owen Cooper) and its excellent writing, it's one of the most important crime drama series of the 2020s.

Beth Macy's non-fiction masterpiece Dopesick: Dealers, Doctors, and the Drug Company that Addicted America deals with the origin and evolution of the opioid epidemic that began in the United States around 1996 with the release of OxyContin. In 2021, Hulu adapted that story in Dopesick, which just so happens to be one of the most perfect miniseries of the last 25 years. It's every bit as ambitious, expansive, and insightful as the book it's based on.

It's such a devastating investigative drama that it's most definitely not an easy watch, but boy, is it an important one. It happens every December. You bring home a full, bright red poinsettia, set it on the table, and a week or two later the colorful bracts start to curl and fall. By Christmas morning, the plant looks tired and half bare.

Some of that can be blamed on poinsettia care after you get home, like a cold windowsill or a pot left sitting in water. Much of it starts at the store, though. The plant was already on its way out before it ever went in the cart.

There's an easy way to tell a fresh one from a tired one, and hardly anybody looks. Forget the red for a second. Down in the center of each cluster of colored leaves are some tiny yellow-green buds, and those say a lot more about how long the color will hold up. It's kind of a strange thing to judge a plant by, but trust the process.

Those colorful "petals" on a poinsettia aren't petals at all. They're bracts, and they color up in fall as the nights get longer. Right in the middle sit the real flowers, small knobby clusters called cyathia. Each one is a little cup holding a single female flower and several male flowers, often with a yellow gland on its rim.

Cyathia go through the same cycle on every plant. They start out tight and green or red-tipped, open to shed yellow pollen, and then drop off. After the cyathia fall, the colored bracts usually follow before long.

Some of the newer poinsettia varieties hang on to their cyathia longer. So it isn't a sure thing, but it's still one of the better clues you get in the store. Any color works for this check, whether you're eyeing red or the pink poinsettias at Walmart (which come in a four-pack for maximum festive joy).

Bend down and look closely at the center of a few bracts. Plants on the same table can be at different stages, so check more than one. Ideally, the cyathia are mostly closed, green or tipped with red, and fresh-looking. Some yellow showing is fine on its own. That's often just the gland on the side of the cup – it doesn't mean the flower has opened.

Now compare that to a plant where the buds have split open and yellow pollen is dusting the bracts below them. Classic red poinsettias come in a four-pack from Costa Farms, and you can order them on Amazon – and the same quick look at the center works on a plant that shows up at the door.

The green leaves under the bracts deserve a look too. Check for yellow leaves low on the stems, or bare spots where leaves have already dropped. Cold, poor light, and watering trouble can all cause that. Pass on a plant that droops or looks wilted, along with any that have torn, bruised, or creased bracts. Flip a few leaves over as well. Whiteflies, such as the silverleaf whitefly (Bemisia tabaci), are common on poinsettias, and a puff of tiny white insects lifting off when you brush the plant is a bad sign.

Lift the pot if you can. Soggy soil in a heavy pot may mean it's been sitting in water inside its foil, and roots kept that wet can rot. Pay attention to where the plants are displayed, too. The journey home is where poinsettias tend to get hurt. When it's below 50°F (10°C) outside, sleeve or wrap the plant before it leaves the store, even for the short walk to the car. Just a few minutes of cold air can damage the bracts and leaves, and the damage may not show right away. Save the garden center for your last stop so the plant isn't stuck in a cold car while you're in other stores, and warm the car up first. Once you're inside, get the sleeve off pretty quickly.

“Whoever runs Nvidia next will inherit a narrower, more closely supervised international business and the complete reorganization of the AI equilibrium that comes with it,” Birmingham said.

Export restrictions are also just one piece of the puzzle Nvidia needs to solve. The company has to contend with the financial structure supporting unprecedented expansion of AI infrastructure, according to Birmingham.

The concern is that demand for Nvidia’s processors are tied to the ability of hyperscalers and smaller cloud providers to finance enormous data center investments. But that creates a fragile relationship between Nvidia’s hardware sales and the financial health of its customers, Birmingham said.

“Given the AI ecosystem is being heavily supported by vendor financing and debt markets, the potential for a telecom style bust is significant,” he warned.

Unlike established hyperscalers, neocloud providers have less flexibility to absorb rising hardware and financing costs, Birmingham said.

“They need near-perfect utilization to stay profitable,” he added. “In general, they can only pass along higher costs to new contracts, and only when compute stays scarce.”

This means if demand weakens, neocloud providers could find themselves paying for expensive computing infrastructure without being able to generate the revenue to service their debt.

“Investors are assessing whether the AI supply cycle is running too hot, and if a break point in compute demand will force a pullback in datacenter build out that becomes self-reinforcing across the system,” he explained.

Americans are paying $290 to meet strangers as friendship apps spread to more than 200 cities

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Startups are betting on America’s loneliness epidemic, and Americans are buying in. Apps such as 222, Real Roots, and Timeleft are turning friend-matching events into a growing consumer market, and some users pay hundreds of dollars to make new friends offline.

Alexandra Pitzer paid $290 through Real Roots last year to meet a group of strangers, The New York Times reported. She had just moved from Nashville to Honolulu for her husband’s Army job and was looking for connection. The app placed her at a brewery with eight other women.

“I can see most of them being lifelong friends,” Pitzer told the Times. “We have a genuine connection.”

The business is working, at least for now: each of the three companies brings in millions of dollars in revenue a year, according to the Times. But the model carries an unusual tension. The better it works, the less its customers may need it.

These companies are tapping into demand for in-person connection at a time when many people struggle to build relationships in a digital age. Rather than meeting people the old-fashioned way, users are matched with strangers based on shared interests and personality, then sent to curated events.

222 began as a college research project. Its co-founders later started hosting dinners in their backyard, and the project became a full-time business in 2023, according to the Times.

The company has since raised $13.7 million in venture capital, according to Business Insider, including $10.1 million in 2025. It uses machine-learning models trained on users’ feedback after each meetup to decide who should meet whom.

“I think our generation craves the in-real-life environment of meeting each other in a more intimate setting, without digital preconceptions,” Arman Roshannai, one of 222’s co-founders, told the Times.

222 charges $22 a month to match users and curate events, before the cost of the activity itself, such as a yoga class or a meal. Interested in dinner and collage-making? That will be $38, not including food and drinks.

The company now operates in 21 cities, employs 27 people and hosts at least one experience a day. Its co-founders told the Times that revenue tripled last year and is on track to quadruple this year, into the high seven figures.

Competitors are scaling, too. Real Roots, which coordinates activities for groups of women, is in 120 cities, while Timeleft, which matches strangers for dinners, is in more than 200. Newer entrants keep arriving, including the professional-networking app Clockout, whose founder raised $3.5 million to help Gen Z feel less alone.

All of them are competing for users who want a genuine relationship beyond social media. Ironically, building these friendships starts with an app.

About 21% of U.S. adults reported feeling lonely in a May 2024 survey by Making Caring Common, a project of the Harvard Graduate School of Education. Among those lonely adults, 81% reported anxiety or depression.

Sixty-five percent of lonely respondents said they felt fundamentally disconnected from others or the world. And when all respondents were asked what contributes to loneliness in America, technology topped the list, cited by 73%.

Others pointed to families not spending enough time together, people working too much and mental health challenges that strain relationships.

Founders in the space argue that this gap is a market in its own right. As the co-founder of group-travel startup WeRoad argued in a Fortune commentary, people who haven’t found meaningful connection online are now willing to pay for it in person.

Turning friendship into a business doesn’t guarantee meaningful connections. Some users have complained on social media that they didn’t click with their group or that the experiences felt awkward and forced, the Times reported.

There is also a larger question about the sustainability of the business model. If the product works and customers develop genuine friendships, they may no longer need the service.

That creates an unusual challenge for companies built around solving loneliness. Their success hinges on helping people establish relationships, and every relationship that sticks could mean one fewer customer coming back.

The companies appear to be designing around that problem. If a Real Roots group gets along, its members can pay the company to keep arranging meetups for the same group in the weeks that follow. Business Insider reported that 222 is building tools that help users plan their next meetup together and says its users are split roughly evenly between those looking for friends and those open to romance.

For investors, the question isn’t simply whether Americans will pay to make friends. It is whether friendship can be a durable business without turning human connection into a subscription.

From Diego Forlan’s unforgettable double to Juan Mata’s spectacular volley, Manchester United have enjoyed some memorable victories at Anfield over the years.

With Mauricio Pochettino preparing for his first match in charge against Liverpool on Sunday, we look back at five occasions when United emerged victorious from one of English football’s fiercest fixtures.

United travelled to Anfield on 19 April 1997 knowing Liverpool could overtake them at the top of the Premier League with a victory. Instead, centre-back Gary Pallister scored twice with headers from David Beckham deliveries, either side of John Barnes’s equaliser.

Andy Cole added a third after the interval, heading home from Gary Neville’s cross following an error by Liverpool goalkeeper David James. The victory helped Sir Alex Ferguson’s side take a decisive step towards retaining the Premier League title, which they secured the following month.

Fresh from winning the Treble, United returned to Anfield for a remarkable match in September 1999. Jamie Carragher headed into his own net inside three minutes before Andy Cole doubled United’s advantage.

Sami Hyypia pulled a goal back for Liverpool, but Carragher’s second own goal restored the visitors’ two-goal lead. Patrik Berger reduced the deficit again, and United faced a difficult finish after Cole was sent off for a second bookable offence. Despite playing the closing stages with 10 men, Ferguson’s side held on for a 3-2 victory.

Diego Forlan had endured a frustrating start to his United career before producing a performance that supporters would remember for years. The Uruguayan opened the scoring after Liverpool goalkeeper Jerzy Dudek failed to gather the ball, allowing him to finish from close range.

Forlan added a second just minutes later, beating Dudek at his near post to put United in control. Sami Hyypia pulled one back for Liverpool, but Ferguson’s side held on for a victory that proved valuable in their successful pursuit of the 2002-03 Premier League title. Forlan’s double also inspired a terrace song that remains familiar to United supporters.

Few United victories at Anfield have been as dramatic as the one secured by John O’Shea in March 2007. Liverpool had enjoyed the better opportunities, and United’s task became harder when Paul Scholes was sent off late in the second half.

With a goalless draw appearing increasingly likely, Cristiano Ronaldo’s free-kick was parried by goalkeeper Pepe Reina. Substitute O’Shea reacted quickest, firing the rebound high into the net in stoppage time. The unexpected winner gave United three valuable points on their way to reclaiming the Premier League title.

Juan Mata produced one of the most memorable individual performances in this fixture during Louis van Gaal’s first season as United manager. The Spaniard opened the scoring after 14 minutes, finishing a move involving Ander Herrera, before doubling United’s lead with a spectacular scissor-kick volley after half-time.

Liverpool captain Steven Gerrard had been sent off just 38 seconds after coming on as a substitute at the interval. Daniel Sturridge reduced the deficit, while Wayne Rooney missed a late penalty, but United held on for a 2-1 victory. Mata’s second goal remains one of the outstanding moments of United’s Premier League visits to Anfield.

Those five victories underline the different ways United have succeeded at Anfield, from outstanding individual performances to late winners and determined defensive displays. Pochettino now has an opportunity to begin his managerial reign with a memorable result against United’s biggest rivals.

Scientists Discover That a Simple Change in Walking Habits Could Improve Blood Sugar Control

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Scientists have discovered that a simple change in how daily exercise is distributed could improve blood sugar control, particularly in people at risk of type 2 diabetes.

How you distribute exercise throughout the day may influence how well your body regulates blood sugar. A randomized trial published in Obesity found that frequent, short walks improved several measures of glucose regulation more than completing the same amount of walking in one continuous session.

The differences emerged after six weeks, despite both groups performing similar amounts of physical activity and burning comparable amounts of energy.

Researchers recruited 30 inactive adults with overweight or obesity who reported sitting for more than six hours daily. Participants were assigned to complete 45 minutes of brisk walking five days per week for six weeks, either in one continuous session or in approximately five-minute breaks spread throughout the day.

Continuous glucose monitoring showed that the short-walk group increased its time within the target blood sugar range from 82% to 94%. In contrast, the continuous-walking group experienced a decline from 89% to 80%.

Participants taking frequent walking breaks also had more stable glucose levels, largely because they experienced fewer unusually low readings rather than lower average or peak blood sugar levels.

Laboratory tests showed that participants who took frequent walking breaks experienced more favorable changes in glucose tolerance, indicating differences in how their bodies cleared glucose from the bloodstream. However, neither approach produced a detectable improvement in insulin sensitivity.

Researchers suggest that repeated muscle activity may influence glucose uptake and utilization, although the exact mechanisms remain uncertain.

“Our findings suggest that how we distribute physical activity throughout the day may be just as important as how much we do,” said corresponding author Audrey Bergouignan, PhD, Director of Research at the French National Centre for Scientific Research (CNRS) and Associate Professor at the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus. “Rather than relying solely on a single exercise session, regularly interrupting prolonged sitting with short walks may offer additional benefits for blood glucose regulation, especially in people at risk of developing type 2 diabetes.”

The findings suggest that interrupting prolonged sitting may offer glucose regulation benefits beyond those associated with total exercise duration.

However, the trial was small and lasted only six weeks. It also lacked a non-exercise control group, and participants’ diets were not controlled during glucose monitoring. Further research is needed to determine whether these improvements translate into long-term health benefits, particularly for people with prediabetes or type 2 diabetes.

Netflix shares have fallen nearly 47% from their 52-week high. Is this a rare opportunity to buy a highly profitable entertainment business at a reasonable valuation?

By Lawrence Nga – Nov 22, 2026 at 7:15AM EST

Netflix (NFLX -2.96%) has had a difficult year. Its shares closed at $62.15 on Nov. 13, nearly 47% below their 52-week high. Yet the streaming giant continues to generate billions of dollars in revenue and profits. The sell-off raises an interesting question: Has Netflix become a bargain, or are investors right to worry about its future growth?

I cautiously lean toward the bull case. Netflix remains a highly profitable business with several ways to grow earnings over the next five years. However, its slowing growth and advertising challenges mean investors shouldn't expect an immediate recovery.

Netflix's ability to turn revenue into profits has improved over time.

In the third quarter of 2026, revenue increased 11.8% year over year to $12.87 billion. Operating income grew much faster, rising 37% to $4.46 billion. Its operating margin also improved to 34.6%, compared with 28.2% a year earlier.

In simple terms, Netflix is keeping more money from every dollar of revenue.

Management expects full-year revenue of $51.1 billion and operating income growth of more than 20% in 2026. It also expects its operating margin to reach 31.5%, up from 29.5% in 2025.

While operating leverage contributed to the expansion of operating margin, Netflix has also demonstrated pricing power. For instance, management said customers responded to its 2026 subscription price increases as expected, while customer cancellations improved year over year across every region.

Advertising offers another opportunity. Netflix expects advertising revenue to reach approximately $2.7 billion in 2026, despite delays in launching some advertising products. Management expects growth of more than 80% this year.

If Netflix can improve its advertising technology and earn more from its existing audience, advertising could become another meaningful contributor to future profits.

The biggest concern is that Netflix's growth is becoming harder to sustain. For perspective, revenue growth declined from 17.6% in the fourth quarter of 2025 to 11.8% in the third quarter of 2026. Management expects 11.5% growth in the fourth quarter.

That's still healthy, but investors have become accustomed to high growth. As Netflix grows, attracting new customers and raising subscription prices become more challenging.

Competition is another concern. Alphabet’s YouTube and TikTok compete for people's attention, while Amazon and other streaming services continue investing in entertainment. Netflix must keep producing popular shows and films to remain relevant.

Management has acknowledged that its execution could improve, particularly in the United States. Recent changes to its content leadership suggest it wants to strengthen its entertainment offering.

Advertising is also taking longer to develop than expected. Netflix reduced its 2026 advertising revenue forecast from $3 billion to $2.7 billion because some advertising products were delayed in launching.

These problems don't threaten Netflix's business today. But they could limit how quickly it grows over the next several years.

While nobody likes a declining share price, Netflix's valuation has become much more reasonable as a result.

At $62.15, its market capitalization is approximately $255 billion. Its price-to-earnings (P/E ratio) is around 17.7 times, compared with a much higher valuation in previous years. For instance, the stock traded at a P/E ratio above 30 just a year ago.

If Netflix can sustain or even improve its growth rate from here on, the stock's valuation looks rather attractive at the current level. Of course, if growth continues to slow further, the opposite is true as well.

On balance, investors may consider buying Netflix at today's prices, particularly those with a five-year investment horizon. The company has improving profit margins, a global audience, and opportunities to grow its advertising business.

But I'd build a position gradually rather than invest everything at once. The next few quarters should reveal whether Netflix can maintain its profitability while stabilizing revenue growth and improving advertising execution.

Netflix doesn't need to return to its previous growth rates to reward shareholders. It simply needs to keep growing profits at a healthy pace.

GLP-1 drugs could have a surprisingly large environmental payoff

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

GLP-1 drugs could cut food demand enough to free farmland larger than Italy.

GLP-1 weight-loss medicines such as Ozempic and Wegovy could affect much more than the waistlines of the people who take them. If widely used, our new research suggests these appetite-suppressing medicines could transform the global food system and free up vast areas of agricultural land.

The global food system is ultimately driven by demand. If people eat less, producers respond, affecting production, prices, trade, and how much land is needed. We used a powerful computer simulation to explore scenarios ranging from GLP-1 use concentrated in high-income countries to widespread global access.

With widespread use of these medicines among adults living with obesity, we estimate that reduced calorie intake could prevent up to 6.6 million deaths each year by 2050, while freeing up as much as 32 million hectares of agricultural land—an area slightly larger than Italy—for other uses.

The biggest effects, however, occurred when reduced calorie intake was combined with a shift toward healthier diets. In this scenario, up to 7 million deaths could be avoided each year by 2050, while around 46 million hectares of agricultural land would have become available for other uses. This suggests that alongside weight-loss medicines, we need to make healthier options more widely available, affordable, and more appealing.

Food systems are interconnected across countries. If demand falls in one part of the world, this can affect farming, trade, and food prices elsewhere, with consequences for people who are not taking the medications at all.

Lower demand from GLP-1 users, for instance, could make food cheaper. That sounds positive, but cheaper food could also encourage greater consumption among non-users, offsetting some of the intended benefit. The effects of widespread GLP-1 use, in other words, cannot be understood just by looking at individual patients’ weight loss. They ripple through the global food system.

It’s tempting to read these findings as an argument for putting more people on weight-loss medication. That would miss the bigger picture.

Obesity has often been framed as a “lifestyle disease,” blaming individual people for not having enough willpower. But diets are shaped by what food is available, what it costs, how it’s marketed, where shops are located, and what’s most convenient. In many high-income countries, it is remarkably easy to consume large amounts of cheap, energy-dense food. Society tells people to make healthier choices while surrounding them with an environment that often encourages the opposite.

The UK government-commissioned national food strategy, finalized in 2021, made this point clearly: improving diets can’t simply be about asking individuals to choose better. The wider food environment needs to change to make the better choice the easy one.

Our findings shouldn’t be taken to mean that people living with obesity have a responsibility to lose weight to reduce the environmental impacts of food production. Rather, our research examines the wider consequences that changes in food demand could have across the food system.

A healthier diet is not necessarily just a diet with fewer calories. It could mean more fruit, vegetables, and other nutritious foods, alongside less excessive consumption of foods linked to poor health. These changes affect not only human health but also agriculture, land use, and the environment.

Rather than asking whether GLP-1 medications or healthier diets are the answer, let’s ask how they can work together. Imagine a food system where healthier foods are affordable and accessible, unhealthy options aren’t relentlessly promoted, agricultural policy supports nutritious and sustainable production, and people who need medical treatment can access it. That combination could be far more powerful than any single intervention.

GLP-1 medicines can play an important role in tackling obesity, but they shouldn’t become a technological fix that allows the wider food system to escape scrutiny. They can help people eat less in an unhealthy food environment, but the long-term goal should be to make it easier and more affordable to have a healthier, more sustainable diet.

The remarkable thing about GLP-1 medications may not be that they change how much food people eat. It may be that they show us, with new clarity, just how closely human health, food production, and environmental health are connected, and how much could be gained if we tackled all three together.

What's the coolest automotive museum you've ever seen? What about the most mind-blowing piece of automotive history you've ever beheld with your own eyes? I live right down the street from the National Automotive Museum in Reno, NV, which is a great time, but it doesn't quite take the cake. There are some real gems out there, like the Gilmore Car Museum, which lets you drive a Model T, or the Studebaker National Museum, where you can watch footage from 1932 of grown men throwing cars down a hill for safety testing. But if I had a time machine and could visit any car museum anywhere in time and space, there's one in particular that I'd just love to see.

That would be the Schlumpf collection in Mulhouse, France, specifically during the two-year period in the mid-to-late-70s when it was run by laid-off textile workers after their bosses shut their factories down and fled the country. Yes, the story is as wild as it sounds.

Now housed in the Musée National de l'Automobile in Mulhouse, the Schlumpf collection is an astounding piece of both car collecting and labor history. It began as the personal (and, sure, slightly obsessive) collection of Fritz Schlumpf, a long-time Bugatti driver who, along with his brother Hans, ran a textile business with factories in Mulhouse. Born around 1906, Fritz Schlumpf began collecting Bugattis intentionally in the 1960s. He hunted them down with the help of the British Bugatti Owners Club, who helped him locate around 50 individual cars, plus a stash of 30 vintage Bugattis owned by John W. Shakespeare of Hoffman, Illinois. Over two years, Schlumpf and Shakespeare negotiated a purchase price, bringing Schlumpf's personal Bugatti collection up to 80. Then he bought 18 more off Ettore Bugatti himself. Schlumpf was only getting started.

Eventually, Fritz Schlumpf owned an estimated 105 Bugattis, which might've comprised as much as 20% of the Bugattis in existence at the time. Who can blame Schlumpf for apparently wanting them all? After all, Bugattis do drive like a dream, even the old ones. But Schlumpf hadn't just been buying up cars of his favorite French brand. He'd actually been amassing a mind-bogglingly large stable of rare cars, both vintage and contemporary.

By the 1970s, Schlumpf had over 400 cars stashed in an unused factory in Mulhouse that was being fitted into a museum. But in order to acquire them all during a worsening economy, he'd spent money from a government grant intended to keep his factories in operation. When his transgression was discovered, he promptly shut down all his businesses and fled to Switzerland, putting about 1,800 workers out of business.

In response, the workers decided they didn't need a boss and could keep things running without Schlumpf's oversight, so they formed a workers' co-op to keep themselves employed. When members of the co-op traveled to the supposedly unused factory in Mulhouse, what they discovered was a building packed to the gills with exotic cars. A collection the likes of which had never been seen. The workers collectively decided to run the museum while using it to spread the message of their cooperative to the wider world.

The museum was open for two years, with public access to the vehicles for a few hours a day, and it contained some real stunners, including gems like two Bugatti Type 52s, which were electric race cars sized for children. You can still see the collection today, though not exactly as Schlumpf originally had it. The collection was seized by officials and eventually reopened in 1982.

The best way to pack a suitcase, according to science

While recently packing for an overseas excursion, I stuffed every spare space I could find in my carry-on. I used what I call the “Mary Poppins” packing method: willing myself to believe that whatever I wanted to put in my luggage would fit, regardless of the size of the object or the size of my bag. Somehow my system worked, but when I arrived at my destination and unzipped my bag, everything within it exploded onto the floor. “There’s gotta be a better way,” I thought.

To find out how best to pack according to science, Popular Science turned to Anne McAlpin, a travel expert and author of the book, Pack it Up: the Essential Guide to Smart Travel. Over the course of her lifetime, McAlpin has estimated she’s packed—and unpacked—more than 25,000 times! She’s a whiz at minimizing dead space, maximizing volume, and keeping clothes looking more like they came off a closet hanger than straight out of a laundry bin.

Many carry-on bags have “ruts” or internal grooves to accommodate a recessed trolley handle. This handle uses two narrow metal poles that retract straight into the middle or sides of the interior lining, causing the interior surface of the suitcase to be uneven.

“If you don’t have a flat foundation, make one,” says McAlpin. Having a flat foundation maximizes space, prevents wrinkles, and adds stability to your bag. To do this, consider evening out the space between ruts with packing or compression cubes. “You can put all of your underwear into a compression cube,” a small bag made of breathable nylon or polyester mesh used in part to organize items, and then place it between the ruts. This will even out the bottom of a hard-shell suitcase. Flexible items such as toiletry kits and rolled-up socks can help to form this base layer as well. “I then pack my clothing on top of that flat foundation.”

McAlpin puts her shoes (she usually packs two pairs and wears a third) towards the bottom of the suitcase so that the weight is on the wheels. “You don’t want to put your heavy items on the top of the inside of your suitcase,” she says. “This will cause it to constantly topple.”

When it comes to clothing, McAlpin rolls some items and flat packs others. “I call it interlayering,” she says.

For example, with pants, rather than folding them to stack in a pile, McAlpin lays each pair out along its natural creases as if she were going to put them on a hanger. Then, she places them in her suitcase: waistband at the top of one side of the bag, legs dangling out the other side of the bag. They’re not folded, but laid out straight. This way, McAlpin can then wrap them around the rolled items that she’ll later place on top of them.

She does the same with cotton shirts, sundresses—anything that might wrinkle. With each item, McAlpin alternates the direction, so that there are no two waistbands or shirt collars bulking up one side of the suitcase.

“I then roll my knit items and synthetic things that don’t wrinkle as much. I roll them up like Tootsie Rolls and I place them side by side,” in the middle of the laid-out items. While folding clothes and stacking them leaves pockets of air both inside the folds and within the layers, rolling wraps fabrics into a smooth, tight cylinder, which reduces the overall amount of space each item uses. It’s the best of both worlds! Your laid-out items aren’t as wrinkled and your wrinkle-resistant items are more tightly packed. This allows for more room in your suitcase overall.

When McAlpin has all her rolled items tightly packed together, she takes the pant legs and skirts that are still hanging off the sides of the suitcase, and “bends them up and over the ‘roll bar’ I’ve made of sweaters, pajamas, and sweats.” This helps avoid the knee creases that come when you’re simply folding pants. It also keeps items compact, and is a game-changer when it comes to unpacking. You can just lift all of the interlayered items out of your suitcase in one fell swoop.

Fill up empty space. For example, McAlpin says that if you’re a couple packing together, you may be able to slide a woman’s sandal right into a man’s shoe, or at least use the shoe’s interior cavity to store phone chargers, socks, or small items like earrings and even an eyeglasses case. Not only is it a space-saver, but filling up unused space prevents transit damage by keeping items inside from shifting.

Stuff the corners of a suitcase with socks, rolled-up t-shirts, etc, “because all those awkward corners offer prime packing space,” says McAlpin. Filling up those pockets also distributes structural stress throughout the bag, helping to keep features like zippers and fabric intact during excessive handling.

Another trick: laying belts around the luggage perimeter so that they’re easier to find.

To keep things condensed and organized, McAlpin suggests compression cubes. These small bags feature two zippers: a main zipper that closes the cube like a standard bag, and a second perimeter zipper that pulls the cube’s top and bottom panels closer together, squeezing out excess air and reducing volume. Compression cubes help eliminate air pockets that come with traditional folding and can reduce the volume of soft clothes like T-shirts and pants up to 30 percent.

Another option: roll-up compression bags. These pump-free, vacuum-free storage bags use one-way pressure valves to push air out, cutting volume by up to 80 percent. “I use these for laundry,” says McAlpin, because they temporarily block odors.

“They’re like an industrial Ziploc bag,” she says, and great for bulky items like a fleece jacket. But use them sparingly. Roll sacks are typically made of plastic and cause a lot more wrinkles than traditional compression cubes.

McAlpin carries dry laundry sheets and a two-gallon Ziploc bag for washing clothes. “The bag doesn’t take up any room, and then I can wash my socks and underwear without worrying if I’ve left any toothpaste in the sink.”

McAlpin says her packing “3:1 ratio” (three tops to every bottom) has “changed people’s lives.” She packs nine tops and three bottoms, and everything mixes and matches. “I base everything around two colors,” she says, “like a black and a tan,” and then throws in an occasional splash of color.

This works, says McAlpin, because human memory registers outfits based on what’s closest to the head and face. And while she travels with three pairs of shoes, each one should be for something different. For example, runners for long walks, sandals for the beach, and loafers for evenings.

As for the bag itself, McAlpin says four-wheel bags (spinner luggage) are a must. Not only are they easy to maneuver through packed terminals and airplane aisles, the ability to push the bag upright beside you keeps your arm in a neutral, bent-elbow position. This places less stress on your wrists, shoulders, and lower back compared to pulling a two-wheel bag behind you, and plays a big role in easing ergonomic strain.

They’re also ideal for stacking, since they stay upright. Think about it, says McAlpin. “When you pack your heavy tote bag on top of your suitcase, it’s the bag, not your shoulders, that’s carrying the weight.”

Ultimately, the idea is to make use of all your space, keep items from bumping around inside your suitcase and (if possible) wrinkle-free, and to not throw out your back in the process.

Of course, when it comes to finding room for souvenirs, there’s always the Mary Poppins packing method. But after talking with McAlpin, I’ve been using her method exclusively. My clothes, formerly creased, rumpled, and crinkled, are now decidedly wrinkle-free!

GLP-1 medications have transformed obesity and type 2 diabetes treatment. Now, researchers are investigating whether the effects of this blockbuster class of drugs extend beyond weight loss and affect brain health.

A comprehensive new review published on November 5 in the journal Science evaluated the current evidence on how GLP-1 medications like semaglutide and tirzepatide may affect the brain across aging, cardiometabolic disease, and Alzheimer’s disease.

The authors of the review suggested that GLP-1 medications like Ozempic and Zepbound may offer brain health benefits. However, they were also left with the question of whether the medications act directly on the brain or whether better brain health is actually a consequence of improved cardiometabolic health.

Sheena Aurora, MD, vice president, Medical Affairs, Alzheimer’s Association, who wasn’t involved in the review, told Healthline that more research is needed to better understand the effects of GLP-1 drugs on the brain.

“While research suggests GLP-1s could reduce brain inflammation, improve blood vessel health, and even protect neurons, much broader, randomized clinical trials on GLP-1s and brain health, including in people with Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias, are needed,” she said.

GLP-1 medications like semaglutide, the active ingredient in Ozempic and Wegovy, mimic the GLP-1 hormone. Tirzepatide, the active ingredient in Mounjaro and Zepbound, also targets a second hormone pathway called GIP.

These medications have been shown to produce significant weight loss and improvements in cardiometabolic health.

This is important for brain health because obesity, type 2 diabetes, and vascular dysfunction are all linked with cognitive impairment and dementia. Insulin resistance and chronic inflammation may also link metabolic and brain health.

Analyses of large outcome trials and observational cohorts have suggested that GLP-1 medications may be associated with a reduced risk of cognitive impairment and dementia. However, randomized trials in diseases like Alzheimer’s disease have yet to demonstrate clinical benefits.

Around 200 preclinical and mechanistic studies have shown benefits for learning, memory, synaptic plasticity, and neuronal resilience, though results are heterogeneous in design and direction, so a clear consensus on magnitude and mechanism remains lacking.

It is also unclear how well GLP-1 medications reach the brain. They appear to reach certain areas, such as the hypothalamus, brainstem, and circumventricular organs, more readily than regions protected by the blood-brain barrier.

The report authors noted that some studies have shown that GLP-1 receptor agonists may reduce the risk of cognitive impairment and dementia in people with type 2 diabetes. This may be due to cognitive issues being linked to factors such as blood sugar, blood pressure, vascular health, and sleep.

Mir Ali, MD, medical director of MemorialCare Surgical Weight Loss Center at Orange Coast Medical Center in Fountain Valley, CA, who wasn’t involved in the report, told Healthline, “These findings are interesting, but like many of the other benefits associated with GLP-1 medications, a direct causal relationship has not yet been proven.”

“Rather, the benefits appear to be more closely tied to the reduction in obesity and better control of diabetes,” he said.

Aurora noted that growing evidence supports the heart-brain connection.

She cited new findings from the Alzheimer’s Association’s landmark U.S. POINTER study presented at the Clinical Trials in Alzheimer’s Disease (CTAD) conference November 16–19 in Boston, MA.

Why the Answer Page of a Daily Game Should Be the Least Exciting Page

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Product teams tend to make the answer page a finale. Confetti, a big reveal, a share card. After a year of running a companion site for a daily puzzle, I think the answer page should be the least exciting page in the product, and the reason is a design principle that generalises far beyond games.

The player arrives at the answer page after the interesting act has already happened. The two minutes before that page are where the game lives: the listening, the narrowing, the guess. A dramatic reveal page is competing with that experience rather than consolidating it. Worse, it adds latency to the one thing the user came for, which is the answer itself.

What the page actually needs is three things. The answer, above the fold, without a scroll. The hints next to it, collapsed, so someone still working can use the page without being spoiled. And a way back into the game, because a finite experience usually ends with a desire for one more round. That is it. Everything else is decoration that slows the page and makes the answer harder to confirm.

There is an SEO argument too. Answer pages are the pages that rank for question-like queries, and question queries want immediate answers. A page where the answer is buried under a hero animation and three paragraphs of preamble has a worse answer density, and answer density is what users and search engines are both estimating. Plain HTML that states the answer in the first screenful is not lazy; it is the correct product decision for this page type.

I would go further: the boring answer page is a brand decision. A site that respects the player's time after the round is a site the player trusts before the next round. Trust compounds in a way that confetti does not. You can compare the two approaches in five minutes: see the daily answer as it is laid out here, then look at the practice page and notice how much of the design is just removing steps.

I counted 23,064 agent skills before writing one. Here is what I built instead.

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Agent skills are folders with a SKILL.md file that teach a coding agent, such as Claude Code, Codex or Cursor, how to do one job. There are a lot of them now. I had about 720 skill files installed on my own machine.

Then I looked at my own history. Across several hundred coding sessions, only 26 different skills had ever started. The other 700 were sitting in the agent's context doing nothing, or worse, competing with the ones I needed.

So before building my own library, I counted what exists.

I downloaded twelve public skill libraries and wrote a script to read every SKILL.md: 23,064 files, 10,579 different skill names once the copies are removed.

Then I read a few hundred of them closely, domain by domain. The pattern was the same everywhere. Weak skills are lists of advice. Strong skills run a tool and end with a verdict. An advice skill cannot fail, so it cannot tell you anything.

mstack is the library I built from that. Every skill follows four rules.

1. It runs a script. The script produces the check. The model does not grade its own work.

2. It ends in one of four states: pass, fail, not verified, or blocked. "Not verified" never counts as a pass. If a tool is missing, the skill says so and stops. It does not pretend.

3. It is tested for when it starts. Each skill has ten requests where it should start and ten where it should stay quiet. The quiet ones are near misses: requests that belong to the skill next door. The results are published in the README, including the bad ones.

4. Packs are small. A coding agent only has room for so many skill descriptions. In Claude Code the skill listing gets about 1% of the context window, and when it overflows, the least-used descriptions are dropped. So mstack is split into packs by domain. You install the pack for the work you do.

You do not type commands. You say what you want.

The pack also ships a guard hook for Claude Code. It stops a recursive delete and points to safe-delete, and it asks before commands such as git reset --hard, a forced push or DROP TABLE. It also asks before MCP tools whose names look destructive, because a command filter cannot see what an API call deletes.

Each core skill got ten "should start" requests and ten "should stay quiet" requests, in a clean session with only that pack installed.

Zero false starts in 80 tries. The first time I ran these, debrief started on only 7 of 10, below my pass mark of 8. I reworded its description using the three requests it had missed, ran the test again, and it reached 9.

The other 107 skills have a smaller smoke test so far: two fitting and two near-miss requests each. Across all 115 skills, they started on 277 of 294 fitting requests (94%) and on 1 of 294 requests meant for a neighbour. That one false start is listed in the README with the rest.

These are single runs. With many other skills installed, results will differ. The second kind of test, "is the result better with the skill than without it", exists for every skill and has not been run yet.

Designing a Difficulty Dial Without Changing the Rules

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

A four-letter word ladder has one rule. If the rule never changes, how do you make an easy mode and a hard mode? That question is where most of the interesting design work in a small daily puzzle actually lives, and the answer is not "add a timer".

The trick is to separate the rule from the shape of the board. The rule is always the same: change one letter, every step must be a real word, reach the target. What varies between difficulties is the start word and the route it implies. An easy start sits in a dense part of the graph with several short paths to the target. A hard start sits in a thin corner where the first natural move leads into a pocket with one exit.

That choice has a consequence worth being explicit about: difficulty is a property of the pair (start, target), not of the start alone. The same start word can be easy against one target and brutal against another, because the target's neighbourhood is half the puzzle. If you tune difficulty by only looking at starts, you will ship a hard level that turns out to be a two-move route through a word nobody expected.

Practically, I generate candidate starts, run a breadth-first search from each to the target, and bucket by shortest-path length and by the number of dead ends inside the shortest-radius region. Short path plus high branching is easy. Long path plus low branching is hard. The rule never changes, so a player who learns the game on easy is learning the real game.

The player-facing side matters too. The difficulty has to be visible before the round starts, and switching must be free. If switching costs a streak or a confirmation dialog, players will sit at the wrong difficulty out of politeness, and a difficulty dial nobody turns is not a feature.

There is a free practice version of this: the practice modes offer endless random starts at three settings with no daily wait, while a free daily word ladder keeps one fixed board per day. That combination is the cheapest possible way to let a player find their own level, and it costs nothing to keep running.

Cockpit followed a home-directory symlink in a privileged key helper

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Server administration consoles often perform one privileged action on behalf of a logged-in user. Cockpit's public-key addition workflow is a small example: the user supplies a key and the server places it in the target account's authorized-keys path. The helper runs with elevated authority, so every path it follows matters.

CVE-2026-91151 describes an insecure symlink-handling flaw in ssh-add-public-key.sh. A local authenticated user who controls a target account's home directory can place a symlink in the path used by the helper. When a privileged Cockpit session adds a public key, the helper follows the link and performs privileged filesystem operations on an unintended file.

The Red Hat record describes the outcome as the ability to change file ownership and modes, potentially leading to local privilege escalation. The affected products listed in public CVE metadata include multiple Red Hat Enterprise Linux generations and Red Hat OpenShift Dev Spaces images.

The vulnerable sequence is not a remote command injection. It is a privileged file operation performed through a path that a different local identity can influence. The attacker needs local authentication and control over the home directory, but the effect crosses into the privileged Cockpit context.

This kind of flaw is easy to miss in a web-console review because the browser action looks like a normal administrative feature. The dangerous code is in the helper that runs outside the browser, where path ownership and symlink resolution determine whether the operation stays inside the intended account directory.

Consider hosts where administrators manage other users, shared service accounts, or home directories on shared storage. A department administrator with a Cockpit session may not be the owner of every target home directory. A user with write access to that directory can prepare the link before the administrator adds a key.

Containers and development environments need separate review. OpenShift Dev Spaces images contain the affected package according to public CVE metadata, even if the runtime does not expose the same management workflow. Inventory the image as part of patch planning and verify whether the helper is reachable in the deployed service.

Apply the Cockpit package update from each distribution. The fixed commit in the advisory references changes to the helper's path handling. Confirm the installed package version on the host and inside long-lived images, then restart the affected service according to the vendor instructions.

Until the update is deployed, restrict the public-key workflow to administrators who are trusted to manage the target home directory. Do not allow untrusted local users to write the home of an account that a privileged Cockpit user will modify. Use a dedicated administration account and avoid shared home directories for role-bound service accounts.

Review other helper scripts that accept a username and construct a path under /home. Validate the resolved path with a file-descriptor-based operation, refuse symlinks in privileged writes, and perform the final open as the intended account rather than as root. A string comparison of the path before the open is not enough if a link can change after the check.

Create a test home directory owned by an unprivileged user, place a symlink where the helper expects the authorized-keys file, and run the workflow through the patched Cockpit version. The helper should reject the link or write only to a verified target. Check both the file contents and the ownership and mode changes.

Inspect audit logs for ssh-add-public-key.sh executions, target usernames, and file changes outside the expected home directory. Correlate them with Cockpit login sessions and local account changes. If an affected host accepted an untrusted home-directory link, review ownership and modes of sensitive files and rebuild if the privileged operation cannot be bounded.

The patch is important, but the durable control is to treat user-controlled paths as hostile input even when the caller is already authenticated. A console's privilege should not flow through a directory another user can rewrite.

Dead Calm is a thriller that only needs three people and a lot of open water. A new version would just need to deal with the fact that satellite phones and GPS make getting stranded at sea much harder today, which could actually add an interesting twist to the story. All a Dead Calm remake needs is a smart director and two strong leads to become the kind of adrenaline-fueled, nerve-wracking thriller that has been missing from the big screen for a while now.

Brian De Palma’s Blow Out bombed at the box office when it first came out, but it has since been recognized as one of the greatest conspiracy thrillers ever made. When Jack plays back his tape, he hears something that sounds like a gunshot right before the tire blows out, and he soon realizes he has recorded a murder that powerful people want covered up.

Blow Out is a thriller story about how easily the truth can be buried, and that idea feels even more relevant now. In 1981, Jack’s audio tape was the only proof that something was wrong. A modern remake of the film could explore what happens when evidence can be faked, edited, or dismissed as a deepfake in seconds. Jack’s obsession with proving what he heard would hit even harder in a world where nobody knows what to believe anymore. The original’s devastating ending is also a big part of why audiences rejected it back then, but it works perfectly today. The problem is that much of the first half has aged poorly, since almost everyone around Susy treats her like a helpless child. A remake could keep the brilliant premise while giving Susy the strength and intelligence she deserves from the very first scene. Hepburn earned an Oscar nomination for the role, but Wait Until Dark is ready for a brand-new heroine who can outsmart her attackers right from the start.

Scientists Catch Water Molecules Breaking Apart Before Their Electrons Can Respond

📅 Nov 22, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

When X-rays strike a water molecule, the molecule can break apart faster than its electrons can complete a fundamental relaxation process. Scientists have discovered that both bonds holding hydrogen to oxygen can rupture within just a few femtoseconds, leaving behind an excited, electrically neutral oxygen atom.

The discovery challenges established interpretations of how water responds to X-ray radiation. It also identifies a previously overlooked source of highly reactive atomic oxygen, which can participate in further chemical reactions.

When a water molecule absorbs a sufficiently energetic X-ray photon, an electron can be ejected from an inner shell of its oxygen atom. The resulting vacancy normally triggers Auger decay, a rapid process in which another electron fills the vacancy and transfers enough energy to eject a second electron.

However, the researchers found that the molecular structure can change even faster. The particular electronic state produced by X-ray absorption creates strong repulsive forces, allowing both oxygen-hydrogen bonds to break before Auger decay occurs.

“What makes this result particularly exciting is that we see the molecule break apart before the electronic relaxation has even finished. Both O–H bonds rupture within the lifetime of the oxygen core hole, so the nuclear motion is essentially winning the race against the electrons,” explained Florian Trinter of FHI in Berlin.

The team examined isolated water molecules in the gas phase using high-resolution electron spectroscopy at two synchrotron facilities: PETRA III at DESY in Hamburg, Germany, and the FlexPES beamline at MAX IV in Lund, Sweden.

Both experiments detected unusually sharp peaks at specific electron energies. These features could not be explained by the electronic decay of intact water molecules or the molecular fragments typically expected after X-ray absorption.

“Seeing the characteristic signal also at MAX IV was an important part of establishing this as a genuine molecular fragmentation pathway. The measurements at the two facilities give us a very consistent picture of what happens to the water molecule after X-ray excitation,” said Gunnar Öhrwall of MAX IV in Lund.

To determine what produced the unusual electron signals, the researchers performed detailed molecular and atomic calculations. The predicted electron energies matched the decay of excited neutral oxygen atoms rather than hydroxyl (OH) fragments.

This distinction matters because the energies of emitted electrons depend on the electronic structure of the atom or molecule producing them. The exceptionally narrow spectral features provided a recognizable signature of oxygen that had already separated from the original water molecule.

“The energy of the emitted electrons provides a kind of fingerprint of the state that is formed. Our calculations show that the observed features are consistent with the decay of an excited neutral oxygen atom that has already separated from the water molecule,” explained Ludger Inhester of CFEL at DESY in Hamburg.

The results offer a new explanation for high-energy electron features observed in earlier water experiments. Signals previously associated with hydroxyl fragments may instead originate from excited neutral oxygen atoms produced through extremely rapid molecular fragmentation.

Because neutral atomic oxygen is highly reactive, understanding how it forms could improve models of chemical processes initiated by X-ray radiation. The findings also provide a more precise basis for interpreting electron spectra from water and related molecular systems.

William Gibson is one of the most influential authors in the sci-fi genre. Often credited for pioneering the cyberpunk subgenre, his most seminal novel, Neuromancer, remains a sci-fi masterpiece in 2026. Despite the timelessness and popularity of his literary work, however, his cyberpunk stories and books have hardly been adapted for the screen.

While many video games, movies, and TV shows have loosely drawn from his work, there are only a handful of direct mainstream adaptations of his stories. Two of these, Johnny Mnemonic and New Rose Hotel, premiered in the '90s and struggled to leave their mark. One, titled The Peripheral, premiered on Prime Video in 2022 before being canceled after one season.

Considering the long-standing legacy and influence of William Gibson's work, it is unfortunate that none of his adaptations have worked too well on the screen so far. However, this will soon likely change. Apple TV is now adapting one of William Gibson's most iconic cyberpunk sci-fi books, Neuromancer, which has the potential to become one of the biggest events of the genre in this decade.

Most cyberpunk TV shows and movies focus a little too much on the visual aspect, featuring neon-soaked cityscapes and edgy characters. Prime Video's The Peripheral, on the other hand, truly understood the essence of William Gibson's near-future cyberpunk. In its 2032 timeline, the show features a very familiar, economically broken rural America.

Technology is deeply grafted onto the blue-collar lives of regular people. There are 3-D printed pharmaceuticals, while even veterans with disabilities use military implants called "haptics." The series also reveals its central VR technology as a form of escapism for the main characters who place it in a run-down trailer.

Then comes the show's 2099 timeline where it contrasts 2032's "low lives" with London's high-tech and elite. This is when The Peripheral also introduces "The Jackpot," to reveal the high stakes of its apocalyptic conflict and how humanity's unchecked ambition in the future has pushed civilization to the brink of collapse.

In its 8-episode runtime, Prime Video's The Peripheral did enough to earn more follow-ups and expand its sci-fi vision in season 2. It was a rare adaptation that embraced the hard-edged sci-fi and weirdness of its source material. Unfortunately, despite being promising, its renewal was reversed by Prime Video after SAG-AFTRA and Writers Guild of America's strikes in 2023.

The works of many sci-fi authors, like Philip K. Dick, are not easy to adapt. Yet, Philip K. Dick's stories have already received multiple worthy on-screen adaptations. William Gibson's adaptations, in contrast, have almost been cursed. The ones like Johnny Mnemonic became cult classics, but never achieved mainstream acclaim or success.

Meanwhile, The Peripheral had immense potential, but failed to last beyond one season. After being considered "unfilmable" for quite some time, William Gibson's Neuromancer is finally being adapted by Apple TV. And so far, it looks like the show will finally spell redemption for William Gibson's adaptations.

Apple TV's history of bringing challenging adaptations like Isaac Asimov's Foundation to the screen alone is a proof of concept for its ability to successfully translate "unfilmable" sci-fi literature. William Gibson, too, has been directly involved with the show's production, which also heavily favors the adaptation.

Not to mention, after its first trailer, it looks like Apple TV's Neuromancer is not bogging down William Gibson's original vision. If all goes well, the upcoming sci-fi series could become a massive hit and make up for the pitfalls and cancelation of Prime Video's The Peripheral.
