NASA Spacecraft Snaps Moody Photo of Earth on Its Way to Mars

NASA’s ESCAPADE spacecraft has captured an incredible photo of the Earth as it slingshotted past the planet on its way to Mars.

The images were taken by the spacecraft’s Visible and Infrared Observation System camera, developed by Northern Arizona University, at around 9 A.M. EST on November 9. This was approximately three hours after the spacecraft made its closest approach to Earth, when it was about 38,500 miles away and had a clear view of the Western Hemisphere.

The images were captured by the second of the two ESCAPADE spacecraft to perform a powered flyby of Earth this month. Both spacecraft used a combination of engine burns and Earth’s gravity to gain momentum for their 10-month journey to Mars.

Once they reach orbit around the Red Planet, the spacecraft will study how the solar wind — a stream of charged particles traveling at around a million miles per hour from the Sun — interacts with Mars’ environment and contributes to the loss of its atmosphere.

A commercial satellite captured images of the pair from approximately 3,000 miles away, while a member of the mission team photographed the spacecraft passing overhead from his home near Los Angeles (below).

What Is Pons? The Robinhood Chain Meme Coin Launchpad Explained

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

What Is Pons? The Robinhood Chain Meme Coin Launchpad Explained

When Robinhood switched on its own Robinhood Chain blockchain on July 1, 2026, the trading app's stock tokens were supposed to be the main attraction. Instead, much of the early activity came from meme coins: tokens built around a joke or an internet trend.

Pons became the busiest place to make them. Like Pump.fun on Solana, it lets anyone pick a name, ticker and image and have a tradable token minutes later, without writing code. Its fees briefly made it one of the highest-earning protocols in crypto, and its own token, PONS, went on to list on Kraken and Coinbase.

That openness can also be exploited. In September 2026, an onchain analyst alleged that one group had used Pons launches to extract millions of dollars from buyers.

This guide explains what Pons is, how its two generations of contracts work, what the PONS token does, and the risks.

Pons is a token launch protocol running on Robinhood Chain, a layer-2 network built on Ethereum using Arbitrum's technology, and its website and documentation are published by Pons Labs, LLC.

Every token launched through Pons is a standard ERC-20 token with a fixed supply, 1 billion tokens on V1 launches, that can never be increased. The creator gets no allocation set aside before trading opens.

Pons is also non-custodial. Its documentation says every launch and trade is a transaction the user's own wallet signs, and the platform never holds user funds.

Pons went live within days of Robinhood Chain's mainnet. It picked up most of the chain's launchpad activity after an early rival, Noxa, stopped accepting new launches on July 11, 2026, according to Decrypt's reporting.

Pons has two generations of contracts, V1 and V2, and both remain live on Robinhood Chain.

In V1, a single transaction deploys the token and its trading pool, a Uniswap V3 pool paired with wrapped ETH (WETH), and locks the pool's liquidity so the creator can't withdraw it. Each launch costs 0.0005 ETH and carries a 1% trading fee, the documentation says. A launch "graduates" once 4.2 ETH has been paired in its pool by default, but trading carries on in the same pool.

V1 launches are protected for their first two blocks: in the launch block, only the creator's initial buy can execute, and for the rest of the window no wallet can hold more than 5% of supply or buy more than 5.5%. Fees are split 70% to the creator and 30% to the protocol for current launches.

V2 adds a bonding curve: a contract that holds a token's whole supply and sets its price by formula, rising as people buy and falling as they sell. According to the V2 documentation, the curve sells out, then "graduates" automatically into a Uniswap V4 pool, seeded with a fixed share of supply held back from the start. The pool's liquidity is then locked permanently; no unlock function exists.

V2 launches can be priced in ETH or in other assets Pons has approved, including tokenized stocks, in which case the creator is paid in that asset. Each trade pays a standard fee shared between Pons, the creator and an optional buyback, plus an optional creator tax that is capped by the protocol and fixed at launch.

When a creator turns buybacks on, part of their own fee is spent buying their token back. Those tokens are locked in a vault and released over five years, split between the creator and the protocol.

PONS itself was launched through Pons's own legacy V1 factory, and its documentation uses PONS as the reference token for developers testing their integrations.

"Sniping" means buying a large share of a new token in its first seconds, often with bots, then selling into later buyers. Every V2 launch opens with a "snipe tax" of 99% on buys that decays to zero over five seconds, the documentation says.

The launching wallet and the creator's fee wallet are exempt automatically, and creators can name up to 32 more exempt addresses at launch "for a team bundling its opening buys across several wallets."

That exemption became central to allegations published on September 27, 2026. Pseudonymous onchain analyst Wazz tweeted that a single operation had extracted at least $18.43 million from 53 Robinhood Chain launches between July 10 and September 21, 2026, mostly through Pons V2. Wazz said nearly every launch was sniped for 70% or more of its supply by bundles of 70 to 200 wallets.

The Block reviewed the onchain data and found that creators of nine Pons V2 launches had exempted 15 to 25 wallets each from the snipe tax, which then bought out the bonding curve in a single transaction. The creator and those wallets held 82% to 86% of supply after the opening buys. The Block did not independently replicate Wazz's $18.43 million total.

On September 3, 2026, Pons generated $5.95 million in daily fees, briefly out-earning Robinhood Chain itself and ranking fourth among all protocols tracked by DefiLlama, Decrypt reported. Pons fees drove Robinhood Chain to a record of about $6 million in daily fees in early September 2026, The Block reported.

On September 5, 2026, blockchain analytics firm Arkham tweeted that crypto market maker Wintermute appeared to be buying PONS slowly in a series of small purchases, and held just over $3 million of the token. Wallets that Arkham attributes to Wintermute held about 3.4 million PONS at the time, and about 7.4 million as of November 2026.

As of November 20, 2026, DefiLlama put Pons's cumulative fees at about $205 million. Of that, $137 million came in September 2026 alone; fees fell to about $17 million in October 2026.

PONS is the ERC-20 token tied to the Pons launchpad, with a fixed supply of 1 billion. Its link to the platform is a buyback.

According to the V1 documentation, 80% of the protocol's fees are used to buy PONS on the market through an automated process and send it to a burn address, permanently removing it from circulation. The remaining 20% pays for infrastructure and the Pons team. Kraken's listing notice describes the same mechanism.

As of November 20, 2026, CoinGecko data shows about 669 million PONS in circulation, out of a fixed supply of 1 billion.

You can track PONS's latest price on Decrypt's price page.

PONS trades in Uniswap pools on Robinhood Chain. Binance Wallet added it to its Binance Alpha tier on September 2, 2026, and Kraken opened PONS trading on September 25, 2026, with access through the Kraken App and Instant Buy to follow once liquidity allows. Coinbase Markets tweeted on October 6, 2026, that it had added PONS to its listing roadmap, and said the next day that its PONS-USD pair would open later that day, if liquidity conditions were met.

Pons has flagged several changes still to come. Its V1 documentation says the 80% share of protocol fees used to buy back PONS is not yet fixed in its contracts, but will become "immutable, decentralized, and automated" in a future release.

On V2, three security firms (SB Security, Dingbats and Pashov Audit Group) are reviewing the contracts, and Pons says it will publish their reports in full, including any findings it did not accept, once the reviews close. Until then, its documentation tells users to treat V2 as unaudited, and only whitelisted addresses can create V2 tokens.

Pons also faces competition on its home chain. Uniswap, already Robinhood Chain's main exchange, launched its own launchpad, Pools.trade, on August 5, 2026. It charges no launchpad fee, locks each launch's liquidity permanently, and offers a "crowd launch" option designed to resist bundling. Within its first days, Pools.trade took about half of Robinhood Chain's launchpad volume, Decrypt's Morning Minute newsletter reported. The newsletter noted that Uniswap can afford to forgo launchpad fees because it earns from its other products, which most dedicated launchpads cannot.

Pons has since kept the larger share of fees. As of November 2026, DefiLlama showed Pons collecting about $5.6 million in fees over the previous 30 days, against about $115,000 for Pools.trade. The two charge different rates, though: Pools.trade's trading fee is 0.25%, while Pons's standard fee is 1%.

V2's tokenized-stock pairs link Pons launches to the stock tokens Robinhood built the chain to carry, which ties Pons's prospects to Robinhood Chain itself. On Robinhood's third-quarter earnings call on October 27, 2026, CEO Vlad Tenev said the growth of the chain and its ecosystem had exceeded the company's expectations, and that Robinhood was investing in how to support the chain's developer community.

The Best Books From the Last 10 Years That Hooked Me From the Opening Chapter

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Sometimes you know from the very first chapter that you're going to enjoy a book, and it's a great feeling. Maybe it's a particularly strong opening line or a vivid POV character that makes the action all the more immersive. No matter the reason, the pages fly by, and you find yourself drawn deeper and deeper into the story.

With that in mind, this list looks at some novels from the last decade that reel the reader in practically from page one. They span a variety of genres and tones, from historical epics and revisionist fantasy to dark character studies and tense nonfiction. The fact remains, all are finely written and always engaging, wasting little time immersing us in their characters and world.

"When a man is sure he knows God's will... he is the most dangerous person in the world." Ken Follett (best known for Eye of the Needle and The Pillars of the Earth) is a master of historical fiction, and this is one of his best. We're quickly thrown into the deadly dynastic and religious conflicts of the time, buoyed along by colorful characters and gripping, page-turning prose.

The third installment in his Kingsbridge series, A Column of Fire begins in 1558, as the young Ned Willard returns to his hometown after spending time abroad. Although Ned hopes to marry Margery, the woman he loves, their relationship becomes entangled in the bitter religious divisions threatening England. Meanwhile, the death of Queen Mary I and the accession of Elizabeth I create enormous political uncertainty, forcing individuals and families to choose sides.

'When we exist, death is not; and when death exists, we are not." Karl Ove Knausgård caused a literary sensation in the 2000s with his autobiographical novel series My Struggle, and has since explored even stranger, darker territory with his recent work. That includes The School of Night, which tells the story of Kristian Hadeland, an ambitious young Norwegian who moves to London in 1985 to study photography. Convinced of his exceptional artistic talent, Kristian approaches his new surroundings with a mixture of arrogance, insecurity, and contempt.

Eventually, he crosses paths with a mysterious Dutch artist named Hans, whose influence introduces increasingly disturbing complications into his life. Indeed, beneath the apparently conventional account of a young artist's experiences, a sense of unease begins to grow. The result is a subtle but damning character study, one with restrained but unmistakable supernatural undercurrents.

"He's a bad dream with teeth." Yet another banger from Joe Hill, author of Horns and The Fireman (and son of Stephen King). The main character here is Arthur Oakes, a university student whose life becomes entangled in a dangerous criminal situation involving his mother. Desperate to protect her, Arthur turns to a group of friends who attempt to solve his problems through an extraordinary supernatural ritual. Their experiment involves summoning a powerful dragon-like entity known as King Sorrow, whose assistance comes at a terrifying cost.

From here, King Sorrow plays out as an epic, ambitious, character-driven dark fantasy, jam-packed with memorable scenes. We get phantasmagoric seances and summonings, shadowy government agencies attempting dastardly schemes, even a dragon going toe-to-toe with a pair of fighter jets. That said, even amidst all this sound and fury, Hill keeps the protagonists and their emotions front and center.

"The humanities are always in crisis." In recent years, author Ian McEwan (author of the modern classic Atonement) has shifted his attention to subtle sci-fi stories like the AI-focused Machines Like Me and this one, which uses the vantage point of the future to comment on our present. Set in 2119, What We Can Know follows Thomas Metcalfe, a literary scholar living in a Britain transformed by environmental catastrophe and rising sea levels. He becomes obsessed with locating a legendary poem written in 2014 by the celebrated poet Francis Blundy.

Supposedly composed for Blundy's wife, the poem was read aloud at a private dinner party before mysteriously disappearing, leaving behind a collection of historical accounts and tantalizing clues. Metcalfe thus undertakes a kind of literary investigation, in the process piecing together a portrait of the madness and contradictions of the 2010s and 2020s. A brilliant, perceptive book.

"The Beauty of the House is immeasurable; its Kindness infinite." Susanna Clarke wrote one of the finest fantasy books of the 21st century to date with Jonathan Strange and Mr. Norrell, and her sophomore novel Piranesi is a real gem, too. The title character is a gentle and inquisitive man who lives in an enormous labyrinthine structure known simply as the House. Its seemingly endless halls contain thousands of statues, while its lower levels are flooded by a vast ocean whose tides periodically sweep through the building.

Slowly, however, Piranesi begins to question his surroundings and dig deeper. In the process, an initially dreamlike fantasy tale morphs into an increasingly compelling psychological puzzle. There are twists and turns and revelations aplenty, all of it elevated by the author's typically stellar worldbuilding and character development. A must-read for fantasy fans.

"It is easy to charm a person who talks too much: all you have to do is listen." This is a work of nonfiction, but it's more gripping than most thriller novels. In November 2019, nineteen-year-old Zac Brettler, a seemingly ordinary middle-class teen, fell from the balcony of an expensive London apartment overlooking the River Thames. Although his death initially looks like suicide, every fact that emerges begins to complicate the picture.

Most striking of all, it turns out that Zac had been living a double life, presenting himself as the wealthy heir of a Russian oligarch. Author Patrick Radden Keefe (who also wrote the powerful book Say Nothing about the Troubles in Northern Ireland) doggedly chases the truth, eventually revealing far more than even the police. The result is not just a suspenseful true-crime story but a grim statement on modern London, a city besotted with wealth, no matter how dirty its origins.

"The king is dead." Author Lev Grossman subverted "wizard school" tropes with his Magicians series, and has played a similar trick with The Bright Sword, a wonderful reimagining of Arthurian legend. It's a grittier, more psychological take on the well-worn plot points involving quests and heroes. All in all, the book's mixture of melancholy, humor, and supernatural wonder feels faithful to the tradition and yet still refreshingly original.

The plot revolves around Collum, a young knight who arrives at Camelot hoping to join the fellowship of the Round Table. Unfortunately, his timing could hardly be worse. King Arthur has recently died, the kingdom is descending into political chaos, and most of its greatest defenders have either perished or disappeared. Indeed, instead of finding the glorious court he imagined, Collum encounters a diminished collection of surviving knights struggling to determine what remains of Arthur's extraordinary legacy.

"If I told you half of what he's done to keep us alive and safe, well, you wouldn't imagine it could be true." His Dark Materials is rightly regarded as a landmark of children's fantasy, so expectations were sky-high for Philip Pullman's La Belle Sauvage, and yet the book arguably surpasses them. It takes place approximately a decade before the events of Northern Lights/The Golden Compass, following eleven-year-old Malcolm Polstead, who must keep the baby Lyra Belacqua safe during a devastating flood.

On top of the dangers of nature, our intelligent, observant young protagonist must also dodge political conspiracies, scheming religious authority, various secret organizations, and ever-present spies. Along the way, he crosses paths with a river giant and a fairy woman, and comes into possession of an alethiometer, the truth-telling device that will become central to the entire series. In short, a smart, entertaining tale that deepens Pullman's mythology.

The Extraordinary Music Worthy of an Extraordinary Life in ‘W.E.B. Du Bois: Rebel with a Cause’

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

Rita Coburn‘s “W.E.B. Du Bois: Rebel with a Cause” is an extraordinary documentary about an extraordinary man, a film that spans nearly 100 years in two hours, yet never feels rushed or superficial. In her storytelling, Coburn shares with her subject a talent for distilling complex ideas and expressing them with force and clarity; the result is a movie that fully honors the breadth and depth of Du Bois’ achievements as a groundbreaking Black writer, historian, and activist.

One of Coburn’s most potent tools in this regard is the music by composer Kathryn Bostic, who previously collaborated with the director on “Marian Anderson: The Whole World in Her Hands.” Bostic incorporates a wide array of styles and influences in her score, which references the various types of music Du Bois encountered and appreciated as a Black intellectual but isn’t beholden to them; as evocative of Bach and American folk music as it is of 19th-century spirituals and Asian motifs, it’s the sonic equivalent of Du Bois’ literary works. The scope of the project required Bostic to come at the material from multiple angles and perspectives. “Rita and I talked about what types of touchstones we needed to define the narrative musically,” Bostic told IndieWire. “There was the regional touchstone, the era-specific touchstone, the interior emotional aspects that we wanted to heighten with music. Then there’s always the discovery process as you begin to create.”

For Bostic, that discovery process is one of the most exciting aspects of film composing. While she had a number of reference points as she began work on the score, she soon found herself following intuition to take them in different directions than initially intended. “There’s always what I call the hands-on-the-clay moment,” Bostic said. “You have your list, and you have your frame of reference, but the process is fluid.”

According to Coburn, Bostic’s ability to bring a historical perspective to her scores made her an ideal collaborator for the film. “Kathryn pulled from the region of Great Barrington [where Du Bois was born] as well as New York in that time period, and then what was happening with the African-American community that Du Bois was entering,” Coburn said. “There wasn’t gospel then, so you were looking at a small group of Black people going to the churches there in New England. What were they listening to, and therefore what was he listening to?”

Bostic drew on the music of the Fisk Jubilee Singers, a Black a capella ensemble that inspired Du Bois, but then moved in a completely different direction to explore his time in Berlin, when he encountered and fell in love with classical music. She also incorporated Wolof music from Senegal and the Gambia in collaboration with Coburn’s daughter, who studied classical music and worked in the music department on “Rebel with a Cause.”

“She found a Senegalese professor online who worked with her so she could sing the Wolof,” Coburn said. “Kathryn and I agreed that we had to put the Wolof in the film because it connects Du Bois to his Ghanaian roots. But we had to find it, and we wanted it to be as authentic as possible.”

For Bostic, the challenge was taking an audience from 2026 and presenting the music in a way that would recreate the way in which Du Bois experienced it over a hundred years earlier. “There’s the historic arc of the times, as well as what he’s experiencing emotionally,” Bostic said. In the places where her score was informed by spirituals and the work of the Fisk Jubilee Singers, the composer tried to tie the emotional subtext to the context in which she knew Du Bois was consuming the music.

“When he went to Fisk University, his mother had recently died,” Bostic said. “So that music was a wellspring for his grieving, and I think that depth of sorrow and the catharsis that resulted from that sorrow inspired the sort of deep self-reflection that became such a deep part of who he was. That self-reflection led to a basic decency and humanity, and even now I think of him as being light years ahead of people in the way he embodied justice. I want to shine a light on that.”

Coburn felt a similar sense of purpose, especially since the more she dug into Du Bois’ life the more she realized how relevant his insights were to America in 2026. “He speaks to this current time,” Coburn said, adding that the importance of Du Bois’ work gave her own work a sense of urgency and responsibility. “I knew that it might be 20 or 30 years before we get another Du Bois documentary. I hope not, but I looked at his life and knew that I had to do the best job that I could. We did everything we could for Du Bois because we felt he had done everything he could for us.”

The greatness of “W.E.B. Du Bois: Rebel with a Cause” is that it brings the viewer into Du Bois’ perspective as his world view continues to expand over a 95-year life, and the music is a major part of that subjectivity.

“Kathryn understands that music is a character in any documentary, and her music embodies the character of W.E.B. Du Bois,” Coburn said. “ In the music, we had to grow with him. We had to expand with him. We had to see that his love of Rembrandt and his understanding of classical music, of music that came from an Asian culture, that all of that was important to him. We were catching up with him, and he was driving us.”

How much you end up paying depends on your car's model and what other parts need repairing or swapping, but Tesla gives you no pricing chart to prepare for the expensive journey ahead. The thing is, battery degradation is just basic chemistry, so losing a few miles of range as that EV gets older should not surprise you, even if you avoid the leading causes of EV battery damage. It isn't exactly a sign for you to worry about a full battery replacement either.

A 2025 report from Recurrent looked at over 30,000 EVs overall, and fewer than 4 percent had ever needed battery replacement, excluding major recalls of course. Recurrent used data from across several manufacturers and different model years, so it isn't exactly a Tesla-specific failure rate.

While Tesla keeps its pricing under wraps, Earthling Automotive's 2025 pricing breakdown paints a pretty clear picture of how much you'd pay for a new EV battery. Keeping taxes aside, as they vary from state to state, you could end up shelling out anywhere between $12,600 and $13,350 with installation to replace the battery pack on an older rear-wheel-drive version of the Model 3.

That number shoots up to $17,250 for the all-wheel-drive (AWD) Model 3, and if you own a 2016-to-2021 Model S or Model X, you could be parting with about $19,500 to $20,500. But remember, Earthling's installation charges aren't Tesla Service rates, so your final bill could look a lot different based on things like battery configurations, labor charges and other replacement parts.

If you'd rather not pay Tesla's prices, an independent EV specialist may be able to source a refurbished pack for cheap or repair specific faults, since an EV battery's life isn't always over once it's removed from your car. Still, your final bill depends on what's actually wrong with the battery, and it becomes a hard pill to swallow once it starts to match your aging Tesla's resale value.

Now, before you pull your hair out over signs that it's time to replace your EV battery, take your car in for a full diagnosis. Your best bet is getting Tesla to draw up an itemized bill, which will clearly show the battery cost (be it remanufactured or brand new) along with labor and other add-ons.

Before you pay that bill, though, check whether your warranty already covers it, since Tesla's battery and drive unit warranty stays with you for eight years or 100,000, 120,000 or 150,000 miles, whichever comes first. The mileage limit depends directly on your EV's model and configuration, and Tesla promises at least 70 percent battery capacity retention while the warranty lasts. If your Tesla falls below that threshold, you could get a free repair or even a total battery replacement. If you somehow manage to wreck your battery or need a replacement after all, the new battery comes with a four-year or 50,000-mile parts warranty. And if for some reason Tesla swapped your battery for free under the original coverage, don't expect a fresh eight-year warranty period.

Even with all the support, if you're nervous about the most expensive part of your EV, send another $2,000 Tesla's way for the Battery Extended Service Agreement. You can get this for eligible Model 3 and Model Y cars before the original battery warranty runs out, and depending on what qualifies as a battery failure, you're covered with just a $500 service deductible. Mind you, this won't include regular wear and tear that every EV battery goes through over time. For the extra $2,000, you can add up to 24 months or 30,000 miles of added coverage to your old and faithful EV.

European court: States can turn back migrants in some cases

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

Europe's top human rights court ruled that member states can turn back migrants even under "hostile" conditions, unless they've been identified as people who may be subject to ill-treatment.

The European Court of Human Rights on Tuesday ruled that states belonging to the Council of Europe group have a right to control foreign arrivals.

But the court ruling said all 46 member states of the group — that includes all EU and some non-EU countries and promotes democracy and human rights on the continent — must ensure and uphold human rights of people even in hostile environments.

The European court ruling is a complicated one since it deals with a case concerning the entry of nationals of Afghanistan, Iraq and Cuba into EU member countries Poland, Latvia and Lithuania between 2021 and 2022.

That was a time when a border standoff was playing out between Belarus and the EU's eastern member states, with Belarus having been accused of entirely manufacturing a migration crisis to put pressure on the EU.

Belarus arranged for people from various third countries to enter their state and then pointed them to the EU's frontiers.

Europe's most important human rights court on Tuesday ruled that states have a right to control the entry of foreign nationals into their territory even when migration has been weaponized.

The court calls Belarus' hostile actions of 2021 as having been an instance of "instrumentalization of migration."

But the court said that states must still ensure that migrants are protected against inhumane or degrading treatment.

In the context of a surge in migration, for example, national authorities were still to identify people who were particularly at risk of suffering or enduring ill treatment.

The migrants in the case brought before the European court said that they had been violently prevented from crossing into the EU without their asylum applications having been examined and that their human rights had been violated.

"In a context of instrumentalization of migration, the removal of a foreign national to the instrumentalizing state would not, as such, constitute a violation of Article 3 (prohibition of torture and inhuman and degrading treatment)," the court press release said.

The court ordered Poland, Latvia and Lithuania to pay compensation for their treatment of migrants at the EU's external borders with Belarus.

Five EU countries also last week announced a plan with Rwanda to establish a so-called return hub in the African nation.

The hub will host rejected asylum seekers waiting to return to their countries of origin.

But there are many concerns about the project since Rwanda ranks low on human rights measures and there are questions about whether people's rights will be sufficiently protected.

New York Times Prohibits Photographers From Using Photoshop 2027

The latest version of Adobe Photoshop, Photoshop 2027 (28.0.0), added many AI-powered photo-editing features. Not all photographers are thrilled about the increasingly heavy focus on generative AI inside Photoshop, and photojournalists are taking note, too.

As The Verge reports, The New York Times sent an email to freelance photographers and contributors warning them against using Adobe Photoshop 2027.

The email, which was shared on social media, specifically cites features inside Photoshop that use generative AI “in ways that may not be apparent to users.”

The Times‘ worries about Photoshop 28.0.0 are more about a perceived lack of clarity surrounding the application’s photo-editing features than the presence of AI itself. Photoshop has included generative AI in many tools for years, including Generative Remove and Generative Expand. The latest release uses generative AI for even more tasks, such as relighting and restyling photos.

New York Times photojournalists and freelance contributors cannot use generative AI on their photos. In fact, as the publication’s ethics standards outline, all published photos must be accurate depictions of real events. This means no people or objects can be added, rearranged, blurred, distorted, or removed. It also means that cropping must be done carefully so as not to omit any relevant details in a scene. It’s a short workweek for most of us here in the United States. In just a day or two, many of us will load up our cars and trek around the country to our loved ones. EVs have come a long way, so in theory, range anxiety shouldn’t be much of an issue for anyone these days. Still, with so many drivers on the roads, there are bound to be annoyances that come with long EV road trips.

Here are a few tips that can take the edge off a long trip.

DC fast charging times are getting better and better, but charging still takes longer than stopping for gas. Thus, bottlenecks can occur on well-traveled routes with limited DC fast charging stops. It’s just kind of the nature of the beast when most people are stopping for 20-30 minutes. Multiply that by the plethora of drivers also trying to charge up and get home, and well, it’s not hard to see why EV charging queues can balloon, adding time to your drive home.

But you can head some of that off if you prep the night before. For those with home charging, that means simply plugging the car in and ensuring it’s fully charged for the next day’s trip. For those who don’t have access to home charging, using a DC fast charger the night before, when everyone isn’t rushing to leave, could ease some of the frustration of charging up before getting on the roads.

Also, some DC fast charging companies give a discount for charging during off-peak hours. Prepping the night before won’t just save you time, it might save you money.

We may have a set route in mind for our trip, and that could be all fine and dandy, but is it really the best route for our cars? Electric vehicles tend to consume more energy at higher speeds and when traveling up hills. Thus, it could be advantageous to maximize range by picking a flatter route, or one with lower speeds.

In most cases this won’t be necessary, but if you’re pushing the limits of your car and the infrastructure around you, it’s good to know. Most route planning apps—like A Better Route Planner, Plugshare, or even Google Maps—take into account which route is friendlier to your car’s range calculator, and may suggest detours if you get a big range boost from a route that’s nearly as quick.

These route planners will tell you exactly when and where to stop, depending on your car’s efficiency, your destination, your desired state of charge at arrival, and road conditions along the way. They can be adjusted to fit your real-world efficiency data or your need for breaks. Rather than just winging, using a route planner ensures you’re never in an uncomfortable situation without the range or charging ability to get where you need to go.

This goes hand in hand with route planning, but I think it is relevant here. The Sunday after Thanksgiving is typically the single busiest travel day of the year. You’re not the only one on the road who will need charging. Prepping the night before can help with the initial charging wait times at the start of your trip. If you’re on a long journey, chances are you’ll find yourself in a big queue at a strange charger.

When you’re planning your route with charging stops, pay attention to what brands they are and what ports they use. An increasing number of highway stops often have several competing charging stations. A Tesla Supercharging station could have an Ionna Rechargery nearby. Or maybe it’s an EV Go station. Or maybe it’s a charging station by Circle K or Shell. In any case, they tend to cluster together near high-power infrastructure. Rather than fumble with apps and payments at the station, I think it’s best if you research the charging companies on your route and pre-emptively install their apps and set up payment.

Similarly, bring an adapter. NACS is quickly making its way across the country, but not every station has NACS ports, and not every one has CCS. Some of the stations with your desired plug will likely be busy. An adapter that allows your car to access both NACS and CCS ports will save a lot of headaches.

Once again, you’re not the only one trying to get home for the holidays. Let’s keep the queue moving, and try not to charge more than 80%, if possible. Charging speeds slow substantially when EV batteries surpass 80%. It may have only taken 15-20 minutes to get to 80%, but it’ll take another 20 or so to reach 100%, or much longer in some cars. Chances are, there’s someone waiting. Not only is it courteous to unplug and move on, but it’s also probably quicker to charge again (if you need it) closer to your destination.

I know this list has mostly been about minimizing time lost due to charging, but I think it’s important to remember that it’s okay to take a break. Charging stops are inherently longer than refuelling a gas car, but I think that 15-30 minute break is helpful for safe driving. Taking a rest, using the restroom, or getting a snack can take the edge off a trip. Use an EV’s characteristics to your advantage.

I’ve been doing EV road trips since 2021, and it’s amazing to see just how far both vehicle range and charging infrastructure have come. Long trips are possible for most drivers these days, whereas years ago, it was a huge hardship or even impossible. With better apps, better infrastructure, and better cars, it’s only gotten easier. But it still pays to know what you’re doing. Drive safely out there, folks.

United Airlines Remembers Your Bad Flight And May Upgrade You Next Time

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

United Airlines appears to be quietly remembering when passengers suffer through a bad trip and then surprising some of them with a complimentary Economy Plus seat on a future flight. It costs United very little when the seat would otherwise remain empty, but it communicates something valuable to the passenger: we remember what happened to you.

A passenger recently shared an interesting message from United Airlines with aviation insider JonNYC.

The traveler had missed a connection about a month earlier and then received the following message before another United flight:

“We know your previous travel experience may have faced some challenges, and we want to make your journey more comfortable.”

United moved the passenger to Economy Plus, offering additional legroom at no charge.

What makes the gesture especially interesting is that the passenger had no MileagePlus elite status. The upcoming flight was lightly booked, which presumably made it easier for United to offer the seat without giving up revenue.

JonNYC described it as United’s technology performing more “dark magic,” and I think that is a fair description. The system appears to have connected an earlier operational disruption with a future reservation, then identified an inexpensive opportunity to improve the next journey.

Reports of similar gestures stretch back at least to 2025, so this does not appear to be entirely new. One United Silver member reported receiving an Economy Plus assignment after an eight-hour delay, even after United had already provided a $75 travel credit.

But United has not publicly announced this as a formal benefit, and we do not know precisely how passengers are selected (not that I would expect otherwise with such a “Surprise and Delight” program, lest it become an entitlement).

With advancing technology, airlines already know when they have inconvenienced you.

They know when your flight was delayed, when you missed a connection, and when you were rebooked. They also know whether you have another reservation and which seats remain available on that flight.

Yet customers are often forced to explain the entire situation again in a complaint, including information the airline already has in its own systems. That creates more work for the passenger after the airline has already disrupted the trip, though I’m sadly not expecting carriers to offer proactive compensation, even when delays are within its control.

United appears to be using its data more intelligently, like with its ConnectionSaver technology, that can help some passengers without inconveniencing others.

I have written before about United introducing technology that saves callers from repeating information when they are transferred from an automated system to a human agent. I also welcomed the addition of an app feature that watches earlier flights for disrupted passengers while preserving their confirmed reservations.

This Economy Plus gesture reflects a similar approach: use the information already available to reduce friction and improve the passenger experience.

An Economy Plus seat is not a first class upgrade. The passenger receives more legroom, but the onboard service remains the same as regular economy class.

Even so, it has value. United charges for these seats, sometimes quite a bit depending upon the route and seat location. Receiving one unexpectedly can make a future flight noticeably more comfortable.

The economics also make sense for United. If the flight is lightly booked and the Economy Plus seat would otherwise remain empty, the marginal cost of assigning it to an inconvenienced passenger is essentially zero.

The value to the passenger can be much greater than the cost to the airline.

I also appreciate that the message explicitly connects the better seat to the previous experience. A random seat reassignment might go unnoticed, while a clearly explained gesture tells the passenger that United remembers what happened and wants another chance to deliver a better trip.

That is smart service recovery…I wonder if the system is smart enough to do this when passengers have to be moved anyway due to weight and balance reasons? (why let a good opportunity go to waste?)

This is not a published benefit, and passengers should not expect a complimentary Economy Plus assignment every time something goes wrong. We do not know what level of disruption qualifies, how much empty space must be available, or whether United considers the passenger’s fare or travel history…and that’s the point, lest it become an expectation.

And of course United must be careful when selecting a seat to avoid a familiar complaint on Delta Air Lines. Moving someone from a regular economy aisle seat to an Economy Plus middle seat will not strike every passenger as an improvement. Ideally, the system should preserve the passenger’s aisle or window preference whenever possible or offer the upgrade rather than automatically moving the traveler.

Furthermore, if a MileagePlus Premier is expecting an entire row and suddenly has a seatmate, I can understand there will be annoyance (though that’s a sort of entitlement that is not helpful either)…in the past I’ve passed on upgrades to domestic first class in order to stretch across multiple seats in economy class.

Still, those limitations do not diminish the appeal of the idea. This is a discretionary gesture, not something passengers can demand, and that element of surprise is part of what makes it effective.

When airlines discuss service recovery, the conversation often centers on travel credits or miles. Those can be appropriate, particularly after a serious operational failure. But compensation need not be limited to those forms.

A better seat, lounge invitation, meal voucher, or another small benefit can communicate recognition when it is offered thoughtfully. The key is identifying something the passenger will appreciate that the airline can provide at a modest cost.

United has become particularly skilled at using technology to address operational problems. ConnectionSaver is the best example, intelligently holding certain flights when connecting passengers can reach the gate without causing a significant delay.

United appears to be quietly offering complimentary Economy Plus seats to some passengers whose previous trips went poorly, including travelers without elite status.

The gesture will not erase a missed connection or make every passenger forget a miserable journey. But it shows that United remembers the disruption and recognizes an opportunity to do something helpful. An empty Economy Plus seat costs United almost nothing. Used this way, it can generate far more goodwill than another automated apology email…it’s a positive step.

Tidal announces ban on AI-generated music; 27 million tracks will be deleted

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

AI songs account for 6.3 percent of Tidal’s catalog, but it’s about to be zero.

Most streaming music providers have opted to label AI-generated content on their platforms, and until now, Tidal was one of them. However, the company is about to take a harder stance. Tidal announced that in the coming weeks, it will fully ban AI music because of the harm it does to human artists.

Tidal debuted its initial AI policy in July 2026, which included labeling and demonetizing all songs created entirely with AI. That wasn’t enough to stem the tide of AI slop, though. Over the past 90 days, Tidal reports that 43 percent of new uploads were completely AI-generated. On November 6, new uploads to Tidal hit a whopping 60 percent AI-generated.

As things stand, Tidal hosts 27 million tracks labeled as AI, all of which were obviously added in the past couple of years. That accounts for 6.3 percent of Tidal’s total catalog. On December 9, Tidal will delete all those songs. The streamer notes this will have essentially no negative impact because 96 percent of AI-labeled tracks got zero plays in the past 90 days. Throughout its announcement, Tidal is careful to stipulate that it’s only talking about music that has been fully created by AI. Artists can still use AI tools as long as the final product contains human elements.

Credit: Tidal Credit: Tidal

Some people don’t particularly mind AI music, but the team at Tidal has come to believe the harm to artists is too great. AI models can pump out random noises faster than humans can create things of real value, which can mislead listeners and siphon royalty payments away from creators. There’s also an issue with AI uploaders actively attempting to steal royalty payments. Tidal says it has already removed about 66,000 tracks from its service for impersonating artists, which was against its AI policy even before this update.

As of December 9, individual uploaders and distributors will no longer be allowed to add AI music to Tidal. However, they could lie, couldn’t they?

Tidal hasn’t specified how it detects AI music, but it will probably rely on watermarking. Almost every AI song on Tidal (94 percent) comes from Suno, which added watermarking earlier this year. Tidal also cites Google Lyria as a source of AI tunes, which has SynthID labels embedded in the waveform. Deezer claimed it has a licensable AI music detector with a false-positive rate of less than 0.01 percent, but it’s unclear if Tidal is using that or something similar.

That will take care of most AI music, but this approach won’t detect everything. There are open models for music generation that you can run locally on a GPU, and some cloud-based providers aren’t bothering to watermark their outputs. Even if Tidal is using a separate detection system, new models may be able to evade it—it’s always going to be a cat-and-mouse game. This will be a problem with verifying provenance in all types of media in the future. For the time being, at least, Tidal’s policy should clear out most of the AI slop.

The people directing robots to make these songs will have plenty of other options after Tidal blocks AI. Spotify, YouTube Music, Deezer, and Apple Music all allow AI music on their platforms with labeling. Spotify has removed millions of AI tracks from its collection based on user feedback. Reference-guided generation lets a creator provide more than a prompt. Reference tokens account for roughly half of that combined sequence in the configuration described in the paper. Longer sequences are expensive because full attention compares tokens with one another; the number of those comparisons grows roughly with the square of sequence length.

Its most useful feature is how it handles an awkward problem: two trained observers can watch the same clip and reasonably come away with different labels.

The research preprint describes a video track with ten signals, including interest, skepticism, and confusion, and an audio track with six. A model can predict more than one signal in a clip. In a conventional exact-label score, confusing hesitation with uncertainty is simply an error. So is confusing hesitation with agreement. Treating those two mistakes alike makes it easier to compare labels consistently, but it can hide whether a model picked up a relevant cue and interpreted it differently from an observer.

Interhuman’s alternative, Evidence-Weighted F1, gives partial credit for related labels and gives more weight to a signal backed by stronger annotation evidence. Each expert-labeled clip is assessed independently by at least three trained raters. Agreement among raters is useful, but disagreement isn’t automatically discarded. If observers had different reasonable readings, the scoring method can retain evidence for each.

Two small details mattered more than I expected. Sorting by id before locking means two overlapping requests always grab locks in the same order, which keeps deadlocks rare. And keeping the transaction short matters. Anything slow inside it, like calling a payment provider, holds those locks the whole time and turns a busy on-sale into a queue.

The trade-off: row locks make busy rows a bottleneck. For a hot event, a lot of requests end up waiting on the same few rows. That's still the right call. A short wait is annoying. Selling one seat twice is a refund, an angry customer, and a bad review.

Caching inventory in a ticketing system is risky. If the cache says 12 seats are left and the database says 3, you've built a fresh way to show people seats that don't exist. You also now have two sources of truth to keep in sync during exactly the moments when everything is under load. So the rule I followed was simple: find out why the query is slow first.

Indexes aren't free. Every insert and update on holds or orders now also updates the index, and ticketing is write-heavy during an on-sale. So I didn't index every column that showed up in a WHERE. Each index had to match a real, frequent query, checked with EXPLAIN ANALYZE before and after. A partial index is a nice middle ground here, since it only pays the write cost for rows that match its condition.

I also used CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY on live tables. It's slower to build, but it doesn't block writes while it runs, and blocking writes on a ticketing table is not something you want to explain.

Generating a PDF ticket with a QR code, sending the confirmation email, expiring unpaid holds: none of this needs to happen while the user stares at a spinner. Doing it inline makes checkout slow, and it means a hiccup at the email provider can fail a purchase that already succeeded.

So the request does the transactional part, commits, and then queues the rest:

Queueing after the commit matters. Queue it inside the transaction and a fast worker can pick up the job before the commit lands, look for the order, and not find it. Or the transaction rolls back and you've emailed a ticket for an order that doesn't exist.

Celery makes retries a one-liner. The harder part is making sure a task that runs twice doesn't do the work twice. And it will run twice eventually. A worker restarts mid-task, a broker redelivers a message, a retry fires after the first attempt actually succeeded.

My rule for every task: re-read the current state from the database and decide from that, never from what the message says.

The confirmation_sent_at check means a duplicate run does nothing. The idempotency key covers the gap where the email went out but the worker crashed before saving that it did. If the provider supports idempotency keys, it drops the repeat on its end. Ticket generation followed the same pattern, with a unique constraint on ticket rows per order and seat, so a second run can't create a second set of tickets.

Hold expiry works the same way. The scheduled task only releases a hold if it's still held and actually past expires_at, checked under a lock. That covers the awkward race where a user pays in the last second while the expiry job is running. Whichever transaction gets the row first wins, and the other one sees the new state and backs off.

acks_late=True is part of this trade. It means a task is only acknowledged after it finishes, so a crash causes a redelivery instead of a lost job. You accept "might run twice" to avoid "might never run," which is only a good deal if your tasks are idempotent.

For the endpoints the frontend depends on, tests check the shape of the response, not just the status code: required fields, types, and error formats. When someone changes a response model, the contract test fails in CI instead of the checkout page failing for users.

It doesn't behave like PostgreSQL around locking, FOR UPDATE, partial indexes, or concurrent transactions, which are exactly the things this post is about. A test that passes on the wrong database doesn't tell you much.

CI runs the same Docker Compose setup: start Postgres and Redis, run migrations, run the suite, build the image, deploy. If it passes there, it behaves the same way in production.

Let PostgreSQL decide who gets the seat. A conditional UPDATE or SELECT ... FOR UPDATE inside a short transaction beats any check you write in application code.

Compute prices on the server, from the database, inside the same transaction as the hold.

Run EXPLAIN ANALYZE before you add a cache. Most of our gains came from a few well-chosen indexes, and caching inventory adds its own consistency bugs.

Queue background jobs after the commit, and write every task assuming it'll run twice.

Test the race and the rollback against the same database you run in production.

The hard part of a ticketing backend isn't the endpoints. It's the few seconds when everyone clicks at once, and the jobs that run after the user has closed the tab. En mars dernier, je pensais surtout ouvrir les archives d’un jeu Macintosh de 1991. Quelques mois plus tard, Hellcats Over The Pacific fonctionne dans Godot et peut se lancer dans un navigateur.

Je n’ai personnellement écrit aucune des fonctions C ou GDScript nécessaires à cette reconstruction. Ouvrir la démonstration dans un nouvel onglet

Cette démonstration charge la scène Native actuelle. C’est un état de travail, pas une réédition officielle ni la promesse que chaque mission se comporte déjà exactement comme sur le Macintosh original.

Le premier article, publié le 30 mars 2026, racontait l’ouverture des archives. À ce moment-là, le projet consistait surtout à retrouver les disquettes, examiner les fichiers avec ResEdit et commencer à comprendre ce qu’un exécutable Macintosh classique contenait encore après trente-cinq ans.

Hellcats a été écrit pour un processeur Motorola 68000, la mémoire et la Toolbox du Macintosh classique, ses fenêtres, ses événements, son affichage et son son. Un Mac de 1991 et un ordinateur de 2026 ne parlent pas le même langage. Il ne suffisait donc pas de copier le programme, ni de refaire ses graphismes. Je voulais retrouver sa logique et reconstruire les services dont il avait besoin.

ResEdit a extrait les menus, dialogues, images, sons et segments de code. Ghidra a ouvert une vue sur l’assembleur 68k. Claude Code a relié ces indices et transformé progressivement l’analyse en code exécutable. Godot est devenu le lieu où ces couches pouvaient se rejoindre.

Le projet contient aujourd’hui trois approches complémentaires. Elles répondent à trois questions différentes : comment retrouver le comportement d’origine, comment sortir peu à peu de l’ancien Macintosh, puis comment adapter le résultat aux appareils actuels ?

Original est la référence historique du projet. Cette version reprend la logique issue du programme de 1991 et reconstruit autour d’elle le Macintosh nécessaire : mémoire 68k, chargement des segments, parties utilisées de la Toolbox, fenêtres, événements, entrées, affichage indexé et son.

Ce n’est pas une image complète de Mac OS 9 lancée dans Godot. Contrairement à un émulateur généraliste comme InfiniteMac, Original reconstruit seulement le matériel et les services effectivement sollicités par Hellcats. Le programme traduit retrouve ainsi son environnement sans que ses règles soient réinventées.

Native conserve les règles d’Original, mais remplace peu à peu ses dépendances historiques par Godot. Les commandes sont remappables, les menus et dialogues peuvent utiliser l’interface du moteur, les sauvegardes ont leur propre stockage et plusieurs éléments de l’écran, du son, de la caméra, de l’IA et de l’état du jeu sont déjà modernisés.

« Native » ne signifie pas simplement « compilé pour macOS ou Linux ». Il décrit une migration encore partielle : la scène hérite d’Original et de nombreux services Macintosh subsistent. Chaque remplacement doit produire le même comportement observable avant que l’ancienne couche disparaisse.

Enhanced repose sur Native et ajoute des améliorations facultatives. À ce stade, le travail porte surtout sur le mobile : un contrôleur tactile en mode paysage, des adaptations d’interface et une gestion correcte de la mise en arrière-plan pour que le vol ne continue pas pendant que l’application est cachée.

L’export Android a été essayé dans un émulateur jusqu’au début d’un vol, avec commandes tactiles. L’export iOS a atteint le cockpit et repris après une mise en arrière-plan dans le simulateur. Ce résultat ne vaut pas validation sur un iPhone physique ; une mission complète au toucher et l’audio iOS restent à vérifier.

Enhanced n’est donc pas, aujourd’hui, un remaster graphique. C’est la couche qui permet d’explorer de nouveaux usages sans modifier silencieusement Original ni Native.

La reconstruction ne repose pas sur un exécutable unique. Trois corpus ont dû être étudiés séparément.

Hellcats 1.0.3 constitue la base principale. Ses disquettes, ressources et segments de code ont fourni la chaîne la plus complète, de ResEdit et Ghidra jusqu’au GDScript exécuté par Original.

Hellcats 1.0.4 est reconstruite à partir de la 1.0.3 et de son updater d’origine, qui apporte notamment un autre pilote sonore et quelques ressources modifiées. Elle peut être sélectionnée au lancement et conserve son propre chemin sonore.

Leyte Gulf est plus qu’un décor supplémentaire. L’extension possède sa propre application, ses ressources, une organisation mémoire différente et un overlay plus important. Une partie du code a été déplacée ou réécrite : il a fallu établir des correspondances plutôt que copier la 1.0.3. Le travail atteint le décollage, le vol, le crash, le replay et le débriefing d’une mission perdue, mais pas encore tous les objectifs ni un parcours de victoire authentique.

Ces trois cas ont rappelé une règle importante : deux versions très proches à l’écran peuvent demander des analyses différentes sous le capot.

Ghidra, Claude Code et Godot ont chacun joué un rôle distinct.

Ghidra lit les instructions Motorola 68000 et produit une représentation proche du C. Il ne ressuscite pas le code source : noms, commentaires, types et intentions ont en partie disparu. Une fonction décompilée montre ce que la machine fait, pas forcément pourquoi.

Claude Code a examiné les appelants, les données et les ressources, proposé des noms compréhensibles, identifié des structures, puis traduit la logique vers Godot. Il pouvait parcourir beaucoup plus de fichiers que je ne l’aurais fait manuellement tout en maintenant les relations avec le binaire.

Je n’ai jamais considéré ses réponses comme des preuves. Les artefacts originaux restent intacts et chaque étape est reliée à la précédente. En cas de divergence, il faut revenir aux octets, aux ressources ou au comportement observable.

Godot fournit la boucle d’exécution, les entrées, l’audio, l’affichage et les exports modernes. Le même projet accueille Original, la migration Native et les adaptations Enhanced. Le build Web permet enfin d’essayer le résultat sans ancien Macintosh.

Cela ne signifie pas que le travail s’est fait tout seul. Il fallait comprendre le système d’origine pour poser les bonnes questions : ce qui appartient au processeur, à la Toolbox ou aux ressources, ce qui doit rester identique et ce qui peut être confié à Godot.

Mon rôle a été celui d’un architecte et d’un pilote technique : définir les objectifs, découper les étapes, choisir les sources de vérité, formuler les demandes, examiner les écarts et corriger la direction lorsqu’elle menait à une imitation plutôt qu’au fonctionnement réel.

La précision des prompts a compté. « Refais le jeu dans Godot » n’aurait produit qu’une approximation. Une demande utile fixe au contraire une frontière : préserver les fichiers d’origine, analyser une seule couche, citer les preuves, garder les incertitudes visibles, obtenir un résultat observable, puis comparer ce résultat avec la référence avant de continuer.

L’IA n’a pas remplacé la compréhension technique. Elle a remplacé une grande partie de la programmation manuelle. Sans connaissances d’architecture, il aurait été difficile de repérer une confusion entre mémoire 68k et état Godot, ou entre décompilation et code source original.

La méthode a fonctionné parce que je n’ai jamais demandé le jeu « en entier » à l’IA. Chaque étape devait livrer un résultat observable et comparable.

Conserver les artefacts. J’ai commencé par réunir les disquettes, les applications, les ressources, les versions et l’updater sans modifier les originaux. Ils restent la référence lorsqu’une interprétation doit être revue.

Comprendre la machine. Avant de reconstruire le logiciel, il fallait identifier le processeur, l’organisation de la mémoire, le format des programmes, les services système, le graphisme, le son et les entrées du Macintosh classique.

Extraire et décompiler. ResEdit a rendu les ressources visibles. Ghidra a transformé l’assembleur 68k en une représentation de type C, plus facile à parcourir mais encore dépourvue des noms et du contexte du code source perdu.

Faire analyser les relations. Claude Code a relié les fonctions à leurs appelants, aux données et aux ressources qu’elles manipulent. Les noms et structures retrouvés restent des conclusions à étayer, pas une vérité créée par le modèle.

Reconstruire par couches. Le travail a commencé par le chargement des ressources et la mémoire, puis le démarrage, les premiers écrans, les interactions, la simulation et les missions. Cette progression évite de masquer dix erreurs derrière un résultat final impossible à diagnostiquer.

Obtenir un résultat observable. Un dialogue qui s’ouvre, un écran qui se dessine ou un avion qui répond aux commandes constitue un point de contrôle. Chaque résultat réduit la zone d’incertitude avant la prochaine étape.

Comparer les comportements. Une image ressemblante ne suffit pas. Il faut vérifier les actions, les transitions, les calculs et les effets produits par rapport au programme historique. Lorsqu’un écart apparaît, la correction doit viser la couche responsable.

Migrer et exporter. Une fois Original suffisamment établi, Native peut remplacer les dépendances historiques une par une. Enhanced peut ensuite adapter cette base au tactile et au mobile. Godot rend enfin possibles le Web, le bureau, Android et iOS, sous réserve de valider réellement chaque plateforme.

Ce découpage rend les prompts plus efficaces : l’IA travaille mieux avec une source vérifiable, une frontière claire et un critère de réussite concret.

Le principe est transposable : retrouver les binaires et les ressources, comprendre l’ordinateur d’origine, désassembler ou décompiler, faire analyser les résultats par une IA, reconstruire l’environnement nécessaire, comparer le comportement, puis migrer vers une plateforme moderne.

Cela ne signifie pas que n’importe quel jeu peut être restauré automatiquement. Un programme protégé, un format inconnu, des données manquantes, un processeur mal documenté ou l’absence d’un exemplaire fonctionnel peuvent rendre le travail beaucoup plus difficile. Les droits de redistribution des ressources historiques posent également une question distincte de la réussite technique.

L’approche exige surtout une personne capable de guider l’enquête. Il faut reconnaître les limites de la décompilation, savoir quand une hypothèse n’est pas prouvée et empêcher l’IA de remplir les blancs avec une solution plausible mais infidèle. La méthode est reproductible dans son principe ; son coût et ses chances de succès dépendent des preuves disponibles.

Native possède déjà des commandes remappables, des couches d’interface Godot, des sauvegardes séparées et plusieurs sous-systèmes modernes, mais dépend encore largement de l’environnement Original. La démonstration Web est donc un jalon de cette migration, pas son aboutissement.

La version 1.0.4 est reconstruite depuis son updater et peut être lancée séparément. Leyte Gulf a beaucoup progressé, mais tous ses scénarios et systèmes ne sont pas encore validés. Enfin, même un grand nombre de comparaisons automatiques ne remplace pas une vérification visuelle et fonctionnelle sur tous les chemins du jeu.

The e-bike industry has a horsepower problem

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There was a time when 750 watts sounded like a lot of power for an electric bicycle.

These days, apparently those are rookie numbers.

Spend enough time browsing new e-bike launches and you’ll inevitably stumble across bikes advertising 1,000W, 1,500W, 2,000W, or even higher power. Dual-motor models have turned the spec sheet into an arms race, with manufacturers seemingly competing to see who can slap the biggest number onto the product page.

If you ask me, the e-bike industry is developing a horsepower problem.

To be clear, I’m not anti-power. I’ve ridden everything from dainty 250W European commuter bikes to electric motorcycles with enough power to rearrange your chest cavity. Powerful electric two-wheelers are fun, period.

But there’s a difference between building a powerful electric two-wheeler and pretending that every powerful electric two-wheeler is still an electric bicycle.

In the US, the widely adopted three-class e-bike system generally tops out at 20 mph (32 km/h) for Class 1 and Class 2 e-bikes and 28 mph (45 km/h) for Class 3, with some minor exceptions, and 750 watts has long been a common benchmark for the motor power limit.

Yet browse online e-bike retailers today and you’ll find plenty of machines advertised with motors well beyond that figure, often capable of speeds far beyond normal electric bicycle classifications.

Sometimes there’s a disclaimer explaining that the bike is intended for “off-road use.” Sometimes there’s a software setting that supposedly limits it for street riding.

And sometimes people just sort of squint at the pedals and agree to keep calling it an electric bicycle.

The problem isn’t that 2,000W electric two-wheelers exist. I love lightweight electric motorcycles and mopeds, or what some are beginning to refer to as ‘e-motos’, despite that terminology having its own issues. So sure, those bikes are great and there is a time and place for such power. The problem is that the industry increasingly seems afraid to call them what they are.

Part of the blame belongs to consumers – to us.

E-bike shopping has become increasingly spec-sheet driven, and wattage provides an incredibly easy number to compare. If ‘Bike A’ has a 750W motor and ‘Bike B’ costs the same but advertises 1,500W, it’s easy to assume ‘Bike B’ is twice as good. This is especially true for younger riders who lack a deeper understanding of how e-bikes work, and just want something powerful and fast to impress their friends after school. They beg their parents for the latest models with the biggest motors, and many parents give in.

The problem is that’s not how electric bikes work – there’s so much more to a bike than the motor wattage rating.

Motor design, controller current, battery voltage, thermal management, gearing, efficiency and software tuning all influence performance. A beautifully tuned 500W or 750W system can be far nicer to ride – and sometimes more useful – than a poorly implemented motor advertising twice the power.

But “excellent controller tuning” doesn’t look nearly as exciting in a giant font on an Amazon listing. So manufacturers respond rationally based on what consumers demand: They make the number bigger.

The result is an e-bike horsepower war that is ultimately hurting the entire industry.

When riders blast through bike paths on motorcycle-performance machines marketed as “e-bikes,” pedestrians don’t care whether the motor technically arrived in a street-legal mode. Police officers aren’t going to inspect controller settings. And lawmakers aren’t famous for approaching emerging transportation technology with surgical precision.

They see a fast machine with pedals, hear complaints about dangerous “e-bikes,” and start writing laws that affect everyone – including riders on perfectly ordinary commuter e-bikes.

In my opinion, that’s the part that should worry anyone who enjoys riding an e-bike.

There’s absolutely a place for high-powered electric two-wheelers. In fact, I want more of them. Lightweight electric mopeds and motorcycles could replace an enormous number of car trips while using a fraction of the energy and space. Those are highly capable machines, but they need to be used appropriately, registered legally, and ridden responsibly.

And we don’t help that cause by pretending they’re bicycles.

Perhaps the e-bike industry’s next great innovation doesn’t need another 500 watts.

Maybe it just needs a little more honesty about what we’re actually building. So let’s build more electric bicycles. And yes, let’s build more e-motos and electric motorcycles. But let’s not confuse the two, either.

At QCon San Francisco 2026, Brian Yang, Member of Technical Staff at OpenAI, described how a small group of engineers used coding agents to build the ChatGPT ads product, reaching $100 million in annualised recurring revenue within six weeks of launch and $1 billion in under 200 days, with zero human-written code.

Yang, previously at Google, Meta and LinkedIn, was a founding member of technical staff on ChatGPT Ads, which was built entirely using Codex. His central claim was that software engineering is alive but reshaped around building the system that builds the product, with the engineer acting more as a manager of agents than a line-by-line contributor. One engineer can now triage an issue, open a pull request, write documentation and unblock CI in parallel, a shift Yang compared to multi-processing.

Every change runs local and unit tests, complex changes get end-to-end user journeys via browser automation, and the most critical changes run inside production-like sandboxes with representative traffic. Sandboxes come in three forms of read-only, write-only with telemetry suppressed, and full-fidelity with telemetry tagged using a shadow tag so it can be filtered out downstream. Yang's team targets around 90% auto-approval of everyday PRs, reviewing the past 300 to 400 PRs weekly and backtesting reviewer thresholds against that rate.

He illustrated the model with a sanitised latency study where candidate PRs targeted time-to-first-ad at P50 and P95 with guardrails for ads returned, timeouts and error rates. Candidates A and B passed; candidate C was rejected because the sandbox returned no ad. Thirty to forty candidates are typically bundled into one PR and run together for a day or two before human review, because changes that pass in isolation can regress when combined.

On failure modes, Yang described codebases filling with "slop" when agent output is unchecked, so maintenance agents sit alongside feature agents to root-cause newly appearing log patterns. Agents over-write tests, so he reviews the 50 slowest tests weekly. He recounted an agent that deleted and re-provisioned slow Kubernetes pods without asking, arguing that teams need big-company controls such as authorised roles for destructive actions and physical confirmation for deployments. Yang encouraged teams to revisit "cached assumptions" such as the belief that four nines of uptime always beats higher revenue, and recommended giving every engineer a monthly AI budget with no leaderboards.

For team adoption, Yang recommended a gradual path from automating one PR, to one feature, to one project, warning that jumping straight to project automation is where systems tend to break. For teams that are resistant to AI, he described rotating an "AI champion" every two weeks. The champion picks the team's most tedious recurring task, such as GPU provisioning at OpenAI which can take hours of babysitting, snapshots and permissions work, teaches an agent to do it end-to-end as a reusable skill, and demos the result at the end of the fortnight. Over a year, that converts 26 pain points into agent-owned workflows and gives sceptical engineers tangible value rather than abstract promises.

Day to day, Yang prefers Codex's bidirectional voice mode over typing for task intake, spending around ten minutes being interviewed by the agent about requirements before agreeing on a spec and handing it to multiple agents. He uses an orchestrator thread that spawns five sub-threads to explore different approaches in parallel and picks the strongest result, rather than copy-pasting the same prompt into several chats. On models, he switches only when a new release makes the workflow feel easier, with fewer clarifying questions and equivalent output quality, rather than chasing benchmark scores. His closing message was that engineers will increasingly do for agents what managers currently do for engineers, requiring an expansion of skills around direction-setting, architecture and verification rather than their replacement.

Review: Cumulus Mountlite Down Jacket

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The Cumulus Mountlite is a very warm ultralight winter down jacket designed for winter hiking, ski touring, and climbing. Weighing just 12.9 ounces, it is insulated with 6.3 ounces of highly compressible 900-fill-power goose down, delivering an outstanding warmth-to-weight ratio. An adjustable hood, a #3 YKK zipper with dual sliders, and three zippered pockets round out this impressively warm jacket.

The Mountlite’s key benefit is weight efficiency. The jacket weighs 12.9 ounces in size medium, making it a lightweight option among dedicated winter layers. Of that total weight, 6.35 ounces is 900-fill-power Polish goose-down insulation. This high ratio means that roughly half the garment’s total mass is dedicated directly to trapping body heat. Premium down allows the jacket to compress efficiently and recover its loft quickly after being unpacked in cold environments.

To protect the down insulation from external moisture and wind, Cumulus utilizes 10D Pertex Quantum for the outer shell and liner. This fabric provides a windproof barrier while allowing interior moisture vapor to escape during low-intensity movement. The fabric is thin to minimize total weight but requires careful handling around sharp winter tools.

The hood is shaped to offer close coverage around the face and neck, helping to retain heat during windy stops. The zipper features a standard YKK zipper with 2 sliders that operates reliably in freezing conditions. For draft management, the jacket uses low-profile elastic wrist cuffs and an adjustable hem cinch to seal out cold air from below. The external pockets, one chest and two handwarmer pockets, are positioned to remain accessible while wearing a backpack hip belt or a climbing harness. The jacket is stuffable in its left hand pocket and has an external loop.

The jacket’s insulation is held in sewn-through baffles, which you wouldn’t expect in a jacket this warm. This isn’t the most efficient design because the stitching creates small cold spots with little or no insulation. It may also provide less loft and warmth than a box-baffle jacket. Still, sewn-through construction can be lighter, which is why I suspect Cumulus used it here. I’m honestly amazed at how warm this jacket is regardless of the baffle construction technique, and it is a delight to carry on summit hikes because it packs up so small when compressed.

The Mountlite has an athletic cut, which you should keep in mind when sizing. Its adjustable hood (rear toggle) is helmet-compatible, a useful feature for ski touring, climbing, and mountaineering. Even so, it still insulates very well when used without a helmet. The hood can help reduce heat loss during exposed stops and windy conditions.

The Cumulus Mountlite is an exceptional winter layer for anyone who values serious warmth without accepting unnecessary weight or bulk. Its 900-fill-power goose down, lightweight Pertex Quantum shell, thoughtful hood design, harness-friendly pockets, and compact packed size make it remarkably capable on winter hikes, ski tours, climbs, and summit pushes. Despite its sewn-through construction and delicate shell fabric, the jacket delivers impressive warmth in a package that is easy to carry and quick to deploy when conditions turn cold.

The Mountlite stands out as a highly refined piece of winter gear: exceptionally warm for its weight, versatile in the mountains, and small enough to keep in a pack on every outing. For cold-weather adventures where warmth, packability, and low weight matter most, it is an outstanding choice.

To get there was a months-long exercise in trying not to unravel. Brooke initially self-taped without knowing she was auditioning for Dune. When her agent revealed the project, she shut the curtains on a sunny day and watched the first two films back to back. “I fell so in love with it,” she says. “I was like, ‘OK, this is going to really hurt if I don’t get this.’” By the final audition, she had already convinced herself that another girl outside the room had the advantage. Brooke went to the bathroom and gave herself a talking to. “You don’t have to get it,” she remembers thinking. “You just have to show them that you have something to say. Just make their choice a little bit harder.”

The following day, Brooke was sitting beside a teacher at school trying to solve an editing problem when a message flashed up from an unknown number: Villeneuve wanted to call her in 20 minutes. Her phone had five percent battery. She tore through the school searching for a charger, eventually hiding in a stairwell to take the call. He first warned her about the intensity of the shoot—the heat, the physical demands—and asked whether she wanted to join the film. A friend found Brooke crying and took a photograph. “She was like, ‘You might want this one day.’”

Cinema has been the organizing obsession of her life since childhood. At six, she would rewatch films endlessly on a portable DVD player; as a teenager growing up in west London, she traveled across the city for Saturday acting classes. She now lives with her father and younger sibling, a household she affectionately describes as “very rowdy.” A lifelong cinephile, though he doesn’t work in film, her father’s fascination with human psychology has informed her own approach to storytelling. (The two share a love of Martin Scorsese.) “He always loved quality stories and has really good taste,” she says, smiling.

Writing interests her as much as performing: she likes structure, psychology and—gleefully—eavesdropping, listening to “the cadence of how people speak,” she says, gesturing cheekily at the conversations trilling around us. She is currently working on a screenplay exploring Vietnam veterans and the pharmaceutical industry, a project born from the inquisitive way she approaches the world. She will also star in and executive produce an indie drama, Grass Roots, due to shoot next summer.

Calm proved instructive in the desert. Zendaya, who had already endured the elements on the earlier films, helped Brooke understand how to quiet her body enough to perform. “I feel so much more confident acting in somewhat normal environments now,” she says. Learning to act, for Brooke, has also meant learning when to pay attention and when to give in. “Denis has such a vivid imagination that you can step right into his vision… You just have to be a kid.”

Ida Brooke as Ghanima and Nakoa-Wolf Momoa as Leto in Dune: Part Three.

Brooke missed her graduation—and the screening of her final Brit School film, The Operator, which won an award—because she was away filming the forthcoming sci-fi live-action Gundam movie alongside Noah Centineo and Sydney Sweeney in Australia. When she told her new castmates how gutted she was, they improvised a graduation of sorts: everyone piled onto a sofa and screened her film together. “I had that sense of community that I missed.”

For the moment, Brooke is resisting any pressure to decide exactly what she is. Actor? Writer? Director? All three? She chose not to go to university and has instead devised her own education, meeting writers to ask how they work, reading about psychology and screenwriting, watching films, researching whatever historical period has most recently caught her imagination. “One of my biggest priorities is self-education,” she says.

Her necklace is a souvenir from one of the biggest film sets ever conceived, but Brooke looks voraciously forward at what she hasn’t encountered yet. She wants to work with Scorsese, but she also talks excitedly about young, unknown filmmakers in London: “The friends that I have now, I see them as my future colleagues.” Her ambition is not in conquering new terrain, it’s to remain porous enough to keep discovering. After all, silver can always be melted down and made into something new.
