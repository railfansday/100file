David Schwartz, the co-creator of the XRP Ledger, confirmed on 11th November that his XRP holdings fell from roughly 26 million to about 3 million. When describing his intentions behind the move, he clarified,

I wish I was more comfortable with risk, but I’m just not that person.

The timing of this announcement landed just as XRP lost the $1.15 support zone, leaving the altcoin to trade near $1.05 at press time.

XRP’s RSI had a reading of 32, highlighting the altcoin’s weak momentum. This also implied it wasn’t exactly oversold. $1.00 – $1.03 is the next demand zone, but sustained crashing of the price will open up $0.85 – $0.88.

CoinGecko’s data showed that the market cap fell from $81 billion on 3rd November to $61 billion on 10th November — a $20 billion wipeout in a week. At press time, though, the market cap had risen slightly to $67.22 billion.

The risk-adjusted picture seemed to be just as concerning. According to CryptoQuant data (via ArabxChain), XRP’s Sharpe-like ratio on Binance plummeted to roughly -0.32 as of 10th November, hitting a multi-month low.

A negative Sharpe-like ratio means that the returns XRP generated didn’t even compensate investors. Instead, they bore the risk for no reason while the price cratered from $1.25 to $1.04.

Now, here’s where it gets interesting — On 11th November, analyst Ali Martinez showed that XRP whales continued to accumulate 65.32 million tokens over four days, thereby pushing their holdings to 4.00 billion.

AMBCrypto previously reported that wallets holding at least 1 million XRP bounced to 2,147, a 2026 high, even as the market cap fell by 29%.

Unfortunately for XRP, wallet balance changes don’t prove intent. There is ultimately no proof that whales are accumulating to sell.

Santiment underlined this notion, observing that the growth during price weakness merely “suggests conviction.” So, for now, the 1M+ wallet count is the metric to track. If it keeps climbing without a price hike, XRP’s bull case will get concerning.

XRP is simply caught between two clashing signals. The first is the insider who knows the ledger best and holds almost none of the altcoin. The second group are the largest players in the game, who are persistent in their buying.

The chart presently favors the former CTO’s caution, not the whales’ conviction.

But a national housing demand signal doesn’t mean every local market is following. And even within the same metro area, buyer activity can tell very different stories.

The Deltona–Daytona Beach–Ormond Beach metro area in Florida offers a useful example.

New pending listings across the metro area increased 20.2% year over year in October, according to HousingWire Data. A closer look at individual cities, however, reveals that the improvement was far from uniform.

In Ormond Beach, Fla., new pending listings increased 116.7% year over year in October, rising from 72 in 2025 to 156 in 2026.

ElevenLabs Unveils Model Customization, ElevenAI and Partner Program

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

ElevenLabs announced on November 11, 2026 that it will help businesses fine-tune selected open-source language models and its voice models using data from live deployments, alongside a new ElevenAI workplace app and partner program introduced at its New York Summit.

The package extends ElevenLabs beyond voice generation and transcription into software for managing customer conversations and employee work. CEO Mati Staniszewski said conversational agents would become “critical infrastructure for every enterprise, as essential as cloud or payments” within a few years, framing the products as a broader enterprise deployment push.

ElevenLabs plans to begin the customization work in the coming months. Businesses will work with its researchers to tune models for their own customers and operating context, selecting optimization metrics such as customer satisfaction or reliability. Because the data comes from live deployments, companies can continue refining the models as interactions accumulate rather than relying only on a general industry model.

ElevenLabs already applies the industry-specific approach through Scribe v3 Medical, a healthcare transcription model built to recognize specialized terminology. Staniszewski said a domain-optimized financial-services model is also in development.

The previewed Interaction Engine addresses a different problem: deciding how a conversation should run. It manages turn-taking and interruptions and can assign different language models to different parts of an exchange, using a cheaper model for triage and a frontier model for complex tasks. The design keeps routine exchanges from requiring a frontier model at every step and is intended to limit token use.

ElevenLabs says ElevenAgents, its platform for deploying voice and chat agents, will add Memory and Routines. Memory will recognize returning authenticated customers and distill each conversation into a structured record of preferences, constraints and future occasions, while leaving the customer relationship management system as the primary customer record. A customer who prefers Spanish and can only talk after 5 p.m., for example, would not need to repeat those details on the next call.

Routines, also forthcoming, is designed to automate multi-step work before, during and after conversations, connecting with existing tools and systems. Examples include pre-screening a mortgage applicant and calling back within minutes, reminding patients about visits and offering canceled slots to waitlisted patients, emailing reports, writing to a customer relationship management system or initiating calls. Together, the features move ElevenAgents beyond answering inquiries toward retaining context and completing the work surrounding a conversation.

For employees, Staniszewski previewed ElevenAI, an app that listens to spoken conversations, including in-person meetings, and produces notes, summaries and action items. It can also provide live assistance and pass context between people and agents. ElevenLabs says the app takes meeting notes with participants’ consent and includes notice and consent mechanisms.

Free early access to ElevenAI opened with the announcement. Dictation and meeting notes use Scribe v3, which ElevenLabs describes as its recorded-audio transcription model covering 102 languages; the separate Scribe v3 Live variant is built for low-latency, real-time audio.

ElevenLabs reports more than 550 partners across 44 countries, including Google Cloud, IBM, Deloitte, DXC and Genesys. The new Partner Program lets consultancies, systems integrators, software vendors and agencies integrate ElevenAgents into enterprise technology stacks or work with ElevenLabs’ forward-deployed engineers on complex deployments. An alliance with McKinsey and QuantumBlack, AI by McKinsey, pairs the platform with McKinsey’s enterprise-transformation work.

The accompanying Marketplace initially offers a curated catalog of partner-built applications for healthcare, financial services, insurance, education and retail. Enterprises can browse the applications and contact their developers; purchasing through existing ElevenLabs commitments is planned to start in 2027.

ElevenLabs and NVIDIA announced that Nemotron 3.5 Super VL is available in ElevenAgents and that ElevenLabs plans to add 15 MW of NVIDIA Vera Rubin compute capacity on top of its existing Blackwell Ultra systems. The added capacity is intended for large deployments with security or data-residency requirements and to give ElevenLabs researchers access to optimized compute.

The expanded collaboration also covers an on-premises voice agent for Centralna e-Rejestracja, the central appointment system in Poland’s national e-Health platform, with EasyCall supporting delivery. The agent calls patients who only have landlines to remind them about appointments and help them reschedule. ElevenLabs says the central system handles 40 million appointments annually and has no-show rates of 10% to 20%; the deployment uses NVIDIA Confidential Computing to keep citizen data and audio within government infrastructure and protect them during processing.

The Summit package makes ElevenLabs’ enterprise strategy dependent on more than model performance: tuning and Memory draw on data accumulated in live use, Routines connects conversations to business systems, and the partner program and Marketplace create routes into enterprises. The central execution challenge is therefore twofold: moving the previewed and early-access products into broader production use, and progressing Marketplace browsing to purchases through existing ElevenLabs commitments.

The last commit to its code was on 2026-03-23 and the Deployment had pointed at the same image tag since then. The outage started with a disk filling up on the node. It lasted because of what a registry cron job had done to that image on 2026-03-24.

This is a two-node k3s cluster that I run alone for a few side projects. Times are UTC.

By the morning of 2026-11-11 the Deployment's ReplicaSet had three pods. The original, created in March, had ended on 2026-11-08 at 13:46:42 with exit code 0 and phase Succeeded. A replacement created one second later had never run and ended with ContainerStatusUnknown, exit code 137. A second replacement, created at 16:10:52, was Pending with this event repeating every five minutes:

The audit tool I mention below counted 1,565 of these events in 24 hours on that node. On the registry side, the server log had 1,068 requests for that manifest digest since 2026-11-09 23:58, every one answered 404 manifest unknown.

The usual causes of manifest unknown did not fit. The tag existed in the registry's filesystem. The same node had pulled other images from the same registry that afternoon. The Deployment had not changed since March.

I found the outage on 2026-11-11 at 05:08, from a read-only audit of the cluster (kubegap) that I had run for another reason. It listed the Deployment with zero available replicas and the pull failures next to it. The investigation then went through the disk, the registry, and the node cache, in that order.

The node's root filesystem is 106.7 GiB. On 2026-11-08 free space went from 19.4 GiB at 12:45 to 2.6 GiB at 13:48. The node reported DiskPressure from 13:47 to 14:10 and again from 16:10 to 16:20.

I caused this. That afternoon I pushed 25 deploy commits for a new service, and one of them shipped a 4.5 GiB image with the service's ML models inside. The Deployment picked it up at 13:37 and the pod started at 13:40. The kubelet's evictionHard is imagefs.available<5% and nodefs.available<5%; on k3s with a single root filesystem those are the same disk. When a node crosses that threshold the kubelet first reclaims node-level resources, which for an image filesystem means deleting every image no container is using, and then evicts pods if it is still under. The jump from 2.6 GiB to 27.3 GiB free between 13:48 and 14:05 is consistent with that. By 16:15 the node's eviction counters read 23 for imagefs and 27 for nodefs, and 16 pods across the cluster carried the Evicted reason.

The signaling pod is not among those 16. It ended at 13:46:42, one minute before the DiskPressure condition was recorded, with exit code 0. That is not what an eviction looks like, and I could not read the kubelet journal on that node that day, so I do not know what stopped it. The sequence I can show is: the container stopped inside the disk-pressure window; the first replacement died during the second window; the second replacement needed an image that was no longer in the node's store.

The models-in-image problem got the obvious fix the same evening: models on a volume, 1.6 GiB image.

The pull was failing on a tag that had been pulled without trouble in March. The registry is docker/distribution 2.8.3 with filesystem storage, so I looked at the files:

The tag pointed at an image index. The index was there. Both manifests the index pointed at had been unlinked from the repository, and 6 of the 10 blobs behind the platform manifest were gone. The revisions directory had last been written at 04:00:01 on 2026-03-24, the night after the push.

I turned it off after another image broke, and did not go back to check what else the flag had removed.

These Will Be the Snowiest Adventure Towns This Winter, According to a Meteorologist

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: World  |  Read Source →

Colorado’s San Juan Mountains are heavily favored for atmospheric rivers to create stellar snow totals. (Photo: Getty)

Every year, America’s snowiest towns are typically located in the mountains or on the banks of the Great Lakes. With a few ski resorts already open, if you’re a powder chaser you are likely chomping at the bit for ski season to get into full swing—especially with projections of Super El Niño turbocharging snowfall.

I’m a television meteorologist with over 30 years of forecasting experience, as well as an avid skier and snowshoer. Growing up in Greenville, South Carolina, I prayed for snow days. Now, as an adventurer, I chase the powder.

A prime sign of Super El Niño’s influence is what just happened in Chile. In late August, a resort called Ski Portillo got multiple snow dumps tallying over 30 inches in a few days. Its record-breaking season hit almost 500 inches, double the  typical average.

As Super El Niño continues to strengthen, the NOAA Climate Prediction Center is still forecasting a greater-than-83-percent  chance of a strong-to-very strong El Niño, likely from January through March. But not everywhere is going to get a stellar snow season.

With the strengthened subtropical jet stream, Super El Niño means an active southern storm track and wetter-than-normal conditions for the southern tier of the United States. That favors the Southwest, the Southern Rockies, and California’s Sierra Nevada.

The kicker to the season—one that’s not getting a lot of buzz—is the Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO), a fluctuation of ocean temperature in the Pacific, entering a cooling phase. That was a big factor in Juneau, Alaska, getting its record-setting snowfall of 201.2 inches during the 2006-7 winter. A strong El Niño and a cool PDO are a highly unusual pairing we haven’t seen since 1972-73, a winter with a significant snowstorm that buried parts of the Southeastern U.S.

Then there is the recent weird behavior of the polar vortex, a rotating mass of cold air and low pressure. Its unusual weakening would normally send a significant Arctic blast into the United States, but there’s not enough cold air across Canada yet for this to happen.

Simply put, it looks like we’re in for supersized and wild weather this winter.

These are my picks for the adventure towns most likely to receive the snowiest winter, including a couple of spots that don’t normally top the “most snow” list.

Based on historical records during very strong or Super El Niños, and its maritime air pipeline at full throttle, Mammoth Mountain Ski Area is pretty much a lock for a big snow year. While the 2015-2016 very strong El Niño recorded a near-average season of 393 inches, the 1982-1983 Super El Niño produced a whopping 546.3 inches. In 1983, January picked up 118 inches of snow and March had 112 inches, extending Mammoth’s ski season through late July.

Off-Slopes Snow Fun: Take a thrilling ride on the mountain coaster or go snow tubing at Woolly’s Adventure Summit. You can zipline year-round too, or zoom through scenic terrain with Snowmobile Adventures. For a surreal, scenic stop, head to Mono Lake, just 30 minutes away, to see its pogonip ice fog. Because of the lake’s high salinity, it doesn’t freeze. The low-lying ice fog against the limestone tufa towers is otherworldly. Plus, it’s a prime place for wintering waterfowl, such as bald eagles.

Basecamp: The Village Lodge Mammoth puts you in the heart of The Village, whereas the historic Mammoth Mountain Inn is best for ski-in/ski-out access.

Where to après: For a next-level après, book the VIP Après Experience at Lincoln Bar at Canyon Lodge. For late-night shenanigans, join the “Night Shift” party at Outbound’s Bar Sierra. The best place to chill on a slopeside sundeck is Yodler Restaurant & Bar. For local sips, stop by Shelter Distilling, Mammoth Brewing Company, or Distant Brewing.

Yes, there’s skiing in Arizona, and it’s not far from the Grand Canyon. For a high-desert ski experience, the historic settlement of Fort Valley makes the perfect playground for the Arizona Snowbowl. This tiny unincorporated community is just northwest of Flagstaff, which averages more snow annually than Denver or Salt Lake City.

With a front-row seat to atmospheric river impulses riding the subtropical jet, Fort Valley and Arizona Snowbowl should have well-above-normal snow totals, with the heaviest snow likely in February.

Located in the San Francisco Peaks, Snowbowl historically gets spikes in snowfall during strong El Niño years. During the 2023-24 El Niño, Arizona Snowbowl recorded 281 inches of snow. Its six inches of snow October 24-25, 2026, is a good sign for Arizona’s only ski resort, and for Fort Valley. It is also interesting to note that during the 1972-73 winter of strong El Niño and cool PDO, nearby Flagstaff got a record-setting 210 inches of snow.

Off-Slopes Snow Fun: After a morning on the slopes, make a stop at the Museum of Northern Arizona. Don’t miss the “Native Peoples of the Colorado Plateau” and “Southwestern Jewelry” exhibits. Go cross-country skiing or snowshoeing at Arizona Nordic Village or the Peak View Winter Recreation and Parking Area in the Coconino National Forest. Just 20 minutes away, Flagstaff Snow Park is perfect for snow tubing and sledding fun.

Take a day trip to Grand Canyon National Park. The South Rim is just over an hour away. The bonus of a winter visit is the lack of crowds. You can easily visit three national monuments in a day: see Ancestral Puebloan dwellings in Walnut Canyon and Wupatki, and experience a moon-like landscape at Sunset Crater Volcano, where every astronaut who has  stepped foot on the moon has trained. Bundle up for stargazing at nearby Lowell Observatory in Flagstaff for a truly out-of-this-world evening.

Basecamp:  Book a mountain cabin for a cozy stay. Basecamp at Snowbowl is a good budget-friendly and pet-friendly option. Plus, you get two complimentary lift tickets per night.

Where to après: Basecamp Restaurant is the go-to spot, as it’s less than 5 minutes from Fort Valley and on-site at Basecamp at Snowbowl, located 7 miles from the ski area. You’re also close enough to Flagstaff to devour delicious eats at favorites like Diablo Burger, The Toasted Owl Cafe, or MartAnne’s Burrito Palace.

Isaiah Knowles' daily routine looks quite different nowadays.

Instead of suiting up as Florida A&M's starting quarterback, the graduate student is now holding a practice itinerary, teaching, and observing the offense from the sidelines during games.

"Man, I wish I was able to play," Knowles told the Tallahassee Democrat. "But given the cards have been dealt, I'm just happy that I got an opportunity to help in any way that I can."

After winning back-to-back Southern Intercollegiate Athletic Conference Offensive Player of the Year awards and an NCAA Division II Black College Football National Championship at Albany State, Knowles made the no-brainer decision to transfer to FAMU to follow head coach Quinn Fordham Gray Sr.

The Tampa native entered as the presumed starter for Gray's first season at FAMU following their successful tenure together in the Peach State.

But his time under center was cut short to two games.

On Sept. 6, Knowles broke his right throwing-hand thumb in the Rattlers' Orange Blossom Classic football game against South Carolina State. While attempting a pass, his hand hit off of a teammate's helmet.

The quarterback attempted to pop his thumb into place himself. FAMU trainers tried also to no avail.

Knowles ran to the sideline, and then X-rays later revealed the severity of the injury that ended his season and ultimately his college football career.

"They told me there were too many bones moving," Knowles recalled the injury, "So we covered it up, walked back to the X-rays, and that's when they told me what happened."

Knowles' injury was the first domino to fall in a disastrous FAMU season as the Rattlers (2-8, 1-5 in SWAC) are on track for their fewest wins since 2015.

"You know, I look at that kid every day and just think, 'What if?' But you can't harp on things that happened in the past that aren't in your favor," Gray said of Knowles. "Losing a guy like that was tremendous for us in terms of our development this season."

After two starts in relief of Knowles, backup Dustin Fletcher suffered a similar throwing-hand thumb injury and has been out since mid-September.

So, the Rattlers have turned to true freshman quarterback Armond Parker, who's started six games this year fresh out of high school.

Parker was an early FAMU enrollee, arriving on campus this past January for organized team activities and spring football training camp, wanting to learn everything from Knowles, who's spent four years in Gray's offensive system. They've "been attached at the hip" ever since, and Knowles has "been there every step of the way."

"It's a testament to his character," said FAMU running back Jamal Hailey. "Still waking up at five in the morning, showing up so he can be there so our freshman quarterback can be prepared week to week."

Knowles began watching games from the coach's box for an aerial view of the offense.

That stopped once Parker asked him to be on the sidelines to be a mentor, explain situations and playcalling rationale, and to bring a calming voice during the madness of a college football game.

"He keeps me confident, making sure I know what I'm doing, and takes me through progressions with my decision-making," Parker explained Knowles' impact. "With him being on the sideline, that's a big help for me — keeping my head up, keeping me confident."

Parker has experienced freshman highs and lows.

He's fifth in the Southwestern Athletic Conference with 1,790 passing yards and 14 touchdown throws in eight appearances out of 10 possible games.

However, the 18-year-old is still in his developmental phase, completing 56% of his passes and also tossing 11 interceptions, many of which turned into pick-sixes.

"Helping him battle through adversity, especially as a freshman quarterback," Knowles said of Parker. "See him grow each and every game; I think FAMU's going to be in good hands."

After his arm was in a sling for a few weeks after the injury, Knowles has recovered from surgery and began treatment last week. His range of motion and strength are beginning to return, and he should be able to throw in December.

Knowles is hoping to get an invite to February's sixth-annual HBCU Legacy Bowl, an all-star showcase game for draft-eligible players from Black Colleges. Knowles shared that the Legacy Bowl invited him the last two years, but he wasn't leaving college yet. FAMU coaches have been in constant contact with the Legacy Bowl, hoping Knowles gets the nod to make the trip to Atlanta.

Knowles will then perform before scouts at FAMU's Pro Day, typically held in March.

The National Football League, Canadian Football League, United Football League — the opportunities are endless for aspiring pro players.

Knowles is encouraged by scouts telling him that they have enough film on him from Albany State that the season-ending injury may not hinder his professional football dreams as much as one might expect.

"Lord willing, I go get an opportunity somewhere," Knowles said. "If not, I'll jump right into coaching."

A hosta in a garden bed has a lot of soil wrapped around its roots when the cold comes. Put that same hosta in a pot on the patio, though, and it's an entirely different story. Cold air reaches the roots from every side of the container, and even tough perennials and shrubs can experience winter damage that way.

The usual advice for overwintering container plants involves hauling pots into the garage or wrapping them up. But there's a cheaper option in the yard right now – the leaves piling up along the fence, waiting to get bagged for the curb.

This isn't the regular leaf mulch over the potting mix that it sounds like. Mulch on top does nothing for the pot walls. Dry leaves packed around the sides, in bags or inside a wire cage, put back some of the protection the ground would have given.

Garden soil cools down slowly, and roots in a bed sit inside a huge mass of it. Pots don't have much of that buffer at all, and the root ball can drop close to the outdoor air temperature. Roots are also less cold-hardy than the stems and buds above them. As a rule of thumb, treat a potted plant as if it were two growing zones colder than your yard.

So what do the leaves actually do? Squeeze a handful of dry ones, and you'll notice how much of it is just air. Still air doesn't carry heat away from a pot very fast. A sunny afternoon can thaw the edge of a root ball – then it freezes hard again overnight, and that back-and-forth can tear roots and shove plants up out of the mix. The leaves slow those swings down.

So, there are two ways you can use fallen leaves to insulate pots, both using leaves against the pot walls.

Start with dry, fairly clean leaves, removing any sticks or diseased foliage. Loosely fill sturdy garden bags or old feed sacks – don't stomp the leaves down. The extra space lets air circulate, and that’s what does the work. Walmart sells a three-pack of reusable 72-gallon leaf bags that stand 30 inches (76 cm) tall – taller than the rims of many patio pots.

Cluster the pots against a wall of the house, on the ground instead of a deck rail or plant stand. Water them well before the soil freezes, since moist potting mix holds heat better than dry mix. Then line the bags up snugly around the outside of the group. Leave the foliage uncovered, and keep a gap to check that the soil isn't soggy and the drainage holes are clear. You can lift any bag whenever a pot needs moving.

For one big pot, or a few crowded close together, try a wire cage. Bend chicken wire or flexible garden fencing into a loose ring around them, with several inches of space between the wire and the pot. The Otryad 36-inch galvanized chicken wire roll from Home Depot will do the job, and it snips to fit.

Pour the leaves loosely into the gap, up to about the rim, keeping them off green foliage. A light cage can tip in wind. Pin its bottom edge down with 12-inch steel landscape staples.

After heavy rain or snowmelt, check the leaves in the middle. Wet, flattened leaves don't trap much air anymore, and they hold moisture against the pot, so swap them for dry ones. Take the cage down in spring, after the last hard freeze.

This trick works best for plants that are hardy where you live but have their roots stuck above ground.

Leaves don't generate heat; they simply slow the rate at which a pot loses its warmth. Citrus or a tender salvia ringed with leaf bags still gets hurt in a hard freeze. Those, along with tropicals and anything else outside its hardiness range, need a bright, frost-free spot indoors before the first frost.

Those leaves were going to the curb anyway. Stacked in bags around the patio pots, they won't look like much, honestly. Any hydrangea that comes through winter with healthy roots won't need replacing, though, and nobody paid for the leaves.

When spring settles in, dump the bags over a garden bed or into the compost bin, and let the leaves break down there. Next fall, you'll have a new pile along the fence to start over with anyway.

In the 1950s, American geochemist Clair Patterson presented findings that showed Earth is around 4.55 billion years old. His evidence was based on lead isotopic analysis that he used to calculate the age of the Canyon Diablo meteorite, which was discovered in Arizona in 1891. That result is now widely accepted as the age of our planet, but the buildup to this discovery was a long time coming.

Earth's true age was a subject of much debate among scientists in the 19th century. And rather than geologists taking the helm, it was physicists who realized that this giant ball of rock we live on must be far older than the thousands of years that had been assumed in prior centuries. Their main reasoning was heat.

In this excerpt from "This Is Life: A Journey from Inside Your Body to Beyond Earth" (University of Chicago Press/Picador, 2026), author and physicist Christophe Galfard looks at the progression among scientists who were coming to realize that thermodynamics was an essential piece of this long-standing puzzle.

Some two and a half centuries ago, the foremost experts on scientific matters knew a thing or two about heat. They knew, for instance, how heat could be transferred from one place to another. A new science was about to emerge out of this knowledge, a science called thermodynamics, which means "movements of heat" in Greek.

Thermodynamics isn't a force. It is more like a guidebook, a set of rules that apply to large

numbers of particles when they come together. It gives us, humans, a way to understand how particles behave when there is a lot of them without needing to refer to each one of them individually. It shows us how properties that don't exist on their own arise when many particles are together. It deals with these things we call emergent phenomena, such as heat and pressure, and which [Philip] Anderson's "More Is Different" tried to make sense of.

Understanding the dynamics of heat meant a lot to nineteenth-century scientists — so much so that many saw in it the roots of a philosophy so universal and powerful that they thought it should guide us through all our lives. Some even went as far as believing that heat was the source of eternal life. A prominent scientist became so convinced that this was the case that he locked himself up in his house and overheated it, thus shielding himself from all mortal diseases (ageing and death included). The experience was a true torture for the occasional guests who came to visit him, not to mention his wife, who had to endure it day and night.

The scientist held fast to his belief, though, piling on layer after layer of clothing to keep himself ever warmer. Unfortunately, the bulk made it difficult to move, and he eventually missed a step on his own staircase and died from the fall.

As a scientific discipline, thermodynamics was born with a book written in 1824 by a 27-year-old French physicist named Nicolas Léonard Sadi Carnot, who, as he explained how heat could produce work, told everyone how the steam engine worked.

Lord Kelvin giving a lecture at Glasgow University in 1899.

With Carnot's vision, the way hot objects cool down began to be understood, raising hopes that the resolution of one particular problem might be imminent: the determination of the Earth's age. Were we to somehow guess how hot our planet was at birth, thermodynamics could then be used to tell us how much time had gone by for it to have cooled to its present temperature. Many scientists tried it, keen to be the first one to nail the true age of our world.

Considering our planet in all its complexity to be far too difficult, simplifications were in order, and British physicist William Thomson shot first by picturing our world as a ball. A very simple, rock-solid ball made of pretty much the same stuff throughout, from surface to core. No ocean, no mountain, just a smooth ball the size of the Earth. He then calculated how long it would take it to cool down from being red hot to its present temperature, and declared that the Earth was something between 40 and 400 million years old.

Refining his calculations, he then announced that the world was 100 million years old, before settling for 40 million again.

Thomson's result does not correspond to the true age of the Earth, but it was a start. It was the result of a model, an attempt at figuring out something about our world using still-to-be-refined laws, and mathematical sense. What was more problematic was that no one dared question his results. Even as it shifted from one paper to another, the age of the Earth given by Thomson was always greeted as the right one. A reason for this is that at the end of the nineteenth century Thomson had become Lord Kelvin.

The same Kelvin after whom the Kelvin unit of absolute temperature is named. A scientific superstar who had the power to undo, in a snap of fingers, the career of anyone who disagreed with him.

Lord Kelvin was a physicist, not a geologist, so geologists could have questioned his work, but they didn't. In fact, they weren't bothered. Many of them back then believed that being a geologist was all about intuition and proper English, a language they deemed far more efficient than mathematical gibberish.

That being said, and however wrong the results, Kelvin's calculations were beautiful, giving whoever wanted to think about the problem of the age of the Earth a way forward, which is exactly what one of his former students, Northern Irishman John Perry, did. He thought that Kelvin had overlooked something. As a man of science talking to his former mentor (and job provider), Perry explained his vision to Kelvin but was ignored.

John Perry, a student of Kelvin's, who realized Earth was billions of years old — and that its mantle was a liquid, not a solid.

Still, he published his thoughts in Nature. But again, the article was ignored. Not just by Kelvin, but by the scientific community at large, including geologists.

It would have saved us all quite a bit of time had this not been the case, because Perry not only found a much more correct way to picture the Earth and estimate its age than Kelvin had, but he also found the source of the power that makes our planet's continents drift.

In his model, Perry didn't picture the Earth to be uniformly the same from surface to core as Kelvin had. Instead, he modelled the Earth's interior as being divided into three regions: a metallic core at its centre, a solid crust at its surface, and a mantle that lay in between the two. Rather unexpectedly, Perry didn't envision the mantle as everyone else would have; he believed that it didn't behave like the solid rock it was, but as a liquid.

No wonder his former master didn't take him seriously. However, he pictured the mantle behaving like a liquid only when considered over long periods of time. Sandwiched between a hot core and a colder crust that very much looked like a lid, he believed the mantle had no choice but to behave just like water does in a heated pan, with the hottest parts at the bottom rising and cooling before diving back down and rising up again, in repeated loops, albeit in extremely slow motion. This type of movement is called convection, and it happens in solids, too.

If he was right, there would be two immediate consequences. The first one was that the time it would take for our planet's surface to cool down from red hot to its current temperature could now be far longer than Kelvin had estimated. The lid and the convection patterns prevented our planet from quickly radiating away all its energy into space, so we were now inhabiting an Earth that could be as old as 2 or 3 billion years. Maybe even more. Such timescales were unheard of in the scientific community. Back then, no one knew that the universe itself was 13.8 billion years old. They knew next to nothing.

The second consequence of Perry's model was that the mantle's circular movements underneath the crust could drag the crust itself via friction. The solid surface of our world would then move, and split into pieces that would drift, and collide, and plunge underneath — or slide above — one another. Where they collided, mountain ranges would rise, like the Alps, the Himalayas, the Rockies . . . Where they split apart, rifts would appear, probably in the middle of oceans, along ridges . . . just like [Marie] Tharp had found.

Nowadays, these broken pieces of our planet's surface have all been mapped. They are our world's plates and their movements through time are called plate tectonics. Today, most of us have heard about plate tectonics. Back then, however, no one had, and if very few people remember Perry today, it is because another scientific breakthrough made the estimation of the age of the Earth much easier and precise, showing us that our world isn't 2 or 3 billion years old, as Perry suggested, but precisely 4.567 billion, give or take a few million.

Engineers in China have built a new computer architecture they say can one day make up to a million artificial intelligence (AI) chips work as one. For comparison, Elon Musk's AI company, SpaceXAI (soon to be SpaceXSI), describes its Colossus cluster as the world's most powerful AI training system, comprising 200,000 interconnected chips.

Traditionally, the AI chips used to train foundation models are housed in separate servers and communicate over a network to share data and divide workloads. But the new architecture, Peerium, uses a high-speed interconnect system designed to maintain efficient communication between chips across multiple racks, even as the number of chips scales into the hundreds of thousands.

Huawei unveiled the new architecture Sept. 17 at the company's annual CONNECT conference.

Representatives from Huawei said in a statement that computers powered by Peerium could train models with up to 40 trillion parameters. For comparison, competing hardware, like GB200 NVL72 from NVIDIA and CS-3 from Cerebras, have reported training at the trillion-parameter scale, rather than the tens-of-trillions.

"Over the next two years, there will be roughly 6 or 7 frontier AI labs in China, and all will aim at training foundation language models of sizes ranging from 10 to 40 trillion parameters," Liao Heng, chief scientist for Huawei's chip design arm HiSilicon, told the Associated Press. "So you need such a scale of infrastructure to train these models."

Peerium's performance relies on three elements: nested parallelism, unified memory addressing, and peer-to-peer interconnects, Huawei representatives said in the statement.

Nested parallelism allows workloads to be broken into smaller tasks and organized across hierarchical groups of chips that work in parallel. It's similar to how a company breaks down work into departments and then smaller teams, all of which coordinate their efforts toward a common goal. Unified memory addressing allows processors to access memory across other servers, as opposed to only that attached to the processor's own server. Peer-to-peer interconnects allow components to communicate more directly with one another, rather than relying on an intermediary.

All three principles are designed to make more efficient use of computing resources so that a single system can coordinate larger numbers of chips to handle demanding AI workloads.

According to Huawei, existing interconnect technologies — which link processors and servers to enable them to communicate — cannot support the Peerium architecture at the scale it envisages. For instance, NVIDIA uses NVLink for communication within racks and InfiniBand for communication between racks. The inter-rack communication bandwidth in this setup is 200GB per second, Eric Xu, Huawei's rotating chairman, told the AP.

"With such a low inter-rack communications bandwidth, there is no way you can make one million processors work as one computer," he said.

Huawei instead developed its own interconnect technology, UnifiedBus, which the company says delivers inter-rack communications bandwidth of up to 800GB per second.

"When one million processors work as one computer, this lays the foundation for better and more efficient AI training and inference," Xu added. "Therefore, I see UnifiedBus as the path for the future AI era. I believe, in the years to come, other players would certainly follow suit."

The wallet flagged about 37,000 signing requests a week for phishing in Q3 and said better monitoring likely contributed to the increase.

Crypto wallet provider Bitget Wallet said Wednesday it was expanding AI-assisted threat detection after its weekly average of signing requests flagged for potential phishing rose 7.4-fold in the third quarter of 2026.

The wallet flagged about 37,000 such requests per week in Q3, up from 5,000 in Q2, according to Bitget Wallet chief operating officer Alvin Kan.

“The increase likely reflects both greater threat activity and improved detection capabilities, although the data does not allow us to quantify each factor’s contribution,” Kan told Cointelegraph.

The wallet introduced AI-assisted risk detection in Q2, he added.

Kan said that the tally counted flagged signing interactions, not unique attacks or confirmed financial losses.

Bitget Wallet said its expanded AI checks examine website code and domain registration details for signs of fraud. The wallet also simulates transactions to spot hidden permissions before users sign.

Bitget Wallet also said AI helped identify 90% of newly blocked risky domains in October, compared with 36% in Q2. Nearly 70% of domains its AI flagged that month had no matching alerts in the external threat feeds it checked.

The findings come amid continued phishing threats targeting crypto users. In July, a trader lost nearly $1 million after signing a malicious Ethereum token approval.

An August investigation by Rapid7 uncovered a phishing campaign that targeted 885,000 phone numbers and directed recipients to websites impersonating crypto wallet providers.

LinkedIn Is Testing a Feature That Lets You See Your Profile Stalkers

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Big news for LinkedIn stalkers: If you keep visiting the page of a potential hire—or ex-romantic partner—you might not be lurking as stealthily as you once were.

LinkedIn is testing a "frequent viewer” feature that allows certain users with LinkedIn Premium to know if someone has looked at their profile multiple times. Krista Mollion, the founder of creative agency Unignorable Brands, first noticed the added detail last month.

“I called it the ‘stalker alert,’” Mollion tells WIRED.

Every few days, Mollion, who has nearly 79,000 followers on LinkedIn, gets a notification to “check out who has viewed your profile.” LinkedIn has long allowed users to see who has looked at their profile. If you pay for LinkedIn Premium, like Mollion, you can access even more details on who has visited your page, including a full list of every viewer over the previous year. But now when Mollion visits the Who Has Viewed Your Profile section, she sees certain people tagged as Frequent Viewers.

LinkedIn confirmed the test but declined to share further information about the feature.

For Mollion, the new insight has been a boon. She runs the names of frequent viewers through Claude, asking the AI to identify any she should be paying attention to. Claude groups the viewers into three groups: friends, people who probably want to sell her something, and business development or sales leads.

Mollion reaches out to those who aren’t trying to pitch her and, she says, nine times out of 10 it leads to a good conversation. One frequent viewer was an organizer looking for speakers for an online summit; another was a host seeing if Mollion was a good fit for their podcast.

“This stalker button—the Frequent Viewer—is a way to capture those invisible lurkers who are considering you for an opportunity,” Mollion says. For the average LinkedIn user, the best-case scenario might be the chance to reach out to a recruiter looking to hire for a new role.

It also lets you know if an unwanted individual—like a former boss or an ex—is keeping tabs on you. Since the frequent viewer feature is triggered by someone looking at your profile, not just seeing your posts or comments in their feed or as the result of a search, it’s a good signal that this person is specifically seeking you out. While LinkedIn tends to be “safe-for-work” compared to other social media platforms, Mollion says that there are “definitely weirdos.” She herself has blocked one frequent viewer.

If you don’t want to show up as a Hinge match’s frequent viewer before a first date, it might be time to change your privacy settings. As a reminder: LinkedIn allows users to peruse profiles in public, semi-private (showing your job title, industry, and school but not your name), or fully private mode. To change your settings, go to Visibility in the Settings and Privacy section, then click Profile viewing options. If you want to stalk in peace and relative secrecy, we recommend Private mode.

RM’s SFMOMA Show Changed My Mind About Celebrity Curators

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

An exhibition co-curated by a K-pop superstar isn’t the kind of show most art critics rush to review. But I couldn’t resist. Call it curiosity, or perhaps a little FOMO. Even my preparation for writing this review felt different: Instead of poring over essays by art historians and curators, I scrolled through fan-made reels, virtual walkthroughs, and videos featuring excerpts from RM’s audio guide. By the time I arrived, the show already felt strangely familiar.

The numbers alone make it a sensation. Since “RM x SFMOMA: Between You and Me” opened in San Francisco last month, more than 66,000 tickets have sold, advance sales ran six times higher than for any previous San Francisco Museum of Modern Art exhibition, and merchandise vanished within hours. In Korea, the show has become a major cultural story. Yet when I pitched this review, the art press had given the exhibition surprisingly little critical attention. Everyone was counting the crowds. Who was looking at the walls? I understand the reluctance. Celebrity sells, and famous names increasingly anchor exhibitions through their collections, their art, their merch. When fame becomes the main draw, what happens to the art?

RM, born Kim Namjoon, is harder to dismiss. After a 2018 visit to the Art Institute of Chicago exposed how little he knew about Korean artists, he began looking, learning, and collecting. His museum visits, shared through vlogs and social media, have sent fans on their own “RM Tours” around the world. Now, with SFMOMA curators América Castillo and Hyoeun Kim, he has arranged nearly 180 works from his collection and the museum’s holdings across eight rooms organized around color, home, friends, and people. Can personal taste carry a museum exhibition?

The exhibition starts strong. The first gallery, “Bluewater,” opens with Kim Whanki’s 1970 field of tiny blue dots, a painting that the artist’s widow donated to SFMOMA. Around it, blue keeps changing shape: Yves Klein’s saturated pigment, Kim Tschang-yeul’s water drops, Tony Feher’s plastic bottles, Roni Horn’s thick glass, uncannily like water or ice. Color is a loose, even obvious, curatorial thread, but it works on the eye: The same blue reads as weightless, liquid, dense, or impossibly still. In his audio guide, RM marvels that a painting by a Korean artist who died in New York could bring together him, the museum, and strangers from around the world.

“Friendscape” trades serenity for charm. Ugo Rondinone’s rough-hewn bronze fish hang overhead; below, old Korean wooden geese sit on a rippling, water-patterned floor, each bird differently carved, like an eccentric relative. Tiny objects perch on tiled pedestals resembling Korean floor cushions. In the exhibition’s audio guide, RM speaks fondly of the geese, traditionally associated with weddings, as objects that make him feel at home. Tigers and other animals populate the surrounding paintings and ceramics, while Lee Daiwon’s speckled Bugaksan Mountain (1976), RM’s first acquisition, recalls the Pointillist paintings of Seurat that had enchanted him.

In “PhotoBuddha,” visitors take selfies beside Nam June Paik’s television-watching Buddha, a cheeky mirror for our screen-obsessed lives, while a solitary Buddha by Kwon Jinkyu keeps to its corner. RM’s hand is everywhere: handwritten Korean wall texts, polite “please do not touch” signs, an audio track full of laughter and the occasional ramble. One gallery is titled, simply, “I Don’t Know But I Like It.”

That title is either the show’s weakness or its manifesto. I suspect it’s both. Critics are trained to look beyond mere liking. But fandom begins there: you fall for something first and study it later. RM’s audience will learn the story of a wooden goose the way they learned the lore behind an album, because someone they trust cared first. As a critic, I found that oddly nostalgic. It’s how most of us started.

The show is at its best when RM turns that openness on himself. “Home” resembles a living room: worn wooden furniture, shelves of ceramics, a sofa, small paintings, a suspended light by Minjae Kim. Chang Ucchin and Park Soo Keun keep domestic company with Giorgio Morandi. Then comes Son Sangki’s Artificial City–Irony (1987), a lavish living room with a painting hanging on its wall. Son, who lived with a disability and endured considerable hardship, often painted Seoul neighborhoods facing redevelopment. Now his canvas hangs in an interior much like the one it depicts. “Am I being a boring rich person?” RM asks, questioning his own privilege. “It makes me really uncomfortable, and I like it.” The display’s comfort suddenly becomes its critique.

In “Passion,” that discomfort deepens. A spare Park Rehyun abstraction, its floating form resembling a sail or moon, looks effortless. It wasn’t. Park raised four children, helped her deaf husband, the painter Kim Kichang, communicate with others, and painted after everyone slept. Rhee Seundja, who left three sons in wartime Korea to pursue painting in Paris, imagined each brushstroke as a spoonful of rice for her children. Suddenly, her repeated marks suggest separation, labor, and longing. Biography risks flattening complex practices into inspirational stories. Yet listening to RM reflect on his privilege in light of their perseverance, I understood why visitors were moved. The exhibition’s curators told me some visitors had cried, and that more than 700 post-visit comments had been submitted to the museum’s digital mural.

The exhibition begins in blue and ends in umber. In the final room, Yun Hyong-keun’s dark vertical bands bleed into the canvas beside a Rothko. Yun has long been introduced as the “Korean Rothko,” a convenient Western shorthand that RM challenges simply by hanging the two side by side: Rothko’s color hovers and dissolves at the edges, while Yun’s sinks into the cloth, dense and earthbound. The histories differ, too. Yun survived war, imprisonment, and the threat of execution, and his paintings carry that weight. RM admits he cannot fully grasp what those dark stains have swallowed. After the geese and the selfies, it’s an unexpectedly grave farewell, and proof that a fan’s taste can do critical work.

The timing is striking. The show opened days after Ha Chong-Hyun’s retrospective at the Asian Art Museum and the Samsung-backed “Korea” exhibition at the British Museum, and a week before Haegue Yang’s Dia Beacon commission. Korean art’s moment rests on decades of scholarship, institutional effort, and corporate and government backing. RM brings what none of that could guarantee: an audience ready to follow him.

Of course, BTS fans come first, and that should give us pause. A collector’s taste is no substitute for art history. Once it enters a museum, private taste gains public authority, shaping which artists get seen and, potentially, how their work is valued. Star-driven exhibitions shouldn’t become a museum habit. Yet the usual objection, that fame eclipses the art, misses what works here.

RM isn’t standing in front of the paintings. He’s pointing at them. His personal voice brings viewers close enough to form attachments of their own. We don’t always know why we like something. Sometimes that’s where looking begins.

‘Wake-Up Call’: Trump’s Former Housing Commissioner Says New Congress Must ‘Get Serious’ About Housing Affordability

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Democrats and Republicans both understand Americans are broadly worried about the cost of living, and a former Trump administration official says it's time for both parties to take action.

In a bitterly divided Washington, DC, it remains to be seen whether lawmakers will heed that call when the 120th Congress convenes in January, with Democrats back in control of both the House and Senate.

After campaigning hard on affordability concerns, Democratic leaders say they'll use their new majorities to focus on the cost of living. Republican leaders admit they didn't heed voter concerns about the economy. Meanwhile, President Donald Trump has threatened to use his veto power freely.

The summer's 21st Century Road to Housing Act, with its dozens of provisions aimed at cutting red tape, was a rare example of bipartisan consensus. What follows that landmark legislation remains an open question.

Republicans want more deregulation while Democrats aim for more support for housing. And each side must now contend with an upswell of hard-liners pushing policies that could radically change the market.

Many of the prominent names in both parties who built the housing bill are returning, including Rep. French Hill (R-AR), Rep. Maxine Waters (D-CA), Sen. Elizabeth Warren (D-MA), and Sen. Tim Scott (R-SC). They're likely to return to the House and Senate committees on housing and banking.

"Now is a wake-up call," Trump's former Federal Housing Commissioner Frank Cassidy tells Realtor.com®. "Senate, House, White House. Everyone needs to get serious about tackling and addressing housing affordability. It can't be ignored anymore."

Congress had some bipartisan momentum to provide tax incentives that encourage the buying and selling of homes.

Colin Allen, executive director of the American Property Owners Alliance and a former housing issues adviser to Sen. Lindsey Graham (R-SC), said that could mean some ideas pitched in this Congress could come back as part of larger bills.

"They have good working relationships and have already done some bipartisan work," Allen said.

Take, for instance, the Neighborhood Homes Investment Act, which would create a new tax credit to buy and renovate homes in economically distressed areas. The bill drew 70 sponsors from both parties.

Real estate groups put in significant groundwork this year to update the capital gains tax scheme for the first time in more than 30 years. The More Homes on the Market Act came close to 200 sponsors in Congress. It would double the exclusion for many filers. It's been floating around Congress for several years and has been a target of the National Association of Realtors® and other groups.

Trump expressed some support for changing the capital gains tax scheme, though a specific plan hasn't yet materialized from the White House.

"We've done a lot of legwork to get folks behind it," Allen said. "If we're looking at a larger bipartisan bill, you can see a scenario where a few of those ideas get together. The debt limit is coming up, and there are a few other opportunities to package together."

Hill and Republicans have lobbied for more changes to the financing system. They were able to get several banking reform provisions into the Road to Housing bill. They've also floated new financial reforms to change and rescind banking rules codified in the Dodd-Frank Act and the Basel III accords after the subprime mortgage crisis. Republicans say they've chased smaller banks out of the housing market.

This summer, Hill called Dodd-Frank a "massive overreaction."

"For our smaller banks, the new regulations were a poor fit, burying them in compliance work and bureaucratic red tape," Hill said. "Instead of helping customers, their attention was diverted to filling out forms and satisfying examiners."

Mortgage reform is also a lever Congress can pull. A bipartisan group of six members of Congress pitched the First-Time Homebuyer Affordability Act this summer. It would expand access to low-interest mortgages for first-time homebuyers.

"By expanding access to financing, we can help more hardworking families achieve homeownership, strengthen our communities, and build long-term financial security," said Rep. Darin LaHood (R-IL) said.

Then there's the matter of new construction. Democrats and Republicans found some bipartisan ground in cutting some of the federal red tape that blocks construction of new homes.

Republicans also said they favor rolling back Build America Buy America provisions. Builders, they say, have complained that the sourcing requirements are time-consuming and expensive. But they've also targeted some environmental regulations that Democrats passed.

Dennis Shea, chair of the J. Ronald Terwilliger Center for Housing Policy at the Bipartisan Policy Center, tells Realtor.com that some public housing authorities saw costs climb by double digits. The U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development's waiver process is every bit as time-consuming for builders.

"A sentiment exists in both parties to change the rules, but ultimately it takes an act of Congress to exempt housing," Shea said.

Hill and other lawmakers such as Rep. Emanuel Cleaver (D-MO) have said that Congress' action should shift some burden onto states to implement reforms at the local and state level. The American Enterprise Institute said this year that state and local policy changes could boost annual homebuilding by six figures. But many of those new homes are accessory dwelling units.

Congress and the White Hose also found common ground on manufactured housing reform. All sides recognize they need to keep eyeing supply-side reforms, says Cassidy.

"Ultimately, the problem in this country is supply," Cassidy adds. "You could lower rates to 2% tomorrow, and all that will do is increase home values. It's not going to solve the underlying issue."

The other big question will be funding of the many provisions created under the Road to Housing Act. While the provisions in the bill were bipartisan, Congress must still approve funding for some of those programs. Some of those discussions are coming in the 2028 fiscal budget and beyond.

Over the months of negotiations on the Road to Housing bill, some Republicans took issue with unfunded mandates in it. Concerns about funding programs, and at what level, could spill over into congressional proceedings in coming years.

Tony Marquez, director of American University's real estate program, who has a long career in real estate and banking, tells Realtor.com that the industry is still amenable to a few different ideas for how to solve housing. It doesn't rely on one side's control of the government.

"My un-professional assessment is we'll have a certain amount of gridlock over a number of years where Democrats may come up with some suggestions," Marquez says. "But the answer to affordable housing is not just one silver bullet. It can have many elements."

Bitdeer has delivered one of the clearest examples yet of a Bitcoin mining business trying to transform itself into a provider of broader computing infrastructure. Its latest quarter brought record revenue and a sizable artificial-intelligence cloud order pipeline. It also remained deeply unprofitable under standard accounting measures.

In its November 10 third-quarter results announcement, the company reported $320.7 million in revenue, an 89% increase from the year-earlier period. Adjusted EBITDA rose to about $69.6 million, while the GAAP net loss reached approximately $125.5 million.

Those measures differ, and the gap requires careful reading. Management highlighted changes in the fair value of digital-asset-related borrowing, including a $54.6 million noncash item, as one factor in the accounting result.

The figure likely to attract the most attention is Bitdeer’s reported AI-cloud backlog of approximately $2.9 billion. A backlog represents contracted or expected future services under the company’s reporting definitions, rather than cash already received or recognized quarterly revenue.

The amount eventually converted into sales will depend on contract delivery, customer demand, capacity coming online and the terms attached to commitments. Investors should seek disclosures about timing and any conditions that could delay or reduce the total.

Nevertheless, a substantial backlog can signal customer interest for specialized infrastructure. That is particularly valuable in a business where constructing or equipping data centers involves significant upfront spending.

Bitdeer reported production of 3,855 Bitcoin during the quarter and said it held 47 Bitcoin at quarter-end. Those figures help distinguish operating output from Bitcoin retained on the balance sheet. Production can be high even if a firm sells most of the coins to fund operations, equipment or other projects.

The company also reported approximately $213.7 million in cash, cash equivalents and restricted cash. A cash balance should be considered alongside capital commitments, financing costs and planned expansion rather than treated as unencumbered profits from AI.

Our coverage of Soluna and Bitdeer’s Texas infrastructure agreement adds a concrete example of why access to powered sites and commercial partnerships remains important as the competition for computing capacity grows.

Adjusted EBITDA may help investors evaluate operating performance before certain expenses and valuation changes. It is not identical to net income or free cash flow. A company can report a positive adjusted measure and still incur losses, financing costs and heavy investment requirements.

That is why the $125.5 million net loss deserves to stand alongside the $320.7 million revenue headline. Both are true, and neither alone gives a complete picture.

Bitdeer’s next reporting periods need to show when AI-cloud backlog turns into delivered services, how those services contribute to margins and how much capital they consume. Improvements in mining efficiency and power costs will also remain relevant.

The latest results point to striking revenue momentum and a potentially meaningful new business line. They do not yet demonstrate that the shift toward AI has resolved the financial volatility historically associated with Bitcoin mining.

Broward schools ‘no longer the only game in town’ as enrollment drops

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The Florida district is doubling down on student recruitment and retention by stepping up marketing and engagement efforts.

This is the second installment of a three-part series on the impact of private school choice on public school enrollment.

When Valerie Wanza started as a teacher in Florida's Broward County Public Schools in 1992, the district was at the beginning of what would be explosive growth in student population over the next decade.

Throughout the 1990s and early 2000s, Broward County Public Schools saw such an increase in enrollment that by 2004, more than 100 of its 315 schools were deemed "critically overcrowded."

Now, 34 years later as the district's chief strategy and innovation officer, Wanza is part of a team of administrators working to turn around an enrollment decline that began in the 2015-16 school year. The student population plummeted by 17.5% over the next decade and is forecast to continue decreasing through at least the 2030-31 school year, according to district data.

The district attributed the drop to various factors: lower birth rates leading to fewer children entering schools, rising housing costs that make it harder for families to live in the desirable area bordering the Atlantic Ocean, fewer immigrant students due to shifts in federal policies, and growing competition from private school choice options.

And with the area's school choice proliferation, Wanza said, Broward County Public Schools is "no longer the only game in town."

Now, to stay financially healthy, the district is taking a comprehensive approach that includes consolidating and closing some school campuses, cutting at least 1,000 staff positions, and implementing other measures to save money. It's also attempting to boost enrollment by vigorously recruiting new students through media campaigns, personalized phone calls, and hosting events to drum up interest in and excitement for the district's schools.

Broward is even turning to an outside vendor for help. Earlier this year, the district approved a one-year contract with student recruitment firm Caissa K12 to help connect with families who have left its public schools, determine their reasons for doing so, attempt to resolve those concerns and inform them about the district's benefits and schooling options.

The performance-based contract said Broward County Public Schools will pay Caissa K12 $850 per recruited student who enrolls in a district school, up to 176 students or a maximum payment of $150,000.

“If we aren’t real about, No. 1, recognizing what adjustments we need to make, and then be bold enough to make them, we’re going to become the Kmart … of the public schools,” Wanza said, referring to the once-dominant discount retailer that filed for bankruptcy in 2002 amid growing competition and other pressures.

The enrollment decline that Broward County Public Schools is experiencing mirrors the challenges faced by many other school districts across the country.

While falling birth rates and rising school choice are often to blame, the multitude of factors can be complex and specific to each region's ability to draw new residents with young families. Nationally, enrollment in traditional public schools is expected to drop from 54.7 million in 2021 to 49.4 million in 2035, according to a 2025 analysis from the Brookings Institution based on U.S. Census and other federal data.

On the other hand, some states are predicted to see increases in public school student populations between fall 2022 and fall 2031. Among those are Idaho (+11%), North Dakota (+7.4%) and Tennessee (+2%), according to data from the U.S. Department of Education.

Enrollment in Florida's public schools is projected to increase by 3% over the same time period.

From 2015-16 through 2025-26, Broward County Public Schools lost 17.5% of its student population, or 38,640 students. That translated to a loss of $352 million in per-pupil revenue over that 11-year span. In fiscal year 2026, which ended June 30, the district expected to show an $80 million shortfall because of continued enrollment loss.

The district has already closed seven schools since the 2025-26 school year and is exploring other closures, consolidations and grade configurations for the future. New data released this fall shows district enrollment dropped by another 10,272 for the 2026-27 school year.

Even so, with 315 schools educating 224,324 students, including at charter schools, Broward County Public Schools is still the 6th largest district in the U.S., according to its website.

Broward leaders are carefully analyzing data that can explain why families aren't enrolling their students in county schools. Joseph Beck, the district's director of demographics and enrollment planning, said his team is constantly coming up with ways to look at the data differently to better understand why students are coming or going.

For instance, the data shows that about 20,000 students, on average, withdraw from Broward County Public Schools annually. That number, which doesn't include graduating seniors, has been consistent over many years, Beck said.

The district also investigates how many new students enroll each year, a figure that can be affected by immigration policy changes. For instance, the district had 3,400 fewer newcomer students in 2025-26 than it did the previous year, Beck said.

The data also shows that 1,700 students enrolled in the 2024-25 school year transferred to a private school the following year using a Florida Family Empowerment Scholarship to help pay tuition.

At the state level, about 561,000 students participated in taxpayer-funded scholarship programs during the 2025-26 school year, according to a Florida Senate analysis. That same year, the state's public schools enrolled about 2.8 million students, state data shows.

Across Broward County, some 35,000 students participated in one of Florida's taxpayer-supported private school choice programs in the 2025-26 school year.

Beck said the data further shows that many of the county's private school choice participants weren't previously enrolled in the district's public schools. "They just completely bypass us altogether," Beck said.

He and other Broward County Public School leaders are aiming to reverse that.

Farrah Wilson, Broward County Public Schools' director of marketing and strategic communications, rapidly ticks off the efforts being made to court new students and their families. There are mailers, social media campaigns, TV ads, school tours, home visits and more.

As public schools across the country get more intentional and strategic about recruitment, they are taking a playbook from private schools. That includes hosting school tours, having "shadow days" for prospective students, and sending mailers to families encouraging them to add public schools to their list of options.

We have the best programs. We have the best teachers. We have the best facilities, athletics. We offer a comprehensive experience that many of our charter and private schools can't offer.

Broward County Public Schools' director of marketing and strategic communications

Broward County is home to 245 private schools serving about 54,000 students, or about 18% of students in the county, according to Private School Review, a site that provides information on K-12 private schools for families.

But competition for students can also come from nearby public school systems.

In a mailer that Broward sent out in mid-July, it targeted families living across the district's borders to the north in Palm Beach County and to the south in Miami-Dade County.

"This is just a way to let parents know that there are options, and if they are interested, they can go to one of our schools on the county line and participate and take one of the open seats," Wilson said.

The mailers led to one student from Palm Beach County and 17 students from Miami-Dade County enrolling in Broward County Public Schools, the district said.

While it might seem unfair to try to poach other districts' students, Broward administrators said it goes both ways. The School District of Palm Beach County has likewise attempted to recruit Broward students living near its border, Broward officials said.

The Palm Beach system shares information about its schools through various channels, and marketing reaches families across the region, including those in neighboring counties like Broward, said Sean Cooley, chief of communications and enrollment for the Palm Beach district, in an Oct. 29 email. However, Palm Beach does not track how many Broward residents enroll in its schools.

"Many families living near county boundaries commute to work within Palm Beach County, making our high-performing schools an attractive and convenient option for their children's education," Cooley said.

Florida has an open boundary policy that lets families enroll in schools outside of their home school district as long as there are open seats available. "We're used to having open lines, if you will. Now we just got more intentional about it," said Wanza.

Broward is also expanding its school choice options by catering to what families want. The district has an online school as well as 32 schools offering dual language programs. It has magnet schools, a high school that operates on a 4-day-a-week schedule, several K-8 schools, a grade 6-12 school and a K-12 school.

In another enrollment initiative, the district is encouraging and supporting each school to tell its own stories through various marketing and communications efforts. For example, on "testimonial Tuesdays," students, families and staff share on social media why their school is great.

Initiatives like this enable families to make connections with individual schools, not necessarily the district as a whole, Wilson said. Building excitement at individual schools is "the secret sauce" to boosting enrollments, she said.

Every school also has an "Enroll Now" button on its website. If a family clicks the link and shares their contact information, the school's principal is automatically notified and is encouraged to reach out directly to the family, answer any questions they have, offer a tour and make other recruiting efforts that will "close the deal," Wilson said.

One reason Broward is taking such an intentional approach to marketing, said administrators, is to combat misinformation or outdated information about district schools, including about its academic performance. Broward County Public Schools has earned an "A" rating from the Florida Department of Education for three consecutive years and continues to "demonstrate strong academic growth," the district said in an email to K-12 Dive.

Negative perceptions of the public school system are what the district is "always battling against," Wilson said. This is why the district is heavily investing in marketing and recruiting, she said.

"We have the best programs. We have the best teachers. We have the best facilities, athletics. We offer a comprehensive experience that many of our charter and private schools can't offer.”

3 ways public schools are competing for enrollment

School leaders are borrowing pages from marketing and advertising executives to develop tactics that will rightsize their enrollment.

This is the third installment of a three-part series on the impact of private school choice on public school enrollment.

The decision to close 129-year-old Perry Elementary School in Belvidere, Ill., earlier this year devastated many community members who didn't want to see their historic building shuttered.

While scores of schools across the country are closing as enrollment declines empty their classrooms, that was not the case with Perry. The 174-student school, in the end, just got old. Its aging structure needed a prohibitive $14 million to $16 million in repairs, according to Cassandra Schug, superintendent of Belvidere Community Unit School District 100.

The district's enrollment has in fact remained relatively stable, with only a modest decline in recent years, Schug says. Enrollment stands at 7,348 students in 2026, down from 7,470 in 2021.

Still, the decision to close Perry and the reaction it engendered from the community offer constructive guidance for administrators everywhere who are staring down school closures brought on by shrinking student populations.

When Perry's closure became inevitable after a 6-1 school board vote in April, the community rallied to usher its beloved school out in style, Schug says. Longtime teacher Susan Thomas spearheaded a committee to honor the school’s legacy with a citywide celebration ultimately attended by hundreds of people in May.

Thomas, who taught 5th grade at Perry for 19 years, says the neighborhood school was “so much more” than just a building. The closure decision, she says, was nothing short of traumatic.

Perry “has been a cornerstone for Belvidere. It needed to be celebrated. It needed us to be able to show our appreciation for what it had meant for so many years, and I just wanted it to be done right,” Thomas says.

The celebration allowed people across the community to visit and view old yearbooks, photos, letters, newspaper articles and student projects — all organized by decade and displayed on tables and walls throughout the gym.

“So you could walk through and just see all of that Perry history and recognize how education had changed over the years, how the building had changed over the years, and again, just really spend time sort of saying goodbye,” Schug says.

The celebration “helped us all to process what had been a really difficult time," honor the school's legacy, "create that closure, and give us a chance to celebrate those positive memories that Perry had brought to the community.”

Beginning this school year, Perry's staff members and students dispersed to other schools in the district, with students transferring through a lottery system.

Though Belvidere enrollment is expected to stay steady, district officials are carefully watching enrollment numbers and projections, especially as more public schools nationwide experience declines, Schug says.

And, she adds, she hopes she never has to close another school in her career again.

As some school communities grieve shuttered schools, districts are proactively working to boost enrollments and stave off closures. Here are three examples of how school leaders are striving to stay competitive.

The days of traditional public school systems having a monopoly on education and just worrying about teaching students are gone, says Brian Stephens, CEO of Caissa K12, a Memphis-based strategic communications firm.

In 2009, Stephens co-founded a public strategy firm that specialized in complex communication crisis management. But in 2017, as he saw more states adopt private school choice programs, the firm converted to focusing exclusively on student recruitment and retention for traditional public schools.

Currently, Caissa K12 — named for the mythical goddess of chess and strategy — works with nearly 100 districts in 26 states. The company says it has helped recruit about 55,000 students for its clients through research, communications, events and knocking on doors.

The firm is one of a growing number of players in this space.

Taking a page from the retail industry, one of Caissa K12's services for its district clients is performing "secret shopper" outreach. Caissa K12 employees or contractors act as families interested in enrolling their child and visit or connect with public and private schools to look for enrollment or communication barriers or other customer service problems.

The company works with school districts including Florida's Broward County Public Schools through performance contracts, meaning Caissa K12 only gets paid if the company meets an agreed-upon student recruitment goal.

When it comes to recruiting and retaining students, Stephens says, the greatest mistakes public schools make are not advocating for themselves and not being willing to improve flaws like hard-to-navigate websites.

Those steps may sound easy, but they are difficult for school systems with a lot on their plates, he says.

If the government and the people want public schools to "act like a business instead of a public good, then we have to act more like a business," Stephens says. "That means we need to advocate for ourselves."

Districts that are innovative and want to build trust with families "absolutely have to reach out and let people know that they exist and let people know how awesome they are," he says.

Stephens points to creative approaches public schools can take to help attract and keep students.

One district client, he notes, installed a washer and dryer at a school to support school attendance by making sure children could have clean clothes. Other schools have provided feminine hygiene products, which also boosted school attendance, Stephens says.

Eliminating outdated and abrasive policies can make a difference too. For instance, schools need to remove attendance policies that punish students for having overdue library books or unpaid fees for band uniforms, he says.

“Stepping up those things has really helped. You have to identify what the issue is that's stopping a child from attending.”

A new federal school choice program launching next year has many public school communities wondering how their students might benefit from billions of dollars in expected donations for K-12 educational expenses.

Nevada's Clark County School District, for example, is considering a collaboration with its Public Education Foundation, which would serve as a scholarship-granting organization to direct donations to benefit the Las Vegas-area district's students. Those extra scholarship funds could be targeted toward career-connected learning opportunities, technology, tutoring and more, according to an Aug. 13 presentation to the district's board of trustees.

Elsewhere, public school advocates from Iowa to Denver are also making plans to direct some of these taxpayer contributions to public schools.

And at least one new national scholarship-granting organization has formed to funnel donations to services supporting public school students. The Future School Fund, founded by Deborah Gist, former Rhode Island commissioner of education and state education superintendent in the District of Columbia, intends to help schools identify programs where scholarship funds can be used.

The federal Education Freedom Tax Credit program, which launches on Jan. 1, will guide taxpayer donations to students attending either public or private schools, who can then use the scholarships to pay for allowable educational expenses.

This first-ever nationwide school choice program is projected to raise nearly $26 billion annually for about 2.2 million scholarships by 2030, according to the administering agencies, the U.S. Department of the Treasury and the IRS.

This could overshadow the nation's current largest education program, Title I, which has an $18.4 billion budget for fiscal year 2026 budget to aid children at low-income schools. Title I serves about 26 million students in 90% of the country's school districts, according to the Bipartisan Policy Center.

Only students living in states that opt into the Education Freedom Tax Credit program would be eligible to receive scholarships. As of Oct. 22, 30 states have indicated that they will opt into the program, according to the IRS.

Participating states will approve scholarship-granting organizations to operate in their states, which will then direct taxpayer donations to families to use for education expenses.

ACE Scholarships is one such SGO. The Denver-based nonprofit is working with public and private school systems in several states interested in soliciting donations for the federal tax credit scholarships that can then be used at their schools, according to CEO Norton Rainey.

ACE Scholarships already has several charter school clients and is in talks with public school districts to manage and distribute their donation accounts, he says. The organization, he adds, will focus on directing scholarships to students with the greatest financial needs.

But while some are urging public school systems and supporters to embrace the federal scholarship program, critics have many concerns. They worry about a lack of accountability, a financial drain on public schools and potential inequities.

"Simply permitting some public school students to benefit does not ensure that resources are equitably distributed or that schools and students most in need are served by the program," according to a coalition of 16 civil rights advocacy organizations in a Sept. 28 letter to state governors.

The rapid increase in school choice options is leading more public school leaders to consider creative ways to invest in marketing to keep their enrollment numbers up, says Mellissa Braham, associate director of the National School Public Relations Association.

But that doesn’t mean they necessarily have the budgets to do so, Braham says.

“Because districts nationwide are facing really tough times right now, a lot of them are having to reduce staff, and in that light, it's painful to put additional focus on marketing, even though ultimately it does help your budget,” she says.

One way districts are approaching enrollment marketing in a small yet impactful way is by having dedicated in-house staff solicit student and teacher testimonials and create videos to post on their communication channels.

Other low-cost marketing strategies include paying to boost social media posts or have their school or district website appear on search engines more frequently instead of relying on organic search, Braham says. Some districts are paying for targeted marketing emails, too.

She points to successful elements she's seen in school marketing campaigns, including firsthand testimonials from students and families, a focus on what makes a school and district unique, and targeted emails that feel specific to the person receiving it.

But districts in highly competitive areas may need to invest in more costly marketing efforts, she says. That could include buying billboard, television or streaming ads.

“If you're in a really competitive environment with a lot of different schools to choose from, it might be an investment you have to make with the ultimate return of enrollment, which brings in funds, which allows you to support staff programs and services,” Braham says.

In districts where school choice options are fairly new, Braham says, sometimes the biggest challenge for school leaders is to understand that families are now customers and that neighborhood public schools are not the only option.

Shifting to a marketing mindset is different from sending out regular school communications, which aim to share information and build relationships, she says. And that change is “new for a lot of public schools.”

Is private school choice a ‘tipping point’ for school closures?

As declining birth rates drive enrollment challenges, district leaders and public school advocates worry that school choice programs will further strain budgets.

That's the message families across the country are hearing from both public and private schools as competition for students heats up in the wake of declining birth rates and the rapid growth of taxpayer-supported private school choice. And while private schools are used to marketing their offerings, it's a new world for public schools that historically were the first — and most often only — choice for the great majority of families.

Now school districts large and small are scrambling for students as they watch their enrollments fall. They are boosting academic programs, giving personalized tours, and analyzing data on why students stay or go as they face greater rivalry with private schools. All of this is adding strain to already cash-strapped public schools as government funding is tied to enrollment. Throughout the U.S., public schools are closing or consolidating because they simply don't have enough students to fill their classrooms.

When Pflugerville Independent School District’s board of trustees in May approved closing four elementary schools in the 2027-28 school year, the district’s superintendent said a $20 million budget shortfall made the decision necessary.

The closures are expected to save the Texas district $15 million to $16 million a year as it grapples with declining enrollment alongside rising or fixed costs, said Pflugerville Superintendent Quintin Shepherd. Between the 2019-20 and 2025-26 school years, enrollment fell 5% from 26,400 students to 25,046, according to district data.

And Pflugerville ISD may face more difficult decisions about its budget and school consolidations in the future, district officials fear.

All of these things, we have to respond to simultaneously ... It sort of feels like an avalanche.

Superintendent, Pflugerville Independent School District

The district’s enrollment has dropped for a variety of reasons, Shepherd said, including a decline in birth rates, challenges with local housing affordability, changing demographics, charter school growth, virtual school options, migration patterns, open enrollment — and more recently a $1 billion universal statewide school choice program in Texas that’s rolling out this school year.

“All of these things, we have to respond to simultaneously,” Shepherd said. “It sort of feels like an avalanche.”

The state projected in April that Pflugerville ISD’s enrollment could drop as much as 1.6% — or by 389 students — in the 2026-27 school year directly because of the state's new Texas Education Freedom Accounts. That would amount to a loss of $4.3 million in state funds for the district.

“This is where the voucher conversation becomes so darn important,” Shepherd said.

While not all enrollment declines are driven by vouchers, he said, for those districts "already facing demographic challenges, the vouchers could become a tipping point. And that tipping point is what’s going to trigger the avalanche.”

For Pflugerville ISD specifically, Texas’ new school choice program “could become another tipping point,” Shepherd said.

“I think it puts everything back on the table for us,” he added. “Potential future closures, potential program cuts, and so on and so forth. It’s that our system has got to remain flexible and adaptive to the situation and the context as it continues to evolve.”

As enrollment challenges continue for public school districts nationwide, school closures have steadily increased in recent years.

While 755 schools closed in the 2021-22 school year, the number jumped to 1,011 in the 2024-25 school year, according to the National Center for Education Statistics. That seems to signal a return to school closure trends from before the aberration of COVID-19 pandemic times. For instance, NCES data shows 1,122 closures in 2018-19.

However, the national data on school closures is limiting, said Marguerite Roza, director of the Edunomics Lab and research professor at Georgetown University, as it does not yet capture the spike in school closures over the past two school years. During that time, she said, closures appear to have drastically picked up mostly due to sweeping birthrate declines.

Even where you see a lot of school choice, a lot of the vouchers are going to kids who are already in private schools.

School choice policies may be a variable that is impacting closures in certain communities, Roza said, but they are not the primary factor driving school closures. The key underlying issue is declining birth rates, she said.

“Even where you see a lot of school choice, a lot of the vouchers are going to kids who are already in private schools,” Roza said.

At the same time, some district leaders, public school advocates and researchers worry that large statewide school choice programs like those in Arizona, Florida and now Texas, could make matters worse and speed up public school closures.

Between 2022 and 2031, public school enrollment is expected to fall nationally by 5.5% — or 2.7 million students, according to an April analysis of NCES data by the Education Commission of the States. That enrollment loss would lead to a widespread decline in state revenue for public schools of $11.5 billion a year by the 2030-31 school year, projects a separate report by Bellwether and WestEd.

The ongoing enrollment drops are largely due to decreasing birth rates, the two education nonprofits said. But contributing factors include a “national slowdown in immigration,” families migrating out of state in some places, and growing interest in private school and homeschooling fueled by school choice initiatives.

Private schools, meanwhile, are not immune to the enrollment challenges and pressures from school choice competition. For instance, of 5,829 member schools of the National Catholic Educational Association, 62 closed or consolidated during the 2025-26 school year, and only 13 schools opened, for a net loss of less than 1% of the total number of NCEA member schools.

The competitive pressure public schools are feeling from private school choice programs will likely intensify as the first federal school choice program available nationwide launches next year. The massive and controversial Education Freedom Tax Credit program is expected to fund 2.2 million taxpayer-supported student scholarships totaling $26 billion each year by 2030, according to the IRS, which along with the U.S. Department of Treasury where it is housed, is administering the program.

Only students living in states that opt into the program will be eligible to receive funds through a scholarship-granting organization. According to the IRS, 30 states indicated they will participate as of Oct. 22. The Treasury Department issued temporary and proposed rules for the program Oct. 1.

Opponents consider the program a threat to public school funding that will only benefit wealthy families. Supporters, on the other hand, say that because scholarships can go toward both private school tuition and public school expenses, the program will expand educational opportunities and funding for all students. Covered public school expenses can include tutoring, ed tech and services for students with disabilities.

"Everyone is a winner in the education freedom tax credit, whether it's a private school or it's a public school," said Norton Rainey, CEO of ACE Scholarships, a Denver-based nonprofit scholarship-granting organization.

But Augustus Mays, vice president of partnerships and engagement at EdTrust, said taxpayer funds shouldn't be “funneled” to private schools, which often don't accept all applicants. Instead, federal tax credit dollars should go into public funding streams like Title I and the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act that “support all students,” he said during a forum hosted by Sen. Mazie Hirono, D-Hawaii, on Capitol Hill in Washington, D.C., on Sept. 22.

“This is not free money or a targeted investment in students with the greatest needs,” Mays said. “It is a[n] uncapped federal tax subsidy steering public resources toward private education with few safeguards.”

Meanwhile, public school advocates and researchers have said a majority of state private school choice program funds in Arizona, Florida and Texas have primarily benefited students already enrolled in private school or being homeschooled.

While that may mean there hasn’t been a “mass exodus” of public school students moving to private schools, even small groups transferring out can still “meaningfully impact” public schools — especially when enrollment is already slipping because of declining birth rates, said Joshua Cowen, a professor of education policy at Michigan State University.

When a state obligates more of its budget to expand voucher programs, he said, over time the state won’t be able to keep public school revenue above rising inflation costs.

“That leaves districts on their own at the local level, which leads to closure if you’re underenrolled or you’re just under financial distress," Cowen said. “So there’s like a downstream effect of all this.”

In the 2011-12 school year, Arizona implemented the nation’s oldest education savings account program. The state also became one of the first to expand its private school choice program to include all eligible students, beginning in the 2022-23 school year.

Education savings accounts, or ESAs, are funded from taxpayer dollars and can be used by parents for various educational purposes, including private school tuition, school transportation and tutoring. ESAs differ from vouchers, which give eligible families a portion of taxpayer-supported funding to pay for private school expenses.

Arizona’s school choice program’s participation boomed as more students gained eligibility, jumping from 12,000 students in 2021-22 to 88,000 by 2024-25, according to a Rand Corp. analysis published in November 2025. By the 2024-25 school year, about 7% of students in the state had an education savings account for private school tuition and other educational expenses.

Since 2022, Arizona school districts have shuttered 41 of the state's 2,418 public schools, according to a tracker by Save Our Schools Arizona, a public school advocacy group. Of the closures, 25 — or 61% — were Title I schools serving low-income communities.

One reason schools are closing in Arizona is because districts in the state historically overbuilt schools to the point that about a third of classroom seats are empty, said Katie Ratlief, executive director at Common Sense Institute Arizona, a nonpartisan research organization focused on fiscal policy. Ratlief served in leadership roles in then-Gov. Doug Ducey’s administration at the time when he signed the ESA expansion into law.

According to Ratlief, families were already leaving Arizona public school systems before the state’s ESA program launched. The slow student exodus that began in 2008 “created the political environment where ESAs could pass and move forward in Arizona because parents were demanding them,” she said.

Nonetheless, some public school leaders and advocates in the state have attributed the latest school closures to enrollment declines driven by Arizona's ESA program.

For instance, one official at Arizona’s Kyrene Independent School District partially blamed the expansion of private school choice for a recent decision to close six of its 25 schools starting this school year.

During a board meeting on the initial proposal, Superintendent Laura Toenjes said the district’s declining enrollment was being compounded by rising housing costs and charter school growth. Moreover, she said, the state's ESA funds "unfairly incentivize families to leave traditional public schools.”

Tyler Kowch, communications manager at Save Our Schools Arizona, said the state is a bellwether as school choice policies go national.

“We've long said that Arizona should serve as a warning sign for the rest of the nation of what a universal voucher program will do to public education."

A tale of two families: Why one left Broward schools — and another stayed

📅 Nov 11, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Let K-12 Dive's free newsletter keep you informed, straight from your inbox.

The reasons for coming or going are as varied as families themselves, but learning what drives their decisions can help public schools retain students.

This is the second installment of a three-part series on the impact of private school choice on public school enrollment.

To better understand parents' school choice decisions, we interviewed two Broward County, Florida, families — the Frys and the Gibson-Petersons. While each family has their own reasons for leaving or staying in the district's public schools, learning about these families' experiences may be helpful to the broader school choice discussion.

When Nancy Fry was a student in Florida's Broward County Public Schools in the mid 1990s to early 2000s, a population surge meant that the district was literally bursting out of its buildings. Fry's high school, in fact, had so many students that her entire 9th grade learned in portable classrooms.

Now, as she raises her two children in the same district, she's watching how underenrolled some schools are becoming. As a volunteer on school advisory boards, Fry has a front row seat to the struggles Broward faces as reduced funding is leading to staff layoffs and school closures.

"I saw — from one year to the next — my daughter's elementary school lose almost $4 million in funding just because of enrollment," said Fry, who is also a community organizer for Families for Strong Public Schools, a nonprofit that advocates for high-quality public schools across Florida.

Even with the reduced staffing and funding, Fry said, the public school system provides more learning and extracurricular opportunities for her children than private schools would. Her son is in 9th grade and participates in the international Cambridge program, an academically rigorous curriculum.

Additionally, her son has access to college-level courses, Advanced Placement classes, sports, theater and other activities. Fry's daughter is a 5th grader in the gifted program. A benefit of the public school system is that it must educate all students, regardless of ability, income or other factors, Fry said.

"Public schools are the great equalizer," she said. "Everyone has access to the same type of education, regardless of your income and your ZIP code."

At the same time, Fry said she knows public school is not a perfect fit for everybody.

Still, she said she knows Broward County Public Schools provides a quality education, and added that she's hopeful the district can attract more students and be more financially secure.

"Just having the stability of not worrying about: Is there going to be funding? Is there going to be a state takeover? Is the school going to have to close? Not having to worry about that, I think, would also help draw people back into the public schools," Fry said.

She also said Florida should invest more in its public school systems.

"I hope that the state decides to prioritize our students again and fund our public schools throughout the state accordingly, not just for Broward, but every school district. I believe every school district should get funding to meet the needs of the students, and that we can do better by our kids and make sure that they don't have to struggle to get what they need."

After two years of her daughter being bullied in 2nd and 3rd grade at her Broward County public school, Simone Gibson-Peterson enrolled her daughter in a private Christian school with tuition assistance from Florida's Hope Scholarship program for students who have experienced bullying, harassment, assault and other violations.

The Hope scholarship is now part of the state's universal Family Empowerment Scholarship program.

"I should not have to send my child to school and worry about her coming home feeling intimidated or not wanting to go back to school, and that was my biggest problem. Like, I cried every day having to watch my daughter go back into the same situation that she left the day before," said Gibson-Peterson. The mother said that as a student in Broward County Public Schools in the late 1980s, she herself was bullied and got into fights.

In the 12th grade, Gibson-Peterson dropped out of school, but later earned a GED and continued her education. She eventually earned a master's degree in criminal justice with a concentration in child abuse.

Her daughter, now a 7th grader, is thriving at her new school, Gibson-Peterson said. "There's so much accountability. They have zero tolerance for bullying, like that's not even going to happen," she said.

Her daughter's grades improved, and she is flourishing socially, her mom said. The school provides tutoring and extracurricular activities, as well as extra time for tests for her daughter, who has ADHD. "She's into track, she's into cheerleading, and she's just overall fully involved in school and happy about it."

The $8,000-a-year scholarship doesn't fully meet the $16,000 annual tuition, so Gibson-Peterson and her ex-husband split the expense, which comes to about $560 each a month. But, she said, the tuition covers most costs, including field trips.

Even though Gibson-Peterson is pleased with her family's decision to leave the public school system and enroll in private school, she said that because she works in child protection services, she's a firm supporter of a healthy and safe public school system.

The children she works with "need the public school system, but I also believe in school choice because not all of the schools have great teachers or great staff," Gibson-Peterson said.

"I want to be able to know that when I send my children — not just my biological child but my children that live in my foster care village — I want to know that when I send them to school, that they're safe."
