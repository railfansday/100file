‘The Baddest Speechwriter Of All’ Trailer: Ben Proudfoot And Stephen Curry Combine On Portrait Of Extraordinary Adviser To MLK

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Sports  |  Read Source →

EXCLUSIVE: Netflix is releasing a trailer for the Oscar-contending documentary The Baddest Speechwriter of All, directed by two-time Academy Award winner Ben Proudfoot and four-time NBA champion Stephen Curry.

The short film, winner of numerous awards including the Grand Jury Prize at Sundance, offers an “insider’s view of the Civil Rights Movement” through the lens of Dr. Clarence B. Jones, a close adviser to the Rev. Martin Luther King Jr. and a gifted writer who contributed to a legendary address by MLK that’s considered one of the most important speeches in American history.

The Baddest Speechwriter of All premieres on Netflix on December 8. Watch the trailer above.

The documentary “is rivetingly told by 95-year-old Clarence B. Jones — Juilliard-trained clarinetist, music copyright lawyer, and ultimately Dr. Martin Luther King Jr.’s trusted lawyer and speechwriter,” notes a synopsis. “Personally recruited by Dr. King in the early days of the Civil Rights Movement, the headstrong attorney was forced to choose between the security of his successful life or a path of purpose that would forever alter history. From the steps of Juilliard to the steps of the Lincoln Memorial, Jones reflects on the decisions that led him to advise Dr. King and help craft the first seven paragraphs of the iconic ‘I Have a Dream’ speech.”

In addition to Sundance, the film has earned Best Documentary prizes at AmDocs and the Newport Beach Film Festival, the Grand Jury Prize for Best for Short Documentary at the Dallas International Film Festival, Best Short Doc and the Audience Award for short documentary at the Annapolis Film Festival, and a Special Jury Award for illustrative excellence at the SCAD Savannah Film Festival, among other awards.

The Baddest Speechwriter of All is a production of Breakwater Studios, Unanimous Media, and Madison Wells Productions. The film is produced by Stephen Curry, Erick Peyton, and Ben Proudfoot.

Proudfoot won his first Oscar for The Queen of Basketball, a 2021 short documentary on which Curry served as an executive producer. Proudfoot won his second Oscar for The Last Repair Shop, a 2023 doc short he directed with Kris Bowers. He and Bowers were Oscar-nominated for their 2020 short A Concerto Is a Conversation.

Along with being the record holder for most 3-point baskets made in NBA history and one of the greatest to ever play the game, Curry co-founded with Erick Peyton the multimedia and production company Unanimous Media. Curry is the focus of two feature documentaries — Portrait of an Artist: Stephen Curry, directed by Gotham Chopra, and Stephen Curry: Underrated, directed by Peter Nicks and produced by Ryan Coogler.

“Evil All Along”: How Russell Brand Got Caught

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A once-deified celebrity who lured women under the guise of humor has been jailed for 10 years. Here, two sexual assault attorneys and the woman who spearheaded the investigation that started it all weigh in on the work to expose Russell Brand — and what precedent his landmark conviction sets for fellow industry predators: "They should be very scared."

“Whatever women wear, wherever they go, indeed whatever they drink, they retain the right to say ‘no’ and to demand justice from criminal courts when sexual predators like you, Russell Brand, act without their consent.”

Those were the words that rang out across Southwark Crown Court as Justice Joel Bennathan sentenced the once-worshipped presenter, comedian and actor to 10 years and six months in prison on Monday. Hordes of journalists and paparazzi effectively set up camp for four weeks while the jury was taken through extensive allegations of rape and sexual assault. In total, six complainants accused Brand of frantic attacks from 1999 to 2009.

Here, the man who went on to carve out a career for himself in Hollywood films like Get Him to the Greek, Forgetting Sarah Marshall and Rock of Ages — a previously famed womanizer, sell-out stand-up and the ex-husband of pop superstar Katy Perry — was bleakly depicted in court sketches showing the now-devout Christian with his head bowed, rolling a rosary between his fingers.

And last week, after consistently denying any criminal wrongdoing whatsoever, he was found guilty on four of seven counts. Three were frenzied sexual assaults, and one was a violent anal rape that took place after a woman had consensual vaginal sex with Brand at his Hampstead home in 2009. Bennathan’s sentencing remarks capture the harrowing reality of the victim’s experience. He told Brand: “You flipped her over, removed the condom and, without asking, forced your penis into her anus, causing her great pain. She asked you to stop, then asked you again loudly. You replied, and I quote, ‘I want you to feel my cum.’ You ejaculated. She was distraught and in severe pain. The bed was heavily soiled by blood, sperm and faeces. You told her it was her mess, and made her try to clear it up. Shortly after, you went off to get ready, then went to work. She was driven to a nearby underground station by your driver, who for some reason had tissues ready to hand to her as she wept. She was left distraught, in pain and terrified she might have contracted HIV.” The courtroom was completely silent.

Over the last month, this trial has dominated the media, spawned entire podcast shows, and divided the British public. Brand admitted during the trial that no one would “touch him with a bargepole” and that he had failed to persuade anyone to testify on his behalf. Naturally, many of those familiar faces on the British comedy circuit distanced themselves long ago from the sex offender. Jonathan Ross released a statement this week saying he is “horrified” by Brand’s crimes and was “genuinely unaware” of his conduct; there are quite literally hundreds of others who have not — and won’t be — saying anything.

And as Brand now embarks on what looks like a seven-year stay at HMP Wandsworth prison, a number of questions remain unanswered. Is it true that his lurid behavior was “an open secret” across the British TV industry, and why were so many of these allegations about incidents in the workplace? Why had Brand’s defense, led by a barrister named Ian Winter, claimed that journalists “poisoned the well of justice” by publishing various investigations into Brand’s behavior? How, with the sheer brazenness of Brand’s actions palpable in each attack he was found guilty of, did this predator stay undetected for at least 17 years? And what message does his conviction send to others who still operate as he did?

Two sexual assault attorneys, a scholar of work, pay and insecurity in the creative industries at the University of Warwick, as well as the woman who spearheaded investigations at Channel 4 and Sunday Times that would kickstart a police probe into Brand’s crimes, talked to The Hollywood Reporter about how Brand’s conviction has changed the U.K. entertainment industry. They lay bare the specifics of a freelance-heavy industry that allowed Brand to evade punishment, how women were lured into spaces with him under the guise of flamboyant comedy, and the dedicated, years-long work of investigative reporters who helped bring a celebrity rapist to justice.

Dorothy Byrne was head of news and current affairs at Channel 4 when Jemima Goldsmith, Brand’s ex-girlfriend, came to talk to her in 2019. “People would say, ‘How did you get onto the story?’ And I would say, ‘Oh, well, somebody told me about it,'” Byrne begins to THR.

It is only in the last 48 hours that Byrne has been able to publicly name Goldsmith as that whistleblower. The TV producer, who dated Brand for around a year in 2014, took to social media after his sentencing this week to detail her own experience of “violence, coercive control and abuse” at the hands of Brand. “I didn’t know about Russell Brand,” says Byrne. “I thought he was creepy and sleazy — and we now know that massive numbers of people in the media industry knew what a terrible person he was.” (Brand scarcely attempted to hide it himself; one of his stand-up tours in 2006 included an entire section dedicated to Brand telling the audience he likes “blowjobs where mascara runs a little bit.” A former friend testifying for the prosecution said Brand bragged about wanting to sleep with 5,000 women.)

Byrne continues: “But I worked in news and current affairs. Not comedy and entertainment. [Goldsmith] told me of her experience and she said, ‘I think he should be exposed to protect other women.’ Even to have that conversation was, for her, difficult, and I really admire and thank her for it.”

This would be the spark that would ignite a joint investigation between Channel 4’s Dispatches, The Times and Sunday Times, with two reporters — Rosamund Urwin and Charlotte Wace — leading the charge into reports, and eventually interviewing hundreds of people on the topic of Brand’s behavior. Byrne left her Channel 4 role in 2020 and maintains she was only involved in the early investigation. But even then, she says, other national outlets were tracking the story. “[They’d] found it very difficult,” she tells THR. “In this case of Brand, women were particularly afraid to talk. Firstly, because he was just so famous. They felt he was so famous and powerful. He’s got loads of money. ‘If I speak, he’s got all the power.’ And nearly everybody who works in TV now is freelance.”

And so came one of the first major hurdles in this case against Brand — the enduring trickiness of temporary contracts. Dr. Heidi Ashton, an expert in work, pay, and insecurity in the creative industries, says of the freelancer-filled TV industry in which Brand preyed on women: “A freelancer’s future employment depends heavily on maintaining a good reputation with the individuals who hire them, rather than with the organization issuing their contract. Raising concerns about bullying, harassment or unsafe working practices can therefore carry substantial professional risks — who would they go to with these issues? Workers may fear losing not only their current role but also jeopardizing their career.”

Much has been said of the men around Brand who watched the attacks unfold and did nothing. One of the sexual assaults involved Brand grabbing a woman’s breasts in a busy Soho bar in 2004; another saw him pushing a victim up against a wall, kissing her body and squeezing her breasts and buttocks “aggressively” — in the Capital Radio office. Last week, a former associate of Brand’s, Jack Bayles, was accused of watching and laughing while the entertainer placed his head and nose in a co-worker’s “vaginal area,” one of the assaults Brand was found guilty of. It took place as they worked on a Channel 4 project. (Bayles, whose credits include Michaela Coel’s I May Destroy You and Kat Sadler’s Such Brave Girls, has officially been asked to “step back” from his producing role as head of comedy at independent TV production company Various Artists Limited.)

But this complainant did speak up immediately after Brand forced his head into her groin. “It was brushed under the carpet by senior management,” she explained in her victim impact statement on Monday. “This really affected me and had something similar happened again, at the time, I know I would not have had the confidence to speak up again. There was a culture, at the time, which meant I felt I had to just carry on and not talk about it anymore.”

“[Brand] was considered this treasure, and every time he got fired, he’d get promoted to another job. He kept moving around, and I don’t think it’s because he’s got a lot of talent,” says Dr. Ann Olivarius, a leading women’s rights attorney and co-founder of McAllister Olivarius, a law firm working across the U.K., U.S. and Ireland. Brand did indeed find himself bouncing between roles in British TV and radio: in the 2000s, he hosted Big Brother spinoff Big Brother’s Big Mouth and worked on The Big Fat Quiz of the Year, Russell Brand’s Got Issues and Russell Brand’s Ponderland. “His employers didn’t stop it,” Dr. Olivarius continues about his behavior. “It was all considered well and good. What is that old thing men say? ‘If you can get laid, then lucky you.’ I haven’t met a woman who’s told me that yet.”

Byrne, however, believes that the men in these offices were also frightened of how speaking up might affect their careers, with the risk of retaliation higher because of their freelance status. “These contracts are often so short that people’s fear is you just won’t get asked back, and it will get around that you are a complainer or a troublemaker.”

“But there was another thing as well with Brand — women described him as frightening,” she adds. “If you think of some of the evidence in the court … one minute he’s funny and sexy, and then in a moment he changes.”

Women were routinely deceived by Brand’s status as a comedian. “The fact he was a comedian…” Byrne trails off. “This thing of when a man assaults you and he laughs. He’ll laugh and grab you. Brand treated it like a joke.” After Channel 4 began investigating, Byrne quickly became aware of how notorious Brand was among female comedians. “I discovered that female comedians warned each other about him,” she tells THR. His flamboyant nature and sharp wit — Brand himself also admits in his 2007 autobiography My Booky Wook: “I decided to act gay to attract girls” — allowed the shocking violence of his actions to be quickly diluted by that guise of drollery. “When a man laughs and grabs a woman, if the woman then complains, she’s regarded as having no sense of humor… In the world of comedy,” Byrne asks, “what could be worse than being branded as a woman with no sense of humor?”

New York-based sexual harassment and assault attorney Megan Thomas, of Megan Thomas Law, concurs. “I think he did a lot of things in the name of art and in the name of being a comedian and being in show business,” she tells THR. “People could laugh it off as ‘Okay, that’s just part of his persona.’ But it isn’t real. He was really very derogatory towards women, and he had very little respect for them.”

It’s worth noting that Brand is not the first predator to have been brought to justice in the film and TV world, and he certainly won’t be the last. A fellow comic, David Walliams, is the subject of another bombshell investigation from The Guardian and ITV News on Tuesday that alleged he abused his power as a beloved comedy actor and Britain’s Got Talent judge to prey on teenage girls, some as young as 15, for sex. Walliams has vehemently denied he is a sexual predator and said: “I have not broken the law, but I am devastated that some of the women I met 10 to 20 years ago have now expressed hurt or anger towards me.” The Hollywood Reporter has reached out to his reps for comment.

Walliams himself said in that same statement, “a power imbalance is inevitable when one party is famous.” It is a facet of these cases that arises time and time again, which makes women in the entertainment industry particularly vulnerable. “The element of fame really plays a role because when someone is so famous, it is easy to overlook red flags,” Thomas continues. “These women are maybe starstruck to a certain extent. … Everyone around this star is often creating a bubble and just constantly saying good things about them; they think to themselves, ‘No one will ever believe me,’ and this is why we see these cases happen so frequently with celebrities because they get to this godlike state where they think they’re untouchable. And to an extent, they’re not wrong, right?”

Indeed, a couple of complainants in Brand’s case admitted to being fans of his. The anal rape victim met the disgraced star at his comedy gig at the Hammersmith Apollo (after which he saved her number in his phone as “T” for “tart,” according to Justice Bennathan). Not only was Brand able to exploit that sense of awe, but he also had the power — and money, as many of these male celebrities do — to put together a pretty intimidating legal team, who can advise their clients to resort to threats. “We have heard time and time again men using that threat,” says Thomas. “‘Oh, no one will ever hire you. I’ll tell everyone that you’re horrible.’ I don’t know that we’ve heard that in [the Brand] case. I didn’t read that specifically, but we’ve heard that repeatedly in cases involving actors and directors. And even if a woman’s not in show business, she realizes, ‘If I speak out because this person’s a celebrity, my name will get dragged through the mud too.'”

Dr. Olivarius says women fear retaliation in the workplace and can often lose their entire careers. “You lose your job, you can’t get hired, and then [your] whole future is destroyed. So the calculation is: ‘If I just keep quiet about this, and try to put it in a perspective that it’s a bad date as opposed to an actual rape and attack, then I can maybe live with this and I will still have a career,'” she tells THR.

Even though the women testifying against Brand are entitled to lifelong anonymity, living through the trauma every day in court was clearly taxing. Some of them told the jury so themselves, and would fight back tears as they described how Brand forced himself on them.

Winter attempted to argue that these women were spurred on by the 2023 release of Channel 4 and The Sunday Times‘ investigation, in which Brand was accused of several attacks by a slew of women. Winter said Urwin, Wace and the team of journalists working on that documentary had “poisoned the well of justice” by prompting these complainants to reframe their encounters with Brand, decide they were nonconsensual, and press criminal charges. Brand, says Winter, was denied a fair trial because of the media.

Though the jury was ultimately unconvinced by this line of reasoning, Byrne says Winter — who reportedly snarled at Urwin outside the court after Brand’s guilty verdicts came in: “Are you happy now? You f***ed it” — deployed a rhetoric imported by President Donald Trump. “This attempt to say that when journalists do an investigation, it’s criminal… That is new,” she says. “Where does it come from? Donald Trump. This is his playbook. You don’t just attack journalists. You don’t call them liars.” Dr. Olivarius makes a similar point — that the rise of Trump, who himself faces extensive allegations of sexual assault, has led the way, especially in the U.S., for a post-#MeToo dismissal of women’s experiences. “Are women really such liars?” Dr. Olivarius asks. “Can you not trust a woman with her testimony?” She cites the recent Cornell 7 case as an example of that trend.

Putting the other criticisms around Winter’s misogynistic and “shameful” defense aside, both Thomas and Dr. Olivarius maintain that it is extremely rare for women to lie about their rape and sexual assault experiences. “Who wants to brag about sexual interference? Nobody,” says Dr. Olivarius. “I don’t know those women, but there seems to be this image, these rape myths, that somehow women make it up. I’ve never had anybody in my thousands of clients who I felt had made it up.”

These women, now widely commended for their bravery, were indirectly found through the tireless work of investigative reporters at Channel 4 and The Sunday Times. Brand was brought to justice because of that work, no matter how Winter spun it in court. “That investigative journalism helped more victims come forward,” says Thomas.

Brand’s conviction and lengthy sentence have set a precedent — a warning — for fellow industry predators, who are likely still operating. “They should be very scared,” says Byrne. “One of Brand’s offenses was that he reached over and grabbed a woman’s breasts. That has happened every day in TV. Well, it won’t be happening now because he got several months in jail for that.”

Such was the media coverage around the trial — and the livestream of the firm, unemotional, victim-commending statement from Justice Bennathan — that Brand now serves as an illustration of what powerful investigative reporting and courageous victims can do: hold the previously unimpeachable to account. “Several women I know who work in TV said to me before the case, ‘He’ll never be found guilty,'” Byrne admits. “I said, ‘Why not?’ and they said, ‘Because they never get found guilty. They always get away with it.’ But the evidence was there… The coverage was massive, and the public reaction has been huge.”

With the confirmation from the Met Police that Brand is under investigation for two more alleged rapes, Byrne thinks there will be a change. “I think more women are going to come forward. Look at how long he worked in the industry, nearly 30 years, and look at how prolific he was. This man could have hundreds of victims. I think this is a turning point.”

Dr. Olivarius hopes that civil cases will allow the women to receive compensation for what they’ve been through. “With criminal cases, they get no money damages. They get no therapy costs. They get nothing. They just testified. They did the right thing.” She lauds the introduction of Britain’s Employment Rights Act, which introduces sweeping changes across 2026 and 2027 and is set to significantly strengthen rules around workplace harassment, whistleblower protections and non-disclosure agreements. The country remains in dire need of stronger law enforcement and funding for victims’ therapy, she adds as a caveat.

While sexual assault and rape remain “an epidemic” in British, American and Irish society, Dr. Olivarius concedes that Brand’s conviction is unusual. “It’s really rare that they’ve got this guy,” she says. “He was so flamboyant, so bragging, and even in the courtroom, he’s got his Bible, waving it in the air as if it were air freshener. And what this will do to his arrogance and sense of self-satisfaction, I [am] very curious to see.” Thomas believes it proves the existence of a harsher spotlight on male predators in our current climate. “I don’t know if anyone’s listening, but I think we are seeing a second wave of the #MeToo movement,” she tells THR.

And Byrne, who has been privy to Brand’s reign of terror longer than most, is simply just relieved that women across the industry now have proof of what’s possible. “You can do something about it, you can go to the police, you can report them to your employer, and your employer has to do something about it,” she says. She is content that the man now in prison is exactly the man those women said he was. “He’s been left wing, he’s been right wing, he’s been an atheist, he’s been a Christian, he’s been a conspiracy theorist. But he hasn’t been any of those really,” says Byrne. “He’s been evil, criminal Russell Brand, all along.”

Karin Slaughter Said “Absolutely Not” to Adapting Her Novel ‘The Good Daughter.’ Then She Wrote the Whole Series

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

The bestselling novelist initially turned down Bruna Papandrea, wrote all six episodes of the Peacock series on her own and only then pitched it: "I wanted to make sure I could land the plane."

Some stories refuse to stay on the page. The Hollywood Reporter’s Beyond the Book column explores what happens when books make the leap to screen and beyond — unpacking what stayed, changed, how it was done and why it matters with the creatives who made it.

Karin Slaughter never planned to adapt The Good Daughter.

When producer Bruna Papandrea asked whether she’d be interested in bringing one of her novels to the screen herself, the author’s answer was immediate. “I said, ‘Absolutely not,’” Slaughter tells The Hollywood Reporter. “I don’t have time.” Related Stories TV 'The Good Daughter' Review: Rose Byrne and Meghann Fahy Elevate Peacock's Overly Familiar Southern-Fried Thriller General News Foundation Books Unveils First Titles in Macmillan Partnership; John Goodman to Narrate Animated Short Adaptation (Exclusive)

She’d already watched two of her books become hits in other hands: Netflix’s Pieces of Her, adapted by Charlotte Stoudt, and ABC’s Will Trent, run by Liz Heldens. Though she declined, Slaughter couldn‘t move on, and the idea that she might do it herself stuck. So she started writing the scripts, and she told no one.

“I wrote them completely on my own because I didn’t want to tell anybody if I’d done a bad job,” she tells THR. When she finally told her agent, she made one request: Don’t tell Papandrea — or anyone for that matter — yet. “Because she’d want an option. I just wanted to do this in secret.”

The secrecy also gave her time to take on the creative challenge. “I know from my novel writing that the beginning of a story is important, but just as important is the end,” she says. “People can read a book they think is mediocre, and that’s fine. But if it starts out really great and craps out at the end, they’re furious. So I wanted to make sure I could land the plane.”

With the scripts done, Slaughter went back to Papandrea. “I said to Bruna, ‘This is the show. If you’re interested, that’s great. If not, obviously I’ll take notes, but I haven’t wasted your time and money on an option.’ She read it overnight and said, ‘Let’s go, let’s do this.’”

The result is The Good Daughter, Peacock’s six-episode adaptation of Slaughter’s 2017 bestseller, which drops on the streamer in full on Nov. 12. Slaughter is the sole writer on every episode and serves as showrunner and executive producer.

The series stars Rose Byrne and Meghann Fahy as Samantha and Charlotte Quinn, sisters still living with the aftermath of a single event of violence during their childhood, with Brendan Gleeson as their father, Rusty. The cast also includes Shayna Benardo, Olivia Williams, Harper Steele, Jake McDorman and Michael Dorman. Made Up Stories, Papandrea’s banner, produces with Fifth Season.

When the team took the project to streamers, Peacock “just got it,” Slaughter says. “They had thoughtful questions, and they seemed like people who would be good partners.”

Though an author taking on the showrunner role for the first time, Slaughter‘s résumé shows why she could afford to skip the usual option-and-develop process. Will Trent was renewed by ABC this spring for a fifth season and had 10 million cross-platform viewers last season, making Slaughter’s catalog one of the more reliable book-to-TV pipelines. The Good Daughter, though, is the first time she’s been in charge of that pipeline herself, and she set ground rules early. Drawing on her long relationship with her book editor, she made a request of the network about notes.

“I said, ‘Obviously there are going to be notes, but I need you to speak with one voice,’” she recalls. “You can have all the meetings in the world and discuss it, but I don’t want a note that Kevin from accounting suggested and you don’t agree with.”

She also wanted the reasoning behind each note on the table, so she could interrogate it. “I’m not a ‘note behind the note’ person — I want to know the note. So let’s have a conversation so I can figure out what you want, how to do it and how to make sure we’re both happy with the outcome,” she says.

As a first-time showrunner, the biggest adjustment, she says, was losing a novelist’s room to explain things. “With a book, I get 500 pages to tell you what people are thinking,” she says. “So it was figuring out how to convey information without making it clunky. I never wanted anything to feel written. As a novelist, dialogue is probably one of my strengths, so it felt natural.”

To keep the mystery working and span across six hours, she made clue sheets as her way “to figure out how to make the puzzle work and give all the pieces.” When a note came in on a script, she could show exactly where a detail paid off. “I’d say, ‘We need this piece of information because it pays off in [episode] five or six,’” she says. “After a while, you develop a certain level of trust.”

She also kept the season concise. “I learned very quickly that as much plot as a novel has, there’s not enough for eight episodes. I made the decision early on: This is only going to be six episodes, because I’m not going to do a filler episode,” she says. “Pacing is a hallmark of my writing. I wanted you to be emotionally invested and on the edge of your seat. There’s the tension you get from the plot, but this is a story that relies on emotional tension, and I needed those emotions to hit at the right times.”

She also pushed for Peacock to release every episode at once. “I was scared they’d say no and parcel it out.”

The reasoning for the immediate rollout is because Slaughter thought of the series as “a six-hour movie.” That interpretation also shaped the choice of director Steph Green (The Americans), who directs all six episodes. “She understood that it was a movie and not standalone episodes,” Slaughter says. Each installment has its own genre — one is a legal drama, another a family drama then a murder mystery — but Green kept a “symmetry of place, setting and atmosphere” across all of them, Slaughter explains.

“I think the first thing I said to her was, ‘I’m so tired of thrillers where everything is dark, even in the daytime. Does anyone have a lamp in this house? I want the bad things to happen in daylight,’” Slaughter says. “That’s what we focused on, and our cinematographer, Zoë White, totally got it.”

Slaughter also fought to film in Georgia. “They made us scout in Toronto, and I was such a butt the whole time,” she says laughing. Once the team reached North Georgia, she recalls showing them the red clay — red is a key color in the story — and the kudzu. “Because we’re writing about things that happen to women and girls every single day, we needed to ground this in a naturalistic environment,” she says.

A Georgia native herself, Slaughter took the story‘s Southern backdrop seriously. “I was very clear: This is not Southern Gothic. Those words have meaning. This is not grotesque, it is not horror. It’s a story set in the South, but it’s not a Gothic story. Steph put together this beautiful look book, and it felt like a perfect fit because she got what I wanted to do and how I wanted it to look.”

The same thinking shaped the score. Composer Jonathan Sanford grew up in Maryland’s Prince George’s County but has family roots in Georgia, and he understood, Slaughter says, “that there’s a way to tell Southern stories that’s just such a trope,” with slide guitar and banjo.

“I said, ‘I don’t want to hear any of that. I want a new, crisp Southern musical language developed for this,’” she says. “As a Southerner, it was very important to me to represent the South for what it is, not all these stereotypes.”

Slaughter teases that Fahy even sings a track that will be featured on the show‘s soundtrack. “I knew she was a singer — she has a beautiful voice — and from the moment we started shooting I was working on everybody: ‘We’ve got to get Meghann to do something,’” she says. “I think they did it just to shut me up. But she did amazingly well. It’s a beautiful cover.”

Fahy and Bryne may star as the leading protagonists, but Jessica Biel was originally attached to star and executive produce the series, which scored a straight-to-series order at Peacock in March 2024. She eventually left the project in September of that year. Slaughter holds no grudge. Biel, instead, went on to star in The Better Sister, based on the novel by Slaughter’s friend and fellow author Alafair Burke. “I loved watching it. What do I have to be angry about?” she says.

Slaughter credits Bryne and Fahy for evolving her characters with Byrne’s casting as Sam inspiring a change in the character. “Sam in the book is a lot colder than Sam in the show, and that’s Rose, because she has this natural warmth,” Slaughter says. After watching dailies, she reworked lines to “lean into and support what she was doing.”

Fahy’s Charlie called for rewrites, too, particularly in her romance with Ben, played by Dorman. “In the book, Charlie is much more thirsty for Ben — she’s chasing him. Meghann Fahy is not thirsty,” Slaughter says. “So I had to find ways to express that longing for Ben in a way that was believable.”

And because Fahy handled Slaughter’s Southern rhythms so easily, Slaughter kept writing her more lines. “I’m going to write the hell out of this scene,” she remembers thinking. “It made more work for her.”

Slaughter has been candid about using violence in her books to open up a dialogue. With The Good Daughter, she hopes the series grounds the conversation in the fact that violent events happen to “real people” and that “there are real people living their lives after these things happen.”

She points to Bryne‘s Sam, who lives with lasting injuries from the shooting she experienced at a young age, including impaired vision and walking with a cane. “I watch a lot of television, and I can’t think of many shows where you see a survivor of gun violence and what their life is like,” she says. “We always hear that horrible statistic — ‘this many people died and this many survived‘ — and we think, ‘Thank God they survived.’ But we don’t think about what that recovery looks like. I wanted to show that.”

Byrne, she says, did “so much work with a physical therapist, figuring out how to move in a body like that. She has such physicality, and to distill all of that into this person recovering from a gunshot wound shows the range of her abilities. The fact that she could elevate that performance is remarkable.”

Despite taking on the showrunner title, Slaughter’s work as a novelist never stopped. After finishing the show’s scripts, she went back to work on her most recently published book, We Are All Guilty Here, and noticed the show has already changed how she writes, no longer keeping characters in one place for long stretches.

“When I write my Will Trent novels, I can have a whole scene in a car, and it’s a chapter,” she says. “But with Guilty, I was moving people around more.”

The reason was a day on the Good Daughter set. “You don’t know guilt until you’ve put Rose Byrne and Meghann Fahy in a car on a trailer in South Georgia, 10,000 degrees in August, and the air-conditioning’s not on,” she says laughing. “I was like, ‘Oh my God, why couldn’t I have set this in an office? Why are they sweating?’”

Whether she’ll take another book to the screen herself, Slaughter isn’t saying yet. For now, she’s finishing her next novel and focused on getting viewers to The Good Daughter. “It’s exactly the kind of show I’d love to watch,” she says. “I’m so proud of it!“

Frida Kahlo’s Image Has Become a Global Brand. A Viral AI-Generated Ad Makes Things Worse.

In our Frida Kahlo–obsessed moment, it’s may be difficult for some to remember that the Mexican artist almost died before she could change art history. In 1925, when she was just 18 years old, she was riding a bus that collided with a trolley, an accident that severely injured her pelvis, abdomen, and uterus. During the painful recovery, she took up painting. “I am not sick,” she said. “I am broken. But, I am happy to be alive as long as I can paint.”

A little over a century after the accident, the fashion brand Les Énervés, which has its roots in Paris and Bali, released an ad campaign that seemed determined to posthumously break Kahlo all over again, this time using AI. In the bizarre ad, which was first posted to Instagram in October and started going viral on X this month, a bubblegum-chewing Kahlo recklessly drives a bus through Paris. Her passengers include the Mona Lisa and Vincent van Gogh, his severed ear replaced with a steel prosthetic. It’s worth noting that the Kahlo depicted here downs multiple drinks before getting behind the wheel; the real artist struggled with alcohol and drug addiction.

“It’s Frida, bitch,” she says, just before the soundtrack switches from an Édith Piaf song to Britney Spears’s “Gimme More.”

According to a release by Les Énervés, the ad was directed by the brand’s founder, Wakim Krari, and was produced using what is described as “AI, and a lot of patience.” The brand’s website adds that AI has been used to recreate Kahlo’s bus, this time as a Renault TN, which might have been seen in Paris when Kahlo staged an exhibition there in 1939.

Les Énervés has attempted to frame the ad as some sort of statement of reclamation. “A hundred years later, she is the one driving,” Les Énervés notes. “Vincent and Mona Lisa are in the back. Nothing is going to hit her this time.”

Not everyone has the same interpretation. One X post with 46,000 likes says the ad is “disrespecting the dead.” I concur.

Worse, it’s far from the first generated AI-generated Kahlo project to disrespect her memory. Another AI-generated video, also making the rounds on TikTok and X, finds Kahlo recast as a Spice Girl, singing “Wannabe” alongside the Mona Lisa, Vermeer’s model from Girl with a Pearl Earring, and the woman from Jan van Eyck’s Arnolfini Wedding. There are also quite a few Kahlo chatbots online, including one that implores viewers with this prompt: “Ask me about my struggles with physical pain, my relationship with Diego Rivera, or my feminist beliefs.”

It’s one thing to feed the Mona Lisa into AI, and quite another to use AI to mess with Kahlo, who carefully fashioned her own self-image in ways that resisted the social norms expected to guide a 20th-century Mexican woman. In her painted self-portraits, she consciously sported a darkened unibrow and facial hair men’s clothing, flouting gender expectations. She was famous, too, for wearing Tehuana skirts, positioning Indigenous Zapotec culture as central to Mexican identity. The nuances of her fashion are increasingly sandblasted by brands ranging from Barbie dolls to tequila bottles. Mari Carmen Ramírez, the curator of a show called “Frida: The Making of an Icon” that is now on view at Tate Modern in London, has said that Kahlo’s image has become “subsumed” by artists and activists, seemingly both for the better and the worse.

To me, all this AI-generated Kahlo content is subsuming the artist by another means. The reason to be concerned about all this, though, is only partly to do with the commercialization of Kahlo—it’s more about AI and the ways it can be used to distort, and perhaps even disregard, art history.

It might seem cute, for example, to place Kahlo at the center stage of art history, in such a way that sidelines her husband, the artist Diego Rivera, who features in the Énervés ad as trying to keep up with Kahlo while she walks down a rain-slicked street.But their 25-year relationship was more complicated than that: Kahlo was both deeply in love with and frequently frustrated by Rivera, who betrayed her by having an affair with her younger sister. The gesture here is a simple-minded one, easily made given that Kahlo is alive to comment. (She died at 47 in 1954, when she was nowhere near as famous as she is today.)

One is tempted to invoke Britney Spears, just as Les Énervés has done, and say to this brand: Leave Frida alone. Perhaps Les Énervés will do just that, but the brand has seemingly already found its next target. “The next chapter is coming soon, with a new muse and new things hidden in the frame,” the brand promises on its website. It reads like a threat.

On Thursday (Nov. 12), the Euclid space telescope released its first major batch of data, capturing the light of more than a billion objects over an area of sky as big as 9,500 full moons. The data release lays the foundation for one of the largest-ever 3D maps of the cosmos and many groundbreaking discoveries about the nature of dark energy and dark matter.

The European Space Agency's (ESA) Euclid space telescope has been called the "Dark Universe Detective" because of its capability to investigate the dual mysteries of dark matter, the universe's most mysterious stuff, and dark energy, the force accelerating the expansion of the universe. Collectively, these cosmic elements are sometimes referred to as the dark universe.

The new data release, designated DR1-Foundation, was built from 13 months of observations and is accompanied by a multitude of images, including large sky mosaics. It marks the first major release of mission data since Euclid launched in mid-2023.

"These are far more than beautiful pictures; today marks a scientific milestone in our study of the cosmos," ESA's Director of Science, Carole Mundell said in a statement. "This major Euclid data release represents the largest space-based survey of its kind, laying the foundation of future discovery in a new regime for astronomy and demonstrating the excellence of European technology, science and international collaboration."

The ESA put into raw numbers the sheer number of celestial objects viewed by the Dark Universe Detective and contained in DR1-Foundation. That includes light spectra from 160 million sources, over 900 million galaxies, and 60 million candidates for quasars, regions of space dominated by feeding supermassive black holes.

"This is a change in scale from Euclid's previous releases, which included a few days of observations over around 60 degrees squared of sky," ESA's Euclid project scientist Valeria Pettorino said. "We're now releasing more than a year of data covering a significantly larger area. This unlocks groundbreaking science."

Euclid can see such a vast number of cosmic objects thanks to the fact that its space-based position helps it avoid interference caused by Earth's atmosphere.

This includes clusters of galaxies and even larger superclusters. The study of these objects, some of the largest structures in the cosmos, can reveal how matter came together across the larger network of gas, dust and dark matter called the cosmic web.

"Euclid doesn't just look far back in time  — it does so across huge areas of sky," Euclid Consortium member Barbara Sartoris of JL Lagrange Laboratory, Nice, France, explained. "This gives us an unprecedented view of large samples of ancient galaxy clusters that were previously out of reach. "We will explore these in the coming months to learn how these giants grew, and decipher what they reveal about the dark universe."

Another exciting element of the new Euclid data release is the 15,000 strong gravitational lensing candidates catalogued by the space telescope. Gravitational lensing is an element of the universe first explained in Einstein magnum opus, general relativity, in 1915.

General relativity says that objects with mass cause the very fabric of space to warp, and that gravity arises from this warping. But there is another consequence. When light passes this curvature, its usually straight path is curved. That means when light from a single background source passes an object with great mass, like a galaxy or galaxy cluster, a strong gravitational lens, it can arrive at our telescopes at different times.

As a result of this strong lensing, background sources can be greatly amplified. It is an effect that the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has been using to great effect to study galaxies in the early universe.

Thus, the prospect of mapping 15,000 strong gravitational lenses is monumental, especially in studying the rate at which the universe is expanding and thus the effect of dark energy.

"Strong lensing provides unique insight into the dark universe, allowing us to 'weigh' individual galaxies, including their invisible dark matter, and explore how the universe is expanding," Natalie Lines of the University of Portsmouth, U.K., and member of the Euclid Consortium, said. "Over 10 years ago, we forecast that Euclid would revolutionize the field of strong lens discovery. That day is finally here."

Arguably, the most exciting thing about DR1-Foundation is that Euclid is just getting started. The area of sky covered from spring 2024 to spring 2025 represents just 14% of Euclid's target for its entire six-year survey.

That means whatever scientists discover from this data release could be the tip of the iceberg in terms of how the Dark Universe Detective will change our understanding of the cosmos.

"DR1-Foundation includes raw data, calibrated images, catalogues, and spectra over a huge sky area, with enough statistics touncover even rare objects," Valeria said. "It will be used to map the large-scale structure of the universe in unprecedented detail.

Astrophysicists Want to Forecast When a Black Hole Launches Its Jets

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

Black holes are not just objects. Some astrophysicists prefer to call them locations. From this vantage point, they're locations where the Universe's laws of physics are pushed to their extreme limits.

Black holes are gravitational gluttons that draw everything in their vicinity towards them. When another star strays too close, the black hole can strip its gas away. The gas doesn't necessarily disappear over the event horizon and leave our observable Universe. It first gathers in a rapidly rotating accretion disk, where its temperature is raised to extreme levels. Some of this superheated material is ejected in a pair of collimated jets from the black hole's axis of rotation.

These jets are streams of plasma and particles that can reach relativistic speeds. They can have an enormous reach; the largest ones come from supermassive black holes and can extend for millions of parsecs into space. Though astrophysicists have witnessed many of these jets, their exact causes remain uncertain. The extreme gravity, magnetic fields, and temperatures around black holes is all a part of it.

Astrophysical jets shape their surroundings by heating star forming gas and moving it around on a massive scale. Stars form from cool gas, and the energy from jets works directly against that. The jets can also inflate bubbles where gas is too diffuse to collapse and form stars.

This JWST NIRCam image shows the jets coming from Messier 87, one of the most massive galaxies in the local Universe. These jets aren't from an x-ray binary, they're from its central SMBH that has billions of solar masses. It's an example of extreme astrophysical jets and an indicator of the extreme physics at black holes. Image Credit: Jan RÃ¶der, Maciek Wielgus et al., Astronomy & Astrophysics (2025)

Astrophysicists want to be able to predict when these jets are triggered so they can quickly gather follow-up observations, and new research in The Astrophysical Journal has made some progress. It's titled "Jet Forecast: Timing of Discrete Jet Ejections of Stellar-mass Black Holes Anticipated via X-Ray Properties," and the authors are Toshihiro Kawaguchi and Kazutaka Yamaoka. Kawaguchi is from the Graduate School of Science and Engineering at the University of Toyama, and Yamaoka is from the Institute for Space-Earth Environmental Research (ISEE) at Nagoya University.

The pair of researchers have been working on this problem for a while. In a 2025 study, they examined an x-ray binary consisting of a black hole and a donor star and found a link between the parameters of the inner accretion disk and jet activity. That research suggested that as the accretion disk's inner edge shrank towards its innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO), the black hole launched its jets. "This fact suggests that the jet materials originate from accreting matter from the innermost part of the disk," the authors wrote.

That was progress, but in that paper the researchers pointed out that more observations were needed. "Both X-ray spectral and timing observations are very important to understand the accretion disk states which can change very rapidly. Dense, simultaneous coverage with radio observations are also crucial to understand diskâjet interactions," the authors wrote in the conclusion to their 2025 paper.

There seem to be changes in an x-ray binary's (XRB) x-ray emissions prior to jets being launched. The spectrum is altered, and variability decreases. Astrophysicists have interpreted these changes as a signal that jets are about to be emitted. But the signals haven't been clear and reliable enough, so astrophysicists haven't been able to use them to time their follow-up observations. The researchers' previous work showed that as an accretion disk's inner edge shrank down to its ISCO, jets were launched. This could be the reliable signal astrophysicists are searching for.

This work expands their observations to more XRBs to see if this holds true. The researchers hope to develop a forecasting model for XRB jet launching so that more complete observations can be obtained.

The results show that the rapidly decreasing radius of the inner disk, called Rin, combined with a decrease in x-ray emission variability (indicated by rms), are a reliable predictor of jet launching. "All the four sources turned out to show the precursors of jet ejections (drops in Rin and rms) 1â10 days before radio activities," the authors write.

In two of the four systems, jets formed below this threshold. How ‘Godmother of K-Culture’ Miky Lee Earned the Asian Hall of Fame’s Centennial Medal of Lifetime Achievement

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

As CJ Group vice chair, Lee helped launch Superstar K, the MAMA Awards and KCON, making K-pop a global phenomenon.

CJ Group Vice Chair Miky Lee has received the Centennial Medal of Lifetime Achievement from the Asian Hall of Fame during an awards ceremony in Los Angeles, which took place on Nov. 5. The honor shows how Lee has steadily built major milestones for both Korean film and K-pop over the years.

The Asian Hall of Fame is a cross-cultural educational and research institution dedicated to recognizing Asian and Indigenous legacy, early-career achievement and interracial collaboration. In a given year, the Centennial Medal of Lifetime Achievement goes to only a handful of people, and recipients are chosen from figures who have built decades of global achievement and influence in fields such as culture, arts and entertainment. That sets the medal apart from the annual Class Inductees, which comprise a larger group of people.

The award carries particular weight because it recognizes Lee’s role over the past 30-odd years as a bridge between Korea and the world. Throughout her career, she has widened the base for the global spread of Korean content and exchange between cultures. Billboard Korea takes a look back at what Lee has done to help build K-pop’s global infrastructure, and at what the Centennial Medal of Lifetime Achievement means.

While Lee’s name is familiar in the film world, she stated during her acceptance speech in Los Angeles on Nov. 5 that music was the starting point. “And me? I simply loved music,” she said, adding that music “has been at the heart of everything we’ve built in culture.” Lee is the figure who created and developed K-pop’s anchor points, building on music platforms that span broadcasting, distribution and live performance.

The origin of the “K-survival audition,” a cradle for K-pop talent

On July 24, 2009, Mnet’s Superstar K first went on air, raising the curtain on Korea’s first modern survival audition show. It went on to produce a long list of standout stars, and countless audition programs followed the show’s model. Lee had been the one to initially suggest that Mnet look into a program of its own after she had taken note of American Idol in the U.S.

In 2016, Mnet’s Produce 101 carried the audition format over into idol survival and set off another craze. The Produce series went on to launch I.O.I, Wanna One and IZ*ONE. Boys Planet series gave rise to ZEROBASEONE and ALPHA DRIVE ONE, and I-LAND2 produced izna.

The stage didn’t stop at Korea. In Japan, the PRODUCE 101 JAPAN series formed JO1, INI and ME:I, and KO1KEYZ debuted through 2026’s PRODUCE 101 JAPAN SHINSEKAI. Today, CJ ENM’s local label business in Japan is broadening its scope beyond audition-linked groups, extending to the development of Japanese boy band DXTEEN.

The “K-audition universe” and “K-star development system” that Lee set in motion are still evolving. Next up is GIRLS PLANET 2027, a global girl group project in partnership with Universal Music Group’s REPUBLIC Collective. The show is targeting a 2027 premiere, and the two sides will collaborate on everything from production to the debut group’s global activities. Superstar K is also set to return next year with its first new season in 11 years, drawing attention from music fans.

From Korea’s first music video awards to the Dolby Theatre: the MAMA AWARDS

The MAMA AWARDS have long been one of the award shows K-pop fans both in Korea and abroad can’t help but look forward to. The show started out as the Mnet Video Music Awards, Korea’s first music video awards. The show combines Mnet’s stage production capabilities with artists’ talents to deliver visuals and performances that are talked about long afterward.

The show’s signature slogan, “Music Makes One,” has become a signal that gets K-pop fans’ hearts racing at the end of each year. Lee invoked the term in her acceptance speech, saying, “‘Music Makes One.’ That was the dream. In 2024, the show planted its flag for the first time at Hollywood’s Dolby Theatre, the iconic home of the Academy Awards.

Leading KCON as it grew into a global fan community

Lee spearheaded the founding and expansion of KCON, the music and culture festival that launched in California in 2012. KCON was different from the start. Alongside concerts, it offered talk sessions, workshops and artist signing events. Back in 2014 and early on in their career, BTS took the KCON LA stage, with RM later stating in an interview with Mnet that the performance there was what first earned the group a strong response in the U.S.

The road she has traveled, and the road ahead, as K-pop’s new frontier

Produced by CJ ENM and Eureka Productions, the show pairs Western and K-pop artists to reinterpret existing hits and perform them in front of a live audience in Seoul. Their speeches doubled as a chronicle of Korean entertainment. Miky Lee looked back on the 1995 investment in DreamWorks and said, “We wanted to help lay the foundation for Korea’s entertainment industry.” She continued, “In the past, nearly 85% of songs on Korean TV were American pop songs, but by the 2000s, it was completely the other way around.” She traced the journey through Mnet, MAMA and KCON, and closed her acceptance by saying, “Now, it’s our turn to believe in the next generation.” The question that now lies is just how far the next generation will carry the K-pop frontier she has expanded.

Every Show in Marvel’s WandaVision Trilogy, Ranked

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

VisionQuest has finally aired its series finale, bringing closure to the unexpected trilogy that began in 2021 with WandaVision and continued in 2024 with the even more unexpected Agatha All Along. At face value, the saga is a tale about how a witch and a synthezoid deal with the loss of their makeshift family, set within the confines of a superhero cinematic universe. In reality, the WandaVision trilogy is about two main things: the first is grief, how we process it, what it does to us, and how we eventually learn to live with it. The second is family. It's a story about grieving mothers, struggling fathers, and lost children, all coming together to find purpose and belonging.

It wouldn't be an overstatement to say the WandaVision trilogy is the single best, most cohesive and narratively impactful thing the MCU has ever produced. For starters, every show prioritizes meaning over spectacle and character over action; the result is a tale of multiple wandering characters who connect through shared experience, learn to process their pain, and ultimately gain new insight about the world and themselves, entering the next chapter of their lives as updated versions of themselves. Declaring which of them is the best is not an easy task, but someone has to do it. Here, we rank every entry in the WandaVision trilogy, not as a way to bring them down, but to celebrate what is undoubtedly Marvel's boldest, most compelling and cathartic creative swing.

The trilogy began in 2021 with WandaVision, written and created by Jac Schaeffer. Set in the aftermath of Avengers: Endgame, the show sees Wanda Maximoff (Elizabeth Olsen) create an idealized version of her life inspired by her love for classic sitcoms. In this world, Vision (Paul Bettany) is alive, and they even welcome twins. Of course, Wanda's life is complicated by the presence of S.W.O.R.D. and a mysterious nosy neighbor named Agnes (Kathryn Hahn). Oh, and there's also the fact that she enslaved an entire town to create this pretty little life, so there's that.

WandaVision is, first and foremost, a character study on Wanda Maximoff, and Elizabeth Olsen makes the most of it, delivering what might just be the best performance in the MCU. The writing is sharp and insightful, using Wanda's trauma to continue her character arc and keep the larger MCU storyline going. Bettany and Hahn are excellent support, as is Teyonah Parris as Monica Rambeau. The show focuses on the destructive power of grief, but also its ability to change and help us grow, finding a perfect way to include the superhero aspect of the story. Alas, WandaVision is not perfect, and it's pretty much undone by its rushed, borderline unsatisfying finale. The subsequent Doctor Strange in the Multiverse of Madness also did a disservice to the show by pretty much committing character assassination with Wanda, which certainly doesn't help.

The most recent effort, VisionQuest, is also the last chapter in the trilogy, and it does a wonderful job tying everything together. Set about 18 months after the events of Agatha All Along, the show centers on Vision as he settles into his new reality with the memories from the old Vision. Several AIs are also present in his programming, including E.D.I.T.H. (Emily Hampshire), F.R.I.D.A.Y. (Orla Brady), J.A.R.V.I.S. (James D'Arcy), and Ultron (James Spader). Vision's life is put in danger when a bounty hunter is sent after him, causing him to reunite with his lost son, Tommy (Ruaridh Mollica).

VisionQuest is a pretty perfect finale to the story, putting the spotlight on fathers and their complicated relationships with their sons. The Vision-Tommy bond is great, but the real center of the story is the Vision-Ultron father-son dynamic, with Bettany and Spader not only sharing excellent chemistry but also making the most of an unexpectedly powerful screenplay. Spader, in particular, is very clearly having the time of his life here, bringing an electrifying energy to the show. The AIs are a worthy addition, too, even if they're used a bit too much like a deus ex machina that benefits from using a lot of technical jargon that may or may not make actual sense. Yet, at its core, VisionQuest is a tale of self-discovery and coming to terms with one's role in life as a father, brother, and son. It continues the family angle while finally giving the spotlight to Vision, one of the MCU's most reliable yet sidelined characters.

Who would've thought the show that was seemingly inspired by a catchy tune would be the best in this trilogy? Alas, that's precisely the case. Agatha All Along picks up two years after WandaVision, with Agatha Harkness (Hahn) breaking out of Wanda's spell with the help of a young Teen (Joe Locke) who wants her help to walk the Witches' Road. Recruiting a coven, the duo ventures onto the Road, followed by the Salem Seven, a group of witches hell-bent on revenge against Agatha. The mysterious Rio Vidal (Aubrey Plaza) also has unfinished business with Agatha.

Agatha All Along is the most cohesive, well-planned, and best-executed series, not only in the WandaVision trilogy but in the MCU as a whole. What might seem like a gag show is actually a very tender and thoughtful exploration of motherhood and grief from the perspective of the MCU's most volatile antihero. Agatha is evil, selfish, and ruthless, a killer through and through, but she's also unexpectedly loving, and her bond with the Teen is as sweet as it's hilarious. Speaking of Teen, Locke is a revelation as Billy Maximoff, bringing a lot of nuance and plenty of sass to his performance. Narratively, Agatha All Along takes bold swings, centering its entire story on a song and delivering some of Marvel's most interesting episodes, most notably the endlessly creative "Death's Hand in Mine." With a terrific ensemble including heavy-hitters like Patti LuPone, a charming visual approach that has a high school play vibe (the Road is very much a high school play of sorts), and a thematic richness seldom found in other superhero properties, Agatha All Along is truly one of a kind.

The amazing disappearing exoplanet

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

An exoplanet seems to stopped transiting in front of its host star.

One nice thing about discovering exoplanets is that, once you work out how long their orbits take, they continue to show up like clockwork. The timing is so regular, in fact, that we can use variations to detect the gravitational influence of additional planets in the same exosolar system. And the precise timing is useful in that we can rely on it to point telescopes at the system when we know some of the light we detect has passed through the planet’s atmosphere.

On Monday, researchers reported on an exoplanet that simply stopped showing up. TOI-2535b is a hot Jupiter-sized planet we’ve been observing as it transits between its host star, TOI-2535, and Earth for about seven years now. But it recently stopped showing up, and researchers suspect that’s due to an unseen companion orbiting in a different plane.

TOI-2535 is one of the many stars with exoplanet candidates identified by TESS, the Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite. It’s a K dwarf, meaning it’s somewhat smaller than the Sun. And TESS found signs of a Jupiter-sized planet orbiting it every eight days, an orbit close enough to make TOI-2535b a “hot Jupiter” exoplanet. At that point, there was nothing especially unusual about it.

But a closer look at the data showed two very strange things. First, the timing of when its transits occurred could shift by as much as six hours, a substantial variation for an orbit this brief. That’s typically a sign of gravitational interactions with another planet; depending on the details of that second planet’s orbit, it may slow the first one or accelerate it through a transit more quickly. Since the second planet doesn’t show up in TESS data, it appears to orbit in a slightly different plane, so it doesn’t pass between its host star and Earth.

The second oddity is that the transit duration grew progressively shorter in more recent observations. This suggests that, while early observations saw the planet transiting across the star’s equator, later ones involved transits near one of the poles, ensuring that the planet spent less time between the star and Earth. This trend eventually reached its logical end point: TOI-2535b simply stopped transiting in the most recent observations.

Obviously, gravitational interactions with a planet orbiting in a different plane could potentially alter TOI-2535b’s orbit so it no longer passes between TOI-2535 and Earth. There is also some precedent for the converse happening: a star around which no transits were originally detected but where transits began appearing in later observations. Still, as a Jupiter-sized planet, TOI-2535b should not be that easy to shift, and as the researchers note, “This is the fastest rate of inclination change observed for an exoplanet orbiting a single star.”

The second, unseen planet “would need to be either extremely massive or on a highly eccentric orbit,” the researchers noted. So they decided to figure out what sorts of configurations could lead to such rapid changes.

To do so, they modeled 300,000 different starting configurations and ran them forward in time. They found there wasn’t a single favored configuration; as long as the planets were in an orbital resonance, things were relatively stable, at least over relatively short periods. So they took the 100 best solutions and ran them forward for a million years.

About two-thirds became unstable during that period, which ruled out a couple of the potential resonances. The remaining third shared a couple of features. One is that the second, undetected planet has to be pretty massive—roughly Saturn-sized at a minimum. The second is that the angle between the orbits of the two planets can’t be that large. If it is, the difference would shrink over time, causing their orbits to become increasingly eccentric. Eventually, this results in a close approach between the two planets, followed by instability.

The University of California, Los Angeles has posted a video of one of the better models if you’re having a hard time visualizing all of this.

While a second planet hasn’t been detected via the transit method, its gravitational pull on the star should create changes in the radial velocity we could potentially detect. Right now, however, we don’t have enough clean data to detect it. The researchers also say there’s enough noise in the transit timing variations that a third planet in the system can’t be excluded.

The question we probably can’t answer with further observations is how this system got into its current state. Large planets are thought to form farther out from the star and then migrate inward because of friction caused by the gas in the planet-forming disk. (This inward motion tends to stop when orbital resonances develop.) But this friction should also limit the angle between the planets’ orbits. Without some additional event that mixed up the orbits, it’s not obvious how this system could have come to be.

Can we please stop calling it a ‘honey trap’?

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

When Jensen Huang told Nvidia employees on November 8 that he had been “unfaithful” to his wife, there’s a phrase that quickly became omnipresent in the media: Huang had been the victim of a “honey trap”, laid by Alice Huang (no relation), a Singapore-based tech executive identified as a Chinese spy by U.S. intelligence agencies.

“Honey trap,” seriously?

The story behind the phrase is that a good, hardworking man was minding his own business when a serpent of a woman lured him off the righteous path by dishonest means and propositions he, being a man, simply couldn’t refuse. It is assumed that male sex drive is some kind of force of nature that no adult can reasonably be expected to control. So there’s a double sexist stereotype here. Can a 63-year-old billionaire CEO really be supposed to have no say over his sex life? Like a bear wouldn’t be able to resist honey?

Anyone who, like me, grew up in the 1980s knows a close relative of this idea: the “homewrecker,” a word aimed at the other woman and never at the husband who cheated. One of the decade’s most successful films, Fatal Attraction (1987), tells the story of a married man (Michael Douglas) whose affair turns into a nightmare when the woman (Glenn Close) refuses to stay discreet and starts asking for more. The film portrays the mistress as mentally unstable and dangerous. The film ends with the wife killing the mistress and family values restored. The husband’s betrayal is never really an issue. I’m ashamed to admit that when I first watched it, some time in the 1990s, I sided completely with the poor husband, hounded by a woman the film had led me to see as a witch.

Both ideas have their roots in Genesis, where Adam, asked why he ate the forbidden fruit, answers that the woman gave it to him. It is easy scapegoating: powerful men keep their reputation for rationality while some snake of a woman takes the blame for their lapses of judgment. There’s a paradox that I find difficult to swallow. For centuries, women were judged too foolish to vote or hold office, yet the same culture credits them with enough cunning to bring down kings and CEOs. Men, meanwhile, are supposed to be fit to lead nations and markets while being too weak to resist the first temptation that comes their way.

Deadly clichés

The term comes from spy fiction: dictionaries credit John le Carré with popularizing “honey trap” in Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy in 1974. Espionage has long been a man’s world, and in its novels a female spy is very often a seductress. Real Cold War espionage looked quite different, since East Germany’s best-known seduction program used men, so-called “Romeo” agents sent west to seduce secretaries with access to government files. 

In the Nvidia story, much of the coverage of Alice Huang also draws on images Western culture has attached to Asian women for more than a century: the mysterious seductress, the Dragon Lady. In the U.S., they go back at least to the Page Act of 1875, the country’s first restrictive federal immigration law, which was enforced on the presumption that Chinese women were prostitutes. Any woman arriving from Asia had to prove she wasn’t one.

In March 2021, a white man killed eight people at three Atlanta-area spas, six of them Asian women, and police relayed his description of his victims as “sources of temptation.” Asian women end up being blamed for men’s desire. And Atlanta showed that this cliché can be deadly. Coverage casting Alice Huang as a seductress is anything but harmless.

Nobody trapped the CEO

A CEO’s sexual relationship with someone working for one of his company’s biggest customers is a clear conflict of interest. Alice Huang’s employer, Megaspeed, is said to have bought about $4.8 billion of Nvidia hardware over the past two years. In most companies, a sales manager sleeping with a key client’s buyer would be expected to disclose it and hand the account to a colleague. Nobody would describe that sales manager as “trapped,” and the CEO of one of the world’s most valuable companies, who has far more power, deserves no more indulgence.

The phrase also shrinks Alice Huang: if she is only “honey,” she cannot be much of a professional. Yet she ran a business that bought chips at enormous scale, and if the allegations hold, she will have to answer for export violations and espionage, serious crimes committed with the very agency the word denies her.

Call it what it is

The honey trap trope has consequences in the workplace. In 2019, a LeanIn.Org and SurveyMonkey survey found that 60% of male managers in the U.S. were uncomfortable mentoring, working one-on-one with, or socializing with a woman, and 36% of men said they had avoided doing so for fear of how it would look. A woman alone with a powerful man is seen as a danger to him, even though she is far more likely to be the one harassed. She also pays in lost dinners, business trips and sponsorship, the informal moments that decide who gets promoted.

Other words would be more honest. A CEO had an affair with an executive at one of his largest customers, a woman U.S. officials reportedly believe was working for Chinese intelligence, and his board is now examining whether the relationship compromised the company. It is less catchy than “honey trap,” but it treats both people as adults responsible for their choices. Isn’t that the standard any employee would expect?

Anthropic says it has met its own threshold for AI-accelerated research

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Anthropic’s latest IPO filing bases its AI research threshold call on what it expects from future models. METR, given rare internal access, estimates its models are improving about twice as fast as before.

Anthropic has formally decided that it meets its own threshold for AI dramatically speeding up its research. It based the call on “what we expect from future models and our overall rate of progress,” not on its current models, it wrote in its amended IPO filing on 10 November.

The wording hardened over three filings in 15 days, as Alex Wilhelm noted in his newsletter Cautious Optimism. The AI research threshold sits in Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy, the company’s own rulebook for when stronger safeguards apply.

The policy counts a “dramatic acceleration” as double the rate of progress seen without significant help from AI. It also requires that AI automating research could plausibly explain the doubling.

The first S-1, filed on 26 October, said Anthropic’s evidence on AI-driven acceleration came mostly from lagging indicators.

“As a result, we may not identify a significant acceleration in our rate of development until after it has occurred,” the filing said.

The first amendment followed on 2 November. Anthropic wrote that it had “in the past not identified” such a speed-up until afterwards. Its estimates for future models showed it “may imminently meet” the threshold, it added. It had not designated any current model as exceeding it.

Three days later, the evaluation group METR published an independent assessment of Anthropic’s research pace. Its staff worked on Anthropic devices from 12 August to 29 October, with what METR called “unprecedented access” to internal data.

METR found that Anthropic’s models were improving on well-specified tasks at around twice the rate of March 2024 to October 2025. Its central estimate was 1.9 times, within a range of 1.5 to 2.8.

“We guess that a majority (perhaps ≈80%) of the current (October 2026) acceleration is due to AI,” METR wrote.

METR also found that AI cannot yet fully automate substantial parts of Anthropic’s model training pipeline. Still, it saw “a significant risk that further capabilities progress could lead to even faster acceleration over the coming year.”

The report carried caveats. The speed-up might be limited to well-specified tasks, METR said. Anthropic also held redaction rights over all non-public information in the report.

“Anthropic redacted a majority of the evidence we found most informative for these conclusions,” METR wrote.

Anthropic’s stated aim was to remove confidential information that could help other AI developers, according to METR. The group said it took no payment, though Anthropic supplied free tokens.

On 9 November, Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.2 alongside Mythos 5.2. Its system card said Mythos 5.2 does not double the internal rate of AI research progress.

The second amendment, a day later, made the call. Anthropic based it on its expectations “rather than on what we have observed from current models.” The policy covers progress the company can “observe or expect,” the filing noted. Anthropic said it continues to assess the underlying data and will publish further reports.

The same filing said Anthropic does not expect to safely let Claude fully build its successors. That would need “meaningful human supervision,” it said. It is weighing policy updates that would require human review of certain research decisions.

The seven co-founders addressed the pace in their letter to investors, which Anthropic posted on its IPO website for the roadshow. Building AI safely may require Anthropic, and ideally the rest of the industry, to modulate that pace, they wrote, “especially when this is driven by AI systems building themselves.”

“There may be times when we choose to advance more slowly than we could in order to make our systems safer, even if that puts us at a competitive disadvantage,” the co-founders wrote.

The roadshow video pitched the speed. In its section on compute, presented by co-founder Tom Brown, Anthropic said Claude Mythos got Claude running on AMD chips in three days “with basically no human intervention,” according to Simon Willison’s transcript.

“We believe that this can accelerate even further as we approach recursive self-improvement,” the video says.

The AI Futures Project, the group behind the AI 2027 scenario, responded on 10 November. Citing METR’s report, all its authors now expect a “Superhuman AI Researcher” at some point in 2027, unless the industry deliberately slows down. They define it as an AI that matches the research taste of the best human researchers and fully automates coding.

“We strongly urge Anthropic to release enough information such that external observers can make a complete assessment,” the authors wrote.

They called for an industry-wide pause on AI research and development. Human review of the kind Anthropic described could “provide too much comfort,” they added.

'A Massive Mistake': Tech, AI and Crypto Founders React to Jensen Huang’s Nvidia Exit

Nvidia co-founder Jensen Huang is out as CEO after 33 years, and some of the loudest names in crypto and artificial intelligence think the company just made a spectacular mistake.

On Thursday, Nvidia confirmed Huang’s resignation as president, CEO, and board member, and named longtime finance chief Colette Kress interim CEO. The move followed reports of two extramarital affairs, including one involving a businesswoman U.S. intelligence reportedly suspected of links to China, as well as concerns over a reported relationship with an Nvidia employee.

Huang acknowledged being unfaithful to his wife in a message to employees on Sunday. Nvidia said in a board statement that it had no reason to believe its secrets or national security were compromised, and its independent investigation remains underway. The allegations about foreign intelligence have not established that Huang knowingly provided information to a foreign government.

But even without any leaks, the scandal was enough for the board to decide to let him go.

The market did not welcome the transition. Nvidia shares fell 14% at Thursday’s opening bell; with more than $500 billion wiped from U.S. stocks in a single minute. The company that was once the most valuable in the world has now fallen to third on that list.

Nvidia was already under pressure before the scandal, and Huang’s departure raised a particular problem for a company built so closely around its founder: Who, exactly, can replace him?

Changpeng “CZ” Zhao, the Binance co-founder and one of crypto’s most influential executives, said “it's a mistake for Nvidia to let Jensen resign.” Bitcoin entrepreneur and former Kraken executive Dan Held agreed, writing, “He shouldn't have resigned,” and argued that Huang’s personal problems should be weighed against the company he built.

Democrats want to stop Trump's arch, golf course and more. What are their options?

📅 Nov 12, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

President Trump signed the final steel beam at the White House ballroom the day after the midterm elections. Andrew Harnik/Getty Images hide caption

President Trump's efforts to remake Washington, D.C., have dominated his second term, even after his party's resounding midterm defeat.

He started his election week at the scandal-plagued Reflecting Pool and ended it with an order declaring his gilded Oval Office a national monument to "make it impossible for anybody ever to destroy this incredible space."

That proclamation, like any executive order, could be undone by a future president. But many of Trump's construction projects are changing D.C. in more permanent ways.

His administration has already downed hundreds of trees at East Potomac Park and demolished the East Wing of the White House to make space for a ballroom — which he called "the safest place in Washington" as he showed it to reporters the day after the midterms.

Trump has taken a uniquely unilateral approach to construction, packing regulatory commissions with his allies and bypassing Congress entirely. Most of his projects — from the triumphal arch to the Kennedy Center — are now playing out in court.

Critics accuse the Trump administration of skirting federal laws and misleading judges in its effort to progress as quickly as possible before the end of his term.

Democrats have vowed to use their soon-to-be congressional majority to push back on what they describe as Trump's "vanity projects."

When asked about that, White House spokesperson Davis Ingle told NPR in an email that "no other president has done more to beautify our Nation's interior than President Trump." He cited park cleanups, the Reflecting Pool and the White House helipad and ballroom as examples, and said Democrats "should work with President Trump to make our nation's capital beautiful again."

Rep. Jared Huffman, D.-Calif., told NPR in an interview that the Trump administration has "played this game of Catch Me If You Can, where they roll ahead with the bulldozers and the tree cutters … and they wait for courts to shut them down." He says a Democrat-led Congress will fight back.

"There's no way that we could look away while these sacred, iconic places are being permanently changed in ways that violate the law and could have really terrible lasting impacts," said Huffman, the top Democrat on the House Natural Resources Committee, which has a legislative purview over public lands and national parks.

Huffman says blocking Trump's construction projects — and holding people accountable for potential wrongdoing — will be a top priority when he leads the committee. Earlier this week, he sent letters ordering dozens of administration officials and private companies to preserve their records for a future investigation.

"Whether it is the arch or … or the East Potomac golf course or any number of other examples around the United States capital, that is federal land, and we have a direct statutory responsibility to either authorize those changes or to stop them if they're proceeding without the proper authorization," Huffman said.

What would that actually look like? Democrats have many tools at their disposal, from legislation to lawsuits. But experts say only two are likely to succeed: funding and oversight.

Congress could — at least in theory — pass laws to block specific projects from moving ahead. Some Democratic lawmakers have already introduced bills to protect the Kennedy Center from demolition and prohibit the construction of an arch at Memorial Circle.

But Trump has promised to "veto everything," and Democrats won't have a big enough majority to override him on their own.

"Legislation in principle is the cleanest way of doing it, but it's also not achievable," said David Super, who teaches administrative and constitutional law at Georgetown. "So what they're likely to do is cut off funds."

The Constitution says that the federal government may spend money only if is appropriated by Congress. But Trump has neither sought nor received congressional authorization for projects like the arch and the White House ballroom — he maintains he doesn't need it.

Rep. Chellie Pingree of Maine is one of many who disagree. She's the top Democrat on the House committee that oversees funding for the Interior Department.

"Certainly Congress should have been involved, because we have the power of the purse," Pingree told NPR. "That is our ultimate tool. And Republicans have refused to use that. But we can use that in very many ways to block him or to use it for leverage."

Pingree says a Democratic majority will be able to write appropriations bills with clear language from the outset about where money can and can't be spent — and potentially take the president to court if he doesn't follow it.

Super expects Congress would prevail in such a challenge, because it has clear-cut authority over federal funds.

For example, he noted that some of Trump's D.C.-area beautification projects have been funded at least in part by diverted national park entrance fees. Congress could "cap how much money can be used in the greater Washington area, and force the money to be used in the Everglades and Yosemite and Great Smoky Mountains," he said.

Congress will have to make some appropriations decisions in the coming weeks, since it needs to pass a short-term funding bill by Dec. 11 to avoid a government shutdown. Democrats could use that opportunity to try to limit funding for at least one of Trump's construction projects.

Huffman has proposed language that would prevent Trump from using federal funds for the East Potomac golf course renovations, limiting them instead to repairs of the park's crumbling seawall. Pingree said she will join him in fighting to negotiate that into next month's bill.

And, it appeared, for good reason: Nvidia was selling at 30 times its earnings over the previous four quarters, a modest number for an enterprise growing its bottom line at an annual clip of 100%.

Today, Nvidia looks like a far greater bargain based strictly on the numbers. Since the summit in early October, it’s tumbled 34% as of mid-morning on November 12 to $161, a downdraft that slashed nearly $2 trillion from its valuation, the biggest dollar drop by far, in a condensed period, in the history of capital markets. As a result, Nvidia’s PE has dropped to 20, a figure that given its current stats, tags it as practically the ultimate value play. For example, Apple and Amazon carry a PE of 40 and 22 respectively, and are expanding at a fraction of Nvidia’s speed.

Indeed, despite its immense size and sorcerous growth rate, Nvidia has so far shown no signs of falling victim to the law of large numbers. In the quarter ended October 31, its sales jumped 26% over the previous three-month span, beating the around 20% jumps of the two previous quarters. What about profitability? In the October quarter, gross margins hit a sumptuous 75%, beating its FY 2026 posting of 71.3%.

Make a sober assessment of Nvidia’s overall condition, however, and the best conclusion is that the market is probably right in imposing this giant discount based on Huang’s departure. Nvidia’s wild profitability arises from its virtual monopoly in the top GPU segment. According to a top industry executive who’s an Nvidia customer, it is 18 to 24 months ahead of all-comers in the quality and performance of its top chips. The rub, says this person, is Nvidia’s dependence on Huang as both an unmatched innovator and excellent operator. “Huang was the tech visionary and he also got Nvidia to operate incredibly well,” says this person. “The question is how much depended on Jensen, and how much of what he did can another person do?”

This executive stresses that Nvidia has maintained its huge margins by keeping several paces ahead of the field, and never slowing, one of the greatest feats in the annals of tech. “That continuing advantage in chips is absolutely essential to Nvidia. It’s an industry where you can’t miss a step. If you do, others will catch up fast. Intel fell behind, and it never caught up.”

Plus, competitors who regularly complain about the high cost of Nvidia chips are racing to create their own, in-house competitors, a list that encompasses AMD, Google, and Amazon.

It’s also revealing to parse how Nvidia’s deploying its huge free cash flow to stay in front. In the past four quarters, it’s generated $105 billion in FCF. In the past, it’s channeled a huge portion of that bounty into share buybacks. But in the 12 months ended in October, repurchases fell to just $32 billion. Nvidia’s cash Hoard of $100 billion in relatively modest, and growing slowly.

So where is all the rest of that capex going? Nvidia is famously making big investments in its customers, including SpaceX, CoreWeave, OpenAI, SoftBank’s SB Energy, and Intel (for CPUs). It’s also provided multibillion-dollar credit guarantees for projects built for hyperscalers and neoclouds where it’s providing the GPUs. The practice of funding its own customers is often questioned as “circular financing.” The question is whether Huang felt obliged to follow that course to protect Nvidia’s future. The reason: Locking in future sales as rivals hatch competing products at lower price points. In other words, Huang sought to forestall the commoditization of Nvidia, the threat that’s eventually hit the profitability of all tech hardware giants.

And that’s just the problem. Huge prices and margins inevitably attract cheaper new supply. The market judges that Nvidia’s margins and profit growth will look far lower in the future than in the past, and that losing the genius who could hold commoditization at bay longest was a big, big loss.

They should have found a different way to punish the guy that didn’t damage shareholders.”

This person admits that this is probably a minority view. “People love to get on their high moral horse and that’s what we’re seeing,” says the observer. “You can’t just plug and play to replace someone so critical to the creation of a company’s one-of-a-kind products and operation. A number of CEOs who couldn’t say it publicly would probably say that the Nvidia board forgot what the role of a board should be, to protect shareholders, not themselves.”

That breathtaking, $2 trillion loss in value may be an enduring number, and not just the result of panic selling that most on Wall Street claim.

And on Thursday, Nov. 12, the company announced that it had lost the only CEO it had ever had.

The story goes back more than a year and reads a little like an HBO drama, with alleged spies, extramarital affairs, and corporate intrigue. Let's break down the timeline of the Jensen Huang resignation based on what came to light and when.

Last year, the New York Times reported that U.S. officials were looking into whether Nvidia chips meant for data centers in Southeast Asia had ended up in China.

The chips in question had been bought by Megaspeed, a Singapore company that rented out computing power. U.S. export rules limit China's access to some of Nvidia’s most advanced AI hardware, to protect Nvidia's trade secrets and the United States' competitive edge over China in the AI arms race. Singapore police were also looking into whether Megaspeed had broken local laws, but at the time, Nvidia said it had no reason to believe the chips had been sent elsewhere.

The Times' report referred to Megaspeed's "mystery C.E.O." and noted U.S. officials were looking into the company's ties to China.

Things were quiet for a while; until two weeks ago, when the Times reported that U.S. intelligence officials suspected Alice Huang, a former Megaspeed executive, of gathering information for Beijing. Officials had learned that Alice Huang had developed a close personal relationship with Jensen Huang. (Yes, the two share a surname. No, they are not related.)

Nvidia initially characterized their interactions as routine meetings with a customer: "Jensen’s interactions with Ms. Huang are not unusual or unexpected," an Nvidia spokesperson told TechCrunch in an email on Oct. 29, adding that "meetings with customers are part of a C.E.O.’s job."

The Times also reported that intelligence officials had briefed President Donald Trump about the relationship roughly a year earlier. By then, Jensen Huang had already spent months pressing the Trump administration to let Nvidia sell more advanced AI chips to China.

Flash forward to Nov. 5, when the chairs of three House committees publicly asked Jensen Huang for a closed-door briefing.
