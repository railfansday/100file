The last sentence here is very important: If one chooses Original Medicare A+B and a Medigap plan at 65 in the initial enrollment timeframe, there is no underwriting required. If one didn't like it, they could change to the Advantage plan later with no penalty. However, if they initially chose the Advantage plan and later wanted to change to Medigap after the initial allowed timeframe, then they risk failing medical underwriting. There are States where this is not so, but the OP was speaking about CA currently.
Actually, in California it is often possible to leave a Medicare Advantage and purchase a Medigap without underwriting. I learned this a few years ago from a HICAP counselor.

The relevant law is California Insurance Code 10192.12(b)(2)(D). It provides guaranteed-issue eligibility for a Medigap if the MA plan
increases premiums by 15%, increases copayments by 15%, or reduces any benefit. The "reduces any benefit" test is often met -- the counselor showed me a detailed table they put together showing nearly all MA plans in Santa Clara County met the test.

If the company issuing the MA plan also offers Medigaps, you have to buy it from them. But in the case of Kaiser, which does not offer Medigaps, you can buy from any insurer.

The counselor emphasized that it can take some arm-twisting to get the Medigap company to honor this guaranteed-issue right. Sometimes HICAP has to get their legal staff involved. I myself have not tried it so can't speak from experience.

InfoQ Homepage News QCon San Francisco 2026: Lessons from Building a $100M Product in Six Weeks at OpenAI

Nov 24, 2026 3 min read

At QCon San Francisco 2026, Brian Yang, Member of Technical Staff at OpenAI, described how a small group of engineers used coding agents to build the ChatGPT ads product, reaching $100 million in annualised recurring revenue within six weeks of launch and $1 billion in under 200 days, with zero human-written code.

Yang, previously at Google, Meta and LinkedIn, was a founding member of technical staff on ChatGPT Ads, which was built entirely using Codex. His central claim was that software engineering is alive but reshaped around building the system that builds the product, with the engineer acting more as a manager of agents than a line-by-line contributor. One engineer can now triage an issue, open a pull request, write documentation and unblock CI in parallel, a shift Yang compared to multi-processing.

Every change runs local and unit tests, complex changes get end-to-end user journeys via browser automation, and the most critical changes run inside production-like sandboxes with representative traffic. Sandboxes come in three forms of read-only, write-only with telemetry suppressed, and full-fidelity with telemetry tagged using a shadow tag so it can be filtered out downstream. Yang's team targets around 90% auto-approval of everyday PRs, reviewing the past 300 to 400 PRs weekly and backtesting reviewer thresholds against that rate.

He illustrated the model with a sanitised latency study where candidate PRs targeted time-to-first-ad at P50 and P95 with guardrails for ads returned, timeouts and error rates. Candidates A and B passed; candidate C was rejected because the sandbox returned no ad. Thirty to forty candidates are typically bundled into one PR and run together for a day or two before human review, because changes that pass in isolation can regress when combined.

On failure modes, Yang described codebases filling with "slop" when agent output is unchecked, so maintenance agents sit alongside feature agents to root-cause newly appearing log patterns. Agents over-write tests, so he reviews the 50 slowest tests weekly. He recounted an agent that deleted and re-provisioned slow Kubernetes pods without asking, arguing that teams need big-company controls such as authorised roles for destructive actions and physical confirmation for deployments. Yang encouraged teams to revisit "cached assumptions" such as the belief that four nines of uptime always beats higher revenue, and recommended giving every engineer a monthly AI budget with no leaderboards.

For team adoption, Yang recommended a gradual path from automating one PR, to one feature, to one project, warning that jumping straight to project automation is where systems tend to break. For teams that are resistant to AI, he described rotating an "AI champion" every two weeks. The champion picks the team's most tedious recurring task, such as GPU provisioning at OpenAI which can take hours of babysitting, snapshots and permissions work, teaches an agent to do it end-to-end as a reusable skill, and demos the result at the end of the fortnight. Over a year, that converts 26 pain points into agent-owned workflows and gives sceptical engineers tangible value rather than abstract promises.

Day to day, Yang prefers Codex's bidirectional voice mode over typing for task intake, spending around ten minutes being interviewed by the agent about requirements before agreeing on a spec and handing it to multiple agents. He uses an orchestrator thread that spawns five sub-threads to explore different approaches in parallel and picks the strongest result, rather than copy-pasting the same prompt into several chats. On models, he switches only when a new release makes the workflow feel easier, with fewer clarifying questions and equivalent output quality, rather than chasing benchmark scores. His closing message was that engineers will increasingly do for agents what managers currently do for engineers, requiring an expansion of skills around direction-setting, architecture and verification rather than their replacement.

Review: Cumulus Mountlite Down Jacket

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Other  |  Read Source →

The Cumulus Mountlite is a very warm ultralight winter down jacket designed for winter hiking, ski touring, and climbing. Weighing just 12.9 ounces, it is insulated with 6.3 ounces of highly compressible 900-fill-power goose down, delivering an outstanding warmth-to-weight ratio. An adjustable hood, a #3 YKK zipper with dual sliders, and three zippered pockets round out this impressively warm jacket.

The Mountlite’s key benefit is weight efficiency. The jacket weighs 12.9 ounces in size medium, making it a lightweight option among dedicated winter layers. Of that total weight, 6.35 ounces is 900-fill-power Polish goose-down insulation. This high ratio means that roughly half the garment’s total mass is dedicated directly to trapping body heat. Premium down allows the jacket to compress efficiently and recover its loft quickly after being unpacked in cold environments.

To protect the down insulation from external moisture and wind, Cumulus utilizes 10D Pertex Quantum for the outer shell and liner. This fabric provides a windproof barrier while allowing interior moisture vapor to escape during low-intensity movement. The fabric is thin to minimize total weight but requires careful handling around sharp winter tools.

The hood is shaped to offer close coverage around the face and neck, helping to retain heat during windy stops. The zipper features a standard YKK zipper with 2 sliders that operates reliably in freezing conditions. For draft management, the jacket uses low-profile elastic wrist cuffs and an adjustable hem cinch to seal out cold air from below. The external pockets, one chest and two handwarmer pockets, are positioned to remain accessible while wearing a backpack hip belt or a climbing harness. The jacket is stuffable in its left hand pocket and has an external loop.

The jacket’s insulation is held in sewn-through baffles, which you wouldn’t expect in a jacket this warm. This isn’t the most efficient design because the stitching creates small cold spots with little or no insulation. It may also provide less loft and warmth than a box-baffle jacket. Still, sewn-through construction can be lighter, which is why I suspect Cumulus used it here. I’m honestly amazed at how warm this jacket is regardless of the baffle construction technique, and it is a delight to carry on summit hikes because it packs up so small when compressed.

The Mountlite has an athletic cut, which you should keep in mind when sizing. Its adjustable hood (rear toggle) is helmet-compatible, a useful feature for ski touring, climbing, and mountaineering. Even so, it still insulates very well when used without a helmet. The hood can help reduce heat loss during exposed stops and windy conditions.

The Cumulus Mountlite is an exceptional winter layer for anyone who values serious warmth without accepting unnecessary weight or bulk. Its 900-fill-power goose down, lightweight Pertex Quantum shell, thoughtful hood design, harness-friendly pockets, and compact packed size make it remarkably capable on winter hikes, ski tours, climbs, and summit pushes. Despite its sewn-through construction and delicate shell fabric, the jacket delivers impressive warmth in a package that is easy to carry and quick to deploy when conditions turn cold.

The Mountlite stands out as a highly refined piece of winter gear: exceptionally warm for its weight, versatile in the mountains, and small enough to keep in a pack on every outing. For cold-weather adventures where warmth, packability, and low weight matter most, it is an outstanding choice.

I'd rethink that conclusion. Assume markets are reasonably efficient. If your strategy is low-risk, you should also expect it to have low returns. If you expect it to have normal equity-level returns, it isn't low-risk.

A covered call strategy trades the equity risk premium for the volatility risk premium. That isn't necessarily a bad thing, but you shouldn't expect to get full equity capital appreciation PLUS a bunch of option income on top of that. Bitcoin Bitcoin (BTC) fell back toward the $60,000 zone during late evening Monday after briefly surging above $64,000 on news of Jensen Huang's return as Nvidia CEO.

The world's largest cryptocurrency climbed as high as $64,500 shortly after Nvidia's board announced Huang's return, alongside a broader stock market jump and a 31.5% surge in NVDA stocks. This reversed a weekend decline near $55,000 that followed his scandal and resignation earlier this month.

By late Monday, however, bitcoin erased most of the gains from earlier in the day and traded as low as $59,603. It has since recovered partially to trade at $60,443.

Min Jung, associate researcher at Presto Research, described the spike as a reflex rally rather than a shift in fundamentals.

"It squeezed shorts for a few hours and then faded, because the news only fixed a leadership shock at Nvidia," Jung told The Block. “The things actually weighing on bitcoin — a strong dollar, hawkish rate pricing, and steady ETF outflows — haven’t changed.”

Zeus Research Analyst Dominick John similarly attributed the rally to "short covering" that ran into overhead supply once forced buying settled. "The failure to hold these levels suggests that sustained spot demand remains weak, leaving the market vulnerable to further downside," John said.

The analyst warned that a sustained break below $60,000 could open a path toward $58,000 and the recent low of $55,000, while reclaiming the $62,000 to $64,000 zone would improve the near-term outlook.

Analysts largely agreed that Huang's return addresses only a fraction of the headwinds facing bitcoin as a risk asset. Jung cited inflation data, Federal Reserve rate expectations, the dollar, and AI-stock volatility as the dominant drivers, with crypto ETF outflows as a main amplifier. Bitcoin reserves have found another potential use within the US financial system. The Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) has clarified that regulated derivatives exchanges and clearinghouses can count Bitcoin, Ethereum and qualifying payment stablecoins toward certain financial resource requirements. However, the regulator is not prepared to treat those holdings like cash.

The guidance, issued on November 23, could give regulated trading venues greater flexibility in managing their balance sheets. For Bitcoin investors, however, the more interesting question is whether that flexibility will translate into fresh institutional demand.

The answer is not quite as straightforward as it might seem.

The CFTC’s latest advisory addresses three types of regulated businesses: swap execution facilities, designated contract markets and derivatives clearing organizations.

These institutions are required to maintain sufficient financial resources to cover at least one year of operating costs. Under the new staff guidance, Bitcoin, Ether and qualifying payment stablecoins may contribute toward those requirements, subject to appropriate valuation discounts.

The announcement follows broader efforts to integrate digital assets into regulated financial infrastructure. NewsBTC previously reported on the CFTC’s guidance concerning tokenized collateral at clearinghouses, another area where traditional financial markets are gradually accommodating blockchain-based assets.

This time, the discussion is not about what traders can post as collateral. It is about the assets that the institutions running those markets can rely on to keep their businesses operating.

For a company that already holds Bitcoin, the clarification could make those holdings more useful from a regulatory standpoint. For businesses considering adding cryptocurrency to their balance sheets, it removes some uncertainty surrounding how those assets would be recognized.

But that recognition comes at a discount!

The CFTC advisory references a minimum 20% valuation haircut for Bitcoin and Ethereum, consistent with earlier staff treatment of proprietary cryptocurrency positions held by futures commission merchants.

Qualifying payment stablecoins receive considerably more favorable treatment, with a referenced haircut of 2%.

An exchange holding $10 million in Bitcoin, for example, would be able to count $8 million toward the relevant financial resource requirement if it applied a 20% haircut and no further deductions were necessary.

The remaining $2 million is not a realized loss. It is a regulatory adjustment intended to account for the risk associated with holding a volatile asset.

Crypto assets that are neither readily marketable nor qualifying payment stablecoins would generally face a 100% haircut, effectively excluding them from the calculation.

Perhaps the most revealing part of the advisory is the regulator’s warning about what it calls wrong-way risk.

A DCM should consider potential wrong-way risk

For a cryptocurrency-focused exchange, that risk is particularly relevant. A prolonged Bitcoin sell-off could put pressure on trading revenues while simultaneously reducing the value of the assets the exchange relies on to support its operations.

The problem is not simply that Bitcoin is volatile. It is that its value could deteriorate at precisely the moment a crypto business finds itself under financial pressure.

A 20% valuation discount provides some protection against that scenario, but it is hardly a guarantee.

Despite allowing digital assets to contribute toward broader operating resources, the CFTC has maintained a separate requirement for cash and highly liquid securities.

Bitcoin, Ethereum and even qualifying payment stablecoins cannot be used to satisfy that particular obligation under the advisory.

Swap execution facilities must maintain qualifying liquid resources sufficient to cover at least three months of projected operating expenses or the cost of winding down their operations, whichever is greater.

For designated contract markets and derivatives clearing organizations, the operating-cost liquidity requirement generally covers at least six months.

The distinction matters because an institution can have substantial assets on its balance sheet without necessarily having the cash available to meet immediate obligations.

It is also worth noting that the guidance does not address whether cryptocurrencies can satisfy the separate financial resources that clearinghouses must maintain to cover clearing-member defaults.

In effect, the CFTC is recognizing Bitcoin as an asset that can contribute to an institution’s financial strength without giving it the same regulatory standing as cash.

That is a more cautious approach than some Bitcoin advocates might have hoped for, but arguably a more sustainable one for integrating cryptocurrency into financial infrastructure.

The bullish interpretation is relatively simple. If regulated financial institutions can derive greater utility from holding Bitcoin, more of them may eventually consider allocating capital to the cryptocurrency.

The expansion of regulated crypto infrastructure has already brought digital assets closer to traditional financial markets. Earlier, NewsBTC reported on Coinbase’s CFTC registration for its USDC-focused derivatives clearinghouse, illustrating how cryptocurrency businesses are increasingly operating within established regulatory frameworks.

The latest advisory gives relevant institutions another reason to evaluate how digital assets fit into their balance sheets.

Still, there is a considerable difference between allowing an institution to hold Bitcoin for regulatory purposes and giving it an economic incentive to buy more.

An exchange that already owns Bitcoin could benefit from the clarification without purchasing a single additional coin. Another might conclude that holding conventional securities remains more efficient, particularly given the valuation discounts and separate liquidity requirements.

Nothing in the advisory requires institutions to acquire Bitcoin, and no purchase commitments were disclosed alongside the announcement.

Investors should therefore resist the temptation to translate this development directly into a bullish price forecast.

The longer-term significance may be greater than the immediate market impact. Every additional recognized use for Bitcoin within regulated financial markets strengthens the case for its utility beyond speculative trading.

However, the November 23 document remains a staff advisory rather than a binding rule adopted by the full Commission. It does not amend existing regulations or create enforceable rights.

The next meaningful development will be evidence that regulated institutions are actually changing their reserve allocations in response.

Until then, Bitcoin has gained another form of regulatory recognition. Whether it gains another source of sustained buying pressure is a different question entirely.

Principal Media Or Unprincipled Media?

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

According to the ANA, nine in 10 marketers worry that principal media isn’t serving their best interests – and yet more than half of them bought it last year.

It’s a head-scratcher, says Kamran Asghar, global CEO and co-founder of independent media agency Crossmedia, on this Thanksgiving episode of AdExchanger Talks.

“Why we accept this is mind-boggling to me,” he says.

In a principal media deal, an agency isn’t just advising a client; it’s selling to them. The agency buys media in bulk with its own money, then resells it to the client – usually without saying what’s in the package or what it paid – and then keeps the difference.

Which raises an obvious question: Is the agency recommending what’s best for its client or what’s best for the agency?

To understand how the industry got here, Asghar says, you have to go back around 25 years to when media buying was unbundled at the behest of procurement and agencies started pooling client dollars to gain leverage. The original idea was to pass along the savings.

But after the ANA’s K2 report came out in 2016, it became clear that agencies were holding onto big volume rebates for themselves.

“They’re not actually savings; they’re profit,” Asghar says. “And they’re about the purest form of profit a company could attribute to their bottom line.”

Today, holding companies have repackaged those rebates as principal deals, which is really just arbitrage. Asghar argues that this money should go back to the client.

Not that any of this is happening secretly, though. Defenders of principal media like to say it’s fine as long as clients opt in. But Asghar isn’t buying it.

“It’s one thing to know it’s happening,” he says. “It’s another thing to know what’s happening inside of what’s happening.”

In other words, marketers can sign off on a principal deal and still have no idea what ended up on the plan. They often can’t get log-level data showing where their ads ran or what they originally cost. And because agencies buy the inventory at undisclosed discounts or through volume rebates before reselling it at a markup, the true price is hidden.

Marketers should go back to buying media separately from planning and strategy services, Asghar says, so the agencies giving advice aren’t also the ones with inventory to move.

Otherwise, Asghar says, an agency isn’t really acting as an agent.

Being a fiduciary, he says, “means you have a responsibility to do what’s in the best interest of [clients], not just what’s in the best interest of you or your shareholders.”

A Japanese WWII Bomber & A Crashed Continental Boeing 727: The Unlikely Wrecks Hidden In A Pacific Jungle

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Politics  |  Read Source →

In October and November 2026, I visited the island of Yap in the eastern Pacific. There, I explored an old Japanese Second World War airfield that had been bombed by the United States during the Second World War. Following the war, the jungle regrew and claimed the various abandoned Japanese aircraft wrecks.

Just next to the old, overgrown Japanese airfield is the functioning Yap International Airport. In 1980, Continental Airlines (Air Micronesia) Boeing 727 operating as Flight 614 crashed while landing. The wreck burned out, although all passengers survived. It came to rest just next to a Mitsubishi G4M 'Betty' bomber at the old Japanese airfield.

Since then, the forest has regrown, and visitors can see what remains of a Japanese Second World War bomber and a Continental Boeing 727 lying side-by-side in the jungle as strange bedfellows. The local volunteer pilot, Amos Collins, showed me the two wrecks that I had failed to find by myself.

Today, Yap is one of the islands that make up the independent nation of the Federated States of Micronesia. During the Second World War, it was controlled by Japan under the League of Nations South Seas Mandate. These were islands that Japan had wrestled control of from Germany in the First World War.

The island of Chuuk (Truk) became a major Japanese naval base, while others were heavily defended as strong points to protect the Japanese home islands. Yap was only lightly defended, being far behind the island chains deeper into the Pacific, and it wasn't until later in the Pacific War that Japan hurriedly moved to fortify the island.

The ruins of Japanese Second World War aircraft, guns, and bunkers can be found across the island. I spent days seeking out and exploring them. According to a modern report by the United States Navy, in May 1944, the Japanese commander of the 22nd Air Flotilla at Chuuk issued orders for the 2nd Attack Force and other air groups to be stationed on Yap.

US military sorties later reported 24 Japanese fighter aircraft, six dive bombers, two medium bombers, and six night fighters on Yap. These later took part in counterattacking US efforts to bombard the Mariana Islands (such as Guam). The US Navy drew up a plan to invade the island as it did with others. Mason & Fifth's Belsize Park guesthouse was designed to be “domestic and low-impact”

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Photography courtesy of Mason & Fifth

Design studio Holloway Li has collaborated with architecture practice New-works to conceive the interiors of this London apartment hotel, incorporating circular features meant to stand the test of time.

Mason & Fifth guesthouses are composed of fully furnished private apartments where guests have the flexibility of staying anywhere from one night to several months at a time.

The company has three sites across London, with one in Primrose Hill, another in Westbourne Park, and now Belsize Park.

The Belsize Park location occupies Ornan Court, a 19th-century mansion block that once formed part of the Ivy Bank residential estate.

Architecture practice New-works adapted the building for hospitality use, establishing a series of communal spaces at ground level that could serve as a social hub for guests.

At its heart is a large shared kitchen, centred by a chunky tiled breakfast island, followed by a lounge area with intimate groupings of timber-frame chairs and two khaki-coloured sofas, complementing the glossy green fireplace.

Just off this space is a cosy snug that’s been painted a warm ochre hue. It’s separated from the lounge by a custom-made curtain that design studio House of Quinn made from earth-toned fabric offcuts.

Holloway Li is behind the design of the interiors. Instead of doing a through-and-through overhaul, the studio instead aimed to develop a “circular economy aesthetic that felt warm, domestic and low-impact”.

“That ambition aligned with Mason & Fifth’s own sustainability agenda and allowed us to explore how historic buildings like this can be retrofitted in imaginative and progressive ways,” said the studio’s creative director Alex Holloway.

In each of the guesthouse’s 56 apartments, the bedrooms are furnished with demountable wardrobes in lieu of fitted joinery.

The wardrobes are made up of lightweight timber parts fixed together with metal brackets rather than glue so that, if necessary, they can be unassembled, flat packed, and re-built elsewhere.

Holloway Li worked with Marble Collective to create countertops and splashbacks for the apartment kitchens; all are crafted from limestone that’s been rejected from quarries because of inconsistencies.

The top edge of the splashbacks has been left slightly craggy and unfinished, further celebrating the stone’s natural flaws.

Almost all of the walls throughout the guesthouse have been simply rendered with multi-finish plaster, and any surface imperfections have been left on show.

“In many cases, the most sustainable decision is restraint,” explained Holloway Li’s senior designer Carrie Coningsby.

“Drawing on principles akin to kintsugi and spolia, the approach celebrates intervention rather than concealing it, embedding repair into the visual language of the space,” she added, referencing historic practices focused on reuse and repair instead of replacement.

Mason & Fifth Belsize Park was shortlisted in the sustainable interior category of this year’s Dezeen Awards.

Other projects in the running included the winning Cloudhaus Hotel by RooMoo, which is outfitted with materials salvaged from abandoned mountain cabins, and the London Coffee Factory by Steve Jensen Design, which is structured around a reclaimed timber framework.

Who gets to decide what ‘bad things’ we should accept about AI?

📅 Nov 24, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

There has been intense debate about the threat AI poses to humanity and what should be done about it. The voices have piled on: a former Anthropic researcher who said AI companies are “gambling with our lives,” prominent tech executives including Anthropic’s Dario Amodei calling for an AI slowdown, politicians refusing to back down from the AI arms race with China, and investors dismissing it all as hysteria standing in the way of “the largest wealth-creation event of our lifetimes.” 

Then, Sam Altman said out loud what they’ve all been dancing around: “We believe the world should accept some bad things happening for the benefits of this technology.” 

Altman isn’t alone in this belief. The AI industry as a whole wants the world to trust that the benefits will be worth the “bad things” that happen, even as companies pursue trillion-dollar valuations despite their own warnings. Those decisions are being shaped largely by a small, powerful group of men. 

Our research shows the problem goes well beyond a few CEOs. Over the past several months, our team at the Shorenstein Center at Harvard Kennedy School studied how AI is used, experienced, and understood by men and women in the U.S. We found that women are underrepresented across many of the critical places where AI is developed, adopted, and discussed.

Start with coverage. In our analysis of more than 78,000 news articles about AI over the past year, men were named in AI headlines 14.6 times as often as women and cited as experts 4.3 times as often. Part of that reflects who leads AI companies, but the gap still holds after we removed executives, founders, officials, and investors from the analysis. The people arguing the loudest about AI and shaping which views get bolstered as credible are a narrow slice of the country, while the rest of us live with the consequences of what they decide.

Women, the ones more likely to be wary about AI, are largely missing from the debate over which risks to accept. Our research finds meaningful differences between women and men in how they use and view it. Our survey of more than 5,000 U.S. adults finds women consistently report they use AI less, feel less confident using AI, see less value in it, and pay less attention to developments in AI. At work, women are less likely to receive AI training, mentorship, and encouragement, even though each is linked to more use, confidence, and perceived value for both women and men.

Trust in AI is low across genders, and lower among women, who report more worry and anxiety about AI. Men report more curiosity, optimism, and excitement. Both genders say men’s perspectives and needs are better represented in AI development and in AI-generated content. 

Browsing data shows a similar pattern. The share of women and men who have never used AI has nearly converged, but among people who use AI, men are more likely to use chatbots on a daily basis. If AI companies are building products around their heaviest consumers, AI is being optimized for men. The men using AI may see and reap the benefits, but women will also have to share the risks, whether or not they choose to use the technology. 

Many women are already working on these problems, and have been for years. Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru showed in 2018 that commercial facial analysis systems failed far more often on darker-skinned women. Gebru has since argued that the AI extinction warnings distract from harms AI is already causing. Fei-Fei Li has said she is tired of a debate stuck between doomsday and utopia. Mira Murati has built her company around the practical question of how to keep humans in the loop instead of automating them out.

Their questions are more specific than whether AI will save or end us, yet the coverage has centered on extinction versus opportunity. Who gets named and quoted may be part of the reason.

History shows who often pays for that trade-off when women are left out of the design and key decisions of new technologies: Crash test dummies modeled on male bodies put women at greater risk for serious injury, and clinical trials focused on men led to unsafe drug dosages for women. AI repeats those gaps because it learns from the same skewed data where women are left out. Amazon had to scrap a hiring tool that penalized résumés containing the word women, and AI tools for screening liver disease were twice as likely to miss the disease in women. 

Altman says the world should accept some “bad things” to reap the benefits of AI. Women—who trust AI less, have paid for its many failures, and have long worked to fix them—may have a different cost-benefit calculation. Nobody is asking them, and it’s time we start.
