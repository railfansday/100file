Grok Bot is designed to work inside other software on your behalf, and SpaceXAI says its Bots are "AI teammates" that can "finish jobs end to end, and only come back when something needs your approval." Remove the marketing spin and the concept is pretty simple: provide it with a task, let it handle the tedious clicks and return when it requires assistance. That sounds spiffy, but what actually makes it tick?

Each SpaceXAI Bot works on a persistent computer in Cursor's cloud and has access to a browser, a file system and a terminal. You give it an outcome and access to the tools it needs, then the job can keep running even after your own computer is closed.

In practice, you could have a Bot research sales prospects, look at a CRM for existing activity, compose personalized outreach and leave it for your approval. SpaceXAI also suggests using it to reproduce software bugs, generate customer-health reports and create executive digests from email, calendars and other related sources.

Bots can carry over context from one conversation to another and can repeat workflows at a scheduled time. That's where Grok Bot becomes less of a conversational bot and more of a bot that you delegate to do work for you.

It still has its limits though. Websites can block automation or throw up a CAPTCHA, login request or other step that only humans can complete. Potentially consequential actions can be queued for approval before the Bot proceeds. Early users have also reported that some workflows still require more babysitting than the "go away and do it" promise implies. It's still in beta, after all.

Cursor Pro costs $20 per month, Pro+ costs $60 and Ultra costs $200. Teams Standard is $40 per user per month and Teams Premium is $120. All members of a self-serve Cursor Teams plan are granted access to Grok Bot, and Enterprise customers can request access from their account team.

Paid access isn't unlimited. Included Grok Bot usage resets weekly, and extra usage can continue through the account's shared on-demand spending (if enabled). Cursor doesn't specify a set number of Bot jobs for the free trial, saying the credit is measured by agent steps and tokens rather than message count.

This seemingly disorganized list of subscriptions makes sense in light of the business moves involved in the initiative. Mammograms May Offer Early Insight Into Cardiovascular Disease

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Mammograms are widely used to help detect breast cancer, but they may also offer insight into heart health.

The routine X-ray screening may identify breast arterial calcification (BAC), which is the presence of calcium deposits within the walls of the small arteries in the breast.

On a mammogram, BAC is commonly identified as thin, white, parallel lines that give a “tram-track” appearance.

While BAC is harmless to the breasts, it is associated with a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular disease. A 2022 study found women with BAC were 51% more likely to develop heart disease or experience a stroke than those without it.

Laxmi Mehta, MD, a cardiologist at The Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center, said BAC is associated with cardiovascular risk factors, such as diabetes, high blood pressure, and high cholesterol. Additionally, BAC has been connected to:

“The reasons for BAC resulting in higher risks for cardiovascular disease are not definitely determined; however, there are some potential mechanisms, one being that they share similar risk factors that result in vascular injury or metabolic stress that result in vascular calcification and atherosclerosis,” Mehta told Healthline.

Cheng-Han Chen, MD, interventional cardiologist and medical director of the Structural Heart Program at MemorialCare Saddleback Medical Center, added that because BAC and heart disease share many of the same risk factors, “calcium in breast arteries therefore suggests disease in the heart (coronary) and brain arteries as well,” he told Healthline.

While 1 in 39 females in the United States dies from breast cancer each year, cardiovascular disease causes one out of every three deaths, resulting in 1 death per minute.

“Cardiovascular disease is still the most common cause of death in premenopausal women,” Richard Reitherman, MD, PhD, a radiologist and medical director of breast imaging at MemorialCare Breast Center at Orange Coast Medical Center, told Healthline.

“The presentation of cardiovascular disease in premenopausal women is notoriously different than in postmenopausal women, primarily [because] there are no typical symptoms such as chest pain.”

Reitherman said that a prevalent and common health screening procedure such as a mammogram can lead to the early identification of women with silent cardiovascular disease.

“Cardiovascular outcome studies that have included breast arterial calcifications as a risk factor are increasingly reported in the medical literature,” he said.

“The hope is that identifying younger women with silent cardiovascular risks [like BAC along with] appropriate integrated risk identification, analysis, and interventions can significantly reduce mortality in this age group.”

Mehta said there are several reasons BAC has been overlooked as a risk factor for heart disease.

“Mammograms are completed for assessment of breast cancer, and BAC has been historically an ‘incidental’ finding, which is not clinically relevant from an oncologic standpoint,” she said.

Additionally, there is no standardization on quantification of BAC severity, nor is it a required component of mammogram reports, said Mehta.

However, routine reporting of BAC on mammograms is an urgent goal of the American College of Radiology, Society of Breast Imaging, and Medicare, said Reitherman.

In fact, it is gaining traction in the United States. A new Maryland law, passed on October 1, requires mammography centers to notify women when a BAC is detected on their mammogram.

“Automated identification and reporting of these calcifications detected on screening mammograms will become increasingly prevalent in the coming months [and] years,” said Reitherman.

The next step is to identify this group of women to primary care physicians so that appropriate risk evaluation can take place, Reitherman added.

In addition to doctors routinely screening for conditions related to heart disease, such as diabetes, high blood pressure, and high cholesterol, he hopes BAC will also be routinely considered.

“Under current development are protocols to identify patients needing a cardiologist’s training and expertise to appropriately stratify this group of patients,” said Reitherman.

“The common healthcare behavior [of] having regular mammograms not only reduces risk of morbidity and mortality from breast cancer but will hopefully contribute to the reduction of morbidity and mortality from cardiovascular disease in younger women.”

He said this type of integrated healthcare approach demonstrates cross-specialty integration and coordination among women, radiologists, and their healthcare providers to improve women’s lives.

“This is an amazing game-changing opportunity,” Reitherman said.

Mehta said it’s reasonable for women to ask if BAC is present on their mammogram report.

“But if it is not uniformly reported by the radiologist, the individual clinician may not have the answer,” she said.

More research is needed before BAC on mammograms can be used routinely to predict heart disease.

Finding out your BAC could provide additional information to help you and your doctors decide how aggressively to address your cardiovascular risk factors, Chen said.

Reitherman agreed. “The goal of reducing morbidity and mortality from any cause such as cardiovascular disease is based on recognition of risk factors, followed by appropriate lifestyle and pharmacologic interventions,” he said.

Every month, an invoice from a steel distributor arrives priced 3% above the purchase order, and every month someone in accounts payable stops, opens the contract, finds clause 7.2 (monthly steel-index adjustment, capped at 5%), and approves it. The matching software flags it perfectly. It just has no idea that this exact question was answered last month, and the month before.

That is the gap I built Ledgerwise for: an agent that sits after the 3-way match and resolves invoice exceptions the way your desk resolved them last time — and knows when that history isn't enough.

An AP desk matches three documents: the purchase order (what we agreed to buy), the goods receipt (what actually arrived), and the invoice (what the vendor wants paid). When they disagree you get an exception: a price variance, a short shipment, a GST rate that doesn't match the contract, a surcharge line nobody ordered, a re-sent duplicate, or a vendor letter saying "we've changed our bank account."

Detecting those is a solved problem, and in Ledgerwise it's deliberately boring: matching.py is deterministic Python with no model and no memory. It finds exceptions and measures them — a percentage for price, rupees for a surcharge, units for a short shipment.

The memory layer is Hindsight, an open-source agent memory system from Vectorize. I picked it over rolling my own vector store because AP history isn't a pile of documents to search — it's a set of decisions with actors, amounts and dates, and I wanted something that extracts and organises those facts rather than just embedding paragraphs.

The obvious design is: recall similar past decisions, and if the top result says APPROVE, approve. Run that against the replay data and it looks great for a few weeks — until a logistics vendor sends a ₹6,800 fuel surcharge. Every earlier surcharge from that vendor was approved, but they were ₹1,200–₹2,400, and the agreed cap is ₹2,500. Similarity says "same vendor, same exception, approved." The money says otherwise.

That case reshaped the whole design. The question stopped being "what does memory say?" and became "is memory enough here?"

Each human decision is retained as one string with two parts: a prose sentence that Hindsight's retain pipeline extracts facts and entities from, and a structured line my policy can parse back out.

document_id keeps a re-decision on the same invoice from becoming a duplicate memory, and timestamp uses the invoice date rather than "now", so temporal questions work.

The magnitude matters as much as the decision. "Approved a 2.8% variance" and "approved a 4.2% variance" are different precedents.

1. The reason field is the product. I made the UI refuse to save a decision without a typed reason. Those sentences are what escalations show, what reflect summarises, and what makes the memory auditable. "Approved" is a log line; "approved under clause 7.2, index sheet attached" is knowledge.

2. Store the magnitude, not just the outcome. Similarity search will happily match "same vendor, same exception" and ignore that this one is three times bigger. Every precedent carries its number, and the policy compares numbers.

3. Decide what memory is allowed to override before you write any recall code. Bank changes, unknown POs and anything outside a learned range are human-only by design. Writing those guardrails first made the rest of the policy much easier to reason about.

4. Don't let the agent cite itself. Retaining the agent's own decisions is useful for audit and reflection. Counting them as precedent is how a system drifts.

5. Make memory configurable, not magical. A skeptical, literal bank disposition (skepticism 5, literalism 4) fit an AP desk better than the defaults. One approval of a 3% variance should not become permission for "price variances" in general.

The honest limitation: Ledgerwise is only as good as the reasons people type. A desk that clicks "approve" with "ok" gets an agent that can count but can't explain.

Drummer Chuck Comeau talks TikTok, Good Things Festival, and why a farewell tour is "not in the cards"

Simple Plan have been playing the same songs for 25 years. Lately, they’re playing them to a lot of people hearing them live for the first time.

“Every night we ask people, ‘Hey, how many people are here for the first time?’” drummer Chuck Comeau tells Billboard. “Sometimes it’s like 85% have never seen the band.”

The Montreal pop-punk band is drawing fans who weren’t born when “I’m Just a Kid” arrived in 2002, alongside the original listeners who are now bringing their own kids to shows. “And you’re like, ‘What are you talking about?’” Comeau says.

“There’s nothing cooler than what’s happening to our band right now,” he adds.

Much of that can be traced to “I’m Just a Kid” going viral on TikTok, where it soundtracked a challenge encouraging users to recreate their childhood photos. Comeau calls the trend a “gigantic gift,” but says meeting fans online has always come naturally to the band, which was filming backstage antics and sharing them through its website and DVDs long before TikTok existed.

“It’s the same thing,” he says. “It’s just a new platform.”

The renewed attention builds on a strong early run. The band’s 2004 album Still Not Getting Any… debuted at No. 3 on the Billboard 200, while “Perfect” and “Welcome to My Life” reached Nos. 24 and 40, respectively, on the Billboard Hot 100.

Next up is a return to Australia for Good Things Festival, where Simple Plan join a bill led by Limp Bizkit and featuring Spiritbox, the All-American Rejects, Bad Religion and MxPx. The festival hits Melbourne’s Flemington Racecourse on Dec. 4, Sydney Showground on Dec. 5 and Brisbane Showgrounds on Dec. 6, marking the band’s third trip Down Under in three years.

“We love Australia. What can I say?” Comeau says. “Any excuse, we come back.”

The lineup mixes pop-punk with nu-metal, hardcore and heavier rock, the kind of bill Comeau admits might once have made him nervous. “Being on this lineup would have been a bit more intimidating 25 years ago,” he says.

He credits streaming with breaking down the scene boundaries that once divided fans, making it easier for younger listeners to move between genres. The band has seen it firsthand at European festivals, sharing stages with acts like Slipknot.

“At first, you’re a little terrified,” he says. “You’re like, ‘How’s that gonna go?’ And then you actually realize that people are stoked.”

He has his own Good Things wish list. Bad Religion, whom he calls his favorite band, are near the top, along with Swedish punks Millencolin, who are playing Pennybridge Pioneers in full. He’s also keen to finally see Limp Bizkit live. “I’ve seen a lot of crazy videos right now that are popping up everywhere,” he says.

Meanwhile, Simple Plan have started writing their seventh studio album, with work set to continue into 2027. There’s no release date, but Comeau says the band has a batch of songs it’s excited about, and no intention of rushing.

“We’ve been around for 25 years and we only have six albums,” he says. “So we take our time.”

The record will follow 2022’s Harder Than It Looks, and Comeau says the aim is to refine the band’s sound rather than overhaul it. “We know who we are,” he says. “We’re not going to turn around and make a black metal record.”

The harder challenge is competing with the band’s own past. Songs that soundtracked fans’ teenage years carry a weight that new material has to earn.

“When you get to this stage of your career, you’re almost playing against yourself,” Comeau says. “You’re playing against your history.”

“You’re not the new shiny young band anymore,” he adds. “So you’ve got to work harder to have people pay attention.”

That’s why he won’t put a deadline on it. “There’s never a good time for a bad record, and there’s never a bad time for a good record,” he says.

Next year also marks 25 years since the band’s debut album, No Pads, No Helmets…Just Balls. Comeau says a special-edition vinyl and a few anniversary performances are on the table, but not a full album tour.

“I think we’re bigger than just one album,” he says. “But that album was really consequential for us. It’s the one that started it all.”

Another milestone arrives March 9, 2027, when the band releases its first children’s picture book, I’m Just a Kid: The Story of Simple Plan, illustrated by Jomike Tejido. Comeau says it grew out of watching the band’s original fans grow up and start families of their own.

“We were with them throughout their difficult moments,” he says, recalling how songs about breakups, school and friendships became part of listeners’ teenage years. “Fast-forward 20 years, 25 years, now they’re starting families, they’re having kids. We want to be there with them in this new chapter of their lives.”

The book explores friendship and perseverance and includes nods for longtime fans. “It’s as much for the parents as it is for the kids,” Comeau says.

For all the anniversaries and nostalgia, Simple Plan have never broken up, gone on hiatus or staged a reunion, which is rare among their peers. Comeau believes the years since the pandemic have been some of the band’s best, with bigger crowds and a sharper focus on the live show.

“When we were young, you were going out and you were partying,” he says. “Now it’s like, no, we don’t do that anymore. We’re all doing yoga and stretching.”

“We’ve always just kept going,” he says. “I feel like there’s a lot of work to be done.”

“There’s no talk about a farewell tour,” Comeau says. “Not in the cards for now.”

Before Captain America, Chris Evans Starred In An Underrated Non-Marvel Superhero Movie

Chris Evans is one of the most recognizable faces in superhero cinema, thanks largely to his portrayal of Steve Rogers in the Marvel Cinematic Universe. For nearly a decade, Evans helped bring the First Avenger to life, becoming a central figure in one of the biggest movie franchises of all time. However, his journey into the world of comic book-inspired adventures began long before he picked up Captain America's shield.

In 2005, Evans played Johnny Storm / The Human Torch, in Tim Story's Fantastic Four. He reprised the role in 2007's Fantastic Four: Rise of the Silver Surfer, bringing plenty of charisma and humor to the hot-headed member of Marvel's First Family. Nearly 20 years later, Evans stepped into the shoes of Johnny Storm again with a memorable cameo in Deadpool & Wolverine.

Between his appearances as the Human Torch and his eventual transformation into Captain America, however, Evans headlined another superhero adventure that deserves considerably more attention than it reserves. Released at a time when Hollywood was becoming increasingly fascinated with comic book stories about people with extraordinary abilities, this ambitious science-fiction thriller offered a decidedly different take on the genre.

It featured an intriguing mythology, an international setting, and a collection of characters whose powers made them anything but traditional superheroes. Unfortunately, the film never became the franchise starter its creators might have hoped for.

Push is a 2009 science-fiction action thriller directed by Paul McGuigan and written by David Bourla. Released in theaters on February 6, 2009, the film stars Evans alongside Dakota Fanning, Camilla Belle, and Djimon Hounsou. Rather than adapting an established comic book, Push creates an entirely original world populated by people with extraordinary psychic abilities. Its premise revolves around a secret government organization called Division, which captures and experiments on individuals with special powers in an effort to turn them into living weapons.

Evans plays Nick Gant, a young man with telekinetic abilities who has spent much of his life hiding from Division. His circumstances change when he encounters Cassie Holmes (Fanning), a teenage clairvoyant who can see glimpses of the future. Together, they search for Kira (Belle), a woman with the ability to manipulate people's thoughts and memories, while attempting to stay one step ahead of Division and its dangerous agents. As their mission develops, the characters must use their individual abilities to outsmart an organization that knows almost everything about their capabilities.

What makes Push particularly interesting is how it approaches the superhero genre. Instead of relying on familiar costumes, secret identities, and spectacular displays of power, the movie builds its mythology around different categories of psychic abilities. Telekinetics, clairvoyants, and mind controllers operate within a shared world, and their powers come with limitations that make confrontations more about strategy than brute strength.

The film’s Hong Kong setting also gives it a distinctive visual identity, combining neon-lit streets, cramped apartments, and elaborate action sequences. The result is closer to a supernatural espionage thriller than a conventional superhero blockbuster.

Despite its ambitious concept and talented cast, Push struggled to win over critics when it arrived in theaters. Its complicated mythology and purportedly convoluted storytelling became major points of criticism, with some reviewers arguing that the movie introduced too many ideas without giving them enough room to develop. Rotten Tomatoes currently lists the film with a 23% critics' score, reflecting the largely unfavorable response it received.

Nevertheless, dismissing Push entirely overlooks some of its most appealing qualities. Evans delivers a charismatic leading performance, while Fanning brings an intriguing combination of confidence and vulnerability to Cassie. The film's visual style, creative approach to psychic abilities, and willingness to experiment with familiar superhero conventions give it a personality that separates it from many other genre releases of its era. It also arrived during a particularly interesting period for superhero entertainment, when films like Iron Man and The Dark Knight were helping reshape audience expectations.

Push might not be a masterpiece, but it remains a fascinating chapter in Evans' expansive superhero career. Its original mythology and unconventional approach make it worth revisiting, particularly for viewers interested in seeing the actor outside his familiar Marvel roles. Before he became the face of Captain America, Chris Evans was already leading an ambitious superhero adventure that deserved a little more recognition.

When adapting a narrative, it's important to either try to stick as closely to the source material as possible, or to veer away from the foundations in meaningful ways while also keeping the spirit and heart of the story and characters.

If an adaptation attempts to adapt a story faithfully but makes a handful of changes, it will often be torn apart by the fanbase as they pick apart how it lost sight of what made the series great in the first place. Alternatively, if the story feels so drastically different that it fails to even evoke a sense of what the original story inspired, it begs the question of why it was considered an adaptation in the first place.

So, when Prime Video's The Boys franchise took its lead from the comics as imagined by Garth Ennis and Darick Robertson, it was exciting and refreshing to see where the show changed course to make a story that would be engaging and surprising for longstanding fans of the franchise and newcomers to the series.

While The Boys comics serve as the foundation for the TV show and its spin-offs, Gen V and soon the prequel series Vought Rising, they are fundamentally different to the Prime Video series. Just on the surface level, the show makes a major narrative arc around how members of The Boys are people without powers, and they generally despise supes, but in the comics, all of the members of the team take Compound V to gain permanent powers and fight against the corrupt supes.

Additionally, there is a pivotal difference in the fact that the show positions the story as this group of underdogs fighting against a massive corporation, Vought International, as they expose corporate greed, manipulation, and coercion. In the comics, The Boys are more focused on challenging three core teams, The Seven, Payback, and the G-Men.

While discussing the G-Men, these characters also serve as the foundation and inspiration for Gen V, which expands the story to include Godolkin University and explores how young supes are trained in the corporate machine to become a part of the super cycle for Vought. In the comics, The Boys simply fight and kill the G-Men, which serves as a small side plot in the wider narrative.

Evidently, the show, and showrunner Eric Kripke, had a lot of love for the comics and the ideas they introduced in a landscape dominated by more traditional superhero stories, but they were able to make it their own and create something that ultimately feels much more prescient and relevant for modern audiences.

But, if The Boys had truly departed from the source material completely, it would lead to a subpar series that failed to capture what the original creators set out to create. Instead, The Boys took the core of the story, an examination of what flawed people would be like if given incredible and veritable powers, and the core cast, and made something that felt fresh while honoring the source material.

Many of the main characters are carried over from the comics to the screen, and in many ways, they thoroughly resemble their drawn counterparts. From the abrasive and gruff Billy Butcher, to the arrogant supe with a god complex, Homelander, these characters feel largely the same.

As a result, the series benefits massively as it draws in the established fanbase alongside fresh eyes, and delivers something that genuinely feels like it belongs in the world of the comics, but does not remain constrained by what came before. With that, The Boys and its wider franchise have become one of Prime Video's biggest success stories.

📅 Nov 08, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Energy stocks have delivered exceptional gains in 2026, but a surprising shift in the oil market could change the outlook for investors.

Energy stocks were the clear winners during the 2022 bear market. The S&P 500 energy stocks delivered a total return of 65.7%, while the broader S&P 500 (^GSPC +0.31%) lost 18.1%, including dividends.

Energy stocks are leading again in 2026, but without a broader bear market. S&P 500 energy stocks have gained 42% so far this year, compared with a 12% gain for the broader S&P 500 index, excluding dividends.

In 2022, rising oil prices boosted energy companies' earnings, while inflation and aggressive interest rate hikes weighed on the broader market. The 2026 rally has been supported by oil supply disruptions in the Middle East, with unusually high refining margins. But a recovery in global oil supplies could put pressure on energy profits and stock valuations.

ExxonMobil (XOM +0.34%) reported earnings of $55.7 billion in 2022, compared with $23 billion in 2021. Chevron's (CVX -0.15%) earnings also more than doubled to $35.5 billion in 2022. Both companies returned billions to shareholders through dividends and share repurchases.

However, April 2025 offered a different picture. President Donald Trump's tariff announcements triggered a sharp market sell-off. The S&P 500 fell 21.3% from its February 2025 peak during intraday trading, but did not officially enter a bear market based on closing prices. But S&P 500 energy stocks fell 13.7% in April 2025, making energy the worst-performing sector.

Rising concerns about slowing economic growth and weaker oil demand negatively impacted energy stocks in April 2025. This also demonstrated that energy stocks do not outperform during every market downturn.

The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) estimated that 4.8 million barrels per day of Middle East crude oil production remained shut down in September 2026, compared with a peak of 10.9 million barrels per day in May. But damaged pipelines and risks to oil tankers continued to affect shipments.

However, EIA expects global liquid-fuels production to fall short of consumption by around 1.3 million barrels per day in 2026. The production is then projected to exceed consumption by approximately 5 million barrels per day in 2027. This represents a potential swing of nearly 6.3 million barrels per day in the global liquid-fuel supply-demand balance.

This forecast assumes that supply routes will reopen and production will also recover. If that happens, rising inventories could reduce oil prices even without a recession in the U.S. The EIA forecasts annual average Brent to decline from $96 per barrel in 2026 to $84 per barrel in 2027. However, renewed geopolitical tensions or slower infrastructure repairs could delay the recovery.

Oil markets are already responding to expectations of easing supply disruptions. Brent crude prices dropped below $100 this week. Hopes of peace agreements involving Iran and Ukraine contributed to the decline, even though the underlying supply disruptions had not been fully resolved.

In the third quarter of 2026, ExxonMobil's Energy Products segment, which includes refining and fuels, reported earnings close to $7.2 billion. Chevron's downstream business earnings also more than quadrupled to $4.9 billion in the third quarter of 2026.

However, these strong downstream earnings may not persist. Disruptions to refineries in the Middle East and Russia have constrained gasoline and diesel supplies. This has pushed their selling prices higher relative to crude oil. However, as damaged refineries resume production and fuel supplies recover, the difference between refined product and crude oil prices could narrow.

Lower crude prices could also hurt earnings from oil production. ExxonMobil estimates that every $1 per barrel change in Brent crude prices would affect its annual upstream after-tax earnings by approximately $700 million, based on expected 2026 operations. Applying this sensitivity to the EIA's projected $12-per-barrel decline in annual average Brent crude oil prices suggests a potential $8.4 billion annual headwind for Exxon's upstream earnings. This estimate assumes that Exxon's production volumes, costs, and other operating conditions remain largely unchanged.

According to FactSet Research Systems' Oct. 30 report, S&P 500 energy stocks traded at 13.2 times expected earnings over the next 12 months, significantly lower than S&P 500's forward 12-month price-to-earnings ratio of 18.7x. However, the valuation of S&P 500 energy stocks was above its own five-year average forward 12-month PE of 12.6.

According to analyst estimates compiled by FactSet, S&P 500 energy sector earnings are expected to decline 7.8% in 2027, compared with projected earnings growth of 16.3% for the broader S&P 500. This suggests that energy's lower valuation relative to the broader market may reflect expectations of weaker future earnings, rather than necessarily making the sector a bargain.

I would avoid aggressively buying energy stocks after their substantial gains in 2026. A recovery in oil supplies could weaken earnings, even if the broader stock market remains strong.

CMS makes it official, codifying Medicare drug price negotiations established under IRA" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Against a backdrop of repeated industry challenges in court, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) is locking in the drug price negotiation framework established under the Biden-era Inflation Reduction Act (IRA).

In turn, the process under which CMS selects certain high-spend Medicare Part B or Part D drugs and negotiates lower prices with manufacturers will carry on into 2029 and beyond. The rule codifies and expands policies previously established through guidance for the initial 2026-28 period.

The final rule is slated to go into effect Dec. 5, CMS said in a fact sheet accompanying Thursday’s announcement. By Feb. 1 of next year, CMS will select up to 20 drugs covered under Medicare Part B or Part D for price negotiations taking effect in 2029, with the potential to also select medicines included in previous rounds for renegotiation.

That upcoming group of drugs will make up the fourth cycle of pricing negotiations, with negotiated prices taking effect in 2029. CMS asserted that the rule gives both patients and drugmakers “a clear path forward as the agency builds on the program’s results and continues negotiating prices for high-cost drugs.”

The initial rounds of price negotiations have already included a slew of prominent brands, including Novo's semaglutide franchise of Wegovy and Ozempic and cancer meds like Bristol Myers Squibb’s Pomalyst, Pfizer’s Ibrance and AstraZeneca’s Calquence.

Meanwhile, the latest tranche of drugs selected earlier this year for negotiation, whose new prices would go into effect in 2028, includes notable entrants like Gilead’s HIV treatment Biktarvy, AbbVie’s Botox and Eli Lilly’s diabetes drug Trulicity, to name just a few.

Beyond codifying the framework for price negotiations in 2029 and subsequent years, CMS has also taken steps to fine-tune several elements of the program.

Namely, the rule finalizes a policy on how CMS classifies certain fixed-combination drugs, with the aim of addressing “potential program integrity concerns” that could encourage drugmakers to evade negotiated prices by steering patients toward new product formulations.

The rule also includes a provision establishing a temporary “small biotech floor” that prevents CMS from offering or accepting a counteroffer below a specified price floor for certain eligible small biotech drugs in 2029 and 2030. It also codifies two policies related to Medicare Part D intended to ensure plan coverage of medicines with a maximum fair price in effect and limit the prices plans pay pharmacies and other dispensing entities.

The IRA and the Medicare price negotiations it empowered have been frequent targets of the biopharma industry, which has broadly alleged that the policy is unconstitutional and that the pricing talks aren’t as voluntary as advertised. But attempts by major drugmakers and powerful trade organizations like the Pharmaceutical Research and Manufacturers of America (PhRMA) to push back on Medicare price negotiations have repeatedly stalled out.

In the interim, the U.S. government has made a separate and more recent push to rein in high prescription drug costs by leaning on President Donald Trump’s “most favored nation” (MFN) strategy.

“President Trump directed us to lower drug costs for Americans using every tool at our disposal,” CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz, M.D., said in a statement announcing the new rule. “This rule provides additional tools to help CMS negotiate better deals, achieve greater savings for taxpayers and beneficiaries, and improve health outcomes for seniors who rely on the Medicare Prescription Drug Program.”

MFN, for its part, has yielded at times, controversial deals with more than two dozen drugmakers, generally involving commitments to price new medicines in line with prices in high-income comparator countries and offer MFN pricing on certain existing drugs through Medicaid.

Through those deals, which have also included investment commitments in the U.S., drugmakers have been able to secure temporary exemptions from the Trump administration’s threatened pharmaceutical import tariffs.

Around this week’s midterm elections, several Big Pharma executives have signaled that they expect little immediate change to their thinking around MFN. Nevertheless, the lack of transparency surrounding the deals has already drawn scrutiny from Democratic lawmakers. With Democrats set to control both chambers of Congress beginning in January, the party may soon press harder to uncover the details of those agreements.

AI-generated code usually works, but it is often not secure. In Veracode's 2026 tests, models produced code that compiled almost 100% of the time, yet failed security checks on nearly 44% of tasks, a pass rate that has not improved since 2025. The fix is not to ban AI tools; it is to treat their output like code from a contractor you have never worked with: review it, scan it, and test it before it reaches production.

Below is a summary of the research, the failure patterns that come up again and again, and a review checklist you can hand to your developer or agency.

In its 2025 GenAI Code Security Report, Veracode gave 80 curated coding tasks to more than 100 large language models. When a secure and an insecure approach were both possible, models chose the insecure one 45% of the time. Java was the riskiest language, with a security failure rate above 70%; Python, C# and JavaScript failed between 38% and 45% of the time. Models failed to defend against cross-site scripting in 86% of cases and log injection in 88%. Larger models did not perform significantly better than smaller ones.

The 2026 edition found the average security pass rate "sits at 56 percent — virtually unchanged since last year's report." The best model on its summer 2026 leaderboard reached 68%, which still means failing nearly one security task in three. Python code passed 63% of the time, Java only 30%. Coding-specialised models (51%) did no better than general-purpose ones (52%), and model size made no clear difference, although reasoning models kept an edge (56% against 51%).

A Backdoor Sat in the Firmware Partition of 1.3 Million Set-Top Boxes

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Posted on Nov 6 • Originally published at blog.neticslabs.com

Capture of the Google Cloud Threat Intelligence post “Shutting the Door on the XCPE Botnet”, taken 3 November 2026 from cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/shutting-the-door-on-the-xcpe-botnet.

On 2 November 2026 Google Threat Intelligence Group published Shutting the Door on the XCPE Botnet, with the disclosure that Google and industry partners had dismantled a backdoor enrolled on more than 1.3 million set-top boxes. The attribution is the part that changes how the story should be read: the researchers assess with high confidence that the malware was developed, deployed and operated for financial gain by Videostrong Technology, an Android original equipment and original design manufacturer.

The malicious software arrived preinstalled, across many device models and brands, built directly into firmware over multiple years. Google found dedicated infrastructure, evolving configurations and tools for managing affiliates, and assessed with high confidence that Videostrong intentionally developed, deployed and operated the backdoor and botnet for financial gain. Its response was to cut ties with Videostrong and ban the company from making devices with preloaded Google apps.

Google's own account of how the code got past certification is worth reading twice. Certified Android devices pass a suite of compatibility and security tests, and the vendor used obfuscation to exploit a gap in those tests, loading malicious code after the device's first boot. Review processes changed as a result. For anyone who buys devices on the strength of a certification mark, this is the uncomfortable lesson: certification covers the tested state, and the vendor retains control over code loaded after first boot.

Netics editorial visual: the scale Google reported for the enrolled device population.

Three stages are documented, and the first one explains why removal fails. A stub package, shipped as x.cpe in some builds and com.oemservice in others, sits in the system partition and runs at android.uid.system privilege. On boot it checks the oem/ directory, installs the corresponding x.cpe package into /data/app/ and reinstalls itself after any user uninstall, which makes the device's own settings screen an unreliable place to look for the truth.

Stage two offloads the work. The backdoor acts mainly as the command-and-control communicator and passes module execution to com.abc.nexus, a file that carries native anti-analysis utilities to defeat sandbox detection and configures the monetisation modules. Since August 2026 that loader has been phased out in favour of tailored droppers that install a single module, delivered through the same command channel. Stage three is the modules themselves, arriving as native ELF daemons, as SDKs compiled into the loader's bytecode, or as secondary APKs fetched remotely. Google says a significant majority of the staging files—modules, shell scripts and legitimate applications—were hosted as GitHub releases on operator-owned GitHub pages.

Google Threat Intelligence Group figure, 2 November 2026: the XCPE ecosystem, from the infected set-top box and its backdoor through staging infrastructure to command servers and modules. Source: cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/shutting-the-door-on-the-xcpe-botnet.

The same bootstrap returns geolocation and STUN parameters so the device can find its public address and traverse NAT, a file-upload URL for logs and diagnostic dumps, and switches for individual advertising modules. All samples analysed to that point used a single broker hostname, mqtt.szbboys[.]com. Command traffic that looks like ordinary telemetry and a broker reachable over the public internet describe a detection problem that lives in network logs, where most small organisations look last.

The economic design is a rented shop window. Videostrong sold backdoor access to criminal affiliates, and the loaded modules turn the box into a residential proxy exit node, an ad-fraud engine, or a host for further backdoors. The named SDKs are specific: Linkfog, AppSalt and NetNut among the proxy networks, CBOXTV and HiddenView among the ad-fraud engines, Moyu and Spirit among the backdoors that load more modules. AppSalt is the residential proxy SDK owned by Honeygain, which is owned by Oxylabs, a detail that shows how ordinary the corporate chains behind these modules look.

Greed shows in the stacking. A single device can run several modules with the same function, and the researchers report as many as three residential proxy SDKs installed on one device. A compromised box becomes a metered egress point for someone else's traffic, with the household or office IP address carrying the reputation damage.

Netics editorial visual: the three-stage chain from firmware stub to monetisation module, and the step that ended it.

The partner list behind the disruption reads like a cross-section of the industry: Japan's National Police Agency, Lumen, HUMAN Security, Plume, Synthient and TrendAI.

This story turns firmware provenance into an inventory problem. A new image can fix a kernel or container flaw on every host that rebuilds. This backdoor was compiled into the firmware partition of devices already deployed in living rooms; the vendor is barred from certification and the hardware remains in the field.

Google's disruption made three moves worth noting for any operator. It took legal action against the domains and servers used to control the botnet and to install modules. It took legal action against command domains used by more than a dozen affiliate malware families, which the researchers assess severs access to at least 10 million additional compromised devices, since those affiliates distribute through other channels too. And it changed how firmware integrity is verified on certified Android TV devices.

Devices keep reporting while the broker answers, so the distribution below shows where the installed base sits.

Google Threat Intelligence Group figure, 2 November 2026: the global distribution of devices reporting the x.cpe backdoor. Source: cloud.google.com/blog/topics/threat-intelligence/shutting-the-door-on-the-xcpe-botnet.

The operator's version of that work starts with a list. Which Android boxes, signage players, conference-room dongles and kiosk displays are on the network, who bought them, which vendor built the firmware and whether that vendor still exists in a position to ship updates. Most small estates cannot answer the last question, and that gap is the exposure this campaign exploits.

Google cut off command infrastructure and tightened firmware verification. Google took legal action to take down the domains, affiliate command servers are down, a manufacturer is out of the certified programme, and Google says it tightened firmware verification. A box already installed retains its backdoor. The researchers are explicit that the illicit market is resilient, with operators pivoting to new loaders or rival SDKs when a component is disrupted.

For a small organisation, the practical controls are unglamorous and cheap. Segment devices that need outbound internet from the rest of the network, deny egress to hosts nobody recognises, and watch for long-lived sessions to message brokers, which almost no television box needs. Prefer hardware whose firmware updates you can verify, and treat a bargain Android box from an unknown ODM as an untrusted endpoint with a screen attached. Prebuilt images carry their own provenance and inventory requirements. The earlier analysis of the Copy Fail container flaw argued from the software side; a full security audit and hardening is where an estate turns that reasoning into a list of devices it can defend.

“Backdoor operators, ad-fraud rings, and residential proxy providers operate as an interconnected supply chain.” The sentence closes the report, and it also describes the buying decision in front of any organisation refreshing its screens this quarter. The supply chain runs through the firmware partition, and the receipt is the only document most buyers keep.

The Sky Today on Friday, November 6: Nab the North America Nebula

📅 Nov 06, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

As fall gets into full swing, we are seeing less of the summertime stars. By 6 P.M. local time, the Summer Triangle — which flies high overhead on short summer nights — is now starting to set in the west. Lowest of its three points is Vega in Lyra; to Vega’s upper left is Altair in Aquila, while Deneb in Cygnus is almost directly above Vega and the highest of the three.

With no Moon in the sky, let’s use Deneb to find a favorite deep-sky object: NGC 7000, the North America Nebula. It’s a great small scope target, and its location just 3° east of Deneb makes it easy to center in the eyepiece. However, this huge emission nebula stretches about 2° across, so note that it requires a larger field of view to capture all in one go. It definitely benefits from a nebula filter if you have one.

On the sky, the nebula’s western side looks like the eastern coast of the continent for which it is named, and vice versa. The curve that mimics the Gulf of Mexico is particularly prominent, so start there if you’re having trouble seeing the shape. Look also to NGC 7000’s west — across a dark gulf, you’ll see a fainter, more compact patch of nebulosity. This is IC 5067/5070, also known as the Pelican Nebula.
