6 James Bond Books That Robert Pattinson's Era Has To Adapt

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Entertainment  |  Read Source →

James Bond is entering a new era, and that means the brilliant books that inspired the movie franchise will be adapted for the screen once again.

Robert Pattinson has officially been cast as James Bond, with Denis Villeneuve tapped to direct the upcoming Bond 26 movie, and there's no shortage of speculation about which Jame Bond story will be adapted first. In the wake of the beloved Daniel Craig era of James Bond, expectations are high for Pattinson and Villeneuve, with every creative choice under extreme scrutiny.

Watching modern spy thrillers before Bond 26 is a great way to prepare for the new era, and catching up on the novels and short stories that have been brought to life onscreen is just as important. Who the next James Bond will be and the kind of missions he embarks on are yet to be discovered, but Pattinson and Villeneuve have plenty of great writing to work with as they pave the way for the next Bond era.

One of Fleming's most enjoyable additions to the James Bond canon, Moonraker is a departure from some of the traditional tropes of the novels. Bond remains in England throughout the entire story and deals with his personal attachments to his country and his place in the world of espionage. The reflective nature of the novel differs from the movie version of the story, but both have their merits.

The 1979 film adaptation of Moonraker stars Roger Moore as Bond and is one of the most campy and outlandish additions to the franchise, and this could be an interesting angle for the next movies to take. In more recent years, James Bond has been a more serious, dramatically-oriented action series, but the origins of Bond are much more outlandish and fun, which could suit Pattinson's take on the character.

The reactions to Amazon MGM Studios' James Bond are in, as Robert Pattinson gives up his cape and cowl for a shaken and not stirred Martini.

Anxieties and tensions surrounding the development of weapons and the threat of the end of the world are higher than ever, and grappling with these fears through a thriller like James Bond makes them easier to contend with culturally. Full of high-stakes card games and the race to harness new weapons and technologies, Moonraker would make for a great next Bond film no matter the tone the film takes.

Not a book, but a short story: 007 in New York is one of the most under-the-radar works by Fleming, but there's something in the narrative that might appeal to modern audiences. Following Bond as he explores New York City and thinly developing some moments of action and intrigue, 007 in New York is almost a blank canvas for Bond 26 to explore.

Pattinson's Bond casting opens the door to a wide array of interpretations for the characterization of Bond and the kind of adventures he's going to go on in his next era. A more introspective, quirky Bond, like the one in 007 in New York, would fit right into Pattinson's persona as an actor. James Bond is known for its glamorous locations, and it would be a fun subversion of the franchise to see Bond in a grittier, more real version of New York.

Of course, Bond 26 could adapt a better-known short story, but taking on a story with the potential of 007 in New York would give Pattinson and the creative team the ability to make Bond whoever they want him to be. However, the next films will have to straddle the line between originality and the expectations of audiences, as every franchise must.

Though Ian Fleming's Bond books started it all, the authors who have taken up the mantle of keeping the novels alive have done an excellent job. John Gardner's Scorpius is a novel full of betrayals, arms dealers, cults, and brainwashing, but this is just another day at the office for James Bond.

The titular Scorpius is a wildly fun villain, and there's no question that whoever is cast to play the antagonist in Bond 26 will have just as much riding on their performance as Pattinson. Scorpius features many pressing moral questions and revolves around the development of a cult engaging in a so-called "holy war." The thematic tension of Scorpius could easily be brought to life in 2026, though the novel was written in the late 1980s.

Harriet Horner, the Bond Girl featured in Scorpius, is another interesting character who could be modernized for the next movie. Her relationship with Bond and their fight against Scorpius is compelling, and there are many great actresses who could easily make Horner an even more complex role in the coming era of Bond.

Raymond Benosn's DoubleShot continues the fight between Bond and the Union, a terrorist organization featured in Benson's contributions to the Bond canon. Though it's always fun when Bond has an arch-nemesis to fight, there's a particularly sinister tension when Bond has to go toe-to-toe with a far-reaching criminal syndicate that he can't quite reach.

With Pattinson's history of playing offbeat characters and lending his talents to weird and interesting independent films, it will be important that Bond 26 pays tribute to Pattinson's ability to be a character actor, not just a leading man. DoubleShot makes use of a Bond double who the real 007 must foil, which would give Pattinson the opportunity to play both parts. It's no stretch of the imagination to envision Pattinson taking on a dual role in his first Bond project.

In the lead-up to the final confrontation with the double, the Union works to make Bond believe that he's hallucinating and losing his grip on reality. This is a scary feeling for Bond, and provides the opportunity for the kind of performance that Pattinson would shine in. There's room for experimentation in style and tone in any adaptation of DoubleShot.

What better story to adapt for Pattinson's debut as Bond than a prequel that chronicles Bond's first mission with a license to kill, set before the events of Casino Royale. It would be a mistake for Bond 26 to produce another take on Casino Royale after Craig's era made such a definitive version in 2006. However, giving Pattinson a proper origin story and letting the audience get used to him could be a smart move for this Bond.

Though Forever and a Day takes place in the 1950s, it would be easy to translate the narrative to the present day, unless Amazon MGM wants to take a big risk and make the next Bond movies period pieces. Since that's unlikely, the more realistic chance the studio might take with Pattinson's Bond would be to develop him from his early stages as a spy instead of jumping into his adventures.

This would surely ruffle some feathers and might not be the direction the movie will go, but Forever and a Day has a strong story that sees Bond travel to the French Riviera to find out what happened to the 007 who came before him. Introducing beloved characters and demonstrating Bond's evolution, Forever and a Day could give viewers the chance to fall in love with a new Bond.

One of the most recent additions to the James Bond canon, the 2023 On His Majesty's Secret Service, was published to coincide with the coronation of King Charles III. Following Bond as he thwarts attempts on the King's life by a would-be usurper of the throne, On His Majesty's Secret Service isn't the most in-depth Bond book, but this creates the perfect opportunity for the film series to take the plot and expand it for a movie.

Topical and relevant, On His Majesty's Secret Service would open the door for Bond 26 to engage with the future of the monarchy in the modern era and show audiences what kind of Bond Pattinson is going to be. The 1963 book by Fleming, On Her Majesty's Secret Service, served as inspiration for the contemporary novel, and the film could draw from both stories in an adaptation.

The future of James Bond might be uncertain now, but with Pattinson in the driver's seat as 007, there's plenty of room for the movie series to bring timely works like On His Majesty's Secret Service to life.

Senate Budget chair called Social Security a 'scam' — said shut it down

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

Wisconsin Republican Ron Johnson made the remarks Wednesday, on the day of a Senate Budget Committee hearing on the program

Sen. Ron Johnson, the Wisconsin Republican who chairs the Senate Budget Committee, said on Wednesday that Social Security is a "scam" that should be "shut down," according to MarketWatch.

Johnson made the comments during a Fox Business segment, according to MarketWatch. "It's grotesque and at this point, there really is no way to fix Social Security," he said. "It is a Ponzi scheme. You can't fix it. You just shut it down and hope you can recover some of your money."

The Senate Budget Committee scheduled a hearing titled "Social Security: The Reality" for 11 a.m. ET on Wednesday in room SD-608. The committee lists Johnson as presiding and Sen. Jeff Merkley, an Oregon Democrat, as giving a member statement.

The committee lists three witnesses: Veronique de Rugy of the Mercatus Center at George Mason University, Jason J. Fichtner of the LIMRA Retirement Income Institute and Kathryn Anne Edwards of the National Academy of Social Insurance.

Johnson previewed the hearing in an X $TWTR post on Tuesday, according to Benzinga. He called Social Security a "legal Ponzi scheme" and wrote that the hearing would lay out how the program has been "grossly mismanaged."

In a video attached to the post, Johnson said the money workers paid into the program was spent "the minute it came in the door," according to Benzinga. He argued that the government could have put program surpluses into investments from 1986 onward, leaving $14.5 trillion in "hard assets" available for paying out benefits.

Nancy Altman, president of the advocacy group Social Security Works, criticized the remarks in a statement, according to MarketWatch. "Millions of seniors and people with disabilities will face homelessness and hunger" if the program were shut down, she said.

Social Security’s trust funds are on track to be exhausted within six years, according to the Congressional Budget Office, as cited by MarketWatch. If that happens, payments to recipients could be reduced by roughly 26%, according to the CBO. Congress has yet to settle on a plan for closing the gap, MarketWatch said.

AARP published a report earlier this month on how shifting income patterns have shortened the program's projected solvency. Social Security's trustees project the combined trust fund will run out in 2034, and AARP said the share of GDP subject to the payroll tax fell from 41% to 34% in 2025. AARP said that had the share held steady, the program could cover scheduled benefits in full through 2059.

Before the first cold snap, have your car’s battery tested free at an auto parts store, check tire tread and pressure, top up antifreeze and winter washer fluid, and replace worn wipers. Then pack an emergency kit for the trunk in case you’re stranded anyway.

Cold weather lowers a battery’s power just as the engine needs more of it to start, so a battery that starts the car slowly in October can fail on the first frigid morning.

AutoZone and O’Reilly Auto Parts test batteries free, right in the car. Replacing a weak one now costs only a battery you’d soon need anyway, while waiting until it fails adds a jump-start or tow, plus a late start to work.

Where snow and ice are routine, switch to winter tires, which keep their grip below about 45 degrees, when all-season tires stiffen. Your regular tires don’t wear while they’re stored, so they last more seasons. If you’ll keep the car a few years, put the winter set on its own wheels, so each spring and fall swap skips paying a shop to remount the tires. Where snow is occasional, all-weather tires can stay on all year, and unlike most all-season tires they carry the mountain-and-snowflake symbol for severe snow.

Stock the trunk with an ice scraper, a blanket, a flashlight, jumper cables or a jump starter, kitty litter for traction, water, snacks, and a phone charger. Save a roadside assistance number in your phone, and decide whether a AAA membership is worth it.

Drivers stuck in snow should stay with the car, keep the tailpipe clear so fumes can’t back up inside, and run the engine about 10 minutes an hour for heat, with a window cracked.

Your Favorite Coffee Shop Is Probably Streaming Music Illegally. It’s the Music Industry’s Fault, Too (Guest Column)

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A new study found 81% of U.S. businesses don't properly license music, costing creators and other rightsholders. The fix starts with education — not lawsuits.

Walk into almost any shop, gym, salon or restaurant in America, and you’ll hear music. Ask the owner whether it’s licensed and you will likely get a confident “yes.” But in most cases, that answer is, unfortunately, wrong.

Soundtrack recently surveyed 1,525 decision-makers responsible for nearly 15,000 business locations across 10 customer-facing industries to find out how U.S. businesses actually play music. Though the law is clear that businesses need specific licenses to play music in public, the results of the survey describe a market that loves music, depends on it, and, overwhelmingly, does not properly pay for it.

Ninety-seven percent of the businesses we surveyed play music for customers, and 77% do it every day. Adjusted for the size of the U.S., that means roughly 3.8 million U.S. business locations play music in their stores.

Nine in 10 of those surveyed said music boosts employee motivation and shapes the energy of their space, while more than eight in 10 called it an essential part of the business and said it has to fit their brand. Rather than merely being a background expense, businesses see music as part of the product.

Yet despite this overwhelming demand, the way music played in businesses is paid for has not evolved. A striking 79% of businesses illicitly use a consumer streaming service, such as a personal Spotify, Pandora or YouTube account, to play music for customers. Only 28% of businesses use a service built and licensed for business. This is one of the biggest failures, and also one of the biggest opportunities, for the music industry today.

Aside from the scale of misuse, it is significant how many businesses are sure they’re doing it right. Seventy-eight percent of respondents were confident the music in their business is legally compliant. Eighty-three percent said they’re familiar with the rules. Yet 81% are misusing music, and 58% believe it’s legal to stream a personal account in their business.

Even I struggle to distill the U.S. licensing rules into just a few sentences; such is the unnecessary complexity surrounding them. Put simply, businesses require both a music service licensed for commercial use and licenses with all four U.S. performing rights organizations (PROs) — ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR — that cover the music they play.

Some licensed music services bundle performance rights, while others require businesses to secure separate public performance licenses from U.S. PROs. For a business owner, working out exactly what’s included can be a job in itself.

Too often I speak to business owners who believe they’re fully licensed but are missing key PRO licenses or still playing music via their personal streaming account. The survey reflects this, finding only 5% of businesses hold licenses from all four U.S. PROs.

However, this isn’t a story about bad actors. Nine in 10 businesses said compliance matters to them, and most said it matters that artists are paid fairly. The businesses using personal streaming accounts are doing it because it’s what they know. It’s easy, familiar and often free. It’s not a problem of intent; it’s a lack of information and a non-user-friendly system.

The cost to music creators and other rightsholders is real. Combining what businesses told us they spend on music with the number of establishments recorded by the Bureau of Labor Statistics, we estimate that annual spending on music would rise by about $1.4 billion per year if every business was properly licensed through a compliant business music service. That money should flow to songwriters, artists, publishers, labels and the services that license them legally.

The music industry shares the blame. No one proactively educates the market or gives a business owner a clear answer about what they need to be fully covered. Consumer platforms offer free tiers, teaching a generation of shop owners that music costs nothing. And business music services, mine included, have not explained the difference loudly or simply enough.

Enforcement alone won’t close a gap this wide. You can’t sue three million locations into compliance, and you shouldn’t want to. These are the customers who say music is essential to them. The answer is a coordinated push across trade organisations, PROs, publishers, labels, consumer platforms and business services to create one plain-language message about what is legal, provide simpler routes to full licensing and build services businesses actually want to use.

That last part matters. Businesses told us what they look for in streaming services designed for businesses: ease-of-use, price and good playlists (the main complaint about business music services is that their music selections are limited and repetitive). The music offered by these services has to be at least as good as the consumer app on the owner’s smartphone — or the consumer app will keep winning.

Streaming rebuilt the recorded music industry by making the legal option easier for individuals than piracy. Now, the same needs to happen for businesses. The demand is already there, and most want to do it right. What’s missing is clarity, and a legal option that’s simple to use and offers better selections.

That $1.4 billion isn’t lost. It’s waiting for the industry to make doing the right thing the easy thing.

Ola Sars is the co-founder and CEO of Soundtrack, the music streaming service for businesses. The study was conducted by Soundtrack and the NMPA in 2025, surveying 1,525 U.S. music decision-makers via Sago’s opt-in B2B panel.

Microsoft Advertising is rolling out what it calls its biggest bidding model upgrade in a decade. The new models use more signals to predict whether an ad click will turn into a conversion and what it might be worth.

Microsoft will apply the upgrade automatically as it reaches more accounts. That means campaigns can start spending differently before you make any changes yourself. Microsoft announced the rollout on November 18 and plans more bidding updates in 2027.

Microsoft says the new models can change how quickly campaigns spend and how closely they stick to your targets. These are the areas to keep an eye on:

A $50 CPA target still leaves room for a campaign to bring in conversions at $40 each, even if it spends its full budget. A max CPC can limit that flexibility. Say you average $3 a click and cap bids at $5. The cap rules out a $6 bid even if Microsoft expects the click could bring in a conversion within your CPA target.

Say you raise your CPA target on Monday and customers usually take a week to buy. By Friday, you know what Monday’s clicks cost, but you may still be waiting on some of the sales. Faster bidding won’t shorten the time those customers take to buy.

Raising a CPA target or lowering a ROAS target can open up auctions your campaign previously passed up. To limit swings in performance, Microsoft suggests making those changes in 15% increments and reviewing each after one conversion cycle. For a bigger change, like switching bid strategies or adding a conversion goal, Microsoft recommends allowing one to two cycles.

Microsoft recommends conversion-based bidding for new campaigns once the account has at least five conversions. Those can come from campaigns you already run. If you’re still gathering your first five, Enhanced CPC is an option.

On LinkedIn, Sophie Westall asked if the same advice applies to low-volume accounts already using Enhanced CPC. Microsoft Advertising Liaison Navah Hopkins replied:

Every account is different so a blanket recommendation still needs to be put to the test. With that said, so long as the account has at least 5 conversions total, the system is smart enough to use that to begin with conversion-based bidding. While we still recommend the 30-day evaluation period and not making too many changes if you’re working with fewer conversions, the system is much more responsive now!

For judging performance, Microsoft recommends around 30 conversions in 30 days at the campaign or portfolio level. A low-volume campaign can reach the end of that first month and still have too few conversions to judge the switch.

Microsoft reported 10% more conversion value per click at roughly the same ROAS after the model upgrade. That result came from August 2026 global Search and Audience Network data for advertisers using Maximize Conversion Value or Target ROAS.

Microsoft also looked at advertisers who switched bid strategies. Search advertisers who moved from Target CPA to Target ROAS saw 8% more conversion value at a similar ROAS. Advertisers who moved from Enhanced CPC to Maximize Conversions with a CPA target saw 18% more conversions at a similar CPA.

If you’re considering Target ROAS, pull up a few recent purchases in Microsoft Advertising and compare the reported values with the order totals. A $40 order and a $400 order can both show up as conversions even if your tracking assigns them the same value. That’s easy to miss if you’re used to checking that purchases are coming through and leaving it there.

Microsoft reported its largest gains from value-based bidding among advertisers who sent actual transaction values. If your purchase goal uses a fixed value, the reported ROAS reflects that estimate. Pedro Bailaderia asked Navah Hopkins about sending an estimate at submission and updating it once more information comes into the CRM. Hopkins supported using the Conversions API or offline imports. She also advised setting a ROAS target that reflects the values you send.

One November storm changed how California’s Sierra Nevada looked from orbit

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Science  |  Read Source →

An early storm brought nearly a foot of snow to Donner Pass, offering a first look at the snowpack that helps supply California with water.

Fresh snow stretches across California’s Sierra Nevada in a satellite image taken on the morning of November 14.

Around Lake Tahoe and farther south, white covers much of the high country.

Along part of the range’s western side, green slopes still show through, making it easy to see where the storm left its mark.

A Pacific storm swept through the previous day, bringing heavy rain to the Sacramento area and close to a foot of snow to some mountain locations.

A break between weather systems gave NASA’s Terra satellite a clear look at the mountains after one of the season’s first major snowfalls.

The instrument that captured the view, the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer, or MODIS, takes in long stretches of the mountain range at once.

From that vantage point, the snow that fell on individual peaks and mountain passes comes into view as a broad band of white.

NASA Earth Observatory paired the November 14 view with an image from two days earlier, when snow was largely confined to the highest ridges southeast of Lake Tahoe.

The earlier view gives a sense of how much changed in the time it took for one storm to cross California.

A separate regional analysis from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) estimated that snow covered about 43 percent of its Sierra Nevada analysis area on November 14.

A month earlier, the estimate was less than 1 percent. The comparison shows how quickly snow can spread across terrain that was largely bare in mid-October.

At Donner Pass, northwest of Lake Tahoe, the Central Sierra Snow Laboratory recorded nearly 12 inches of snow on November 13.

Officials closed Interstate 80 in both directions over Donner Summit that evening.

Farther south, Mammoth Mountain reported 9 to 13 inches of fresh snow. In Yosemite National Park, officials closed several roads ahead of the storm.

The reports from Donner Pass and Mammoth give a sense of how much snow fell at specific locations across the long stretch of mountains visible from above.

The storm also brought heavy rain to the Sacramento area, west of the Sierra Nevada.

Elevation helped determine what reached the ground. Where the air was warm enough, precipitation fell as rain.

Higher in the mountains, colder conditions allowed snow to accumulate.

The green slopes along the western side of the range are a reminder that a storm can bring very different weather to places only a short distance apart.

The broad cover visible on November 14 raises a question that matters well beyond the mountains.

How much water is in all that snow? Hydrologists answer it by measuring snow water equivalent, the depth of water that would remain if the snow at a given spot melted.

A foot of light, powdery snow can hold less water than a shallower layer of dense, wet snow.

California’s Department of Water Resources uses that measurement to help forecast how much water could flow out of the mountains as the snow melts.

Some of that runoff reaches major reservoirs in spring and early summer, helping supply water during the state’s dry months.

Timing matters because California receives much of its precipitation during the cooler part of the year.

Rain can reach streams and reservoirs soon after a storm.

Snow can stay in the mountains for months before melting, releasing some of that water later, when storms are less frequent.

The November storm arrived about six weeks into the water year, the 12-month period hydrologists use to track precipitation and runoff beginning October 1.

The Sierra Nevada snowpack generally peaks around April 1, leaving several months for new storms to add to it or for warm spells to melt some of what has already accumulated.

More snow was already in the forecast when NASA released the image on November 18.

The National Weather Service had issued a winter storm warning for parts of the Sierra Nevada through November 20, with around four feet of snow possible at some locations above 8,000 feet.

The November 14 image captures the mountains at the start of that longer process.

In a single day, snow spread across enough of the Sierra Nevada to change how the range looked from orbit.

Over the coming months, the same slopes will collect or lose snow with each shift in the weather.

By spring, what remains will help determine how much meltwater reaches California’s reservoirs as the state heads into its dry season.

As overdose deaths fall, White House makes misleading claims about who deserves credit

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Families look on as President Donald Trump speaks during a ceremony to sign the "Halt All Lethal Trafficking of Fentanyl Act" in the East Room of the White House on July 16, 2025. Evan Vucci/AP hide caption

When the White House announced big improvements in the number of people dying from fatal drug overdoses earlier this week, officials pointed to hopeful new data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention showing U.S. drug deaths have plunged to their lowest level in roughly a decade, saving nearly 50,000 lives each year.

But leading drug policy researchers say the White House statement also made misleading claims about the timing and cause of the remarkable public health success when it said that gains occurred primarily over the last 18 months due to policies enacted under President Trump's leadership.

"The historic decline in drug overdose deaths demonstrates that America is unequivocally winning the fight against illicit drugs," White House Office of National Drug Control Policy director Sara Carter said in a statement.

Carter credited heightened border security under Trump and claims of success dismantling "narco-terrorist" organizations for the recovery. "Critically, this represents a an approximately 41.5% decrease in total drug overdose deaths compared to the peak under the previous administration," the statement said.

What the White House failed to disclose is that declines in overdose fatalities began much earlier, under the Biden administration, with most of the gains occurring before Trump returned to office.

"Since the spring of 2023, we have seen a steady decline in overdose deaths," said Vanda Felbab-Brown, a drug policy expert at the Brookings Institution. "The fact that it has been sustained is very important."

According to CDC data, reductions in drug deaths began in 2023 and have continued steadily for at least 39 consecutive months — not 18 months as the ONDCP statement said. By the time Biden left office, fatal overdoses had already dropped by roughly one-third, an improvement described by addiction experts at the time as unprecedented.

Nabarun Dasgupta, a street drug researcher at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, was one of the first scientists to detect the stunning drop in overdose deaths. He said the trend toward fewer deaths began in some regions and among some demographic groups as early as 2021 and rapidly gained momentum nationwide.

"It was really the period early 2024 through spring 2025 where we were seeing the greatest rate of decline that we've ever seen," Dasgupta said.

Deaths from fentanyl smuggled into the U.S. from Mexico first began to rise in 2013 during President Obama's time in office and accelerated during Trump's first term. Fentanyl overdoses spiked sharply the first two years of Biden's presidency, during the COVID-19 pandemic. Despite progress, fentanyl remains the leading cause of drug deaths nationwide.

Dasgupta, Felbab-Brown and other and addiction experts credit the Biden administration for rolling out policy responses that included wider access to healthcare, making the opioid overdose medication naloxone more readily available, and negotiating a deal with China that disrupted the flow of industrial chemicals used to make fentanyl.

President Joe Biden met China's President President Xi Jinping in Woodside, Calif., in November 2023, on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperative conference. China agreed to curtail shipments of the chemicals used to make fentanyl, the drug at the heart of the U.S. overdose epidemic. Doug Mills/Pool/The New York Times hide caption

The Biden team also scored significant drug interdiction wins against the Mexican cartels that smuggle fentanyl. "We have certainly been seeing over many years, going back to the Biden administration, important efforts to crack down against them," said Felbab-Brown.

Dasgupta and Felbab-Brown both said improvements in reducing drug mortality have continued under Trump. They said it's important to have an accurate timeline of the overdose crisis because researchers are still trying to understand why deaths involving fentanyl surged and why so many fewer people are now dying.

NPR asked the White House about progress in the fentanyl fight under Biden. In an emailed statement, ONDCP director Carter, a former podcaster, journalist and Fox News contributor, blamed Biden for failing to secure the U.S.-Mexico border and for allowing drug deaths to surge in the first place.

"While drug overdose deaths eventually began to decline following the peak, the Biden Administration doesn't get to take credit for overseeing the highest drug overdose numbers in history, then bringing them down from their own failure," Carter said.

Responding to CDC data showing drug deaths began to plummet much earlier than stated the White House announcement, Carter said "this could be misinterpreted as progress on a graph, but it was not progress."

According to Carter, the Trump administration is now well-positioned to build on overdose death reductions made over the last three years, in part by maintaining border security and by continuing to "relentlessly attack" drug gangs.

"We are dismantling and eliminating the violent cartels and drug trafficking organizations through coordinated operations," she said.

But Felbab-Brown at Brookings said she doesn't share the Trump administration's confidence that there has been significant progress "dismantling and eliminating" narco-terrorist organizations.

"I cannot think of one narco-terrorist organization that has been dismantled," she said. "They all continue operating and they are all very strong. They all continue smuggling drugs."

Military experts have also questioned the effectiveness, and legality, of Trump's ongoing strikes against civilian boats in the Caribbean and eastern Pacific, described as smuggling vessels by the Pentagon.

Dasgupta, the addiction researcher at UNC, said he's also concerned about whether progress reducing drug deaths can be sustained next year as the Trump administration implements deep cuts to Medicaid passed by the Republican Congress.

The Congressional Budget Office has estimated the cuts could mean as many as 10 million Americans losing access to health insurance coverage.

"Medicaid is the largest funder of addiction services in the United States," Dasgupta said. "Cutting Medicaid eligibility or making it harder to access forms of effective addiction treatment is guaranteed to leave people out in the cold."

Each Thanksgiving, nearly 1.3 million Americans participate in a Turkey Trot, typically a morning run with a cute name and sometimes a cute T-shirt. With over 1,500 events organized each year, Thanksgiving is officially the biggest race day of the year. And why do people wake up early on a holiday, when school and work are closed, to run in the cold?

AI will give you plenty of reasons: family bonding, charity, or just dodging kitchen duty. But the real reason most people lace up in the freezing cold? To “earn the feast.” It sounds like an innocent joke, but clinically, treating movement as currency to pre-pay for calories is known as compensatory exercise. When workouts become a tool to cancel out guilt, research shows, it stops being healthy movement and starts predicting disordered eating.

I want to push back on the idea that we need a psychological pass for a holiday meal.

Food is many things: fuel, culture, comfort, and community. And, holistically, culture, comfort, and community can all be pleasurable. Americans have a complex relationship with pleasure; we’re always vacillating over whether pleasure is a human right or a thing to be earned.

We all know, or maybe have been, the friend who cannot (insert something fun) until they’ve zeroed their inbox, cleaned the house, or crossed off the to-do list, and somehow we never quite balance the books. Whether we forgo the fun or dilute it with guilt, we never think we’ve done enough to earn our relaxation.

This issue of feeling undeserving of pleasure comes up in so many therapy sessions. Trying to leave a bad relationship—do I deserve better? Wanting to pursue your passion—have I done enough to earn the right to follow my dream? Exhausted by a life of obligations—can I do something that is not in service of my job or my kids?

If we distill our culture into two teachers, one teacher tells us we need to earn our pleasure by working hard and being good. And since hard and good are never defined, we always feel like we haven’t yet earned the right to pursue pleasure. The other teacher tells us that life is short and to go find the joy. I’d argue that right now, that teacher is not the one hosting the Turkey Trots.

Thanksgiving. Friendsgiving. A holiday where we share one meal with loved ones we don’t see often enough. A holiday where we feel warm nostalgia as we eat the same pie we’ve had since we were kids. A holiday where we get to see elderly relatives, possibly for the last time. A holiday where we laugh in the kitchen, watch football, spend time with people we love, and yes, eat—because it’s a meal. Why do we think we need to earn any of this? We are allowed to pursue pleasure—in food, in friends—simply because we are human.

Brain Computes Motion Direction Independently From the Eye

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Researchers have developed a high-throughput brain-mapping tool that reveals the brain does not simply rely on pre-calculated motion signals from the eye, but also computes the direction of movement independently. The study mapped nearly 46,000 mouse brain cells to uncover a dual visual system in the superior colliculus, separating localized object tracking from global self-motion sensing.

Whether ducking a fast-moving ball, stepping back from an oncoming vehicle, or evading an approaching predator, survival depends on the brain’s ability to determine not just that an object is moving, but its exact trajectory in real time.

For decades, visual neuroscientists knew that specialized neurons in the mammalian retina fire selectively when stimuli move in specific directions, left, right, up, down, or diagonally.

Because these retinal ganglion cells already encode directional vectors, researchers widely assumed the brain simply inherited that pre-calculated motion data passively from the eye. However, no mapping technology existed that could simultaneously trace exact synaptic connections from the retina while identifying the molecular identity of every receiving brain cell.

Now, in a study published in Nature, researchers at UC San Francisco (UCSF) have bridged that technological gap.

Led by co-senior authors Dr. Xin Duan, professor of ophthalmology, and Dr. Evan Feinberg, professor of anatomy, alongside postdoctoral fellow Dr. Fei Wang, the team built a scalable eye-to-brain wiring atlas revealing that specialized circuits in the midbrain compute motion direction completely on their own.

Over the past decade, Duan’s laboratory pioneered anterograde trans-neuronal tracing, a technique in which a fluorescent molecular tracer delivered to the eye crosses synaptic junctions to illuminate only those downstream brain neurons directly receiving retinal input.

To scale this tool from single-circuit tracing into a comprehensive atlas, Wang paired the trans-neuronal tracer with MERFISH (multiplexed error-robust fluorescence in situ hybridization), a spatial transcriptomic method that reads gene activity across thousands of individual cells in a tissue slice simultaneously to pinpoint each neuron’s exact genetic identity.

Applying this combined platform across nearly 46,000 cells in the mouse visual system, the investigators uncovered hundreds of distinct retinal-to-brain connection pairs.

“It’s like an entire atlas we’ve built,” Wang said. “We’ve uncovered hundreds of pairs of connections between the eye and the brain. We ended up focusing on only one page of this atlas, but there are many left to study in the future.”

Focusing on the superior colliculus, an evolutionarily conserved midbrain hub essential for visual orientation and rapid motor reflexes, Wang, Feinberg, and Duan recorded real-time neural firing while showing mice high-contrast black bars scrolling across a screen in eight distinct directions.

Combining functional recordings with their molecular wiring map exposed two separate motion-processing architectures operating side by side:

“We think these sets of cells are doing slightly different things from each other,” Wang explained. “The eye cells detecting direction may specialize in small, local motions like prey or food. The brain cells computing direction sense a larger background movement.”

The investigators hypothesize that this global background motion signal informs an organism of its own self-motion and spatial trajectory as it navigates through the environment.

Beyond resolving a foundational question in visual neuroscience, the high-resolution wiring atlas carries direct translational implications for regenerative medicine and neuroengineering. As researchers advance toward optic nerve regeneration, whole-eye transplantation, and high-resolution visual brain-machine interfaces for patients with glaucoma, trauma, or multiple sclerosis, restoring functional sight requires reconnecting damaged axons to their exact functional targets.

“This is a fundamental mechanism that has been understudied from a basic science perspective, but it also has applied value,” said Duan. “If we ever want to repair or restore vision in people, we need to know which neural connections need to be rewired for functional vision.”

“In addition to providing a blueprint for revealing structural maps of the brain, our study illustrates how the resulting maps can be used to answer longstanding questions regarding brain function,” added Feinberg.

The team notes that their scalable tracer-plus-MERFISH platform can be adapted to map how the eye regulates circadian rhythms, and generalized to chart cell-to-cell connectivity across any organ system in the body.

Does the eye do all the work of detecting which way things move?

No. While specialized cells in the retina detect motion direction and send that data to the brain, UCSF researchers discovered a second group of neurons in the brain’s superior colliculus that independently calculates motion direction using basic visual inputs, even when the eye’s own direction-sensing cells are turned off.

Why does the brain have two different systems for sensing motion?

Researchers believe the two pathways handle different visual jobs: the eye’s direction-sensing cells appear specialized for tracking small, local moving targets (like food, prey, or a flying ball), while the brain’s independent motion-computing cells detect large-scale background movement to help track how your own body is moving through space.

How could this brain-mapping tool help restore vision in the future?

To successfully develop eye transplants, optic nerve repairs, or visual brain-machine interfaces for conditions like glaucoma or multiple sclerosis, scientists need an exact wiring diagram showing which eye cells connect to which functional brain circuits. This new atlas provides that precise rewiring blueprint.

TransA-MERFISH reveals divergent post-retinal direction-selective circuits

A major goal in neuroscience is to map the transformation of information as it flows through the brain. For example, neurons at many levels of the visual hierarchy are attuned to motion direction. However, links between motion-selective computations and cell types at various stages of the visual hierarchy remain unidentified. A key first step in delineating these relationships is to map the wiring diagrams that connect different motion-selective cell types.

Here we establish a systematic, cell-type-specific circuit-mapping method, combining an anterograde trans-neuronal fluorescent tracer (mWGA–mCherry) with spatial transcriptomics (multiplexed error-robust fluorescence in situ hybridization (MERFISH)) to establish TransA-MERFISH. We used TransA-MERFISH to comprehensively map the synaptic target cell types of four retinal ganglion cell subclasses in superior colliculus (SC) at cell-type resolution.

These experiments unexpectedly revealed that retinal on–off direction-selective ganglion cells (ooDSGCs) innervate several direction-selective SC cell types, but do not innervate a SC cell type that was previously reported to display strong direction-selective responses.

We validated these results using orthogonal approaches and demonstrated the sensitivity and specificity of TransA-MERFISH. Functional studies of SC activity before and after ablation of retinal direction-selective circuits confirmed that these computationally distinct, ooDSGC-dependent and -independent direction-selective cell types are commingled in SC.

Together, our study establishes TransA-MERFISH as a robust framework for mapping wiring diagrams at the cell-type level and uncovers divergent computational mechanisms of motion detection in early visual processing.

Fed's Waller Calls for 50-Basis-Point Rate Hike, Says Tokenized Finance Still Needs Traditional Banks

Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller wants the central bank to get more aggressive with interest rate hikes, arguing that stubborn inflation may require a 50-basis-point increase in December rather than the smaller move investors expect.

Speaking during a CNBC interview Wednesday, Waller said the Fed must either deliver a half-percentage-point increase at its December 8-9 meeting or explicitly signal that another hike will follow in January.

“You might need to, like, shock people,” Waller said, referring to the central bank's efforts to prevent businesses and consumers from accepting inflation near 3% as normal.

“My view is then you have to go 50 in December to put it in place and get it done,” he said, warning that insufficiently aggressive action could weaken expectations that inflation will return to the Fed's target.

Waller's concerns follow three consecutive months in which core consumer prices rose 0.3%, a pace equivalent to roughly 3.5% inflation annually. That's considerably higher than the Fed's 2% target, which is formally measured using a separate inflation indicator known as the Personal Consumption Expenditures price index.

The governor had already established his threshold for a more aggressive move in a November 10 speech. He said another 0.3% core CPI reading would warrant considering a half-point hike, arguing that moving only 25 basis points could damage the Fed's credibility.

October inflation subsequently met that condition. Headline consumer prices rose 0.4% during the month, while core inflation climbed 0.3%, both exceeding economists' forecasts.

Waller's latest comments suggest he is increasingly concerned about the psychology of inflation, particularly the risk that companies begin treating annual price increases of 3% as acceptable and routinely pass higher costs to customers.

The proposed increase would lift the Fed's benchmark rate from its current range of 3.75%-4% to 4.25%-4.50%. Two consecutive quarter-point hikes in December and January would reach the same destination, but Waller argues that timing and communication matter when inflation expectations are at risk.

Markets remain less convinced that the Fed will move that aggressively in December. Futures pricing Wednesday morning showed roughly a 95% probability of a 25-basis-point increase and only about a 5% probability of a half-point move

The prospect of higher borrowing costs has been particularly painful for crypto investors. Higher interest rates make Treasury securities and other interest-bearing investments more competitive with speculative assets, while tighter financial conditions can reduce the money available for riskier bets.

Bitcoin has already suffered a sharp selloff amid the changing rate outlook. As Decrypt previously covered, persistent inflation fears have coincided with withdrawals from spot Bitcoin exchange-traded funds and weakening technical indicators, adding pressure to an already fragile market.

Waller's increasingly hawkish monetary-policy stance comes alongside a more accommodating view of blockchain networks, although he sees established financial infrastructure as essential to its adoption.

In a separate November 17 speech at The Clearing House Annual Conference in New York, Waller discussed how tokenization—the representation of financial assets on digital networks—could reshape payments and securities markets. That speech focused on financial technology rather than interest-rate policy.

He pointed to a growing convergence between traditional markets and blockchain platforms, with banks and payment providers experimenting with tokenized deposits, stablecoins, and programmable transactions.

Waller described how tokenized money could allow investors to purchase securities outside conventional trading hours, with payments and assets changing hands simultaneously through smart contracts.

But he rejected the idea that the development of blockchain-based markets necessarily makes existing payment infrastructure obsolete.

“Central bank money need not be used in every transaction or on every platform, but its presence at the foundation of the system is essential,” Waller said.

A Bear Market Is Coming Eventually. History Says This Is the Best Investing Move You Can Make.

📅 Nov 18, 2026  |  Category: Business  |  Read Source →

A bear market is coming eventually, but history suggests the bigger risk to your wealth is how you react when it arrives.

By John Ballard – Nov 18, 2026 at 1:15PM EST

Bear markets are a regular part of investing. Since 1928, the S&P 500 has fallen into one -- a drop of at least 20% from a recent high -- about every 3.5 years, on average, according to Hartford Funds and Ned Davis Research. The typical bear market has erased about 35% of the index's value.

That sounds frightening, but bear markets are usually short. The average one has lasted about 9.6 months. These downturns are less a disaster than they are the cost of admission to building long-term wealth. The bigger risk is how investors react when the next one arrives.

For anyone with many years until retirement, here's why I'd stay fully invested.

According to Hartford and Ned Davis, the S&P 500 has had 28 bull markets and 27 bear markets since 1928. Through all those ups and downs, it delivered a compound annual return of about 10%, according to The Motley Fool's research. The market rises more in a bull market than it loses in a bear market.

It pays to keep investing when things look worst. Capital Group tracked the 18 biggest market declines since the Great Depression and found the S&P 500 was higher five years later every time. MFS found that after the 10 worst calendar years since 1975, a $100,000 investment was worth about $207,000 on average five years later.

Don't think that you'll be better off waiting to invest until the market moves higher. In fact, history says you'll be wealthier by staying fully invested through every down year.

J.P. Morgan Asset Management found that $10,000 invested in the S&P 500 at the start of 2006 grew to $80,619 by the end of 2025 for someone who stayed fully invested. However, missing just the 10 best days cut that to $35,866.

Take 2025: The S&P 500 fell nearly 19% in the spring, yet its best day came on April 9, in the middle of the turmoil. Missing that one day would have cut the year's 17.9% return to 7.6%.

Now, some will think: "I'll just sit in cash and wait until a down year to buy bargains and make a fortune." That's not a smart strategy either. Bear markets are infrequent. Over the past 95 years, stocks have been rising about 78% of the time, according to Hartford.

Bear markets are also hard to predict. A normal pullback of 10% or so can easily be mistaken for the start of a bigger decline.

Waiting for the perfect moment tends to backfire. Morningstar found that the average dollar in U.S. mutual funds and exchange-traded funds (ETFs) earned 8.7% a year over the decade ended 2025, versus 9.9% for the funds themselves, largely because of when investors bought and sold.

A bear market is, indeed, coming, but we don't know when. The best move is to stay invested and not sell when the market goes down, because missing the biggest up days after the bottom will cost you a lot more money down the road than if you just stayed invested.

Time in the market is far more important than market timing. From 1970 through September 2026, every 20-year rolling period for the S&P 500 produced a gain, according to Mellon data published by Ave Maria Mutual Funds.

Even the worst timing can still make money for patient investors. Hartford calculated that investing $10,000 at the peak just before each of the six bear markets since 1995 would have still turned $60,000 into $364,774 by the end of 2025. That beats staying in cash and earning almost nothing.

As Warren Buffett wrote in his 1991 shareholder letter, "The stock market serves as a relocation center at which money is moved from the active to the patient."

The best time to start is always when you have money available. Pick a low-cost index fund such as the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO -0.10%), which tracks the S&P 500, or the Vanguard Total Stock Market ETF (VTI -0.02%), which covers the whole U.S. market.

Set up an automatic purchase of a fixed amount each month or with each paycheck, and keep it running no matter what happens in the markets. Keep money you'll need in the next few years out of stocks, and you'll be on your way to growing your net worth.

Elgin found an old photo of a young woman who looked just like Sophia, and the finale then revealed that the Man in Yellow had been taking her form, according to Entertainment Weekly. At least one familiar face in town may therefore be borrowed from its past, and recognizing a face no longer proves who is behind it. If the Man in Yellow can take on the appearance of people from the town's history, the returning dead may be his instruments rather than ghosts acting on their own. In that case, the final season could settle the apparitions without addressing them directly, simply by explaining what the Man in Yellow is.

The ending still needs an answer, because the characters can no longer trust what they see. The figure behind Sophia's face can also cloud the minds of the living, and in the finale Sophia drugged Henry and left him hallucinating, according to Entertainment Weekly. Elgin, the resident who came closest to the truth about Sophia's face, died in the same finale, and he now stands among the dead whose possible return is an open question. Any sighting of him in the final season would raise the same doubt he was chasing. A vision in town can be false in two ways, with a dead face being worn or a living mind being clouded.

The finale does not need a catalog of every ghost. It does need a rule for when the dead are really themselves, because the characters cannot make their last choices without knowing whom to trust.

The residents' only proven weapon against a Creature works by rules the show has never explained. Boyd used blood worms to bring down the Smiley Creature, Primetimer reports, but what the worms and the blood did to Boyd and to Henry is still an open question, according to FandomWire. That makes the question a matter of survival, because the residents can't plan a final fight around a weapon whose rules none of them understand. Boyd can't simply strike again, either, because the worms were expelled from his body once he passed them on. Martin carried the worms before Boyd and died while transferring them, so the only man who might have said where they came from is gone. If the town is going to use the worms again, the last season may have to return to the question of where more of them could come from.

The steps of the transfer are known even though its workings are not. "My blood is your blood," Martin said as he handed the worms on, as reported by Primetimer, and Boyd repeated the vow almost word for word when he passed them to Smiley. Boyd copied what he had seen Martin do, but repeating the words is not the same as knowing why a Creature dies when the worms reach it. Until the show reveals those rules, any victory over the Creatures would look like luck rather than a payoff.

Fatima faces the same unknown from the other side, because nobody yet knows whether her change into something monstrous is permanent. The town's rules can destroy a Creature and can also remake a person, and the show has explained neither process.

The worms are a plot device, but Fatima is a character, and whether she can come back is the part of this mystery the final season can least afford to leave open.

Most of the town's open questions ask who or what is out there, but this one asks where. Nobody knows what part the lighthouse or the Lake of Tears plays in breaking the curse, or what would happen if someone brought bones to the lighthouse or the tower, according to FandomWire. That puts escape itself at stake. If the two places do figure in breaking the curse, the residents may be missing a step in the way out of town without knowing what that step does.

The lake entered the story in Ethan's dream in the first season's second episode, Primetimer reports, and for a long time afterward it remained only a dream image. The show never said what it was for, even as it added other mysteries around it. That changed in "Fray," the fourth season's second episode, when Ethan was given the job of finding the lake. The show still hasn't explained what the lake does, but a character is now searching for it on screen. The answer is therefore more likely to come through Ethan's search than through anyone's theories.

Ethan is not the only resident tied to the lake. Victor has a drawing of it in his room at the Colony House, according to Primetimer. Ethan dreamed of a place that already meant something to another person in town. How Victor came to know the lake has not been determined. The lake now holds two questions at once: what it is, and what Victor knows about it. In this last stretch of the show, several questions may share a single answer, and the lake and Victor may turn out to be one of them.

The two landmarks do not carry equal weight in the story. The lake has a dream, an errand and a second resident behind it, while the lighthouse is so far mainly the other half of a question about the curse. For an ending built around where the characters arrive, that difference matters. Ethan is already looking for the lake, so the finale would have a hard time leaving it unexplained. The lighthouse may get only as much explanation as the curse requires, which would make it the half of this mystery that fans keep arguing over longest.

Every night in Fromville, residents rely on talismans whose origins nobody can explain. The person or force that crafted the charms, and the source of their power, both lack an answer, according to FandomWire. ScreenRant movie and TV news editor Matthew Rudoy counts the makers and the power source as still unknown as well. Residents would have a hard time rebuilding or repairing a protection whose source they can't name. The charms' origin is therefore a stake in the ending and not a piece of trivia.

Part of the talisman's mystery has already been solved on screen. The meaning of the show's recurring symbols remains on the list of open questions, but ScreenRant reports that Tabitha realized in season 4 that the talisman symbol depicts her and Jade. That answer ties the town's protection to two of its current residents. It still doesn't say who put their image on the charms or why.

The question became urgent in the season 4 finale, when the Man in Yellow took the charms away. Disguised as Sophia, he removed talismans and threw them down a Faraway Tree, according to Reel 360 and ComicBook.com. It isn't clear whether she took most of them or all of them, so the final season also has to settle whether any protection is left. An enemy who went to the trouble of removing the charms likely saw them as a real obstacle, and that makes the question of who built them more pressing.

The red lightning looks like the wildest riddle here, but viewers saw when it began. FandomWire lists what sets it off as unanswered, while ScreenRant places its start right after the bottle tree was pulled up. The show hasn't explained how the tree and the storm are connected. The lightning was not the only upheaval, though. Pulling up the tree also turned the daylight to darkness and shook the ground, and Tabitha and Jade ended up stuck underground in the caves. The lightning is probably one part of that wider disruption, and the show may explain it along with the rest. Sophia reacted to the tree's removal with glee, a sign that worse may be coming for the residents.

The ending doesn't need to decode the lightning so much as account for it: who built the town's defenses, and what the red lightning means for the residents now that those defenses have been taken.

Fromville's daily workings remain as unexplained as its deepest secrets. The exits, the spiders, the music box storyline and the source of the town's electricity and supplies all stay murky, according to FandomWire. The explanations for the electricity and fuel that most Reddit $RDDT users accept are fan opinion, and the show has never confirmed them. Questions such as these rarely move the plot forward. Their answers still decide whether the town feels like a built world or an improvised one, and a final season that skips them risks the second.

Residents receive some necessities and not others, and nobody has explained why. Whatever keeps the town supplied has not brought medical care, because there are no doctors, hospitals or surgeons in town, according to TV Insider. That turns a logistics question into a story question. Any account of how the residents get their supplies would also have to explain why medical care is missing. Such an answer would say something about the nature of the place where these people are trapped.

What the town knows about the Faraway Trees begins with one man. Residents use the name Victor Kavanaugh gave the hollow-trunked bottle trees, and Victor is the town's longest-tenured resident, according to TV Insider. No one in town has seen more of the place than he has. The trees' secret is therefore partly a question of how much Victor knows and how much of it he shares with the others.

The trees may also reach back further than Victor. TV Insider reports that no connection between the trees and the town's former residents has been uncovered yet. If that link exists, the trees belong to the town's history as well as its geography, and to the larger story of who came before and what happened to them.

The power supply is the question the finale can most safely leave to fans, because it has no bearing on whether anyone can get out. The trees and the exits are a different matter. They bear on escape itself and on what the town's longest-tenured resident has kept to himself.

The last season needs to explain the trees, and it can leave the electricity a mystery.
